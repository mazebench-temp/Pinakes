summary: Zeus runs through Odysseus' tale. He sends the homecoming of many sorrows, the evil fate at Ismarus and the storm of the North Wind, and his rain grows the vines of the Cyclopes. In the cave Odysseus appeals to him as the avenger of suppliants and strangers, “the god of guests”, and the Cyclops answers that the Cyclopes care nothing for aegis-bearing Zeus; the other Cyclopes take the blinded Cyclops' cry for a sickness sent by great Zeus, and Odysseus taunts him that Zeus and the other gods have punished him. On the island Odysseus sacrifices to Zeus the ram that carried him out of the cave, but Zeus “took no heed of the offering”.

**The god of the homecoming.** Odysseus begins his tale with Zeus: “But come, let me tell you also of my homecoming of many sorrows, / which Zeus sent upon me as I came from Troy” (9.37–38; see [[nostos]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). Nestor laid the Achaeans' homecoming on him in the same way: “then indeed Zeus devised in his mind a grim homecoming / for the Argives” (3.132–133). At Ismarus, when the Cicones of the inland come against the ships, “then an evil fate from Zeus stood beside us, / men of dread fate, so that we might suffer many sorrows” (9.52–53; see [[aisa]], [[cicones]] and [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]]). Διὸς αἶσα is the portion that Zeus allots, the measure the Achaeans of the Iliad would have overrun when they almost won glory “even beyond the portion of Zeus” (*Iliad* 17.321). Then comes the storm: “But against the ships Zeus the cloud-gatherer roused the North Wind / with a heaven-sent storm, and with clouds he covered / land and sea alike; and night arose from the sky” (9.67–69; see [[cloud-gatherer]] and [[boreas]]). The last two verses are those of Poseidon's storm in Book 5 (5.293–294), and the storm that Zeus raises at Thrinacia is told in nearly the same three verses (12.313–315; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). To the Cyclops, Odysseus gives his wandering the same cause: the Achaeans, eager to go home, have come “by another way, by other paths”, and “so, I suppose, Zeus wished to devise it” (9.261–262).

**The rain of Zeus.** The Cyclopes neither plant nor plow, and their wheat, barley and vines grow unsown, “and the rain of Zeus makes it grow for them” (9.111; see [[cyclopes]]). The Cyclops says the same verse of his own wine when he has tasted the wine Odysseus brought (9.357–358; see [[wine]]). Διὸς ὄμβρος is the rain of the Iliad's similes, when “the rain of Zeus falls heavy” (*Iliad* 5.91, 12.286). The Cyclopes live “trusting in the immortal gods” (9.107): Merry finds no devoutness in the words, as the Cyclops' boast shows (9.275), but a careless confidence in the bounty of nature, which spares them all trouble (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.107).

**The god of guests.** Odysseus ends his appeal in the cave with Zeus: “But respect the gods, good sir; we are your suppliants, / and Zeus is the avenger of suppliants and strangers, / the god of guests, who attends on revered strangers” (9.269–271; see [[god-of-guests]], [[supplication]], [[xenia]], [[good-sir]] and [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]]). ἐπιτιμήτωρ, “avenger”, is found only here in Homer. ξείνιος, “god of guests”, is the title under which Menelaus, over the body of Peisander, reproaches the Trojans, who did not “fear the harsh wrath of Zeus who thunders loud, / god of guests, who will one day destroy your steep city” (*Iliad* 13.623–625; see the translation's key-word row). The last verse is the one with which Echeneus and Alcinous called for a libation to Zeus “who attends on revered suppliants” (7.165, 7.181), with strangers in the place of suppliants. Bekker put it at the foot of the page, and Köchly found it feeble after the verse before and taken from those verses (Merry's critical note on 9.271; see the Book 7 note and the translation's note on 9.271). αἰδοίοισιν means only that all guests are revered as guests (Merry on 9.271). On Ithaca Odysseus names the same god in his false tale to Eumaeus: the king of Egypt, who spared him, “had regard for the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests, who most of all is indignant at evil deeds” (14.283–284; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]), and Eumaeus answers that he will be kind to the stranger “in fear of Zeus, the god of guests, and in pity for you yourself” (14.389).

**The Cyclops' answer.**

{{quote:26927005-63f9-5d9a-8cfc-286d40baf1fa}}

Merry finds the boast inconsistent with what the other Cyclopes acknowledge of the power of Zeus (9.410–411) and with the Cyclops' own claim that Poseidon is his father, but quite true to nature as a piece of impious boasting; the scholia, as he reports, say that Polyphemus makes his own offense common to all, since the other Cyclopes were not godless (Merry on 9.275; see the translation's note on 9.275, [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]] and [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). He compares the Cyclops of Euripides' satyr play: “Zeus' thunderbolt I do not shudder at, stranger, nor do I know in what way Zeus is a mightier god than I” (*Cyclops* 320–321; see [[euripides-cyclops]]). In the play Odysseus calls on [[god-of-guests|Zeus of strangers]] to look on what is being done, or be thought a god of nothing (*Cyclops* 353–355). Homer's Cyclops speaks of the hatred of Zeus, Διὸς ἔχθος, as a thing he need not avoid, and of the gods as weaker than his race (see [[olympians]] and [[aegis-bearing]]).

**Hands lifted to Zeus.** When the Cyclops has eaten two of the companions, “we, weeping, lifted up our hands to Zeus, / seeing the cruel deeds, and helplessness held our hearts” (9.294–295; see [[prayer]]). It is the gesture of prayer, as when the people of both armies “prayed, and lifted up their hands to the gods” before the duel of Paris and Menelaus (*Iliad* 3.318). No words of prayer are told, and no answer.

**A sickness from Zeus.** When the blinded Cyclops cries out that Nobody is killing him, the other Cyclopes answer: “If then nobody is doing you violence, alone as you are, / there is no way to escape a sickness sent by great Zeus; / but you must pray to your father, lord Poseidon” (9.410–412; see [[nousos]], [[nobody]], [[cyclopes]] and [[poseidon]]). They give Zeus the sickness and Poseidon the cure. The genitive Διός names the source; Merry compares the saying θεόθεν δʼ οὐκ ἔστʼ ἀλέασθαι, “but from the gods there is no escaping” (16.447, a plain rendering), refers to his note on the father's sickness in the simile of Book 5 for what the ancients thought of disease (5.394–397), and judges that they took him to have gone hopelessly mad (Merry on 9.411; see the translation's note on 9.410–411).

**Zeus and the other gods.** From the ship Odysseus taunts the blinded Cyclops: “Yes, truly your evil deeds were to catch up with you, / hard man, since you did not shrink from eating strangers / in your own house; therefore Zeus and the other gods have punished you” (9.477–479; see [[kertomia]] and [[hard]]). The words “in your own house” mark the offense against the host's part: thereby, Merry says, the Cyclops specially violated the rites of hospitality (on 9.478). Odysseus calls the blinding the punishment of [[god-of-guests|the god of guests]] whom the Cyclops refused to fear (9.270–277).

The verse of the dedication comes again when Alcinous sacrifices an ox to Zeus before the Phaeacians take Odysseus home (9.552 = 13.25; see [[lord-of-dark-clouds]], [[son-of-cronus]], [[sacrifice]] and [[thigh-pieces]]). Zeus refused to notice the offering, Merry explains, because the prayer of Polyphemus had been heard and would be answered (on 9.553; see the translation's note on 9.553 and [[poseidon]]); for ἀλλʼ ὅ γε a scholium reads ἀλλʼ ἄρα (Merry's critical note on 9.554). At the first council Zeus spoke of Odysseus as a man “first in sacrifices / given to the immortal gods”, and laid his long absence on Poseidon's anger for the Cyclops (1.66–75). Odysseus tells the sacrifice knowing what came after: of his twelve ships eleven were destroyed with their crews in the harbor of the Laestrygonians (10.121–132), and Zeus struck the last with his thunderbolt when the companions had eaten the cattle of the Sun (12.415–419; see [[thunderbolt]] and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]).
