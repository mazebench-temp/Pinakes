---
summary: Aristotle quotes two of this book's verses in the Rhetoric and the Poetics, the boast in which Odysseus names himself and the Cyclops' complaint of the small man who blinded him, and two more in the Politics, the Cyclopes' rule over their households and the banqueters listening to the singer. He names the whole tale with the later title of the book, cites the Cyclops dithyrambs, turns the Cyclops' likeness to a wooded peak into a wild boar's, and in the Homeric Problems asks how Poseidon's son could be a Cyclops and why the god was angry.
---

- **The name.** People are calm toward those who will not perceive by whom and for what they are punished, “for anger is against the individual”; “that is why the poet has rightly written ‘say that Odysseus, sacker of cities’, as though he would not have been avenged unless the other perceived both by whom and for what” (*Rhetoric* 1380b20–25, quoting 9.504): [[say-that-odysseus-blinded-you]], [[praotes]] and [[orge]].
- **Small and a nobody.** The Cyclops' “but now one who is small and a nobody / and unseemly”, with ἀεικής where our text has ἄκικυς, put into standard words to show how a verse loses its distinction (*Poetics* 1458b25–28, quoting 9.515): [[small-and-of-no-account]] and [[kyrion]].
- **The tale to Alcinous.** The later title of this book names the whole tale: “the tale to Alcinous, which is retold to Penelope in sixty lines” (*Rhetoric* 1417a13–15), and recognition through memory “in the tale told to Alcinous” (*Poetics* 1455a2–3), a scene that Mayor, as Merry reports, refers rather to Book 8 (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.1): [[the-title-of-odyssey-9]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]].
- **The Cyclops dithyrambs.** Dithyrambs and nomes, like the other arts, can represent men better or worse than we are, as Timotheus and Philoxenus did with their Cyclopes (*Poetics* 1448a14–16): [[cyclops-dithyrambs]].
- **The Cyclopes' households.** Every household is ruled by its eldest, “and this is what Homer says, ‘each one gives the law / to his children and his wives’” (*Politics* 1.2, 1252b19–27, quoting 9.114–115); in most cities each man lives as he wishes, “laying down the law Cyclops-fashion for his children and his wife” (*Nicomachean Ethics* 10.9, 1180a24–29): [[plato-the-cyclopes]] and [[aristotle-man-without-a-city]].
- **The singer at the feast.** Music was given a place in education for the leisure of free men, and Odysseus says that the best pastime is when, men being glad, “the banqueters throughout the house listen to a singer, / sitting in order” (*Politics* 8.3, 1338a13–30, quoting 9.7–8): [[the-praise-of-the-feast-in-later-writers]] and [[singers]].
- **A wooded peak.** Wild boars that have been castrated grow larger and fiercer, “as Homer too composed”, and Aristotle quotes a verse on the boar of Calydon joined to the Cyclops' likeness to a wooded peak, with θηρί, “beast”, in place of ἀνδρί, “man” (*History of Animals* 6.28, a plain rendering; *Iliad* 9.539; 9.190–191): [[like-a-wooded-peak]] and [[lurker-in-the-grass]].
- **The temper of iron.** For the smith's simile, “for this in turn is the strength of iron” (9.393), Merry cites Aristotle's βαφή, “temper”: most states that win an empire in war are lost when they have it, “for they lose their temper, like iron, when they are at peace” (*Politics* 7.14, 1334a6–10, a plain rendering; Merry on 9.393): [[the-smith-tempering-an-axe]] and [[iron]].
- **Problems of the Cyclops.** How could a son of Poseidon be a Cyclops? Why did Odysseus dare to tell Poseidon's descendants, the Phaeacians, that he had blinded him? Why did he say that not even the earth-shaker would heal the eye, and why was Poseidon angry over the punishment of so wicked a son? (*Homeric Problems* frs. 395–397 Gigon, from the scholia on 9.106, 9.333, 9.345 and 9.525): [[aristotle-the-cyclops-and-poseidon]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]].
