summary: Troy is where the tale begins: the homecoming that Zeus sent upon Odysseus “as I came from Troy” (9.38), and the wind “from Ilios” that brings him to the Cicones (9.39). To the Cyclops he presents his men as Achaeans “driven off course from Troy”, the people of Agamemnon, who sacked so great a city (9.259–266).
# The city without its name: "so great a city did he sack" (9.265).
refs: [9.265]
---

**As I came from Troy.** Odysseus begins: “But come, let me tell you also of my homecoming of many sorrows, / which Zeus sent upon me as I came from Troy” (9.37–38; see [[nostos]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). ἀπὸ Τροίηθεν joins a preposition to an adverb that already means “from”, as ἀπʼ οὐρανόθεν, “from the sky” (11.18; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.38). With ἰόντα for ἰόντι the words end the verse in which Priam reminds Achilles of the homecoming that Peleus hopes for every day, “to see his dear son come home from Troy” (*Iliad* 24.491–492; see [[peleus]]).

**From Ilios.** “From Ilios the wind bore me and brought me near the Cicones, / to Ismarus” (9.39–40; see [[ismarus]] and [[cicones]]). Ἰλιόθεν, “from Ilios”, is found in Homer only here and in Sleep's words about Heracles, who “was sailing from Ilios, when he had sacked the city of the Trojans”, until Hera roused grievous winds over the sea and carried him off to Cos (*Iliad* 14.250–255; see [[heracles-sacks-troy]]). The two sackers of Troy sail away “from Ilios”, and the wind takes each of them elsewhere. The first city Odysseus sacks after Troy is given the proem's words for Troy, ἱερὸν πτολίεθρον, “the sacred citadel” (9.165; 1.2; see [[hieros]]).

**Driven off course from Troy.** To the Cyclops he names Troy at once: “We, you must know, are Achaeans, driven off course from Troy / by winds of every kind over the great gulf of the sea, / eager to go home” (9.259–261; see [[achaeans]]), the ἀποπλαγχθέντες of the proem's πλάγχθη, “driven far astray, after he had sacked the sacred citadel of Troy” (1.1–2). He offers the city's fall as his credential: “we claim to be the people of Agamemnon, son of Atreus, / whose fame is now the greatest under heaven; / so great a city did he sack, and destroyed many / people” (9.263–266; see [[agamemnon]], [[kleos]] and [[under-heaven]]). μέγιστον is predicative, “his fame, far as the heaven covers, is the greatest” (Merry on 9.264; see the translation's note on 9.264). The Cyclops answers with the Cyclopes' contempt for Zeus, and asks only where the ship is moored (9.273–280; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]).
