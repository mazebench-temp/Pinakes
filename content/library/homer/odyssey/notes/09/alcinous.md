**The addressee.** Book 8 ended with the king's questions: the stranger's name, his land and city, the lands and men of his wanderings, and why he weeps at the fate of the Argives and of Ilios, “Tell me the name by which they called you there” (8.550; see [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]). Odysseus' first words refer back to them, and at 9.16 he complies with the request for his name (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.1; see [[odysseus]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). He speaks to the king in the verse with which he praised the dancers and Euryalus made his amends, “Powerful Alcinous, preeminent among all the people” (9.2 = 8.382, 8.401; see [[powerful]] and [[preeminent]]), and he will speak it twice more when the king, in the middle of the tale, asks him to stay and to go on (11.355, 11.378), and once when he takes his leave (13.38).

**The feast and the sorrows.** His praise of a feast at which “gladness holds the whole people” and the banqueters listen to a singer (9.6–7) answers the king's own boast, that the feast and the lyre are always dear to the Phaeacians (8.248): Odysseus, Eustathius rightly says in Merry's report, is not propounding a philosophy, only chiming in with the opinion of his host (Merry on 9.5; see [[singers]] and [[feast]]). Then he turns to the king's other question: “But your heart is set on my groaning sorrows, / to ask about them, so that I may groan and mourn still more” (9.12–13), ἔτι μᾶλλον, “still more”, because he will renew their memory in telling them (Merry on 9.13; see [[algos]]). What the king will hear is “my homecoming of many sorrows, / which Zeus sent upon me as I came from Troy” (9.37–38; see [[nostos]]).
