---
summary: The island off the land of the Cyclopes is the fleet's harbor in Book 9. The ships come to it blind, on a moonless night, with “some god” to guide them (9.142–151); the men hunt its goats and feast for a day, looking across at the smoke of the Cyclopes (9.152–169); Odysseus leaves the other ships there (9.170–180), and comes back to them with the Cyclops' flocks to sacrifice, feast and sail on (9.543–566).
---

**A landing in the dark.** “There we sailed in, and some god guided us / through the dark night, and nothing could be seen” (9.142–143). νύκτα διʼ ὀρφναίην is not a standing epithet of night but describes this misty night, and προυφαίνετο is “nor was there light enough to see”, so that they needed a god's guidance (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.143). A thick mist lay round the ships and the moon was held fast in clouds (9.144–145; see [[moon]]): “Then no one saw the island with his eyes, / nor did we see the long waves rolling toward the shore, / before we ran our well-benched ships ashore” (9.146–148). They beach the ships, step out “where the sea breaks upon the shore”, and sleep until dawn (9.149–151; see [[shore]]). The island's good harbor, where ships may simply be run ashore (9.136–139), receives a fleet that cannot see it.

**A day of plenty.** At dawn “we roamed over the island itself, marveling at it” (9.153), the island itself as against its shore and the water around it (Merry on 9.153). The nymphs, the daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus, rouse the goats for the men's meal; they take their curved bows and long-socketed javelins from the ships, arrange themselves in three bands, and the god gives them game to satisfy the heart (9.154–158; Merry on 9.157; see [[nymphs]], [[goats]] and [[bow]]). “Twelve ships followed me, and to each / nine goats fell by lot; but for me alone they chose out ten” (9.159–160): λάγχανον is the only place in Homer where λαγχάνω means “fall by lot” (Merry on 9.160; see the translation's note on 9.159–160), and twelve is the number of the ships Odysseus led to Troy (*Iliad* 2.637; see [[cephallenians]]). They feast all day on untold meat and on the wine of Ismarus, still in the jars (9.161–165; see [[ismarus]] and [[feast]]).

**The land across the water.** “And we looked across to the land of the Cyclopes, who were near, / and saw their smoke, and heard their voices, and those of the sheep and the goats” (9.166–167; see [[cyclopes]]). They judged the land to be near by the smoke and the voices, and αὐτῶν sets the Cyclopes against their flocks (Merry on 9.167). The next morning Odysseus calls an assembly and goes over “in my own ship with my companions”; the rest of the fleet stays at the island (9.170–180; Merry on 9.173; see [[assembly]]), and the crossing is short, to “the place, which was near” (9.181).

**The return.** After the second rock the wave carries the ship forward “to reach the shore”, the shore of the island (9.542; Merry on 9.542; see the translation's note on 9.542). “But when we came to the island, where the other / well-benched ships waited all together, and around them our companions / sat mourning, always waiting for us” (9.543–545), they run the ship ashore on the sand, divide the Cyclops' flocks, and give Odysseus the ram, which he sacrifices on the shore to Zeus, who “took no heed of the offering” (9.546–555; see [[sacrifice]], [[ram]] and [[zeus]]). The island has the book's two feasts, before and after the cave, each with the verses “So all day long, until the sun went down, / we sat feasting on untold meat and sweet wine” (9.161–162 = 9.556–557); after the second they sleep on the shore once more and sail on at dawn, “grieving at heart, / glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions” (9.558–566).
