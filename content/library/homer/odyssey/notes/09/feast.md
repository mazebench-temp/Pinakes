---
summary: Odysseus begins by praising the Phaeacians' feast: no fulfillment is more gracious than gladness among the people, the banqueters listening to a singer, the tables laden with bread and meat, and the wine carried round (9.3–11). His tale has the crews' meals on shore, from the meal at the land of the Lotus-eaters to the feasts of goat's meat and of the Cyclops' flocks “until the sun went down” (9.86, 9.155–162, 9.556–557), and the Cyclops' suppers of men (9.291, 9.311, 9.344).
# δαιτυμόνες (9.7), δεῖπνον, δειπνήσειαν, δόρπον and δαινύμενοι are claimed by the entry and the earlier notes.

**The finest thing.** The tale opens with the guest's praise of his hosts' feast:

{{quote:5f808d5e-40d1-57ee-b5ef-e30b47584108}}

The picture has the feast's parts in order: the people, the singer, the seats, the tables, the wine (see [[singers]], [[cupbearing]] and [[demos]]). τέλος here means the highest perfection of a thing rather than its end (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.5; see the translation's table of key words). Plato's Socrates quotes the verses as unfit for the young, since they make “the wisest man” call this the finest thing of all (*Republic* 390a–b); Merry reports that Lucian made the same objection (*Parasite* 10), but agrees with Eustathius that Odysseus is not propounding a philosophy, only chiming in with his host, who had named the feast, the lyre and the dance among his people's delights (on 9.5; 8.248; see [[different-gifts]]). Verses 5–8 bear obeli in the Ambrosian manuscript Q, which Merry takes for an objection to the sentiment rather than an athetesis (his critical note on 9.5–8; see the translation's note on 9.5).

**Meals of the voyage.** The crews eat on every shore: at the land of the Lotus-eaters “my companions took their meal beside the swift ships” (9.86); on the island of goats the nymphs rouse the goats “so that my companions might have their meal”, and “So all day long, until the sun went down, / we sat feasting on untold meat and sweet wine” (9.154–162), the couplet repeated on the island after the escape, with the Cyclops' flocks (9.556–557; see [[sunset-lines]] and [[untold]]).

**The Cyclops' meals.** The feast's opposite is the Cyclops' supper: he seizes two men, “cut them limb from limb and made ready his supper” (δόρπον, 9.291), and again at dawn “made ready his meal” (δεῖπνον, 9.311) and at evening “his supper” (9.344), the verse for preparing a meal used of eating men (see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]] and [[the-hungry-lion]]). Odysseus' offer of the wine turns the scene back toward a host's table: “here, drink wine, since you have eaten human flesh” (9.347; see [[wine]]).
