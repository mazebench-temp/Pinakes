summary: At Ismarus the Achaeans protected Maron, the priest of Apollo, “with his child and his wife” (9.199), and his wine was known only to him, “his dear wife and one housekeeper” (9.207); from the sacked city they took the wives of the Cicones (9.41). Among the Cyclopes each “gives the law / to his children and his wives” (9.114–115).
# ἀλόχων (9.115); ἄλοχός (9.207) is claimed by the earlier notes.
grc: [ἀλόχων]
en: [wives]
refs: [9.199]
---

The book shows wives in three households. Maron's is the priest's house spared at Ismarus: the Achaeans protected him “with his child and his wife / in reverence” (9.199–200), and only he, “his dear wife and one housekeeper” knew of the unmixed wine (9.205–207; see [[maron]] and [[aidos]]). The wives of the other men of Ismarus were taken with the possessions of the city and divided (ἀλόχους, 9.41–42; see [[captive-women]] and [[division-of-spoils]]). And among the Cyclopes each one “gives the law / to his children and his wives” (παίδων ἠδʼ ἀλόχων, 9.114–115): the plural need not mean that a Cyclops had many wives, being perhaps only assimilated in number and sound to παίδων; Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* quotes the verse with the singular ἀλόχου (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.114; *Nicomachean Ethics* 1180a28–29; see [[themis]]). The goddesses who held Odysseus back longed for him to be their husband, and did not persuade him (9.29–33; see [[posis]]).
