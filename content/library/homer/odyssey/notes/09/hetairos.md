summary: The book is the story of the companions as much as of their leader: six of each ship killed at Ismarus because “the great fools” would not leave (9.44, 9.60–61), the scouts who eat the lotus (9.88–102), the twelve chosen for the cave (9.195), the six the Cyclops eats (9.288–293, 9.311, 9.344), the four chosen by lot to hold the stake (9.331–335), the men carried out under the rams (9.429–431), and the plea, “Hard man, why do you want to provoke a savage man?” (9.494). The raid and the cave end alike, “though we had lost our dear companions” (9.63, 9.566).
# ἑτάροις (9.288; again 11.113, 12.140, 19.196) and ἑταίροισιν (9.421; again 10.155, 23.253).
grc: [ἑτάροις, ἑταίροισιν]
---

**Disobedience and loss.** At Ismarus Odysseus bade them flee, “but they, the great fools, would not obey” (9.43–44; see [[nepios]] and [[peitho]]), and the Cicones of the inland killed six well-greaved companions from each ship (9.60–61). Crates took the number for an average, seventy-two from twelve ships, and Zoilus objected to the suspicious symmetry (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.60). The refrain counts the cost: “From there we sailed on, grieving at heart, / glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions” (9.62–63, 9.565–566; see [[from-there-we-sailed-on]] and [[thanatos]]), and after the Lotus-eaters, where no one was lost, only its first verse is said (9.105); the dead of Ismarus are first called three times each (9.64–66; see [[the-call-to-the-dead]]).

**Leader and men.** He sends two men and a herald to learn what people the Lotus-eaters are, and drags the ones who ate the lotus back to the ships in tears (9.88–99; see [[spies-and-scouts]] and [[the-lotus-eaters]]). On the island of goats the rest stay with the fleet while he goes “in my own ship with my companions” (9.172–173), with an emphasis on “my own”: the remainder of the fleet waits at the island (Merry on 9.173). For the cave he chooses “twelve of my companions, the best” (9.195; see [[aristos]]). They beg him to take the cheeses and go, and he will not (9.224–229; see [[kerdos]] and [[supplication]]).

**The cave.** The Cyclops eats two of them at each of three meals (9.288–293, 9.311, 9.344; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). The four who are to hold the stake are chosen by lot, “those whom I myself would have wished to choose”, and Odysseus counts himself in as the fifth (9.331–335; see [[lots]]); he encourages them with words, and a god breathes daring into them (9.376–381; see [[tharsos]]). For the escape he plans “some release from death for my companions and for myself” (9.421), binds each man under three rams, and himself clings beneath the best ram (9.424–435). In the formula of the false name it is his mother, his father “and all my other companions” who call him Nobody (9.366–367; see [[nobody]]).

**The welcome and the reproach.** At the ship “we were a welcome sight to our dear companions, / we who had escaped death; but for the others they groaned and lamented”, and Odysseus forbids their weeping and hurries them to the oars (9.466–470; see [[tears]] and [[lament]]). When he taunts the Cyclops they try to stop him “with soothing words, one from one side and one from another”: “Hard man, why do you want to provoke a savage man?” (9.492–499; see [[hard]] and [[kertomia]]). On the island they divide the Cyclops' flocks fairly, and give the ram to him alone, “as a special gift” (9.548–551; see [[division-of-spoils]]). The curse names their end: he is to come home “having lost all his companions” (9.534; see [[curses]]).
