---
summary: Odysseus binds the Cyclops' rams together three by three with well-twisted willow shoots, from the Cyclops' own bed, and ties a companion under each middle ram (9.425–430).
---

“There were rams, well fed and thick of fleece, / fine and large, with violet-dark wool; / these I bound together in silence with well-twisted willow shoots, / on which the Cyclops slept, the monster, who knew lawless ways, / taking them three together” (9.425–429; see [[ram]], [[sheep]], [[silence]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). λύγοι, W. W. Merry explains, are properly the pliant twigs of the agnus castus, the λύγος of Dioscorides; with them Achilles bound two sons of Priam on Ida, and with them Odysseus will tie the legs of the great stag on Circe's island (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.427; *Iliad* 11.105; 10.166; see the translation's row λύγοι and the entry). ἐυστρεφής, “well-twisted”, is said elsewhere of ropes, of the twisted wool of a sling and of a bowstring (*Iliad* 13.599, 15.463; 10.167, 14.346; see [[sling]]). τῇς ἔπι Κύκλωψ εὗδε: the shoots were the Cyclops' bedding. In Euripides' *Cyclops* it is the Cyclops' lambs that are found tied up with twisted withies, στρεπταῖς λύγοισι, for the Greeks to carry off (*Cyclops* 224–225; see [[euripides]]).
