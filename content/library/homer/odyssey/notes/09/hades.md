---
summary: Hades is named once, when Odysseus answers the Cyclops' offer with the wish that he could send him “into the house of Hades” (9.524).
---

**Into the house of Hades.** The Cyclops has offered Odysseus good cheer and an escort from Poseidon, who alone can heal his eye (9.517–521), and Odysseus answers: “If only I could make you bereft of soul and life / and send you into the house of Hades, / as surely as not even the earth-shaker will heal your eye” (9.523–525; see [[poseidon]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). Merry renders: “Would that I were able, having robbed thee of life and being, to send thee within the house of Hades, as certainly as Poseidon himself shall never heal thine eye!” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.523). The comparison that binds the wish to a certainty has the form of Hector's wish, “If only I were the son of aegis-bearing Zeus” … “as surely as this day brings evil to the Argives” (*Iliad* 13.825–828; Merry on 9.525). εὖνις, “bereft”, is Priam's word for what Achilles has made him, “bereft of many brave sons” (*Iliad* 22.44; see the translation's key-word row), and δόμον Ἄϊδος εἴσω, “into the house of Hades”, is the Iliad's verse-end for going down to death, as in the armies' prayer before the duel, that “whichever of the two has brought these troubles on both sides” may “die and go down into the house of Hades” (*Iliad* 3.321–322).

**Going there alive.** Odysseus himself will go into that house alive, at Circe's bidding: “No one has ever yet come to the house of Hades in a black ship”, he answers her (10.502), and in Ithaca he tells Penelope of the prophecy of Teiresias, given “on the day when I went down into the house of Hades” (23.251–252, a plain rendering; see [[circe]]).
