summary: The Cyclops' club is a trunk of green olive wood, as long and thick as a ship's mast; from it Odysseus cuts the stake, sharpens it and hardens it in the fire, and drives it, glowing, into the Cyclops' eye, which hisses “around the stake of olive wood” (9.319–328, 9.375–394).

**The club.** “there lay beside the pen a great club of the Cyclops, / of green olive wood; he had cut it to carry with him / when it was dry” (9.319–321; see [[mace]] and [[the-mast-of-twenty-oars]]). ἔκταμεν, “he had cut”, is the verb of felling trees, as of the poplar that the chariot-maker fells in the Iliad's simile (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.320; *Iliad* 4.486); Eustathius reports that the more exact wrote ἔκσπασε, since a Cyclops of such size ought to have pulled the tree up root and all (Merry's critical note on 9.320). Merry doubts whether any limb of an olive could have been found long enough and straight enough for such a staff, and answers those who would read εἰλάτινον, “of fir”, that fresh pinewood blazes up in the fire instead of smoldering and charring (on 9.322).

**The stake.** Odysseus cuts off about a fathom's length, has his companions taper it, sharpens the point himself and hardens it in the fire (9.325–328; see [[stake]] and [[like-a-fire-hardened-stake]]). The green wood serves the plan: “But when the stake of olive wood was just about to catch fire, / green though it was, and glowed terribly” (9.378–379), they drive it into the eye, “so his eye hissed around the stake of olive wood” (9.394; see [[the-smith-tempering-an-axe]]). In Euripides' *Cyclops* Odysseus has found a branch of olive in the house, which he means to sharpen with his sword and put in the fire (*Cyclops* 455–457); Merry finds the Homeric picture spoiled by the branch, ἀκρεμών, in place of the club (on 9.326).
