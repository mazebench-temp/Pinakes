**The racks.** Odysseus and his twelve companions find the cave empty and gaze at each thing (see [[the-cave-of-polyphemus]]):

{{quote:9a791e88-52eb-5742-89c5-470e589937ac}}

The ταρσοί are the racks on which the cheeses dry, the scholia deriving the word from τέρσαι, “to dry”; W. W. Merry takes them for the same things as the “woven baskets” in which the Cyclops lays his curds (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.219; see [[basket]] and the translation's row ταρσοί, σηκοί, ὀρός, γαυλοί, σκαφίδες). τυρῶν βρῖθον is “were loaded with cheeses” (Merry on 9.219). The pens are the [[fold]]'s, the whey and the pails the [[milk]]'s.

**Take and go.** “Then my companions begged me at first with words / to take some of the cheeses and go back” and to drive the kids and lambs to the ship (9.224–227), “but I did not listen—it would have been far better—, / so that I might see him himself, and whether he would give me guest-gifts” (9.228–229; see [[odysseus]] and [[guest-gifts]]). They kindle a fire, make an offering, and “took some of the cheeses and ate” while they wait (9.231–233). ἐθύσαμεν is an offering to the gods of part of the cheeses before they took any for themselves: θύειν in Homer, Merry says after Lehrs, is the offering of firstfruits, never the slaughter of a victim (on 9.231; see the translation's row θύω and [[sacrifice]]).

**The curdling.** When the Cyclops has milked his ewes and goats, “at once he curdled half of the white milk, / gathered it, and laid it in woven baskets, / and half again he set in vessels” to drink at supper (9.246–249; see [[milk]]). θρέψας is “having curdled”, “having thickened”, Merry explains, comparing τρόφι κῦμα, the “swollen wave” of the Iliad (*Iliad* 11.307), and ἀμησάμενος “having collected it together”; in Homeric times, he notes, milk was curdled by stirring in the juice of the wild fig, as in the Iliad's simile (on 9.246; *Iliad* 5.902; see [[fig-juice]] and the translation's note on 9.246–247). The cheese and the milk seem to be his ordinary food: the wood he brings home is for light at supper, Merry observes, not for cooking (on 9.234; see [[fire]]).
