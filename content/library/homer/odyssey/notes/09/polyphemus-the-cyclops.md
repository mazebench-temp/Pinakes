summary: The Cyclops of Odysseus' tale. A monstrous herdsman who lives apart in his cave, he shuts the strangers in, boasts that the Cyclopes care nothing for Zeus, and eats six of the companions; made drunk with Maron's wine and blinded in his sleep, he tells his neighbors that Nobody is killing him, speaks to his ram, learns at last the name of the man who blinded him, and prays to his father Poseidon for vengeance (9.187–542).

**The monstrous man.** Odysseus sees the cave first, high and overhung with laurels, with a fold around it, and then tells of its owner (9.181–186; see [[the-cave-of-polyphemus]] and [[fold]]):

{{quote:ca6f6215-b9ad-50d0-a083-a77701f7d460}}

“Alone, far off”, “he did not go among the others”, “lived apart”: these are so many repetitions to emphasize the isolated, unsociable life of the monster, W. W. Merry notes (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.188), and ἀθεμίστια ᾔδη, “knew lawless ways”, is an accusative of the disposition, “of lawless mood”, or in the language of the Psalms “with his mind set upon unrighteousness” (Merry on 9.189; see [[themis]] and the translation's note on 9.189). He is θαῦμα … πελώριον, “a huge wonder” (9.190; see [[huge]] and [[thambos]]), not like a man “who eats bread” but like a lone wooded peak (9.191–192; see [[who-eat-the-fruit-of-the-field]]), and before Odysseus meets him he forebodes “a savage, who knew well neither judgments nor ordinances” (9.215): the man would be huge and mighty, Merry explains, and would want his wine strong (on 9.214; see [[maron]]). The tale calls him πέλωρ, “the monster” (9.257, 9.428), and the companions “a savage man” (9.494).

**The herdsman.** At home he is a careful herdsman. He brings in a load of dry wood “to have light for his supper” (9.234), for the fuel seems only to have served for light: under ordinary circumstances, Merry notes, the Cyclops ate cheese and drank milk, while the Cyclops of Euripides feeds on his flocks, hunts with dogs and keeps cattle (Merry on 9.234; *Cyclops* 122, 130, 136; see [[euripides-cyclops]]). He milks the ewes and the goats “all in good order, and put her young one under each” (9.245, 9.309, 9.342; see [[sheep]] and [[goats]]), curdles half the milk and keeps half to drink at supper (9.246–249; see [[milk]] and [[cheese]]), and whistles his flocks out to the mountain (9.315). His door-stone is one that “two and twenty wagons, / good four-wheeled ones, could not have heaved” (9.241–242; see [[door-stone]]), a hyperbolical parody, in Merry's view, of the Iliad's stone that two men could not heave onto a wagon, with something intentionally comic in the grave exactness of the number (Merry on 9.241; *Iliad* 12.445–449).

**The host.** He kindles his fire, sees the strangers and asks them Nestor's question, “Strangers, who are you? From where do you sail the watery ways?” (9.252–255 = 3.71–74; see [[strangers-who-are-you]] and [[thucydides-and-the-pirates]]), and his heavy voice terrifies them (9.256–257). To Odysseus' appeal to Zeus, “the god of guests” (9.270–271; see [[god-of-guests]] and [[xenia]]), he answers:

{{quote:26927005-63f9-5d9a-8cfc-286d40baf1fa}}

He calls the suppliant a fool (νήπιος, 9.273; see [[nepios]]), and Odysseus will call him one in turn (9.442). The boast, Merry notes, is inconsistent with what the Cyclopes acknowledge of Zeus (9.411) and with his own claim to be Poseidon's son, but true to the nature of a braggart; Euripides' Cyclops says the same, that he does not shudder at Zeus' thunderbolt (Merry on 9.275; *Cyclops* 320–321; see [[cyclopes]] and [[zeus]]). His question where the strangers have moored their ship is a test (9.279–281; see [[peira]]).

**The meals.** “So I spoke, and he answered me nothing, with pitiless heart” (9.287; see [[pitiless]]): he seizes two of the companions, dashes them to the ground “like puppies”, cuts them limb from limb and eats “like a mountain-bred lion”, leaving nothing, “entrails and flesh and marrowy bones” (9.288–293; see [[dogs]], [[the-hungry-lion]] and [[mountain-bred]]), and lies down among his flocks, “eating human flesh and drinking unmixed milk on top of it” (9.296–298). The cooking of the Cyclops of Euripides, who roasts and boils his victims, is a later refinement, not found in the Homeric story (Merry on 9.288; *Cyclops* 392–404); ἐπὶ … πίνων is “drinking on the top of it”, as Euripides has it (Merry on 9.297; *Cyclops* 327). He takes two more for his morning meal and two at evening, six in all (9.311, 9.344; see the translation's note on 9.311, 9.344).

**The wine.** Odysseus offers him the wine as a libation, in the hope that he would pity him and send him home (9.347–352; see [[libation]] and [[wine]]): Eustathius calls the expression flattery, the wine offered not as a gift to a stranger but as a libation to a god, and “the grosser the flattery the deeper the irony”, Merry adds, though not too gross for a savage who had boasted himself stronger than the gods (Merry on 9.349). He drinks, is “terribly pleased” (9.353), and asks for more and for the stranger's name, so that he may give him a guest-gift (9.355–356; see [[guest-gifts]]): the Cyclopes have wine of their own, “but this is a branch of ambrosia and nectar” (9.357–359). καί stresses the Cyclopes: they know what good wine is, but this is something quite out of the way (Merry on 9.357). Three times he drinks “in his folly” (9.361), and when he has the name his guest-gift is to eat “Nobody” last (9.366–370; see [[nobody]]): μετὰ οἷς ἑτάροισιν is “last in the list of his companions” (Merry on 9.369; see the translation's note on 9.369). Then he falls on his back, his thick neck bent aside, “and sleep / that subdues all took him”, and he vomits wine and gobbets of human flesh, “sodden with wine” (9.371–374; see [[that-subdues-all]] and [[wine-sodden]]). ἀποδοχμώσας is “drooping”, his head bent toward one shoulder, and Merry quotes Virgil's picture of the same sleep, the neck bent, the giant lying through the cave, belching gore and gobbets mixed with wine (Merry on 9.372; *Aeneid* 3.631–633; see [[virgil]]).

**The blinding.** The stake goes into his eye, the blood flows round it, and the eye hisses around it (9.382–394; see [[stake]] and [[augers]]). “And he groaned aloud terribly, and the rock rang round about” (9.395); he wrenches the stake out and flings it from him “with his hands, distraught” (9.396–398), the wild movements of his hands in his agony (Merry on 9.398; see the translation's note on 9.398).

**Polyphemus.** His neighbors name him for the first time in the tale: “What has hurt you so, Polyphemus, that you have cried out like this / through the ambrosial night, and make us sleepless?” (9.403–404; see [[ambrosial]]). “Mighty Polyphemus” answers them from the cave (9.407; see [[krateros]]), “Friends, Nobody is killing me by trickery, and not by force” (9.408): the ambiguity of the name runs on into οὐδέ, for the Cyclopes understand “neither by craft nor by violence”, while he meant “by craft, and not by violence” (Merry on 9.408; see [[nobody]] and [[cunning-not-strength]]). They tell him to pray to his father and go away (9.410–413; see [[cyclopes]]). Groaning “and writhing in agonies of pain” (9.415), where the play of sound is intentional (Merry on 9.415; see the translation's note on 9.415–416), he gropes for the stone, takes it from the doorway and sits in the doorway with his hands stretched out, hoping to catch someone among the sheep, “for so foolish, I suppose, he hoped in his mind that I was” (9.415–419).

**The ram.** At dawn he feels along the backs of all the sheep as they go out, “but the fool did not notice / how the men were bound under the chests of the woolly sheep” (9.441–443; see [[sheep]] and [[woolly]]). Last of the flock comes the ram that carries Odysseus, and mighty Polyphemus speaks to it:

{{quote:581efa9e-3caf-5817-ba8b-200174a08860}}

κριὲ πέπον, “Dear ram” (9.447; see [[ram]] and [[my-dear]]): the Iliad's word for a ram is κτίλος, and Eustathius recalls Hector, Achilles and Antilochus speaking to their horses (*Iliad* 8.185, 19.400, 23.402). Merry sees in the speech the natural craving of the Cyclops for sympathy in his distress, even from an animal, which Cicero failed to understand: Cicero says that Homer makes the savage Polyphemus talk with his ram and praise its fortune, that it could go where it wished and touch what it wished, “rightly, for the Cyclops himself was no wiser than that ram” (Merry on 9.447; Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* 5.115; see [[cicero-polyphemus-and-the-ram]]). μακρὰ βιβάς, “with long strides” (9.450), is the proud bearing of the leader of a flock (Merry on 9.450). In πόρεν, “gave” (9.460), there is a grim reference to the word that was used of Odysseus handing him the wine (πόρον, 9.360; Merry on 9.456), and οὐτιδανὸς … Οὖτις, “Nobody, a nobody” (9.460), plays on the name (see [[nobody]]).

**The rocks and the name.** At Odysseus' first taunt “he grew all the more angry in his heart, / and he broke off the peak of a great mountain and hurled it” (9.480–481): it fell in front of the ship, and the swell it raised, not a flood tide, carried the ship back toward the shore, as a stick afloat is brought to the bank by stones thrown beyond it (Merry on 9.485–486; see the translation's notes on 9.483 and 9.485–486). The companions beg Odysseus not to provoke him again, “so far does he throw” (9.494–499). When Odysseus gives his name, he groans and remembers the prophecy of Telemus, that he would lose his sight at the hands of Odysseus (9.506–512; see [[telemus]] and [[thesphaton]]):

{{quote:111c982f-c741-5bf5-8df1-e1ae224566cd}}

Aristotle quotes 9.515 among his examples of diction (*Poetics* 1458b25–28; see [[small-and-of-no-account]]).

**The offer and the prayer.** “But come here, Odysseus, so that I may set good cheer before you / and speed your escort, that the famous shaker of the earth may grant it” (9.517–518; see [[xenia]] and [[pompe]]): πομπήν τʼ ὀτρύνω go together, and the clause after it defines the escort, “and let me make speedy preparation for your return, that the famous Earthshaker may grant you one” (Merry on 9.518). His father alone, he says, will heal him, “if he wishes” (9.519–521; see [[poseidon]]). When Odysseus answers that not even the earth-shaker will heal his eye (9.523–525), he prays, “stretching out his hands to the starry sky”, though it is day, ἀστερόεντα being a good instance of a standing epithet (9.527; Merry on 9.527; see [[starry]]):

{{quote:24d8dea1-9845-56cb-b017-fc735ce6ef09}}

νηὸς ἐπʼ ἀλλοτρίης, “on someone else's ship”, is the Phaeacian ship that will bring Odysseus home (9.535; Merry on 9.535). The prayer's second wish comes true, clause by clause, and Teiresias foretells it in almost the same words (11.114–115; see [[curses]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). Then the Cyclops lifts “a far greater stone” and hurls it, “whirling it round” (9.537–538), in the verses of Ajax' throw at Hector (*Iliad* 7.268–269); it falls a little astern, and the wave drives the ship forward to the island (9.539–542; see the translation's note on 9.539–540).

**His flocks.** On the island the Greeks divide “the Cyclops' flocks” (9.548–549), and the companions give Odysseus the ram, “the particular ram by means of which I had escaped”, as Merry glosses ἀρνειόν (Merry on 9.550). Odysseus sacrifices it to Zeus, but Zeus “took no heed of the offering”: he refused to notice it, Merry explains, because the prayer of Polyphemus had been heard and would be answered (9.550–555; Merry on 9.553; see [[zeus]] and [[sacrifice]]). For the story as a whole, see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]].
