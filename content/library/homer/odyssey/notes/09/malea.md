---
summary: Malea is where Odysseus' homecoming goes astray: he would have reached his native land unharmed, but as he was rounding the cape the wave, the current and the North Wind drove him off course past Cythera, and nine days of deadly winds carried him to the Lotus-eaters (9.79–84).
---

**So near home.** After the storm that Zeus sends against the ships, they set up their masts and sail on, and Odysseus is almost home: “And now I would have reached my native land unharmed; / but as I was rounding Malea, the wave and the current / and the North Wind pushed me back, and drove me off course past Cythera” (9.79–81; see [[would-have-had-not]], [[boreas]] and [[cythera]]). Malea is where Menelaus' and Agamemnon's voyages home went astray too (3.286–290, 4.514–516; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). The proverb that Strabo quotes, “When you round Malea, forget the things at home”, shows how dangerous the sea off the cape was held to be (*Geography* 8.6.20; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.80; see the translation's note on 9.80). Odysseus, Merry explains, meant to pass between the cape and the island of Cythera, and was carried past it instead; and the same thing befell Jason, who in Herodotus' story was rounding Malea on his way to Delphi when the North Wind caught him and carried him off to Libya, into the shallows of the lake Tritonis (on 9.80; Herodotus, *Histories* 4.179.1–2; see [[herodotus]]).

**Nine days.** “From there for nine days I was borne by deadly winds / over the fish-filled sea” (9.82–83; see [[lotus-eaters]]). πόντον ἐπʼ ἰχθυόεντα, “over the fish-filled sea”, is the verse-end of the storm wind that carried Agamemnon off at Malea (4.516; see [[fish-filled]]). Polybius, as Strabo reports him, took the nine days of “deadly winds” for a voyage within a short distance, since such winds do not blow on a straight course, and not for a voyage out into the Ocean (*Geography* 1.2.17; see [[polybius]]). In Euripides' satyr play the same cape is the turning point: Silenus and the satyrs “had already sailed close to Malea” when an east wind cast them on the rock of Aetna, where the Cyclopes live (*Cyclops* 18–22; see [[euripides-cyclops]]).
