summary: The rams of the Cyclops' flock carry Odysseus' men out of the cave, bound three together, and the best ram of all carries Odysseus himself; the blind Cyclops speaks to it as it goes out last, and on the island Odysseus sacrifices it to Zeus, who takes no heed of the offering (9.425–463, 9.550–555).
# ἄρσενες ὄιες, "rams" (9.425), the males of the sheep, found only here.
grc: ["ἄρσενες ὄιες"]
---

**The rams.** On the first evening the Cyclops leaves the males outside, “the rams and the he-goats” (9.238–239; see [[goats]]); on the second he drives all the flock inside, “either because he suspected something, or because a god so bade him” (9.337–339). Odysseus' plan uses them: “there were rams, well fed and thick of fleece, / fine and large, with violet-dark wool” (9.425–426), and he binds them three together with willow shoots, each middle ram carrying a man while the other two walk on either side (9.427–431; see [[sheep]] and [[willow]]).

**The best of the flock.** For himself he keeps the best: “for there was a ram, by far the best of all the flock— / I grasped him by the back, and curled up under his shaggy belly / I lay; and to the wondrous fleece, with my hands, / turning myself over, I held on unceasingly with enduring heart” (9.432–435). ἐλυσθείς is “curled up”, as the scholia explain it by συστραφείς (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.433), and χερσίν is emphatic: the men were tied, but there was no one to tie Odysseus, and his hands were all he had to hold on with (Merry on 9.434; see the translation's note on 9.434–435). In the morning the ram goes out last, “weighed down by his wool and by me, thinking shrewd thoughts” (9.444–445). Merry calls the unexpected addition of “me” after the wool a comic touch, what later Greek would call a jest against expectation, παρὰ προσδοκίαν (on 9.445).

**“Dear ram.”** The blind master feels him over and speaks to him: “Dear ram, why do you hurry out through the cave like this, the last / of the flock?” (9.446–448). κριὲ πέπον: the Iliad's word for the ram that leads the flock is κτίλος, and Eustathius, as Merry reports, was reminded of Hector, Achilles and Antilochus speaking to their horses (Merry on 9.447; *Iliad* 8.185, 19.400, 23.402). The ram has always led: “you were far the first to graze on the tender flowers of the grass, / with long strides, and the first to reach the streams of the rivers, / and the first to long to go back to the farmstead / in the evening; but now you are the very last” (9.449–452). μακρὰ βιβάς, “with long strides”, is the proud bearing of the leader of a flock (Merry on 9.450), the ram of the Iliad's similes, which goes through a great flock of white sheep and which the flock follows from the pasture to drink (*Iliad* 3.196–198, 13.491–493; see [[ram-among-the-sheep]] and [[sheep-following-the-ram]]). The Cyclops thinks the ram grieves for its master's eye, and wishes it could speak and tell him where Nobody is hiding (9.452–460; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). On Cicero's view of the speech, see the translation's note on 9.447. When the ram has carried Odysseus a little way from the cave, “I first loosed myself from under the ram, and set my companions free” (9.462–463).

**The sacrifice.** On the island the flocks are divided, “But the ram my well-greaved companions gave to me alone, / as a special gift when the flocks were divided” (9.550–551), the ram by which he had escaped (Merry on 9.550). Odysseus sacrifices it on the shore to Zeus and burns the thigh-pieces, “but he took no heed of the offering, / but was pondering how they might all perish, / my well-benched ships and my trusty companions” (9.551–555; see [[zeus]], [[sacrifice]] and [[thigh-pieces]]). Zeus refuses the offering, Merry explains, because the prayer of Polyphemus has been heard and will be answered (on 9.553; see [[poseidon]]).
