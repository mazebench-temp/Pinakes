---
summary: Asked for his land, Odysseus names Ithaca, with its mountain Neritum and the islands around it, low in the sea and farthest out toward the darkness, “rugged, but a good nurse of young men”, the sweetest of lands to him (9.21–28). At the end of the book the island is part of the name he shouts to the Cyclops, “who has his home in Ithaca”, and of the Cyclops' prayer that he may never reach it (9.505, 9.531).
# Ithaca without its name: "my island itself" (9.25-27); the native land he would have reached but for Malea (9.79)
# and the native land of the Cyclops' prayer (9.533).

**The answer to Alcinous.** Alcinous had asked the stranger at last, “And tell me your land and your people and your city” (8.555). Odysseus gives his name and then his island:

{{quote:a97b4437-7a15-51e0-bd06-d13ff7303767}}

For ἐυδείελος, “clear-seen”, Merry refers to his Appendix on Ithaca (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.21; see [[clear-seen]] and the translation's note on 2.167). The mountain is the Neritum of the Catalogue (*Iliad* 2.632; see [[neritum]]), and the islands are those whose best men woo Penelope, “Dulichium and Same and wooded Zacynthus” (9.24, as 1.246; see [[dulichium]], [[samos]] and [[zacynthus]]). Strabo cites the first two verses to show that Ithaca here is the island, not the town, for the mountain is in the island (*Geography* 10.2.11; see [[strabo]]).

**Low, and farthest toward the darkness.** αὐτὴ δὲ χθαμαλὴ πανυπερτάτη εἰν ἁλὶ κεῖται / πρὸς ζόφον (9.25–26; see the translation's key words). Strabo found a contradiction in the verse, χθαμαλή being “low” and πανυπερτάτη “highest”, and set it beside the poem's craggy Ithaca, its rugged path from the harbor, and Telemachus' words that none of the islands is rich in meadows, “and Ithaca above them all” (4.607–608; see [[craggy]]); the interpreters, he says, explain it not badly, taking χθαμαλή as “near the mainland” and πανυπερτάτη πρὸς ζόφον as “farthest toward the north”, the other islands lying “apart toward the dawn and the sun”, that is, toward the south (*Geography* 10.2.12). Merry rejects both readings. The words “toward the dawn and the sun” so plainly mean the east that ζόφος must be the west, and πανυπερτάτη πρὸς ζόφον is “farthest westward”, the words taken together, as Ameis, Fäsi and Autenrieth take them; χθαμαλή, formed like Latin *humilis* from *humus*, is used in Homer only of what is low, beds on the ground, a wall, a rock, the island of Aeaea, and the shores of Ithaca, with the deep water close in, do not deserve it. The passage, he concludes, puts Ithaca in a wrong place among the islands and gives a false picture of its look from the sea: Homer meant the real island, but knew it only from vague report (Merry, Appendix on Ithaca; see [[aeaea]] and the translation's note on 9.21–28). He reports that Hercher, going further, held that for Homer Ithaca belonged to the mythical realm of the western seas (Appendix on Ithaca).

**A good nurse of young men.** τρηχεῖʼ, ἀλλʼ ἀγαθὴ κουροτρόφος, “rugged, but a good nurse of young men” (9.27; see [[rugged]]): κουροτρόφος is like βωτιάνειρα, “nurse of men”, the epithet of Phthia (*Iliad* 1.155; Merry on 9.27). The praise ends in a saying: “and I, for my part, / can see nothing else sweeter than one's own land” (9.27–28). ἧς γαίης is the possessive used of anyone, “one's own”, and the scholia note that he did not say “mine”, so that the saying would be more general (Merry on 9.28; see [[nothing-sweeter-than-ones-own-land]]). He says it again after naming Calypso and Circe (9.34–36; see [[aeaea]] and [[calypsos-cave]]).

**Who has his home in Ithaca.** The island returns at the end of the book, in the name Odysseus shouts from the ship: “say that Odysseus, sacker of cities, blinded you, / the son of Laertes, who has his home in Ithaca” (9.504–505; see [[say-that-odysseus-blinded-you]] and [[laertes]]). The Cyclops turns the verses into his prayer to Poseidon: “grant that Odysseus, sacker of cities, may not reach home, / the son of Laertes, who has his home in Ithaca” (9.530–531; see [[poseidon]]). Since Wolf nearly all editors have bracketed the second verse, which is wanting in twelve manuscripts and in Eustathius' text (Merry's critical note on 9.531; see the translation's note on 9.531); the text prints it. The home he named to the Phaeacians at the start of the tale is what the curse aims at.
