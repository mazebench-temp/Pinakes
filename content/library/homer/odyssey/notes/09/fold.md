summary: A high fold of stones set deep in the earth, with pines and oaks, stands around the Cyclops' cave (9.184–186). Inside, his lambs and kids are penned by age (9.219–222); he leaves his rams and he-goats outside in the fold (9.237–239) until the evening of the blinding, when he drives every animal in (9.336–339), and on the morning of the escape the unmilked ewes bleat around the pens (9.439–440).

**The fold of the cave.** At the far edge of the land the twelve men see “a cave, / high, and overhung with laurels. And there many / flocks, sheep and goats, used to sleep; and around it a high fold / had been built with stones set deep in the earth / and with tall pines and high-leafed oaks” (9.182–186; see [[the-cave-of-polyphemus]], [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]] and [[high-leafed]]). ἰαύεσκον is “were housed at night”: by day the flocks are out at pasture (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.184). Merry pictures the cave running back into the cliff, with a yard before its mouth enclosed by high walls of “Cyclopean” stonework, the stones brought close up to the standing trunks, so that the trees were worked into the wall; the massive walls of early Greece, he thinks, were probably called the work of the Cyclopes in allusion to this passage (on 9.185). δέδμητο, “had been built”, is Aristarchus' reading, βέβλητο Aristophanes' (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 9.185).

**Inside and outside.** Within the cave the pens are crowded “with lambs and kids; each kind was penned / apart, the firstlings by themselves, the later ones by themselves, / and the newborn by themselves” (9.220–222; see the translation's row ταρσοί, σηκοί, ὀρός, γαυλοί, σκαφίδες), and the companions beg Odysseus to drive the kids and lambs out of the pens to the ship (9.226–227). On the first evening the Cyclops drives in only the animals he milks, “and left the males outside the door, / the rams and the he-goats, outside in the high-walled fold” (9.238–239). ἔκτοθεν is the text's reading, which the English follows; most editors since Bekker print Rumpf's conjecture ἔντοθεν, “inside the fold”, and so does Merry, who pictures the Cyclops throwing his firewood down inside the cave, stepping out again, and driving the ewes and she-goats in, while the males stay in the yard (on 9.235, 9.239; see the translation's note on 9.235, 9.239, 9.338). On the second evening he leaves none outside, “either because he suspected something, or because a god so bade him” (9.336–339): the rams under which the men escape are in the cave (see [[ram]] and [[sheep]]). The great club lies “beside the pen” (9.319; see [[mace]] and [[stake]]).

**The escape.** At dawn “the males of the flock rushed out to the pasture, / and the females bleated unmilked around the pens, / for their udders were bursting” (9.438–440; see [[milk]]). When the men have gone “a little way from the cave and the fold” (9.462), Odysseus looses himself from under the ram and sets his companions free.
