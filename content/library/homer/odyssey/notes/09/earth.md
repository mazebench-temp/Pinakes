---
summary: In Book 9 the earth is above all a land: one's own land, sweeter than any other, and the native land Odysseus fails to reach (9.27–36, 9.79, 9.533); the lands of the tale, of the Lotus-eaters and of the Cyclopes (9.84, 9.106, 9.117, 9.166, 9.284); the rich soil of the island of goats and the grain-giving earth that bears wine even for the Cyclopes (9.135, 9.357); and the ground on which the Cyclops dashes two companions (9.289–290).
# οὖδας (9.135) and ἄρουρα (9.357) are claimed by the Book 8 and Book 1 notes.
---

**One's own land.** Odysseus ends the description of Ithaca with a saying: “and I, for my part, / can see nothing else sweeter than one's own land” (9.27–28), and after naming Calypso and Circe he says it again: “So true is it that nothing is sweeter than one's native land and parents, / even if a man lives far off in a rich house / in a foreign land, apart from his parents” (9.34–36; see [[nothing-sweeter-than-ones-own-land]] and [[ithaca]]). ἧς γαίης is “one's own land”, the possessive used of anyone, and the scholia observe that he did not say “mine”, so that the saying might be the more general (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.28). The native land, πατρὶς γαῖα, is what he nearly reaches before Malea, “And now I would have reached my native land unharmed” (9.79; see [[malea]] and [[would-have-had-not]]), and what the Cyclops' curse would keep from him: “if it is his fate to see his friends and reach / his well-built house and his native land, / let him come late and in evil plight” (9.532–534; see [[dear-native-land]] and [[poseidon]]).

**The lands of the tale.** The peoples of the tale are reached as lands: “on the tenth we set foot / on the land of the Lotus-eaters” (9.83–84; see [[lotus-eaters]]), “and we came to the land of the Cyclopes” (9.106), the island “neither near the land of the Cyclopes nor far off” (9.117), from which “we looked across to the land of the Cyclopes” (9.166; see [[the-island-of-goats]] and [[cyclopes]]). In his lie to the Cyclops Odysseus says that Poseidon wrecked his ship “at the borders of your land” (9.284; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). Between the lands lies the sea: Zeus' storm covers “land and sea alike” with clouds (9.69; see [[sea]] and [[sky]]).

**Soil and earth.** The island off the Cyclopes' land would bear a deep field of grain, “since the soil beneath is very rich” (9.135), πῖαρ ὑπʼ οὖδας, “rich soil spreads below the surface” (Merry on 9.135). The Cyclops himself boasts that “the grain-giving earth bears for the Cyclopes too / wine of great clusters, and the rain of Zeus makes it grow for them” (9.357–358; see [[grain-giving]] and [[wine]]). Odysseus' scouts go to learn “what men they might be who eat bread upon the earth” (9.89), the formula of mortal men (see [[spies-and-scouts]]).

**The ground.** The first meal in the cave is told on the ground: the Cyclops “dashed them to the ground like puppies, / and their brains flowed out on the ground and soaked the earth” (9.289–290; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]), the verse-end of the Iliad's dead, whose dark blood “flowed and soaked the earth” (*Iliad* 13.655, 21.119).
