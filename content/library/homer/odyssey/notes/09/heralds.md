summary: At the land of the Lotus-eaters Odysseus sends two men to find out what men live there, with a herald as the third (9.88–90).
# κήρυχʼ (9.90; again 10.102, in the same verse), the elided accusative with its aspirate, is claimed here, in the
# Odyssey only; κῆρυξ, κήρυκα and the plural forms are claimed by the entry and the notes of earlier books, and the
# English "herald" by the Book 1 note.
grc: [κήρυχʼ]
---

“then I sent some of my companions to go and find out / what men they might be who eat bread upon the earth, / choosing two men, and sending a herald with them as the third” (9.88–90; see [[the-lotus-eaters]], [[lotus-eaters]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]). The Lotus-eaters do them no harm, but whoever of them tastes the lotus “no longer wished to bring back word or to return”, and Odysseus has to bring them back to the ships by force (9.91–99). Ameis, as W. W. Merry reports, noted that the verse stands before 9.89 in the oldest and best manuscripts and in Eustathius, supposed it interpolated from 10.102, where the same verse sends the scouts to the Laestrygonians, and rejected it, both because 9.94–95 imply more than three men and because Homer does not represent such a reconnaissance in the shape of a formal embassy (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 9.89–90; see the translation's note on 9.89–90). σῖτον ἔδοντες, “who eat bread”, marks human beings, as the Iliad sets men apart from the gods, who “do not eat bread” (Merry on 9.89; *Iliad* 5.341; see [[who-eat-the-fruit-of-the-field]]).
