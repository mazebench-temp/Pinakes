summary: The narrator. Answering the king's questions, Odysseus praises the feast and the singer, names himself, “Odysseus, son of Laertes”, and begins the tale of his homecoming: the sack of Ismarus, the storm, the Lotus-eaters and the Cyclopes. In the Cyclops' cave his wish to see the man and his guest-gifts costs six companions; he holds back his sword, plans the stake, gives his name as Nobody, blinds the Cyclops and escapes under a ram, then taunts him from the ship and gives his true name, and the Cyclops' prayer to Poseidon follows him home (9.1–566).

**The narrator.** “Answering him, Odysseus of many wiles said” (9.1; see [[of-many-wiles]]): after this verse the poem is his own tale, told to the Phaeacians in the hall of Alcinous, and with one pause in Book 11 (11.333–384) it runs to the end of Book 12 (see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]], [[the-title-of-odyssey-9]] and [[alcinous]]). The time is the evening of the second day after his arrival in Scheria; his first words refer back to the king's questions at the end of Book 8, and at 9.16 he complies with the request for his name, “Tell me the name by which they called you there” (8.550; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.1). He begins with praise of the singer and the feast (9.3–11; see [[singers]] and [[demodocus]]), and with the sorrows the king has asked him to tell, “so that I may groan and mourn still more” (9.12–15; see [[algos]]).

**His name.** “Now first I will tell you my name, so that you too / may know it, and I hereafter, when I have escaped the pitiless day, / may be your guest-friend, though I live in a house far away” (9.16–18; see [[xenia]] and [[pitiless]]).

{{quote:b7f8b7f2-9af6-5dd6-9e88-c49f9d432bba}}

πᾶσι goes with δόλοισιν in the sense of παντοίοις, “tricks of every kind”, rather than with ἀνθρώποισι: the question is settled by Nestor's words, that Odysseus “far surpassed them / in tricks of every kind” (3.121–122; Merry on 9.19; see [[dolos]], [[kleos]], [[son-of-laertes]] and the translation's note on 9.19). Virgil imitates the verse in Aeneas' words to his disguised mother, “I am pious Aeneas, … known by fame above the sky” (*Aeneid* 1.378–379; Merry on 9.19; see [[virgil]]). He names his island, “clear-seen Ithaca”, and its neighbors (9.21–26; see [[ithaca]] and [[clear-seen]]), “rugged, but a good nurse of young men; and I, for my part, / can see nothing else sweeter than one's own land” (9.27–28; see [[rugged]]): ἧς γαίης, “one's own land”, and not “mine”, a scholium says, so that the saying might be more general (Merry on 9.28; see the translation's key words). Calypso and Circe held him back, each “longing for me to be her husband”, “but they never persuaded the heart in my breast” (9.29–33; see [[calypso]] and [[circe]]), “So true is it that nothing is sweeter than one's native land and parents” (9.34; see [[nothing-sweeter-than-ones-own-land]]).

**The homecoming of many sorrows.** “But come, let me tell you also of my homecoming of many sorrows, / which Zeus sent upon me as I came from Troy” (9.37–38; see [[nostos]] and [[zeus]]). At Ismarus, “There I sacked the city and destroyed the men themselves” (9.40; see [[ismarus]], [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]] and [[sacker-of-cities]]): the Cicones, Merry notes, were allies of Troy (*Iliad* 2.846), which accounts for a Greek hero's attack on their city (Merry on 9.39; see [[cicones]]). He shared out the spoil “so that for my part no one might go away cheated of his fair share” (9.42, again 9.549), μοι meaning “as far as I could help it”, like the later ἐμοῦ γʼ ἕνεκα (Merry on 9.42). He bade them flee, “but they, the great fools, would not obey” (9.43–44; see [[nepios]]), and six from each ship died: Crates took the number as an average of six from twelve ships, and Zoilus was offended at the suspicious symmetry (9.60–61; Merry on 9.60). The storm robs him of the homecoming he nearly had: “And now I would have reached my native land unharmed” (9.79–81; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[malea]]). At the land of the Lotus-eaters he sends two men and a herald to learn who lives there, and brings back by force the men who have tasted the lotus (9.88–102; see [[the-lotus-eaters]] and [[heralds]]).

**To make trial of these men.** From the island of goats he looks across at the land of the Cyclopes and goes to see it with his own ship: “but I, in my own ship with my companions, / will go and make trial of these men, who they are, / whether they are arrogant and savage and unjust, / or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods” (9.173–176; see [[peira]], [[hybris]] and [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]). ἐμῇ … ἐμοῖς is emphatic, “in my own ship with my own crew”: the rest of the fleet stays at the island (Merry on 9.173; see [[the-island-of-goats]]). He takes twelve chosen companions and a skin of Maron's wine, for his spirit forebodes a savage (9.193–215; see [[maron]]).

**But I did not listen.** In the empty cave his companions beg him to take some of the cheeses and the young of the flock and sail away:

{{quote:c2f386e4-42a3-5859-bcb2-e45bfb39f0e7}}

The verse “but I did not listen—it would have been far better—” is the Iliad's verse of regret for a fatal choice, Pandarus' and Hector's (*Iliad* 5.201, 22.103; see [[kerdos]]). In the cave, Merry writes, “his old caution has forsaken him: he is foolhardy and thoughtless. The mantle of his wisdom seems for once to have descended on his comrades”: they would take advantage of the Cyclops' absence and drive off his herds, but Odysseus insists on seeing him and partaking of his hospitality, and he is no less reckless when he taunts the Cyclops from the ship (Merry, Appendix II, on the legend of the blinded Cyclops; see [[guest-gifts]]). Eurylochus will say that the men who died there perished through his recklessness (10.435–437).

**The suppliant.** To the Cyclops' question, “Strangers, who are you?”, he answers that they are Achaeans driven off course from Troy, “the people of Agamemnon, son of Atreus, / whose fame is now the greatest under heaven” (9.259–264; see [[agamemnon]] and [[under-heaven]]): μέγιστον is predicative, “his fame, far as the heaven covers, is the greatest”, and ἡμεῖς δʼ αὖτε sets the suppliants against Agamemnon (Merry on 9.264, 9.266). They have come to his knees, “to see if you will give us a guest-gift, or otherwise / give us some present, as is the custom with strangers”, ξεινήιον being the most general word for hospitality and δωτίνη a more specific gift (9.266–268; Merry on 9.267; see [[supplication]] and [[guest-gifts]]), and he warns him of Zeus, “the god of guests, who attends on revered strangers” (9.269–271; see [[good-sir]], [[god-of-guests]] and [[xenia]]), all guests being revered as guests (Merry on 9.271).

**Crafty words.** “So he spoke, testing me, but he did not escape my notice, for I knew much, / but I answered him back with crafty words” (9.281–282): εἰδότα πολλά recalls the epithet πολύμητις, “with my knowledge of the world” (Merry on 9.281), and ἄψορρον means only “I answered him back” (Merry on 9.282; see [[dolos]]). He hides the ship that waits for him on the shore, and in his lie the wrecker is Poseidon, the Cyclops' father (9.283–286; see [[poseidon]] and [[earth-shaker]]).

**A second thought.** That night, with two companions eaten:

{{quote:a46f8286-bba2-5dc4-b29b-752d5d6637cd}}

χεῖρʼ ἐπιμασσάμενος is better “feeling for” the mortal spot, his hand passing in the dark over the monster's body, than “fingering” his sword; θυμός is here “a second thought”; and καὶ ἄμμες is “we as well as he”, assassins and victim together (Merry on 9.302, 9.303; see [[sword]] and [[thymos]]).

**The plan and the lot.** The next day he is left in the cave “brooding evil deep within, / to see whether I might take vengeance, and Athena grant me triumph” (9.316–317; see [[athena]] and [[euchomai]]), and “this plan seemed to me in my heart the best” (9.318, again 9.424; see [[boule]]). From the Cyclops' club he cuts a fathom's length; his men do the rougher work of smoothing it, and he gives the shape of the point (9.319–328; Merry on 9.327; see [[stake]] and [[mace]]). The lot falls “on those whom I myself would have wished to choose, / four of them, and I counted myself in with them as the fifth” (9.334–335; see [[lots]]): καί stresses the wish, the men he would have chosen even without the lot, and ἐλέγμην is “I counted myself in”, as λέκτο δʼ ἀριθμόν (4.451; Merry on 9.334, 9.335).

**The wine and the name.** He offers the Cyclops the wine in an ivy-wood bowl, with a reproach: “Hard man, how should anyone else come to you hereafter / of the many men there are, since you have not acted fair and right?” (9.351–352; see [[hard]] and [[wine]]), however many men there may be, such a welcome will keep them all away (Merry on 9.352). When the wine has come around the Cyclops' wits, he speaks to him “with soothing words” (9.362–363; see [[soothing-words]]): “Cyclops, you ask me my famous name” (9.364), and he gives it as Nobody (9.366–367; see [[nobody]]). The accusative Οὖτιν is made different from the ordinary accusative of οὔτις, because it is used as a proper name (Merry on 9.366).

**The blinding.** He heats the stake under the ashes and encourages his companions, “so that no one might draw back from me in fear” (9.375–377), and “a god breathed great daring into us” (9.381; see [[daimon]] and [[tharsos]]). He takes the top of the stake himself, “throwing my weight on it from above” (9.383): ἐρεισθείς, said to have been Aristarchus' reading, describes a man pressing with his body on the stock of a drill (Merry on 9.383; see the translation's note on 9.383 and [[augers]]).

**My name and my flawless plan.** When the Cyclopes go away, “my dear heart laughed within me, / how my name and my flawless plan had deceived him” (9.413–414; see [[metis]] and [[blameless]]): it is difficult, Merry writes, not to suppose a pun between μή τις, “nobody”, in the Cyclopes' answer, and μῆτις ἀμύμων, “my flawless plan”, for the Cyclops has been over-reached by the μῆτις of the Οὖτις (Merry on 9.408; see [[nobody]] and the translation's note on 9.408–414). Then, “I wove every kind of trick and plan, / as one does where life is at stake” (9.422–423; see [[dolos]]), ὥς τε περὶ ψυχῆς, “in a matter of life and death” (Merry on 9.423; see [[psyche]]). He binds the men under the rams in threes, and keeps the best ram of the flock for himself (9.425–435; see [[ram]] and [[sheep]]). χερσίν is emphatic: the men were tied, but there was no one to tie Odysseus, and στρεφθείς is “turning myself over”, hanging downward, as he will cling to the fig tree like a bat (12.432–433; Merry on 9.434, 9.435; see the translation's note on 9.434–435). The ram goes out last, “weighed down by his wool and by me, thinking shrewd thoughts” (9.445), a comic touch (Merry on 9.445).

**The taunts.** At the ship he forbids the men to weep for the dead, signing no with his brows (9.467–470). Once at sea he taunts the Cyclops: “Cyclops, it was not a weakling's companions after all that you were / to eat in your hollow cave with your mighty strength” (9.475–476; see [[kertomia]]), “He was no weakling whose comrades thou wast minded to eat”, as Merry renders it (on 9.475), and the Cyclops has eaten strangers “in your own house”, which specially violates the rites of hospitality (9.478–479; Merry on 9.478; see [[xenia]]). The rock drives the ship back; he pushes off with a pole and urges the men to row, nodding with his head, for no one dared speak within range (9.487–490; Merry on 9.490). Twice as far out, though his companions beg him, “Hard man, why do you want to provoke a savage man?” (9.494), and though, Merry notes, a man out of range should also be out of hearing (on 9.491), “they did not persuade my proud heart, / but I answered him back with resentful heart” (9.500–501):

{{quote:38e56690-e2dd-5c23-b2eb-f78072b9b734}}

Aristotle took the verse as the mark of an anger that is not satisfied unless the man who suffers knows by whose hand and for what cause (*Rhetoric* 1380b22–24; Merry on 9.504; see [[say-that-odysseus-blinded-you]] and [[sacker-of-cities]]). With the name the Cyclops can pray against him, and he does.

**His answer.** To the Cyclops' offer of good cheer and an escort from Poseidon (9.517–521) he answers: “If only I could make you bereft of soul and life / and send you into the house of Hades, / as surely as not even the earth-shaker will heal your eye” (9.523–525; see [[psyche]] and [[hades]]). Merry renders, “Would that I were able, having robbed thee of life and being, to send thee within the house of Hades, as certainly as Poseidon himself shall never heal thine eye!” (on 9.523).

**The curse.** The Cyclops' prayer names him as he had named himself, “Odysseus, sacker of cities, … the son of Laertes, who has his home in Ithaca” (9.530–531 = 9.504–505), and asks that, if it is his fate to reach home, he may “come late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions, / on someone else's ship, and find bane in his house” (9.534–535; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]], [[curses]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). “And the dark-haired god heard him” (9.536; see [[dark-haired]] and [[poseidon]]). It is the anger of which Zeus spoke in the first council of the gods (1.68–75; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]).

**The ram.** On the island he divides the Cyclops' flocks as he had divided the spoil of Ismarus (9.548–549 = 9.42), and the companions give him the ram “as a special gift” (9.550–551; see [[geras]]). He sacrifices it to Zeus, “but he took no heed of the offering, / but was pondering how they might all perish, / my well-benched ships and my trusty companions” (9.553–555; see [[zeus]], [[sacrifice]] and [[thigh-pieces]]): Zeus refused to notice the offering, Merry explains, because the prayer of Polyphemus had been heard and would be answered (Merry on 9.553).

**The companions.** The book counts its dead: six from each ship at Ismarus (9.60–61) and six eaten by the Cyclops (9.289–293, 9.311, 9.344), one of whom, Antiphus, is named in Ithaca as the last of his suppers (2.17–20; see [[antiphus-son-of-aegyptius]] and [[hetairos]]). Twice the formula closes a loss, “glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions” (9.63, 9.566; see [[from-there-we-sailed-on]]).

**His names and epithets in this book.** πολύμητις Ὀδυσσεύς, “Odysseus of many wiles” (9.1; see [[of-many-wiles]]); Ὀδυσεὺς Λαερτιάδης, “Odysseus, son of Laertes” (9.19; see [[son-of-laertes]] and [[laertes]]); Ὀδυσσῆα πτολιπόρθιον, “Odysseus, sacker of cities” (9.504, 9.530; see [[sacker-of-cities]]); Ὀδυσῆος and Ὀδυσεῦ in the Cyclops' mouth (9.512, 9.517); Οὖτις, “Nobody” (9.366, 9.369, 9.408, 9.455, 9.460; see [[nobody]]). To the Cyclops he is ξεῖνε, “stranger” (9.273), ἀνὴρ κακός, “a bad man” (9.453), and “one who is small and a nobody and feeble” (9.515; see [[small-and-of-no-account]]); to his companions, σχέτλιε, “Hard man” (9.494; see [[hard]]).
