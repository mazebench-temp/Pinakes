---
summary: In his essays on the Republic Proclus defends Odysseus' praise of the feast against Socrates' censure, explains Plato's jest that the myth of Er is no tale to Alcinous, and counts three tellings of Odysseus' wanderings, the second of them the tale that begins in this book.
---

- **Gladness, not pleasure.** Socrates charged Odysseus with setting up pleasure and the filling of desires as the end of life, when he praised the polity in which “gladness holds the whole people” (9.6–10). Proclus answers that the allegorists place the Phaeacians above mortal nature, and that on the surface Odysseus praises not pleasure but gladness, a city made of one mind by inspired music, and plenty of necessities without excess (*On the Republic* I 129–132 Kroll): [[the-praise-of-the-feast-in-later-writers]] and [[singers]].
- **The tale to Alcinous.** The phrase means, Proclus says, Odysseus' tale of the underworld told in the house of Alcinous, and it became a proverb for every such story; Plato set the valiant Er against the peaceful and luxurious Alcinous who heard the other, and Porphyry rebuked Colotes the Epicurean for mocking the play on the names (*On the Republic* II 111–112 Kroll): [[the-title-of-odyssey-9]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]].
- **Three tellings.** Homer, Proclus observes, gives Odysseus' wandering three times, as it happens, in his narration to Alcinous, and gathered up in a few words to Penelope; and Plato, imitating the arrangement, has the *Republic* given first in the Piraeus, then told in the city by Socrates, and a third time in summary to Timaeus and Critias (*On the Republic* I 171 Kroll, plain renderings): [[the-tale-to-alcinous]] and [[odysseus]].
