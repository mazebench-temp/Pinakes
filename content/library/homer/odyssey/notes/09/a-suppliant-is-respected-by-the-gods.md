summary: Odysseus turns the rule on the Cyclops with the verse-end of the Phaeacians' libation, now said of strangers: “we are your suppliants, / and Zeus is the avenger of suppliants and strangers, / the god of guests, who attends on revered strangers” (9.269–271). The Cyclops answers that the Cyclopes care nothing for Zeus (9.275–276).
en: [who attends on revered strangers]
---

In Scheria the rule became a rite, a libation to Zeus “who attends on revered suppliants” (7.165, 7.181; see the Book 7 note). In the Cyclops' cave it is an argument, and the verse-end is said of strangers: “the god of guests, who attends on revered strangers” (ξείνιος, ὃς ξείνοισιν ἅμʼ αἰδοίοισιν ὀπηδεῖ, 9.271; see [[xenia]] and [[supplication]]). αἰδοίοισιν does not single out particular strangers: all guests are revered as guests (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.271; see [[aidos]]). The verse that carries it Bekker set at the foot of the page, and Köchly judged it feeble and borrowed from Book 7 (Merry's critical note on 9.271; see the translation's note on 9.271). The plea begins with the verb of the same root, “But respect the gods, good sir” (ἀλλʼ αἰδεῖο, 9.269), and the Cyclops refuses even to fear them: “you who bid me either fear or avoid the gods; / for the Cyclopes care nothing for aegis-bearing Zeus” (9.274–275; see [[deos]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]).
