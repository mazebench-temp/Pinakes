---
summary: The lotus of the Lotus-eaters is a fruit, “the honey-sweet fruit of the lotus”, which makes those who eat it forget their homecoming (9.93–102). It is not the meadow plant that the Iliad's horses graze, though the men are “munching the lotus” in the words of the Iliad's horses (9.97).
# λωτοῖο (9.93, 9.94, 9.102), the genitive, met first here; λωτὸν (9.97) is the entry's form.
grc: [λωτοῖο]
---

**A fruit.** The Lotus-eaters “eat a flowery food” (9.84; see [[lotus-eaters]]): ἄνθινον εἶδαρ is “vegetable” food, W. W. Merry explains, and he reports Bothe's conjecture ἀκάνθινον, “thorny”, drawn from Polybius' description of the lotus as a small tree, rough and thorny (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.84). They give the scouts “some of the lotus to taste” (9.93), and the lotus has a fruit: “And whoever of them ate the honey-sweet fruit of the lotus / no longer wished to bring back word or to return” (9.94–95; see [[honey-sweet]] and [[the-lotus-eaters]]). Merry keeps this λωτός apart from the plant of the same name in the meadows of the Iliad and in Menelaus' plain (*Iliad* 2.776; *Odyssey* 4.603), and sets beside the honey-sweetness Herodotus' account of the fruit (on 9.94): on the coast of Libya the Lotus-eaters live on the fruit of the lotus alone, which is as big as a mastic berry and as sweet as the date, and they make wine from it too (Herodotus 4.177.1). Modern travellers, he adds, report that the fruit, which they call the jujube, is still eaten on the north coast of Africa and far inland (on 9.94).

**Munching the lotus.** The scouts “wished to remain there among the Lotus-eaters, / munching the lotus, and to forget their homecoming” (9.96–97; see [[nostos]]). λωτὸν ἐρεπτόμενοι is the phrase of the horses of Achilles' men, standing idle by their chariots, “munching lotus and marsh-grown parsley” (*Iliad* 2.775–777; see [[horses]]): ἐρέπτομαι is used elsewhere of beasts feeding, and here of men, as it were browsing (Merry on 9.97). The fear that follows is put in the same words: Odysseus orders the rest on board “for fear that one of them might eat of the lotus and forget his homecoming” (9.102).
