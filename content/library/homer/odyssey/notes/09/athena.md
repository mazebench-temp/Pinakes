---
summary: Athena is named once in the book, in Odysseus' hope in the Cyclops' cave, “to see whether I might take vengeance, and Athena grant me triumph” (9.317). In the tale of his wanderings she does nothing for him, and the poem gives the reason elsewhere: she would not fight against Poseidon, her father's brother.
---

**Triumph from Athena.** When the Cyclops has eaten his morning meal, driven his flocks out and closed the cave behind him, Odysseus is left inside: “but I was left there, brooding evil deep within, / to see whether I might take vengeance, and Athena grant me triumph. / And this plan seemed to me in my heart the best” (9.316–318; see [[odysseus]], [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]] and [[boule]]). εὖχος is not a prayer here but “triumph”, the glory that a god grants to the victor (see [[euchomai]]). Nestor tells his victory over Ereuthalion with nearly the same half-verse, “And I fought him, and Athena gave me triumph” (*Iliad* 7.154; see [[ereuthalion]]), and the warriors of the Iliad look for it from Apollo: Hector in his challenge, “and Apollo gives me triumph” (*Iliad* 7.81), and Apollo himself, in the likeness of Asius, urging Hector against Patroclus (*Iliad* 16.725). In the Odyssey the words return with Apollo's name, when Penelope speaks of the beggar and the bow and when Odysseus aims his first arrow at the suitors (21.338, 22.7). The plan that follows is the stake (9.319–335; see [[stake]]).

**Absent from the tale.** In the tale of his wanderings Odysseus names her for himself only here; in the rest of Books 9–12 she is named only at the judgment of the arms of Achilles, which Odysseus recalls before the shade of Ajax, and in the words of Heracles in the underworld (11.547, 11.626). The poem has given the reason. At Scheria Odysseus prayed to her, complaining that she had not listened to him when the famous shaker of the earth wrecked him (6.324–327), and “Pallas Athena heard him. / But she did not yet appear to him face to face; for she respected / her father's brother” (6.328–330; see the Book 6 note and [[poseidon]]). In Ithaca he tells her that after the city of Priam was sacked he never saw her, nor knew her to board his ship to ward off any pain from him (13.316–319, a plain rendering), and she answers that she did not wish to fight Poseidon, her father's brother, who had laid up anger in his heart against him, enraged because Odysseus blinded his dear son (13.341–343, a plain rendering). In Euripides' satyr play Odysseus calls on her in the cave: “O Pallas, O lady goddess born of Zeus, help me now” (*Cyclops* 350–351; see [[euripides-cyclops]]).
