summary: The adventure fills the rest of the book (9.105–566) and four days of the tale, from the night of the landfall on the island of goats to the dawn of the departure. Odysseus tells it with the knowledge of its end, and in the order of its days: a day on the island; a day that ends in the cave with the Cyclops' first meal; a day of waiting, the stake, the wine and the blinding; and a day of escape, taunts, curse and sacrifice. The Cyclops eats two companions at each of three meals; four are chosen by lot to help with the stake; the six who are left escape under the rams.

**Four days.** The ships come in by night: “some god guided us / through the dark night, and nothing could be seen” (9.142–143), and ὀρφναίην, “dark”, is no standing epithet of night but belongs to this misty night (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.143). The first day is spent on the island, hunting and feasting until the sun goes down (9.152–169). On the second Odysseus crosses to the cave, and the day ends with the Cyclops' supper of two companions and a night of waiting: “So then, groaning, we waited for brilliant Dawn” (9.170–306). The third day has a meal of two at its beginning and at its end, and between them the making of the stake; the wine, the blinding and the cries of the other Cyclopes “through the ambrosial night” fill its night, and the same verse closes it (9.307–436). The fourth day brings the escape, the taunts, the curse and the sacrifice, and a feast until sunset on the island; the dawn verse of the fifth sends the ships on (9.437–566). The dawn verse, “And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered”, marks each of these mornings (9.152, 9.170, 9.307, 9.437, 9.560; see [[dawn-line]]).

**The numbers.** Twelve ships followed Odysseus, and each received nine of the island's goats, and he ten (9.159–160): λάγχανον is the one place in Homer where λαγχάνω means “fall by lot” (Merry on 9.160). He crosses with his own ship alone, ἐμῇ … ἐμοῖς, “in my own ship with my own crew”, and the rest of the fleet waits at the island until he comes back (9.173, 9.543–545; Merry on 9.173). From his crew he chooses twelve, “the best” (9.195); the Cyclops eats two at each of three meals (9.289–293, 9.311, 9.344), four are chosen by lot to help with the stake, and Odysseus counts himself in as the fifth (9.331–335); the six who are left go out under the rams, three sheep to a man, and he under the ram (9.429–435). In the second book the last of those eaten is named, Antiphus, son of the Ithacan elder Aegyptius (2.17–20; see [[antiphus-son-of-aegyptius]]).

**The trial.** The crossing is an inquiry: “I … will go and make trial of these men, who they are, / whether they are arrogant and savage and unjust, / or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods” (9.173–176; see [[peira]]). The last two verses are the verses in which Odysseus asked himself what people he had come to when he woke on the shore of Scheria (6.120–121), and they come again when he wakes on Ithaca (13.201–202; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]] and [[xenia]]). The departure is told in the formula of setting out, πρυμνήσια λῦσαι, “loose the stern-cables”, which Merry takes here as a mere formula for starting, since the ships had been beached, not moored (9.178; Merry on 9.178; see [[sailing-type-scene]]). From the island the land was judged near by its smoke and its voices (9.166–167; Merry on 9.167).

**The teller's foreknowledge.** Odysseus tells the approach with the end in view. As he climbs to the cave with the wine, “at once my proud spirit foreboded / that a man would come upon us, clothed in great courage, / a savage, who knew well neither judgments nor ordinances” (9.213–215): ἄνδρα is the subject of ἐπελεύσεσθαι, “that a man would come upon us”, not its object, as Nitzsch preferred (Merry on 9.214; see the translation's note on 9.214). Against his companions' plea he stays, “but I did not listen—it would have been far better—”, to see the man himself, εἴ μοι ξείνια δοίη, “whether he would give me guest-gifts” (9.228–229), εἰ standing where some word such as “to see” or “to try” is wanting (Merry on 9.229; see [[guest-gifts]]). The verse of regret is Hector's, who did not listen to Polydamas (*Iliad* 22.103), and Pandarus', who did not listen to his father (*Iliad* 5.201). The next verse gives the outcome before the event: “But when he appeared, he was not to be a lovely sight for my companions” (9.230).

**The second thought.** On the first night Odysseus would have stabbed the Cyclops where he lay, but “there we too would have perished by sheer destruction” (9.303): καὶ ἄμμες is “we as well as he”, assassins and victim together, since no one but the Cyclops could move the stone (9.303–305; Merry on 9.303; see [[door-stone]]). The plan that takes its place begins the next morning: “to see whether I might take vengeance, and Athena grant me triumph. / And this plan seemed to me in my heart the best” (9.317–318; see [[athena]] and [[boule]]). That evening the Cyclops drives all his flocks into the cave and leaves none outside, “either because he suspected something, or because a god so bade him” (9.339): ὀισάμενος is “having some foreboding” (Merry on 9.339).

**Out of range and within hearing.** The ship rows out as far as a shout carries before Odysseus speaks (9.473 = 5.400). The rock falls in front of the ship, and the verse that follows, “by a little, and it failed to reach the tip of the steering oar” (9.483), Merry thinks cannot be read here, though it fits 9.540, since a stone that fell before the bows would not go near the steering oar at the stern, which faced the shore; Eustathius reports that it was marked with the asterisk and the obelus (Merry on 9.483 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 9.483 and [[steering-oar]]). The backwash, πλημυρίς, is not the flood tide but the swell from the fall of the stone, setting shoreward, as a floating stick is brought to the bank by stones thrown beyond it (Merry on 9.485–486). Odysseus pushes off with the pole, ὦσα παρέξ, which Merry takes as more than “pushed her off”: “punting” the ship some way along the shore (on 9.488); and he signs to the rowers with his head, κρατὶ κατανεύων, because no one dared make a sound within range (9.487–490; Merry on 9.490). At “twice as far” he speaks again (9.491), and Merry points out the inconsistency: to row out of range should be to be out of hearing, if the first position was as far as a shout carries; yet the Cyclops still hears him, and there is no ground for supposing that he had come down to the water (Merry on 9.491).

**The curse.** The Cyclops' prayer asks that Odysseus may not reach home, “the son of Laertes, who has his home in Ithaca” (9.530–531); the second of these verses, wanting in twelve manuscripts and in Eustathius' text, has been bracketed by nearly all editors since Wolf (Merry's critical note on 9.531; see the translation's note on 9.531). The ship of another, νηὸς ἐπʼ ἀλλοτρίης (9.535), is the Phaeacian ship that brings Odysseus home (Merry on 9.535). For the curse and its fulfillment, see [[the-return-of-odysseus]] and [[curses]]. The second rock falls behind the ship, and the wave carries it to the island, where χέρσος is the shore of the island, not the mainland of 9.486 (9.539–542; see the translation's notes on 9.539–540 and 9.542).

**The ram for Zeus.** The companions give Odysseus ἀρνειόν, “the ram”, the very ram by which he escaped, as a special gift (9.550–551; Merry on 9.550; see [[ram]]), and the flocks are divided in the verse with which the spoil of Ismarus was divided (9.549 = 9.42; see [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]]). Zeus “took no heed of the offering, / but was pondering how they might all perish, / my well-benched ships and my trusty companions” (9.553–555): he refused to notice the offering, Merry explains, because the prayer of Polyphemus had been heard and would be answered (on 9.553; see [[zeus]] and [[sacrifice]]). Then the day ends as the first day on the island ended, in the same four verses (9.556–559 = 9.161–162, 9.168–169), and the dawn sends the ships away in the verses of the departure from Ismarus (9.560–566; 9.62–63; see [[from-there-we-sailed-on]]).
