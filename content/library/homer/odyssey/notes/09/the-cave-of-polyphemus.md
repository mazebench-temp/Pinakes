---
summary: The cave is the scene of the book's long middle: Odysseus finds it at the far edge of the land (9.181–192), goes in with twelve companions while its master is at pasture (9.216–233), is shut in by the door-stone (9.233–243), and gets out only under the rams, through a doorway where the blinded Cyclops sits feeling for him (9.415–463).
---

**At the far edge.** Across from the island of goats, “there at the far edge of the land, close to the sea, we saw a cave, / high, and overhung with laurels” (9.182–183; see [[the-island-of-goats]] and [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). ἐπʼ ἐσχατιῇ, “at the far edge”, returns when the Cyclops asks where the stranger moored his ship, “whether somewhere at the far edge of the land, or near” (9.280; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.182; see the translation's key words), and Odysseus answers with the lie that Poseidon wrecked it (9.283–286; see [[poseidon]]). The digamma of εἴδομεν makes σπέος εἴδομεν hard to scan; one manuscript has ἴδομεν (Merry's critical note on 9.182; see the translation's note on 9.182). Around the cave stands the fold, the walls of which Merry imagines in the massive style later called Cyclopean (on 9.184; see [[fold]]), and in it sleeps the huge man who lives apart from the others (9.184–192; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]).

**Inside.** Odysseus leaves his crew by the ship and goes up with twelve companions and the goatskin of Maron's wine (9.193–215; see [[wine]]): “Quickly we came to the cave, but we did not find him / inside; he was herding his fat flocks in the pasture. / We went into the cave and gazed at each thing” (9.216–218). The racks of cheeses, the pens and the vessels of whey are a herdsman's dairy (9.219–223; see the translation's key words). The companions beg him to take cheeses, kids and lambs and sail away; he stays “so that I might see him himself, and whether he would give me guest-gifts” (9.224–230; see [[guest-gifts]]). They kindle a fire, make an offering, eat, and wait inside (9.231–233).

**Shut in.** The Cyclops throws his wood down inside the cave with a din, and the men “in terror shrank back into the inner part of the cave” (9.235–236). He drives in the flocks he milks and leaves the males outside (9.237–239), and then “so lofty a rock he set against the doorway” (9.240–243; see the translation's note on 9.235, 9.239, 9.338). From then on the cave is a prison: Odysseus could stab him in his sleep, but “we could not have pushed away from the high doorway / with our hands the heavy stone that he had set against it” (9.304–305). The stone goes back in the morning “as one sets the lid on a quiver” (9.313–314), and the stake that will blind him lies hidden under the dung “which lay spread throughout the cave in great abundance” (9.329–330): κατὰ σπείους, Merry explains, is “adown the cave”, from the mouth to the inmost part, and he finds μεγάλʼ “very suspicious” there, since Homer uses μεγάλα as an adverb almost always with verbs of sound (Merry on 9.330).

**The cry and the neighbors.** At the blinding “he groaned aloud terribly, and the rock rang round about” (9.395). He calls the Cyclopes, who live around him “in caves among the windy heights”, and they come and stand “around the cave”, asking what ails him, and he answers them “from the cave” (9.399–408; see [[cyclopes]]). The neighbors never go in: they hear only the voice inside, and the name Nobody (see [[nobody]]).

**The way out.** In the morning the Cyclops takes the stone from the doorway and sits in it, stretching out his hands to catch anyone who goes out among the sheep (9.415–419). The ram comes last, and his master asks it why it hurries out “through the cave” (9.447), and wishes that he could dash Nobody's brains out “this way and that through the cave” (9.458). “When we had gone a little way from the cave and the fold” (9.462), Odysseus frees himself and his companions, and from the ship he taunts the Cyclops: it was not a weakling's companions after all that he was to eat “in your hollow cave” (9.475–476; see [[hollow-caves]]).
