---
summary: Calypso swears to Odysseus by Earth, the wide Heaven and “the down-flowing water of Styx, which is the greatest / and most dread oath for the blessed gods”, that she plans him no harm. The three verses are Hera's oath to Zeus in the Iliad.
---

**The oath he asks.** When Calypso bids him build a raft, Odysseus suspects another purpose and will not set foot on it “unless you would bring yourself, goddess, to swear me a great oath / that you will not plan some other evil bane against me myself” (5.178–179), the verses with which he will bind Circe (10.343–344; see [[oath-formulas]] and [[oath]]). Calypso smiles, strokes him with her hand, calls him a rogue and no fool (5.180–183), and swears:

{{quote:b0d30078-c674-5e77-b1ec-f0eeda0b7bf9}}

The first three verses are Hera's oath to Zeus that Poseidon does not help the Achaeans by her will (*Iliad* 15.36–38; see the Iliad note on Book 15). There a goddess swears to a god, and adds Zeus' head and their marriage bed (*Iliad* 15.39–40); here a goddess swears the gods' own oath to a mortal man, and adds nothing but the matter of the oath, in Odysseus' words turned round (5.187 = 5.179, with τοι for μοι). Earth below, the wide Heaven above and the water of Styx beneath the earth are her witnesses (see [[earth]] and [[sky]]). When Circe swears to Odysseus, the poem says only that she swore as he bade her (10.345–346).

**Hesiod's Styx.** Merry explains the oath from Hesiod (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.185; see the translation's note on 5.185). In the *Theogony* Styx, the eldest daughter of Oceanus, came first to Olympus with her children when Zeus called the gods against the Titans, and he made her “the great oath of the gods” (*Theogony* 383–403, at 400); when a god has lied, Iris fetches her cold water from afar in a golden jug, a tenth part of the water of Oceanus, and a god who swears falsely by it lies a year without breath or voice and without nectar or ambrosia, and is kept from the gods' councils and feasts for nine years more (*Theogony* 775–806; see [[hesiod]]). Hesiod's forsworn god lies ἀνάπνευστος καὶ ἄναυδος (797), nearly the words of Odysseus spent at the river's mouth, “breathless and speechless” (ἄπνευστος καὶ ἄναυδος, 5.456). Merry also describes the Arcadian Styx near Nonacris, a stream falling over a precipice into a basin of black rock, as Pausanias saw it (8.18, as Merry reports him), whose water local tradition said would destroy any vessel it was kept in.
