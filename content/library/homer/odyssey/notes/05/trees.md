summary: The trees of Calypso's island are the wood around her cave, alder, poplar and fragrant cypress, where the long-winged birds roost (5.63–67), and the tall trees at the far edge of the island, alder, poplar and fir that reached to the sky, long dead and dry, of which Odysseus fells twenty for his raft (5.237–244).

**The wood around the cave.** When Hermes comes to the cave he sees first the fire and its scent, then the goddess at her loom, and then the wood: “And around the cave a wood had grown, flourishing, / alder and poplar and fragrant cypress” (5.63–64; see [[calypsos-cave]] and [[calypso]]). τηλεθόωσα, “flourishing”, is said of the wood that puts forth new leaves in the Iliad's simile of the generations of men, and of the fig trees and olives of Alcinous' orchard (*Iliad* 6.147–148; 7.116). Of the three trees Homer names here, κλήθρη, the alder, occurs only in this book, and κυπάρισσος, the cypress, only here, though the doorpost of Odysseus' hall is of cypress wood, smoothed by a carpenter in the verse with which Odysseus smooths the beams of his raft (17.340–341; 5.245); εὐώδης, “fragrant”, gives the cypress its scent (see [[fragrant]]). W. W. Merry takes αἴγειρος for the aspen, *Populus tremula*: in Homer it grows in marshy soil or near water, as here, in Athena's grove of poplars by a spring on Scheria, around the spring of the town of Ithaca, and in the meadow of a great marsh in a simile of the Iliad (6.291–292, 17.205–208; *Iliad* 4.482–483), and the constant movement of its leaves points a simile of the Phaeacian women at their weaving (7.105–106); Curtius, he reports, would connect the name with αἰγίς and καταιγίζω, as *populus* perhaps with πάλλω, “shake” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.64). The long-winged birds roost in the wood, owls and hawks and the long-tongued crows of the sea (5.65–67; see [[birds]] and [[hawk]]), and the garden vine grows over the cave itself, a different kind of growth, as Merry points out (on 5.68; see [[vine]]).

**The trees for the raft.** At dawn Calypso gives Odysseus an axe and an adze, and “she led the way / to the far edge of the island, where tall trees grew, / alder and poplar, and fir that reached to the sky, / dry long since, well seasoned, that would float lightly for him” (5.237–240; see [[axe]], [[adze]] and [[the-building-of-the-raft]]). ὅθι δένδρεα μακρὰ πεφύκει, “where tall trees grew”, is said twice (5.238, 5.241), and is said again of the orchard of Alcinous (7.114); in the Iliad the woodcutter and the boar of Calydon bring down δένδρεα μακρά (*Iliad* 11.88, 9.541). The fir, ἐλάτη, the tall tree to which two sons of Diocles are likened as they fall, and in which Sleep hides on Ida (*Iliad* 5.559–560, 14.286–288; see [[fir-trees]]), is here οὐρανομήκης, a fir “that reached to the sky”, a word found only here. αὖα πάλαι, περίκηλα, “dry long since, well seasoned”, are the words of the firewood set round the braziers in Odysseus' hall (18.308–309): Merry renders “long ago sapless and dry”, and takes the trees to be dead though still standing, which in an emergency might serve in place of properly seasoned timber (on 5.240). περίκηλα is Aristarchus' reading; Chrysippus divided it, περὶ κῆλα (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 5.240). The dryness is the point: wood that has long lost its sap is light, τά οἱ πλώοιεν ἐλαφρῶς, “that would float lightly for him” (5.240).

**Felling.** “Twenty in all he felled, and hewed them with the bronze, / and smoothed them skillfully and made them straight to the chalk line” (5.244–245; see [[the-chalk-line]]). εἴκοσι … πάντα is “full twenty”, all told (Merry on 5.244). The beams cut from these trees are the δούρατα μακρά that the wave will scatter like a heap of husks (5.370; see [[the-heap-of-husks]]).

**The woods of Scheria.** On the other side of the sea the woods are the first thing Odysseus sees with the land, “so welcome did the land and the woods appear to Odysseus” (5.398; see [[the-fathers-recovery]]), and his last refuge: he climbs from the river to “the shady forest” and finds two bushes, of wild olive and olive, to sleep under (5.470–487; see [[olive]]).
