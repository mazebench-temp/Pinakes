**Among the gods.** In the Iliad the rule is for men: no man, however mighty, can hold back the mind of Zeus (*Iliad* 8.143–144). Here a god says it to a goddess. Hermes has come against his will, and gives the reason he obeyed: Zeus bids her send Odysseus away, “But there is no way that the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus / can be evaded by any other god, or made vain” (5.103–104; see [[hermes]]). ἔστι stands for ἔξεστι, “it is possible”, with the infinitive and its subject in the accusative (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.103); παρεξελθεῖν is “to overreach”, and ἁλιῶσαι, from ἅλιος, “vain”, is “to thwart by open resistance” (on 5.104; see the translation's note on 5.104). The verb is used once in the Iliad, of Patroclus' stone, which struck Hector's charioteer: “nor did he waste his throw” (*Iliad* 16.737).

Then she turns them into consent: “let him go to his ruin, if that god urges him on and commands it” (ἐρρέτω, 5.139; see [[calypso]] and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]). νόος is here the purpose of Zeus, the homecoming he gave Hermes to carry, “our unerring counsel” (5.29–42; see [[noos]], [[boule]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). Hermes leaves her with the threat that stands behind it, “have regard for the wrath of Zeus” (5.146; see [[menis]]).
