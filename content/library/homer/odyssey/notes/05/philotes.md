---
summary: Demeter, yielding to her own heart, “lay with him in love and bed” with Iasion in a thrice-plowed fallow field (5.126), and on the last evening Odysseus and Calypso “took their pleasure in love, staying by each other” (5.227).
---

φιλότης is here the love of a goddess for a mortal man, the subject of Calypso's complaint. Demeter, “yielding to her own heart, lay with him in love and bed / in a thrice-plowed fallow field”, and Zeus killed Iasion with his thunderbolt (5.125–128; see [[demeter-and-iasion]], [[thrice-plowed]] and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]). ᾧ θυμῷ εἴξασα is “yielding to her passion”, not under compulsion, as Meleager, “yielding to his own heart”, saved the Aetolians (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.126; *Iliad* 9.598). μίγη φιλότητι καὶ εὐνῇ ends the verse of other such unions: Paris' with Helen on the island of Cranae, the herdsman Bucolion's with the nymph who bore him twins (*Iliad* 3.445, 6.25), and, in Penelope's words, Helen's with “a man of another land” (23.219). On the last evening, when Odysseus has chosen to go home, “the sun went down and darkness came on; / and the two of them went in, and in the inner part of the hollow cave / took their pleasure in love, staying by each other” (5.225–227; see [[calypso]] and [[immortality]]). In Hesiod's catalogue of the goddesses who lay with mortal men, the same word is used of both unions, Demeter's with Iasion and Calypso's with Odysseus (ἐρατῇ φιλότητι, *Theogony* 969–971, 1017–1018).
