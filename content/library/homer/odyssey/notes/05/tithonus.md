# Τιθωνοῖο (5.1) is claimed by the entry.
---

{{quote:82a95bf4-7c29-5520-8e69-7e09d7f812ae}}

Ἠὼς δʼ ἐκ λεχέων is “a poetical way of expressing ‘the next morning’” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.1; see [[dawn-line]] and [[eos]]). ἀγαυός, “noble”, makes Tithonus a youthful hero, whom Dawn carried off for his beauty, as in Tyrtaeus' “not even if he were more graceful in form than Tithonus”, which Merry quotes from Stobaeus (on 5.1; see [[noble]]). Merry finds the first mention of his unhappy gift, immortality without immortal youth, in the *Hymn to Aphrodite* (218–238), and reads the story as an allegory of the fresh morning hours turning to the scorching noon that brings weariness and weakness, the old man's voice being likened to the cicada, the only creature heard in the heat of the day (on 5.1; see [[cicadas]]).

Dawn comes back in Calypso's protest, the first of the goddesses whom the gods begrudged a mortal lover, when she took Orion for herself (5.121–124; see [[dawn-and-orion]] and [[calypso]]). What Calypso promised Odysseus was to make him “immortal and ageless all his days” (5.136; see [[immortal-and-ageless]]), the youth that the Hymn says Dawn did not think to ask for Tithonus (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 223–224).
