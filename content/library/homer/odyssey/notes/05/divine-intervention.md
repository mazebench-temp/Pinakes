summary: The book is a chain of divine acts. The gods decide in council and send their messenger; Calypso obeys Zeus, gives the tools and the cloth, and sends a fair wind; Poseidon raises the storm and breaks the raft; Ino gives her veil; Athena lays the winds, rouses the North Wind and twice puts the saving thought in Odysseus' mind; the river god takes him in; and Athena pours sleep on his eyes. Odysseus knows only some of them.
# Unnamed: the fair wind Calypso promises and sends (5.167, 5.268); Poseidon's storm and wave (5.291-296, 5.366-367);
# Ino's gift (5.346-353); Athena's winds (5.383-387); the river god's calm (5.451-453).

**Decided above.** The homecoming is settled among the gods before Odysseus hears of it: the council meets, Zeus fixes the manner and the day, and Hermes carries the order to Calypso, who cannot evade the mind of Zeus (5.1–148; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]], [[hermes]] and [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). The man learns of it from Calypso, who tells him nothing of Zeus' order, only that she will send him away, and he suspects her of another plan (5.160–179; see [[calypso]] and [[odysseus]]).

**Helped and hindered at sea.** Every turn of the voyage is given to a god. Calypso sends “a fair wind, harmless and warm” (5.268; promised at 5.167). Poseidon, coming back from the Ethiopians, raises the storm, and later the wave that breaks the raft (5.282–296, 5.366–370; see [[poseidon]]). Ino pities Odysseus and gives him her veil (5.333–353; see [[leucothea]]). Athena binds the winds and rouses the North Wind (5.382–387), and twice saves him on the rocks: “had not the gray-eyed goddess Athena put it in his mind”, “had not gray-eyed Athena given him presence of mind” (5.427, 5.437; see [[athena]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). The river god stops his stream and brings him in (5.451–453; see [[the-river-of-scheria]]). Athena ends the day by pouring sleep on his eyes (5.491–493).

**What the man knows.** Odysseus sees two of these gods, Calypso and Ino, and recognizes a third, the river, by its current (5.444). The others he guesses at or does not know. He gives Poseidon's storm to Zeus (5.303–305; see [[zeus]]), learns of Poseidon's anger from Ino and speaks of it himself on the rocks (5.339–341, 5.423), thanks Zeus for the sight of land (5.408–409), and fears that a god may send a sea monster against him (5.421). Of Athena's help he knows nothing: in the next book he prays to her as a goddess who never heard him while he was being wrecked (6.324–326). What she gives him is a thought, and the act stays his own, as when he rushes at the rock and clings to it (5.428–429; see the general article).
