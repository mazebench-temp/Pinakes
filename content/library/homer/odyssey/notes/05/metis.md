---
summary: Odysseus is πολύμητις, “of many wiles”, when he answers Calypso's offer of immortality (5.214), and Calypso calls him “a rogue, and no fool” when he asks her for an oath (5.182).
---

The epithet stands at the head of his refusal, “Answering her, Odysseus of many wiles said” (5.214; see [[of-many-wiles]] and [[immortality]]), and the answer shows the quality: where Calypso laid all the stress on Penelope's beauty, Merry notes, he has the tact to speak only of his wife's wisdom and of his home (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.216; see [[penelope]]). Calypso has named his craft before, in play, when he would not trust her offer of a raft without an oath: “Truly you are a rogue, and no fool, / what a speech you have thought of making!” (5.182–183). Merry hears a sportive tone, a goddess laughing at the prudent way in which he takes care of himself (on 5.182; see the translation's table of key words and [[dolos]]). In the storm his cunning is the choosing between counsels: he stays on the raft while its beams hold and then swims (5.356–364), and on the shore he takes the forest rather than the river bank, as “this seemed to him the better way” (5.465–474; see [[kerdos]] and [[noos]]).
