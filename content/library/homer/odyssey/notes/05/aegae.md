---
summary: When the raft has broken up and Odysseus is swimming, Poseidon lashes his horses and drives home to Aegae, “where his glorious house is” (5.380–381), in the words the Iliad uses of his house.
---

**Home to Aegae.** Poseidon leaves the scene by chariot. After his last taunt he “lashed the fine-maned horses, / and came to Aegae, where his glorious house is” (5.380–381; see [[poseidon]] and [[fine-maned]]). ὅθι οἱ κλυτὰ δώματʼ ἔασιν is the Iliad's κλυτὰ δώματα, said of the same house when the god strides from Samothrace to Aegae, “where his glorious house is built in the depths of the sea”, and yokes his horses there (*Iliad* 13.20–26; see [[chariot-journey]]). The horses explain a word of his threat a few verses before, “But I say I will yet give him his fill of misery” (5.290): ἐλάαν, “drive”, keeps its liveliness in the mouth of a god who comes on the scene with his fine-maned horses, and Merry renders it “I will yet drive him to his heart's content along the road of misery” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.290; see the translation's note on 5.290). As soon as he is gone, Athena binds the paths of the other winds and rouses the North Wind to break the waves before Odysseus (5.382–387; see [[athena]] and [[boreas]]).

**Which Aegae.** Merry takes the Aegae of this verse, with the first explanation in the scholia, for the town of Achaea that the Iliad names with Helice (*Iliad* 8.203; Merry on 5.381; see [[helice]]); Strabo, who knew two places of the name, took the Aegae of the god's house in the Iliad for the Euboean one (*Iliad* 13.21). The other explanations are in the general article.
