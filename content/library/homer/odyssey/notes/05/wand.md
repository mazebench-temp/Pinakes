---
summary: Hermes takes up his wand for the journey to Calypso, in the verses of his journey to Priam (5.47–49 = *Iliad* 24.343–345), and Calypso greets him as “Hermes of the golden wand” (5.87). The wand is not used in the book.
---

“and he took the wand with which he bewitches the eyes of men, / of whomever he will, and again wakes others who are sleeping; / holding it in his hands the mighty slayer of Argus flew” (5.47–49; see [[hermes]] and [[sandals]]). The verses are those of the Iliad (*Iliad* 24.343–345), where the wand has work to do: Hermes pours sleep on the sentinels of the Achaean wall and brings Priam through the camp (*Iliad* 24.443–447). Here it has none, and W. W. Merry finds the mention of the wand and its sleep-bringing power more appropriate in the Iliad (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.43–49). The ancient critics had argued it already. A scholium records the objection that the wand is of no use here, as it is in the Iliad for putting the gatekeepers to sleep, and answers that some things are the gods' own accoutrements, as if one were to blame Poseidon for holding his trident on his way to the Ethiopians (Merry's critical note on 5.43–49; see the translation's note on 5.43–49 and [[poseidon]]). Merry adds that Virgil, adapting the passage, extends the wand's power to giving and taking away the sleep of death (on 5.43–49, citing *Aeneid* 4.238 and following; see [[virgils-mercury]]).

When Hermes comes to the cave, Calypso greets him as Ἑρμεία χρυσόρραπι, “Hermes of the golden wand” (5.87; see [[of-the-golden-wand]]): the Odyssey's wand is golden, as it is when Hermes leads the souls of the suitors to Hades (24.2–4).
