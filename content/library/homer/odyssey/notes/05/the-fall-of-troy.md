summary: Zeus measures the Phaeacians' gifts against the spoil of Troy: they will give Odysseus more than he would ever have carried off from Troy, had he come home unharmed with his share (5.38–40). Hermes dates the sack: the Achaeans fought around the city of Priam for nine years, and in the tenth sacked it and went home (5.106–108).
# Unnamed: "won his share of the spoil" (5.40), and "in the tenth sacked the city" (5.107).
refs: [5.40, 5.107]
---

**The spoil.** “giving him bronze and gold in plenty, and clothing, / a great store, more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy, / if he had come home free of harm and won his share of the spoil” (5.38–40; see [[zeus]] and [[phaeacians]]). λαχὼν … αἶσαν, “having got his meed from the spoil”, is the phrase of Achilles' promise to Menoetius, that he would bring Patroclus home to Opus “when he had sacked Ilios and won his share of the spoil” (*Iliad* 18.326–327; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.40; see [[division-of-spoils]]). Τροίης, “from Troy”, is the land of Troy, as in the envy of Odysseus' companions, who say that he brings home many fine treasures “from Troy”, out of the spoil, while they come home empty-handed (10.40–42, a plain rendering; Merry on 5.39); the scholia read Τρωίης, an adjective with ληίδος, “from the Trojan spoil” (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 5.39). When the promise has been kept, Poseidon repeats the verses in his complaint to Zeus that the Phaeacians have set Odysseus down in Ithaca asleep, with countless gifts (13.128–138; see [[poseidon]]).

**Nine years, and the tenth.** “the men who fought around the city of Priam / for nine years, and in the tenth sacked the city and went / home” (5.106–108; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]] and [[priam]]). The count is Calchas' at Aulis, that they would fight there as many years as the sparrow and her young, “and in the tenth we shall take the wide-streeted city” (*Iliad* 2.328–329; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]); Nestor's, “For nine years we stitched evils together for them” (3.118); and the Cretan stranger's, who fought nine years and sacked the city in the tenth (14.240–241).
