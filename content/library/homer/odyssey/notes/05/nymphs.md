summary: Calypso is the nymph of the book, called so eight times: “the nymph of the lovely tresses” to whom Hermes is sent, who lives in a great cave with four springs before it, “the lady nymph” who goes to Odysseus with Zeus' message, the nymph who no longer pleases him, who sets mortal food before him and dresses in a silver-white mantle.
# νύμφης, the genitive, is in the Odyssey always a nymph: Calypso (4.557 = 5.14 = 17.143; 5.6) and, as νύμφῃς, the
# nymphs of Ithaca (13.355). In the Iliad νύμφης is a bride (9.560), so the form is in the Odyssey's note, not the entry.

**The nymph Calypso.** Athena pleads for Odysseus “there in the house of the nymph” (ἐν δώμασι νύμφης, 5.6), held “in the halls of the nymph Calypso” (5.14 = 4.557; see [[calypso]]), and Zeus sends Hermes to tell “the nymph of the lovely tresses our unerring counsel” (5.30 = 1.86; see [[with-lovely-tresses]]). Hermes finds her where nymphs live, in a cave: “until he came to the great cave, in which the nymph / of the lovely tresses lived” (5.57–58), with a vine stretched about it and four springs flowing with clear water in a row before it (5.68–71; see [[calypsos-cave]]). The nymphs of Homer are goddesses of springs and caves (see the general article), and the cave of the Naiads on Ithaca, with its springs and its stone looms on which the nymphs weave, is the other such place in the poem (13.103–112). Calypso too weaves at her loom (5.61–62; see [[loom]]).

**The lady nymph.** When Hermes has gone, “the lady nymph went to great-hearted Odysseus, / since she had heard the messages of Zeus” (5.149–150; see [[queenly]]). Odysseus' life is ebbing away “as he mourned for his homecoming, since the nymph no longer pleased him” (5.153). In the cave “the nymph set before him all kinds of food, / to eat and to drink, such as mortal men eat” (5.196–197), while she herself has ambrosia and nectar (5.199; see [[ambrosia]] and [[nectar]]). On the morning of the building, “the nymph herself put on a great silver-white mantle, / fine and lovely” (5.230–231), the verses of Circe's robing (10.543–545), the one place where Circe is called a nymph (see [[circe]] and [[dressing-scene]]). Elsewhere in the book she is θεά, “goddess”, and Καλυψώ, δῖα θεάων, “Calypso, brilliant among goddesses” (5.78 and often; see [[brilliant-among-goddesses]]).
