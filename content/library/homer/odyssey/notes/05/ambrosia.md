**At Hermes' table.** Calypso sets a table beside the god, “filling it with ambrosia, and mixed red nectar”, and he “drank and ate” (5.92–94; see [[nectar]], [[table]] and [[xenia]]). Ambrosia is the food and nectar the drink, a distinction that Bergk, as W. W. Merry reports him, thought had grown up over time and was more noticeable in the Odyssey than in the Iliad (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.445; see the note on Book 4).

**Two foods.** On the last evening the goddess and the man eat at one meal but not the same food: “the nymph set before him all kinds of food, / to eat and to drink, such as mortal men eat”, and “the slave women set ambrosia and nectar beside her” (5.196–199; see [[feast-type-scene]] and [[handmaids]]). Merry draws from this meal that ambrosia was the food that sustained immortality, since Calypso eats it herself but gives Odysseus “the bread of men”; and since Achilles is fed with nectar and ambrosia in the Iliad and is not freed from death, it was the regular eating of it, not a single taste, that made the gods immortal (on 4.445, citing *Iliad* 19.353; and on 5.197). Calypso had promised to make Odysseus immortal and ageless, and in the talk after this meal she offers it again if he will stay (5.135–136, 5.208–210; see [[immortal-and-ageless]]). Merry reports Hayman's remark that now that she has no hope of keeping him she feeds him with mortal food, though until then he had lived with her as an immortal (on 5.136). The Iliad explains the gods' nature by the food they do not eat: they are bloodless and immortal because they eat no bread and drink no wine (*Iliad* 5.341–342; see [[ichor]]).

**Elsewhere in the Odyssey.** The Cyclops, tasting Maron's wine, calls it “a piece broken off from ambrosia and nectar” (9.359, a plain rendering; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]), and Circe, describing the Wandering Rocks, tells of the doves that carry ambrosia to father Zeus (12.62–63).
