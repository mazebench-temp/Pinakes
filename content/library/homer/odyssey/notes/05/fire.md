---
summary: A great fire of cedar and thyine wood burns on the hearth of Calypso's cave, and its scent spreads over the island (5.59–61); at the end of the book Odysseus lies under the leaves like a brand that a lonely man hides in the ashes, “keeping alive the seed of fire” (5.488–491).
---

**The fire in the cave.** When Hermes reaches the cave, “A great fire was burning on the hearth, and far off the scent / of cedar that splits easily and of thyine wood spread over the island / as they burned” (5.59–61; see [[calypsos-cave]]). ἐσχαρόφιν, “on the hearth”, is a form of the o-declension made for the meter, and the ἐσχάρα here seems to be a portable brazier (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.59). The wood is chosen for its scent: Homer's κέδρος is a juniper, split into billets for burning, and θύον a resinous tree, not incense (Merry on 5.60; see the translation's note on 5.60). The fire is the first thing in the description, before the goddess at her loom.

**The seed of fire.** At the end of the book Odysseus, cast ashore on Scheria, heaps up a bed of leaves under the olives:

The picture is of a man so remote and lonely that he has no neighbors from whom to get a light if his fire goes out, and so he keeps a brand smoldering under a heap of ashes, to fan it into a flame when he will; the point of the comparison is that Odysseus keeps his spark of life under a warm covering of leaves (Merry on 5.488; see [[the-brand-in-the-ashes]]). Merry compares Xenophon, where Socrates asks whether one would not wish to please a neighbor, so that he may kindle a fire for one when one needs it (on 5.490, citing *Memorabilia* 2.2.12).
