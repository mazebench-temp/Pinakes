---
summary: Zeus presides over the second council and fixes the manner of Odysseus' homecoming, on a raft and without escort, to Scheria on the twentieth day. Hermes and Calypso both bow to “the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus”, and Calypso recalls his thunderbolts on Iasion and on Odysseus' ship. In the storm Odysseus, not knowing that it is Poseidon's, blames Zeus for it, and then thanks him for the sight of land.

**Whose power is the greatest.** The gods sit down to their session “and among them / Zeus who thunders on high, whose power is the greatest” (5.3–4; see [[thunders-on-high]] and [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). Athena addresses him as “Father Zeus” (5.7; see [[father-zeus]]), and “Zeus the cloud-gatherer” answers her (5.21; see [[cloud-gatherer]]) with the verse of the first council and with a reminder that the vengeance was her own plan (5.22–24). Then he gives Hermes the gods' “unerring counsel” (5.30), and fixes the homecoming in more detail than the first council did: no escort of gods or men, “a raft of many bindings, suffering woes”, the twentieth day, Scheria and the Phaeacians, and gifts “more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy” (5.32–40; see [[hermes]] and [[raft]]). The decree ends in the language of fate, “For so it is his fate to see his friends and to reach / his high-roofed house and his native land” (5.41–42; see [[moira]]): what Zeus decides and what is fated are told in one breath.

**The mind of Zeus.** Hermes excuses his errand by Zeus, “It was Zeus who bade me come here, against my will”, and ends his excuse with a verse that Calypso will turn into her own surrender:

{{quote:a4db3fb7-53a4-5df7-9d58-f058a4b24047}}

Hermes speaks the verses first (5.103–104), Calypso repeats them (5.137–138), and both go on to obey (see [[the-mind-of-zeus]] and [[aegis-bearing]]). Merry takes παρεξελθεῖν as “to overreach”, as Circe slips past unseen (10.573), and ἁλιῶσαι as “to thwart by open resistance” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.104; see the translation's note on 5.104). Calypso lets Odysseus go “if that god urges him on and commands it” (5.139), and Hermes leaves her with “have regard for the wrath of Zeus, / lest in time to come he grow angry with you in resentment” (5.146–147; see [[menis]]). She goes to Odysseus “since she had heard the messages of Zeus” (5.150).

**The thunderbolt.** In Calypso's protest Zeus is the god who acts on the gods' jealousy. When Demeter lay with Iasion, “not long without hearing of it / was Zeus, who killed him, striking him with a gleaming thunderbolt” (5.127–128; see [[iasion]], [[demeter]] and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]). And Odysseus came to her island after Zeus wrecked his ship: “Him I saved when he was astride the keel, / alone, when his swift ship, with a gleaming thunderbolt, / Zeus struck and shattered in the middle of the wine-dark sea” (5.130–132; see [[thunderbolt]]). Odysseus tells it in the same words to Arete (7.249–250), and at length to the Phaeacians, how Zeus thundered and hurled his thunderbolt on the ship after the companions had eaten the cattle of the Sun (12.415–419; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). ἔλσας, “struck”, is a crushing blow; Zenodotus read ἐλάσας (Merry on 5.132; see the translation's note on 5.132). On the same evidence Merry holds that 5.110–111, which make the wreck the work of Athena's storm, cannot stand in Hermes' speech (on 5.108; see the translation's note on 5.105–111).

**The wrong god.** Poseidon raises the storm: he gathers the clouds, throws the sea into confusion with his trident, and spurs on the storms “of winds of every kind” (5.291–293; see [[poseidon]]). Odysseus, on the raft, sees the same things and gives them to Zeus: “With what clouds he is wreathing the wide sky, / Zeus, and he has thrown the sea into confusion, and the storms rush on / of winds of every kind” (5.303–305). Not knowing that the storm is Poseidon's work, he lays it on Zeus, as men commonly do (the scholia, in Merry on 5.304; see the translation's note on 5.304). Mortals blame the gods, Zeus complained at the first council (1.32–34; see [[who-is-to-blame]]); here a mortal blames the wrong one. When the land comes in sight, the god he names is Zeus again: “now that he has granted me to see land beyond all hope, / Zeus” (5.408–409).

**Of Zeus.** The book names much else by him. Athena is “daughter of Zeus”, κούρη Διός, as she binds the winds (5.382; see [[daughter-of-zeus]]); Odysseus is διογενής, “sprung from Zeus” (5.203, 5.387; see [[sprung-from-zeus]]); Odysseus objects that not even balanced ships, “glorying in the wind of Zeus”, cross the gulf he is bidden to cross on a raft (Διὸς οὔρῳ, 5.174–176; see [[balanced-ships]]); and Poseidon's taunt sends Odysseus on “until you come among men whom Zeus has nurtured” (5.378), an epithet of kings given here to a whole people, the Phaeacians (Merry on 5.378; see [[zeus-nurtured]] and the translation's note on 5.378).
