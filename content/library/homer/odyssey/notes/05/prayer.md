summary: At the mouth of the river of Scheria Odysseus “knew the god as he flowed forth, and prayed to him in his heart” (5.444): “Hear me, lord, whoever you are” (5.445–450). The god stops his stream and brings him in.
# εὔχομαι εἶναι at 5.450 is "claim to be" (euchomai keeps it), as at 5.211.
except: [5.450]
---

The book's one prayer is spoken by a man come out of the sea. Odysseus reaches the mouth of a fair-flowing river, where the place is smooth, free of rocks and sheltered from the wind, “he knew the god as he flowed forth, and prayed to him in his heart” (5.441–444; see [[the-river-of-scheria]]). Merry takes ἔγνω δὲ προρέοντα as “he recognised him (sc. for a god) as he flowed forth”, and κατὰ θυμόν as “in his heart”, not, as the scholia suggest, because a spent swimmer has no breath for words: Odysseus prays in his heart to Athena in the footrace too (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.444; *Iliad* 23.769). The prayer has its parts. It calls on the god, κλῦθι, ἄναξ, “Hear me, lord”, as Glaucus calls on Apollo (*Iliad* 16.514; see [[hear-me]]), and since the river has no name, “whoever you are” (5.445). It gives the god a reason to hear: the wanderer who comes to them is to be respected even by the immortal gods (5.447–449; see [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]]). And it asks, “But pity me, lord; I claim to be your suppliant” (5.450; see [[pity]] and [[supplication]]); ἱκέτης δέ τοι εὔχομαι εἶναι is εὔχομαι of claiming, not of praying, as in Calypso's “I claim to be in no way inferior to her” (5.211), and both lines are excepted here (see [[euchomai]]). The answer comes at once: the god stops his stream, holds back the wave, makes a calm before him and brings him safe into the river's mouth (5.451–453).
