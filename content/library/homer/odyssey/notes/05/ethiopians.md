---
summary: Poseidon comes back from the Ethiopians, where he was feasting when the gods resolved on Odysseus' return, and sees him on his raft near the land of the Phaeacians: “Ah, truly the gods have changed their purpose / about Odysseus, while I was among the Ethiopians” (5.282–287).
---

**The god comes back.** The absence on which the poem opened ends here. In the first book Poseidon had gone to the far-off Ethiopians to share in a hecatomb of bulls and rams, while the other gods met in the halls of Zeus (1.22–27; see the Book 1 note); he “had gone to visit the Aethiopians and to receive a sacrifice at their hands” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.282). Both councils of the gods were held without him (1.26–95; 5.3–42; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]), and returning “from the Ethiopians” he sees Odysseus from the mountains of the Solymi and guesses what has been done (5.282–290; see [[poseidon]] and [[solymi]]): “Ah, truly the gods have changed their purpose / about Odysseus, while I was among the Ethiopians” (5.286–287). Zeus had said in the first council that Poseidon would let go his anger, since he could not strive alone against all the immortals (1.76–79). He does not let it go: he raises the storm at once (5.291–296).

**Where the Ethiopians are.** Strabo reads the verse as a sign of how Homer thought of the Ethiopians. For him the poet's Ethiopians, like his Oceanus, stretch along the whole southern side of the inhabited world, so that “returning from the Ethiopians, he saw him from far away, from the mountains of the Solymi” is as much as to say “from the southern regions”; the Solymi of the verse are not those of Pisidia but namesakes that the poet invented, standing to the man on the raft and to the southerners there, “as if Ethiopians”, as the Pisidian Solymi stand to the Pontus and to the Ethiopians beyond Egypt (*Geography* 1.2.28; see [[strabo]]).

**The trident.** The scholia defended the wand that Hermes takes for his errand, which some thought carried over without need from the Iliad, by Poseidon's trident: no one would blame the god for taking his trident when he goes to Ethiopia, since such things are the gods' own possessions (Merry's critical note on 5.43–49; see the translation's note on 5.43–49 and [[hermes]]). In this book Poseidon takes the trident in his hands to raise the storm (5.291–292).
