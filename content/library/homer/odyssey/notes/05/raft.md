**The decree.** The raft is in Zeus' command before it is in Calypso's: Odysseus is to return “with the escort neither of gods nor of mortal men; / but on a raft of many bindings, suffering woes”, and to reach Scheria on the twentieth day (5.31–34; see [[zeus]] and [[scheria]]). The escort of gods is such as Telemachus had from Athena, and the rest of the verse is explained by the Phaeacians, who “will send him in a ship” (5.37), as W. W. Merry notes (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.32, citing 2.417; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]] and [[phaeacians]]). Between the two the raft carries him alone. The twenty days come out exactly if the day of the wreck is counted as the first of the “two nights and two days” in the sea: seventeen days of sailing, the land on the eighteenth, and the shore on the third day after (5.278–279, 5.388–390).

**Calypso's command and the oath.** Calypso, having heard the messages of Zeus, bids Odysseus “cut long beams with the bronze and fit together / a broad raft; and fix a deck upon it / high up” (5.162–164; see [[calypso]] and [[deck]]). ὑψοῦ, “high up”, is added because the deck is the highest part of the hull: the craft is to be broad in the beam and stand well out of the water (Merry on 5.164). Odysseus hears in the order another plan than his escort. Not even balanced ships cross that gulf of the sea, “But I would not set foot on a raft against your will, / unless you would bring yourself, goddess, to swear me a great oath” (5.173–179; see [[oath]] and [[balanced-ships]]); he is “cautious to a fault here”, Merry says, since she had already promised to send him away with a ready heart (on 5.177, citing 5.161).

**The parts.** The raft is built in four days (5.228–262; see [[the-building-of-the-raft]]), and many of its parts are named with words found in Homer only in this book: the pegs, the ribs and the side planks, the osier withes, the yard and the ropes. The γόμφοι are wooden pegs, trenails, as distinct from nails of metal (Merry on 5.248), and with the ἁρμονίαι, the “fastenings”, they hold the beams together (5.248; see [[harmonia]]). The σταμῖνες are the ribs, rising from the keel on either side; the scholia and Aristarchus explained them as upright timbers like the warp threads of a loom (Merry, Appendix I §4). The ἐπηγκενίδες are the long planks of the sides, laid lengthwise across the ribs, as an ancient lexicon puts it, like the weft across the warp (the Etymologicum Gudianum, as Merry reports it, Appendix I §6). The fence of osier withes, “to be a safeguard against the waves”, is a bulwark of wattle round the edge, as Eustathius explains it (5.256–257; Appendix I §7). Merry's reading of the decking and the planking, which the translation follows, is given under [[deck]]; the mast and its yard, the steering oar and the rigging under [[mast]], [[steering-oar]] and [[sails]].

**At sea.** On the fifth day the raft is loaded and sent off with a fair wind (5.263–268; see [[provisions]]), and Odysseus sails it alone, sitting at the steering oar, sleepless, with the Bear on his left hand, for seventeen days (5.269–278; see [[steering-oar]] and [[the-bear]]).

**The first wave.** Poseidon sees him and raises the storm (5.282–296; see [[poseidon]]). “As he spoke, a great wave struck him from above, / rushing on him terribly, and it whirled the raft around”: he falls far from it, the steering oar flies from his hands, the mast breaks in the middle, and the sail and the yard fall into the sea (5.313–318; see [[mast]] and [[sails]]). Held under by the garments Calypso gave him, he comes up spitting brine:

{{quote:b335d8c4-c22d-5cd9-b3b5-4cc3df28f99e}}

κατὰ ῥόον ἔνθα καὶ ἔνθα, “with the current this way and that”, is a contradiction if a current runs one way, but Odysseus is in a maze of cross currents and eddies where the winds meet (Merry on 5.327). The winds toss the raft from one to another like thistles on a plain (5.328–332; see [[thistles-in-the-north-wind]]).

**Leaving the raft.** Ino bids him strip, “and leave the raft for the winds to carry”, and swim with her veil under his chest (5.343–347; see [[leucothea]] and [[veil]]). He distrusts the counsel, “since she bids me leave the raft”, and resolves to stay on it “as long as the beams hold together in their fastenings”, and to swim only “when the wave shakes the raft apart for me” (5.356–364). Poseidon sends that wave. As a blustering wind scatters a heap of dry husks, “so it scattered the long beams of the raft” (5.368–370; see [[the-heap-of-husks]]), and Odysseus bestrides a single beam as though riding a horse (5.371; see [[like-a-rider]] and [[the-rider-of-four-horses]]). The raft ends as it began: Calypso's first order was to “cut long beams”, δούρατα μακρά (5.162), and the wave scatters δούρατα μακρά, “the long beams” (5.370).
