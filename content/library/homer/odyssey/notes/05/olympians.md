**In session.** “And the gods sat down to their session, and among them / Zeus who thunders on high” (5.3–4), the ordinary daily gathering of the gods in the hall of Zeus (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.3; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). Athena opens her plea with the address “Father Zeus, and you other blessed gods who live forever” (5.7; again 8.306, 12.371, 12.377; see [[blessed-gods]] and [[gods-who-live-forever]]).

**Among themselves.** The book shows the gods with one another away from Olympus. They are not strangers to each other: “for the gods are not unknown to one another, / the immortals, not even if one lives in a house far away” (5.79–80; see [[seeing-the-gods]]). Hermes answers “You, a goddess, ask me, a god” (5.97), and grumbles that on his long way there is no city of men to offer “sacrifices and choice hecatombs” to the gods (5.101–102; see [[hermes]]). No god, he says, can evade or thwart the mind of Zeus (5.103–104; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]).

**Hard and jealous.** Calypso answers the gods as a body: “You are hard, you gods, and jealous beyond all others”, who begrudge goddesses their mortal husbands, as “you gods who live at ease” begrudged Dawn her Orion (5.118–124; see [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]] and [[who-live-at-ease]]). To Odysseus she promises a homecoming “if the gods are willing, who hold the wide sky, / who are stronger than I am both to devise and to fulfill” (5.169–170; see [[wide-sky]]), a goddess deferring to the Olympians, and she swears to him by the water of Styx, “which is the greatest / and most dread oath for the blessed gods” (5.185–186; see [[styx]]).

**In the storm.** Poseidon finds that “the gods have changed their purpose / about Odysseus” while he was away (5.286–287; see [[poseidon]]). Odysseus had already accepted that “some god” might wreck him on the sea (5.221), and when Ino offers him her veil he fears “one of the immortals” weaving a trap (5.356–357; see [[immortals]]). Ino herself has “a share of honor from the gods” (5.335; see [[leucothea]]). In the simile of the father's recovery, “the gods free him from his misery” (5.397), and at the river Odysseus pleads that a wanderer is to be respected “Even to the immortal gods” (5.447; see [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]]).
