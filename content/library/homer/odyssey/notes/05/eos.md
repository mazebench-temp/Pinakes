---
summary: Dawn opens the book, rising from the bed of Tithonus in the verse of Iliad 11, and twice more brings a day, the first of the four days of the raft's building and the third day after the wreck, when Odysseus sees land. In Calypso's protest she is the goddess who took Orion for her lover and lost him to the gods' jealousy.
# Not counted: ἠῶθι πρό "in the early dawn" (5.469), the time of day, as in the earlier books' notes.
---

**The morning of the council.** “Now Dawn rose from her bed beside noble Tithonus, / to bring light to the immortals and to mortals” (5.1–2 = *Iliad* 11.1–2; see [[tithonus]]), and the gods sit down to their session (5.3; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). The verse is “a poetical way of expressing ‘the next morning’” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.1; see the translation's note on 5.1). Merry takes Tithonus here for a youthful hero, ἀγαυός, whom Dawn carried off for his beauty; the gift of immortality without youth is first told in the *Hymn to Aphrodite* (218–238; Merry on 5.1).

**Orion's lover.** Calypso begins her protest with Dawn: “So it was when rosy-fingered Dawn took Orion for herself: / you gods who live at ease begrudged her so long, / until in Ortygia chaste Artemis of the golden throne / came upon him with her gentle arrows and killed him” (5.121–124; see [[dawn-and-orion]], [[orion]] and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]). Merry sees the star in the story: when at the end of July Orion rises in all his splendor with the dawn in the eastern sky, and then pales before the morning light, he appears as the lover of Eos (on 5.121). Dawn's mortal loves end in three ways in the Odyssey: Tithonus is her husband, from whose bed she rises (5.1), Cleitus she snatched away for his beauty to be among the immortals (15.250–251), and Orion the gods would not let her keep.

**Two more dawns.** “And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered” (5.228), Odysseus and Calypso dress, and she gives him the axe and adze for the raft; this is the first of the four days of the building (5.228–262; see [[dawn-line]], [[early-born]], [[rosy-fingered]] and [[the-building-of-the-raft]]). Then the days pass uncounted by her: the fourth, the fifth on which he sails, seventeen at sea, and the eighteenth, when the storm comes (5.262–279). After two nights and two days in the sea, “But when Dawn of the lovely tresses brought the third day” (5.390; again 9.76, 10.144), the wind falls and he sees the land (5.391–393; see [[with-lovely-tresses]]). τέλεσε here is not “ended” but “brought into full and perfect action”: when daylight has fully come, dawn may be said to be over (Merry on 5.390).
