**The day.** ἤματι τῷ, “on that day” (5.309): one day of the war comes back to Odysseus as the storm breaks, the day when the Trojans massed against him around the body of Achilles (5.309–310; see [[trojans]] and [[achilles]]). Homer tells it in the last book, where the shade of Agamemnon reminds the shade of Achilles how the best sons of the Trojans and the Achaeans were killed around him, fighting over him the whole day, until Zeus stopped the battle with a storm (24.36–42, a plain rendering). Merry adds what Homer does not say: in the struggle for the corpse Ajax bore off the body on his shoulders, while Odysseus kept the Trojans at bay (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.309; see [[ajax]] and the translation's note on 5.309–311). The quarrel over the dead man's arms followed, which Odysseus won and Ajax never forgave (11.543–564; see [[the-judgment-of-arms]]).

**What the day would have given him.** “Then I would have had my funeral rites, and the Achaeans would have spread my fame” (5.311; see [[achaeans]], [[kleos]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). Achilles had both: the Achaeans washed and mourned him, the Muses sang his lament, his bones lie under a mound above the Hellespont, and he did not lose his name even in death, the shade of Agamemnon tells him (24.43–94, a plain rendering; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). For the cry that opens the wish, and for the same reckoning made by Telemachus for his father and by Achilles for himself in the river (5.312 = *Iliad* 21.281), see [[three-times-blessed]].
