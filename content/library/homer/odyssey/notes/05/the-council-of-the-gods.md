summary: The second council. At dawn the gods sit in their daily session, and Athena tells again of Odysseus, held by Calypso, and of the suitors' plot against his son. Zeus answers that the plan of vengeance was her own, bids her bring Telemachus home, and sends Hermes to tell Calypso that Odysseus is to go home on a raft, without escort of gods or men, and reach Scheria on the twentieth day.

**The session.** The book opens with the dawn verse of *Iliad* 11.1, “a poetical way of expressing ‘the next morning’” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.1; see [[eos]] and the translation's note on 5.1), “and the gods sat down to their session, and among them / Zeus who thunders on high, whose power is the greatest” (5.3–4; see [[thunders-on-high]] and [[zeus]]). θῶκος is the session, not the seat: this is the gods' ordinary daily gathering in the hall of Zeus, not an assembly he calls, as he calls one at *Iliad* 8.2 and 20.4 (Merry on 5.3; see the translation's note on 5.3). Six days have passed since Athena's first plea, Merry reckons from the notes of time in Books 2–4, and Hermes has still not been sent (Merry's introduction to Book 5). The poem does not say which morning this is; the structure file counts it as the day after the last day at Sparta (see the translation's note on 5.1). Poseidon is still away. He comes back from the Ethiopians only when Odysseus has been eighteen days at sea, and his first words then are that the gods have changed their purpose about Odysseus “while I was among the Ethiopians” (5.282–287; see [[poseidon]] and [[ethiopians]]). Both councils are held without him.

**Athena's plea.** “And Athena told them of the many sorrows of Odysseus, / remembering them; for he was her concern, there in the house of the nymph” (5.5–6; see [[athena]] and [[calypso]]). Her speech is made of earlier verses: Mentor's rebuke to the Ithacans that no one remembers their kind king (5.8–12 = 2.230–234), Proteus' words of Odysseus held by Calypso without ships or companions (5.13–17 = 4.557–560), and Medon's news of the ambush laid for Telemachus “as he comes home” (5.19–20 = 4.701–702; see the translation's note on 5.7–20 and [[the-suitors]]). Merry allows that this may be an intentional recapitulation, and rejects Hermann's canon that Homer repeats only formulas and messages (on 5.7). He also reports the view that the Odyssey joins two poems, a *Telemachy* and a *Return of Odysseus*, and that the second council was made to cover the seam: the first council (1.76–95) ought to lead straight to Hermes' errand, as if 1.87 ran on into 5.30 (Köchly, Schmitt and Hennings, as he reports them). He answers that the break at the end of Book 4, with Telemachus waylaid by the suitors, is like the end of one volume of a novel, and that each repeated verse brings the hearer back to the principal hero; but he grants that the main difficulty remains, since Athena says nothing of her own proposal to send Hermes and speaks as if nothing were being done for Odysseus (on 5.7).

**Zeus answers.** Zeus begins with the verse of the first council, “My child, what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!” (5.22 = 1.64; see [[what-a-word]]), and turns her complaint back on her:

The vengeance was not in her speech at the first council, which spoke only of the homecoming (1.81–95). The thought is Nestor's (3.216), and Teiresias will give it again (11.118; Merry on 5.23). Zeus repeats the two verses at the end of the poem, when Athena asks him whether, after the suitors' deaths, he will bring more war or set friendship between the two sides (24.473–480; see the translation's note on 5.23–24). παλιμπετές, “back again”, is the reversal of a course, and so “in vain” (Merry on 5.27; see the translation's note on 5.27). The book does not return to Telemachus, who is in Sparta until Book 15.

**The errand.** Then Zeus speaks to Hermes, “his dear son”, in the words Athena had proposed (5.28–31; 1.86–87; see [[hermes]] and [[messenger-scene]]), and adds what the first council did not decide: the homecoming will be “with the escort neither of gods nor of mortal men” (5.32), on a raft of many bindings, and on the twentieth day Odysseus will reach Scheria and the Phaeacians, “who will honor him with all their hearts like a god” and send him home with bronze, gold and clothing, “a great store, more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy” (5.33–40; see [[raft]], [[scheria]] and [[phaeacians]]). The escort of gods is the kind Telemachus had, with Athena sitting beside him in the stern (Merry on 5.32; 2.416–418); none sails with Odysseus. The decision ends with fate: “For so it is his fate to see his friends and to reach / his high-roofed house and his native land” (5.41–42; see [[moira]]). The twentieth day comes true: Odysseus sails for seventeen days, sees the land on the eighteenth, and comes ashore two days later (5.278–279, 5.388–390), and on the coast of Scheria he tells Nausicaa that he escaped the wine-dark sea yesterday, on the twentieth day (χθιζὸς ἐεικοστῷ … ἤματι, 6.170).
