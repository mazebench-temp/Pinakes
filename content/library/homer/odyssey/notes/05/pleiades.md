---
summary: On his raft Odysseus steers through the nights without sleeping, “as he watched the Pleiades and Boötes, who sets late, / and the Bear” (5.271–273), the stars that head the list as they head the stars of the Shield of Achilles.
---

“And he steered with skill with the steering oar, / sitting there, and sleep did not fall on his eyelids / as he watched the Pleiades and Boötes, who sets late” (5.270–272; see [[steering-oar]], [[bootes]] and [[the-bear]]). The Pleiades come first, as they come first among the constellations on the Shield (*Iliad* 18.486). W. W. Merry, who supposes Odysseus to be sailing east about the autumn equinox, pictures the Pleiades rising in the southeast near midnight as Boötes sets in the northwest, and Orion coming up an hour or two later (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.273; see [[orion]]).

Merry's note on the name gathers the stories (on 5.272). Göttling, on Hesiod's verse in which the Pleiades flee the strength of Orion (*Works and Days* 619–620), held that most of the Greeks saw in these stars the hunt of Orion, who with his dog pursues the Bear, the Pleiades as doves, the Hyades and the Hare; with that fancy Merry compares the doves that bring ambrosia to Zeus past the Wandering Rocks (12.62–65, a plain rendering), and a fragment of Aeschylus, quoted by Athenaeus, in which the Pleiades are “wingless doves”. Other stories made them nymphs of Artemis pursued by Orion and changed into doves, or the seven daughters of Atlas and Pleione, whom Hesiod calls “born of Atlas” (*Works and Days* 383; see [[atlas]]). When they rise in the middle of May harvest is near, and when they set toward the end of October the storms of winter are near; Merry finds that Hesiod seems to prefer the derivation from πλεῖν, “sail”, as though they marked the seasons for putting to sea (*Works and Days* 618–623). The setting of the Pleiades ends the season of ὀπώρα, the late summer of the North Wind that tosses the raft later in this book (Merry on 5.328; see [[thistles-in-the-north-wind]]).
