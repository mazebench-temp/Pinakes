# The phrase "εὐνὴν ἐπαμήσατο" (5.482), the bed of leaves; εὐνήν alone is also a lion's lair (4.338).
grc: ["εὐνὴν ἐπαμήσατο"]
---

**Beds of goddesses.** The book opens with the verse of *Iliad* 11: “Now Dawn rose from her bed beside noble Tithonus” (5.1; see [[eos]] and [[tithonus]]). In Calypso's protest Demeter, yielding to her heart, “lay with him in love and bed / in a thrice-plowed fallow field” (5.126–127; see [[demeter-and-iasion]] and [[philotes]]). Calypso's own bed is in the hollow caves, where Odysseus sleeps beside her at night “under compulsion … unwilling beside the willing” (5.154–155; see [[calypsos-cave]]).

**The bed of leaves.** On the shore of Scheria Odysseus crawls under two bushes, a wild olive and an olive grown from one spot, “And at once with his own hands he heaped up a bed, / a broad one; for there was a litter of leaves in great abundance, / enough to protect two men or three / in the winter season” (5.482–485; see [[olive]]). ἐπαμήσατο is “gathered together”, of the piling of a broad cushion of leaves to lie on, as the Cyclops gathers the curd of his milk (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.482, citing 9.247). He lies down in the middle of it and heaps the leaves over himself, as a man hides a brand in the ashes (5.486–491; see [[the-brand-in-the-ashes]]).
