---
summary: About to drown, Odysseus wishes he had died at Troy: “Then I would have had my funeral rites, and the Achaeans would have spread my fame” (5.311).
---

In the storm Odysseus sets the death he expects against the death he would have had on the day the Trojans hurled their spears at him around the body of Achilles: τῷ κʼ ἔλαχον κτερέων, καί μευ κλέος ἦγον Ἀχαιοί (5.308–311; see [[three-times-blessed]] and [[the-fight-over-the-body-of-achilles]]). κλέος ἦγον carries on the force of κε, “would have spread my fame”, like κλέος φέρειν or φορέειν, the Achaeans who “will carry his fame wide” for Orestes (3.204) and the strangers who carry a good man's fame (19.333); others take it as “would have brought back tidings of me” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.311). The word's two senses, fame and report, meet in the verse, and a man lost at sea has neither. Telemachus said as much of his father in Book 1, if he had died at Troy the Achaeans would have made him a tomb, “But now the Harpies have snatched him away without report” (ἀκλειῶς, 1.236–241; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). The κτέρεα that go with the fame are the honors paid to the dead, as in Athena's charge to Telemachus, if he hears that his father is dead, to heap a mound for him and pay him the funeral honors (1.289–292; see [[honoring-the-dead]]).
