**The weaving goddess.** “And she within, singing with a lovely voice, / walked to and fro at the loom and wove with a golden shuttle” (5.61–62; see [[loom]] and [[calypso]]). ἱστὸν ἐποιχομένη, “walked to and fro at the loom”, is the phrase of Chryseis, whom Agamemnon foresees at his loom in Argos (*Iliad* 1.31), and of Circe, whom Odysseus' companions hear singing at her loom (10.254); the weaver walks back and forth along the upright loom to carry the weft across the warp (see the entry). ἀοιδιάουσα, “singing”, is a word that, as Bothe put it in the Latin that W. W. Merry quotes, gives voice to the nymph's own sweet song (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.61). The κερκίς is the shuttle (Merry on 5.62; see [[loom]]).

**Weaving a trap.** When Ino has given him her veil and bidden him leave the raft, Odysseus is afraid: “Ah me, may it not be that someone is weaving a trap for me again, / one of the immortals, since she bids me leave the raft” (5.356–357; see [[leucothea]] and [[dolos]]). ὑφαίνειν δόλον is the metaphor of the Lycian king, who “wove another cunning trap” for Bellerophon (*Iliad* 6.187), and the suitors in Book 4 “were weaving their plan” (4.678); the man who has just left a goddess at her loom fears a goddess' woven trap.
