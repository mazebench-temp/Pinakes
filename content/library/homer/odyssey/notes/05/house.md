---
summary: Calypso's cave is a goddess' house, with its halls and its inner part, where she offers Odysseus to stay and be immortal (5.6, 5.14, 5.208, 5.226, 5.242); he longs instead for his high-roofed house in Ithaca (5.41–42, 5.114–115, 5.204), and Poseidon drives home to his glorious house at Aegae (5.381).
---

**The nymph's house.** Odysseus is “there in the house of the nymph” and “in the halls of the nymph Calypso” (5.6, 5.14 = 4.557), and the cave of the description is that house: Calypso goes back “to her house” when she has shown him the trees, and offers him, if he knew the sorrows ahead, to “stay here on the spot and keep this house with me, / and be immortal” (5.208–209, 5.242; see [[calypsos-cave]] and [[calypso]]). Its inner part, μυχός, is where they lie together on the last night (5.226), as the master of a house sleeps in its inner part (3.402, 4.304). The gods' houses are far apart, but the gods know one another “not even if one lives in a house far away” (5.80; see [[the-gods-are-not-unknown-to-one-another]]).

**The house he longs for.** Zeus decrees, and Hermes repeats to Calypso, that it is Odysseus' fate “to see his friends and to reach / his high-roofed house and his native land” (5.41–42, 5.114–115; see [[high-roofed]], [[moira]] and [[nostos]]), and Calypso's question on the last evening turns on the same word: “is it so, then, that home to your dear native land / you wish to go at once, now?” (5.204–205).

**Poseidon's house.** When he has wrecked the raft, Poseidon lashes his horses “and came to Aegae, where his glorious house is” (5.380–381), the verse of his journey in the Iliad (*Iliad* 13.21; see [[aegae]] and [[poseidon]]).
