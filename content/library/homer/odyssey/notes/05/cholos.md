# ὠδύσατʼ (5.340) and ὀδώδυσται (5.423) are ὀδύσσομαι "be angry", the verb of the play on the name of Odysseus (1.62).
grc: [χώεο, "ὠδύσατʼ", ὀδώδυσται]

**Poseidon's anger.** Returning from the Ethiopians, Poseidon sees the raft from the mountains of the Solymi, ὁ δʼ ἐχώσατο κηρόθι μᾶλλον, “And he grew all the more angry in his heart” (5.282–284; see [[poseidon]] and [[solymi]]). His smouldering anger blazed up afresh, Merry explains, when he saw Odysseus near the Phaeacian land, and so almost out of reach of the sovereign of the seas (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.284). It is the anger of which Zeus spoke at the first council, for the blinding of his son (1.68–79; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). The other two speakers of the storm name it with another verb of anger, ὀδύσσομαι. Ino asks, “why is Poseidon the earth-shaker / so terribly angry with you” (ὠδύσατʼ ἐκπάγλως, 5.339–340; ἐκπάγλως is ἐκπλάγως with its letters transposed, from ἐκπλήσσω, Merry on 5.340; see [[leucothea]]), and Odysseus fears a sea monster, “for I know how the famous shaker of the earth is angry with me” (ὀδώδυσται, 5.423). It is the verb of Athena's question at the first council, “Why are you so angry with him, Zeus?” (ὠδύσαο, 1.62), which plays on the name of Odysseus, “a man of wrath” (see the translation's note on 1.62 and [[odysseus]]); in the Iliad it is the anger of Zeus (*Iliad* 8.37, 18.292).

**Do not be angry.** Odysseus begins his answer to Calypso's last offer, “Lady goddess, do not be angry with me for this” (μή μοι τόδε χώεο, 5.215; see [[calypso]] and [[immortality]]), with the accusative of the thing, as Penelope asks him not to be angry with her when at last she owns him as her husband (23.213; Merry on 5.215).
