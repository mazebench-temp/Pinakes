---
summary: Strabo reads this book as a geographer. The Bear that alone has no share in Ocean is for him the arctic circle, and the mountains of the Solymi, from which Poseidon sees the raft, stand for the heights of the far south, named after the Solymi of Lycia. He sets Calypso's island and the Phaeacians in the Atlantic, reports Apollodorus' censure of Callimachus for calling Gaudos the island of Calypso and Corcyra Scheria, and records that Corcyra was once called Scheria and Delos Ortygia.
---

- **The Bear.** The verse that the Bear “alone has no share in the baths of Oceanus” (5.275 = *Iliad* 18.489) shows that Homer knew the northern edge of the earth to border on Ocean: by the Bear and the Wagon he means the arctic circle, and by Ocean the horizon, for with so many stars turning in the part of the sky that is always visible he would not have called the Bear alone without a share in Ocean. Nor did he know one Bear instead of two through ignorance, since the second was probably not yet a constellation; and Crates was wrong to write οἶος, “he alone” (*Geography* 1.1.6): [[alone-without-a-share]] and [[the-bear]].
- **The Solymi.** Poseidon sees Odysseus “from far away, from the mountains of the Solymi” (5.282–283). Strabo thinks that Homer moved the Solymi out to the Ocean, as he moved the Colchians and the tales of Circe: they held the highest summits of Taurus about Lycia as far as Pisidia, the most conspicuous heights of the south to those who lived on the near side of Taurus, above all around the Pontus, and Homer, he says, wrote the verse “of the man sailing on the raft” (*Geography* 1.2.10). Elsewhere he takes the verse to mean “from the southern regions”: these Solymi are not the Pisidian ones, but others of the same name, imagined to stand to the man on the raft and to the southern Ethiopians as the Pisidian Solymi stand to the Pontus and to the Ethiopians beyond Egypt (*Geography* 1.2.28): [[solymi]], [[ethiopians]] and [[poseidon]].
- **Calypso's island and the Phaeacians.** Against Polybius, who measured Odysseus' wanderings in days and distances, Strabo sets the verses that place them in the Ocean: the ship that left the stream of Ocean, the island of Ogygia “where the navel of the sea is”, where the daughter of Atlas lives, and the Phaeacians who live apart in the surging sea, the farthest of men. All these, he says, are plainly imagined in the Atlantic (*Geography* 1.2.18, quoting 12.1–2, 1.50 and 6.204–205; his text of 1.50 names Ogygia where ours has “a sea-washed island”): [[ogygia]], [[calypso]], [[phaeacians]] and [[scheria]].
- **Gaudos and Corcyra.** Apollodorus, in the second book of his work on the Catalogue of Ships, blamed those who set Odysseus' wanderings around Sicily: he would forgive the others, but not Callimachus, who claimed to be a scholar and yet called Gaudos the island of Calypso and Corcyra Scheria (*Geography* 7.3.6). Corcyra, “formerly called Scheria”, was settled by Chersicrates of the line of the Heraclidae, whom Archias left there on his way to found Syracuse (*Geography* 6.2.4): [[scheria]] and [[ogygia]].
- **Ortygia.** Strabo ends his note on Rhenaea, four stades from Delos, with “it was formerly also called Ortygia” (*Geography* 10.5.5): Merry takes the sentence of Rhenaea, the two islands having once gone under one name, and makes the Ortygia where Artemis killed Orion Delos (5.123; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.123); the sentence follows a remark on Delos and could be read of it: [[ortygia]] and [[dawn-and-orion]].
