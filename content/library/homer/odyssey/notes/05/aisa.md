summary: The Phaeacians will give Odysseus more than he would have brought from Troy had he “won his share of the spoil” (5.40). It is not his αἶσα “to perish here, far from his friends” (5.113), Calypso warns him of the sorrows “it is your lot to fill up” (5.206–207), and Poseidon admits that it is his lot to escape in the land of the Phaeacians (5.288). Calypso's mind is ἐναίσιμος, “right” (5.190), and Athena repeats Mentor's verses on the king who knows “what is due” (5.9).
grc: [αἶσαν, ἐναίσιμος]
en: [share, lot, right]
---

**A share of the spoil.** αἶσα is first a share. The Phaeacians will give Odysseus bronze and gold and clothing in plenty, more than he would ever have carried off from Troy, “if he had come home free of harm and won his share of the spoil” (λαχὼν ἀπὸ ληίδος αἶσαν, 5.38–40; see [[division-of-spoils]] and [[phaeacians]]). Poseidon repeats the verses in his complaint to Zeus when the gifts have been given (13.136–138). Merry renders “having got his meed from the spoil”, and compares Achilles' promise to Menoetius to bring Patroclus home “when he had sacked Ilios and won his share of the spoil” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.40; *Iliad* 18.327).

**The lot of a life.** Then the share is the measure of a life. Hermes tells Calypso that “it is not his lot to perish here, far from his friends, / but it is still his fate to see his friends and to reach / his high-roofed house” (5.113–115; see [[moira]]). Calypso uses the word of his sorrows: if he knew “what a measure of / sorrows it is your lot to fill up before you reach your native land”, he would stay with her (5.206–207; see [[algos]] and [[immortality]]), and in the storm he remembers her words (5.300–302). Poseidon, seeing him near the land of the Phaeacians, owns the gods' change of purpose in the same word, “where it is his lot / to escape the great bounds of the misery that has come upon him” (5.288–289; see the translation's note on 5.289 and [[poseidon]]), and vows to give him his fill of misery first (5.290).

**What is due.** The adjectives keep the sense of a fair measure. Athena opens the second council with Mentor's verses from the assembly in Ithaca: let no scepter-bearing king ever again be kind and gentle, nor “know in his mind what is due”, since no one remembers Odysseus (αἴσιμα, 5.8–12 = 2.230–234; see [[basileus]]). Calypso, answering his suspicion with her oath, says, “for my mind too is right, and my own heart / in my breast is not of iron, but full of pity” (καὶ γὰρ ἐμοὶ νόος ἐστὶν ἐναίσιμος, 5.190–191; see [[noos]] and [[pity]]); ἐναίσιμος is here “right”, beside “of fate” and “rightly” in Book 2 (2.159, 2.122; see the translation's table of key words). Merry hears the stress on αὐτῇ, “my own”: she sets her own temper against that of the gods who have spitefully ordered his departure (on 5.190).
