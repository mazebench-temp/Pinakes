**Calypso's head covering.** At dawn the nymph puts on her mantle and her girdle, “and over her head a head covering” (5.230–232; see [[cloak]] and [[girdle]]). καλύπτρη, from καλύπτω, “cover”, is kept apart in the translation from κρήδεμνον, “veil” (see its row κρήδεμνον, καλύπτρη). W. W. Merry thinks them hardly different in meaning, the same piece of stuff being called κρήδεμνον when it was tied round the head, and καλύπτρη when it was let fall over the face as a veil (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.232). Merry's critical note records that Aristarchus' copies read ἐφύπερθε, the less careful ones ἐπέθηκε, the reading of Circe's dressing (10.545; see the translation's note on 5.232).

**Ino's veil.** In the storm Ino, now the goddess Leucothea, rises from the sea like a diving bird and gives Odysseus her counsel (5.333–350; see [[leucothea]]):

{{quote:3e531bb6-6d42-5d02-a35e-c77467d28384}}

τῆ, “here, take”, is an old imperative, used like the French *tiens* (Merry on 5.346). The veil is ἄμβροτον, “immortal”, a goddess' thing, and with it there is no fear of suffering or death (5.347). Odysseus distrusts the gift until the wave scatters the raft; then, astride a single beam, he strips off Calypso's garments, “And at once he stretched the veil under his chest, / and plunged headlong into the sea” (5.373–374), the tenses, Merry says, being graphic: he scrambles onto the beam, rests there while he strips, then ties the scarf round his chest and plunges (on 5.373).

**The veil returned.** For two nights and two days he is in the sea, and he reaches the mouth of the river. When his breath and spirit come back, “then he untied the veil of the goddess from himself. / And he let it go into the river that murmurs to the sea, / and a great wave carried it back down the stream, and at once Ino / took it in her own hands” (5.459–462; see [[the-river-of-scheria]]). Ino had bidden him throw it into the sea far from the land and turn away (5.349–350): ἀπονόσφι τραπέσθαι expresses the reverence of those who may not look on the working of a power above men, as Circe bids Odysseus turn away at the edge of the house of Hades (Merry on 5.350; 10.528). Odysseus, letting it go, draws away from the river: λιασθείς is “sidling away” (Merry on 5.462).

**Other veils.** Hera's last garment, when she dresses to beguile Zeus, is a κρήδεμνον, “a fine one, new-made, and it was white like the sun” (*Iliad* 14.184–185); Andromache casts hers from her head when she sees Hector dragged (*Iliad* 22.468–472). Elsewhere a woman holds her veil before her cheeks (1.334), or takes it off for play, as Nausicaa's companions do at the river (6.100). Ino's is the only veil in Homer that saves a man's life.
