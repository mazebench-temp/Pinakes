summary: Hermes sums up the returns in his message to Calypso. Odysseus is the most wretched of the men who fought around the city of Priam for nine years, sacked it in the tenth and went home, but sinned against Athena on their homecoming, so that she roused an evil wind and long waves against them (5.105–111). The last two verses, which there make Athena's storm the one that brought Odysseus to the island, belong to Calypso's account of a later wreck (5.133–134), and Merry brackets them.

**The most wretched.** ὀιζυρώτατον ἄλλων, “more wretched than all the others” (5.105), is narrowed by the next verse: τῶν ἀνδρῶν, “the men”, explains and corrects ἄλλων, for Odysseus is not more wretched than all mankind, but than all the Greek heroes who fought around the walls of Troy (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.105–106; see [[odysseus]] and [[troy]]). ἔβησαν / οἴκαδʼ, “went / home” (5.107–108), is qualified at once by “but on their homecoming”, as in the Cretan stranger's tale, where the Achaeans sacked the city of Priam in the tenth year and went home with the ships, and a god scattered them (14.240–242, a plain rendering; Merry on 5.107; see [[the-fall-of-troy]]).

**The sin against Athena.** “but on their homecoming they sinned against Athena, / who roused against them an evil wind and long waves” (5.108–109; see [[athena]]). ἀλίτοντο, “they sinned against”, takes the goddess as its object, as Menelaus, held in Egypt, guesses that he must “have sinned against the immortals” (4.378; Merry on 5.108). It is the story Phemius sang in Ithaca, “the grim homecoming that Pallas Athena laid on them from Troy” (1.327; see [[the-song-of-the-returns]]), and Nestor told at Pylos, where the gray-eyed goddess's deadly wrath set the two sons of Atreus at strife (3.130–136). Merry takes Hermes to be giving a general sketch of the disasters of the return, without special reference to Odysseus, whose champion and protector Athena always was; the storm she sent “for the guilt and madness of one man, Ajax son of Oileus”, in Virgil's words, which he quotes (*Aeneid* 1.41), had nothing to do with the wreck of Odysseus, which came later (on 5.108; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[the-death-of-ajax-son-of-oileus]]).

**Two verses out of place.** “There all his other noble companions perished, / but the wind and the wave bore him and brought him here” (5.110–111) are the verses in which Calypso tells how Zeus shattered Odysseus' ship with a thunderbolt (5.131–134; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). In Hermes' mouth they would make Athena's storm the one that wrecked him and brought him to the island, where by his own account the wreck came from Zeus (12.403 and following). The scholia call the lines superfluous and at odds with the story, since Odysseus was not brought to the island at the time when Athena raised the wind and the others perished, and say that the last two were carried over from the later passage. Buttmann, as Dindorf reports him, referred the note to all of 5.105–111; Merry would keep 5.105–107, without which the pronoun that opens 5.112 would be unclear, and holds that 5.110–111 at least must go; his text brackets them (Merry's critical note and on 5.108; see the translation's note on 5.105–111).
