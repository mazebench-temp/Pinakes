**The second proof.** ὣς δʼ ὁπότʼ, “So too when”, answers the ὣς μὲν ὅτʼ of the first example, Dawn and Orion (5.121, 5.125; see [[dawn-and-orion]]), and the two lead to Calypso's own case, “And so now again you begrudge me, you gods, that a mortal man should be with me” (5.129; see [[calypso]] and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]). This time the god who kills is Zeus himself, the god whose command Hermes has brought (5.99–115; see [[zeus]]). Demeter is ἐυπλόκαμος, “of the lovely tresses” (5.125), as Calypso is when Zeus sends Hermes to “the nymph of the lovely tresses” (5.30, 5.57–58; see [[with-lovely-tresses]] and [[demeter]]).

**The thunderbolt.** Zeus killed Iasion “striking him with a gleaming thunderbolt” (βαλὼν ἀργῆτι κεραυνῷ, 5.128), and three verses later Calypso tells how Zeus struck Odysseus' swift ship “with a gleaming thunderbolt” and shattered it, when she saved him astride the keel (5.130–132; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and [[of-the-gleaming-thunderbolt]]). οὐδὲ δὴν ἦεν ἄπυστος, “and not long without hearing of it / was Zeus” (5.127–128): ἄπυστος is active here, “not having heard”, as in the verse of the suitors' plot: “Nor was Penelope long without hearing” (4.675).

**The field.** νειῷ ἔνι τριπόλῳ, “in a thrice-plowed fallow field” (5.127; see [[thrice-plowed]]): Merry counts the three plowings, at the end of autumn, in spring and in summer (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.127), and the scholia quote, with disapproval, a reading τριπύλῳ (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 5.125–128). Hesiod has the same half-verse of the same love, and sets the field in Crete (*Theogony* 969–971; see [[iasion]]).
