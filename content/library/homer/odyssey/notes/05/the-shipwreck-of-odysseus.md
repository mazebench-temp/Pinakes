**Calypso's account.** She tells it between the two dead lovers of goddesses and her own claim on Odysseus: τὸν μὲν ἐγὼν ἐσάωσα, “Him I saved” (5.130), I and not the gods who begrudge her (see [[calypso]], [[dawn-and-orion]] and [[demeter-and-iasion]]). περὶ τρόπιος βεβαῶτα, “astride the keel”: Merry compares the beam that Odysseus will bestride “as though riding a horse” when the raft breaks up (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.130; 5.371). ἔλσας, “struck”, is from εἴλω, of a crushing blow, or of driving the ship down, and Zenodotus read ἐλάσας (Merry on 5.132 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 5.132). The thunderbolt is the one that killed Iasion three verses before, ἀργῆτι κεραυνῷ, “with a gleaming thunderbolt” (5.128, 5.131; see [[of-the-gleaming-thunderbolt]] and [[zeus]]). Odysseus tells Arete of the wreck in the same verses, of his own ship, with μοι for οἱ (7.249–251, after 5.131–133), and to the Phaeacians he tells the whole storm that followed the eating of the cattle of the Sun (12.403–450; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]).

**Hermes' version.** Hermes has given the cause differently. Of the men who fought at Troy he says that “on their homecoming they sinned against Athena, / who roused against them an evil wind and long waves. / There all his other noble companions perished, / but the wind and the wave bore him and brought him here” (5.108–111; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]). The last two verses are Calypso's (5.133–134), and in Hermes' mouth they would make Athena's storm the one that brought Odysseus to the island. Merry holds that Hermes is sketching the disasters of the Greeks' return in general; the storm of Athena had nothing to do with the wreck of Odysseus, which came later and from Zeus, as Calypso says in the same verses and Odysseus in his own account (12.403 and following); so 5.110–111 cannot stand where they are, and his text brackets them (on 5.108; see [[athena]] and the translation's note on 5.105–111).

**Again.** When Calypso warns him of the sorrows still to come, Odysseus answers: “And if again some god wrecks me on the wine-dark sea, / I will endure it, with a heart in my breast that bears up under sorrow; / for already I have suffered very much and toiled much / on the waves and in war” (5.221–224; see [[odysseus]]). On the eighteenth day of his voyage Poseidon wrecks him again, and he rides a single beam of the raft as he once rode the keel (5.313–375; see [[poseidon]]).
