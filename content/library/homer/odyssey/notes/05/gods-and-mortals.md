---
summary: Book 5 measures the distance between gods and men: the gods know one another at sight (5.79–80), and a god has no reason to visit an island where no men offer sacrifices (5.101–102); Calypso eats ambrosia while Odysseus eats food “such as mortal men eat” (5.196–199), and “it is in no way fitting / for mortal women to vie with immortals” (5.212–213). Ino was once a mortal and is now a goddess of the sea (5.333–335).
refs: [5.79-80, 5.97, 5.101-102, 5.122, 5.169-170, 5.196-199, 5.212-213, 5.218, 5.334-335]
---

The book is spent almost wholly among gods and one man. Calypso knows Hermes at sight, “for the gods are not unknown to one another” (5.79–80; see [[the-gods-are-not-unknown-to-one-another]]), and Hermes' first words set the two of them side by side, “You, a goddess, ask me, a god” (5.97). He has come against his will, since no city of mortals is near to offer sacrifices and hecatombs (5.99–102; see [[hecatomb]]). Calypso calls the gods “who live at ease” jealous of goddesses' mortal lovers (5.118–129; see [[who-live-at-ease]] and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]), and owns that the gods “who hold the wide sky” are stronger than she to devise and to fulfill (5.169–170). At the last meal the difference is on the table: the nymph sets before the man food “such as mortal men eat”, and her slave women set ambrosia and nectar beside her (5.196–199; see [[ambrosia]] and [[nectar]]). She offers to make him immortal, and he refuses, granting that she is “immortal and ageless” and his wife mortal (5.208–218; see [[immortality]] and [[immortal-and-ageless]]). In the storm the one crossing between the orders is Ino, “once a mortal with human voice”, who now has a share of honor from the gods in the open sea (5.333–335; see [[leucothea]]).
