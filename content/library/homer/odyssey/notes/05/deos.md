summary: In the storm Odysseus cries “Ah me, wretched that I am” and fears that Calypso's words were true (5.299–300); off the cliffs he fears that the storm will snatch him back out to sea (5.419), and on the shore that he will become the prey of wild beasts if he sleeps in the thicket (5.473). Ino tells him that with her veil “there is no fear that you will suffer anything or perish” (5.347).
grc: [δείδω]
except: [5.299]
---

Fear speaks in Odysseus' four speeches to his own heart. In the storm: “Ah me, wretched that I am, what will befall me in the end? / I fear that everything the goddess said was true” (5.299–300; see [[wretch]], [[three-times-blessed]] and [[calypso]]). δειλός is here “wretched”, the sense the translation keeps for it in this book (see its table of key words), and Merry notes the aorist indicative after δείδω μή: the anxiety is about a thing already done (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.300). Off the cliffs of Scheria: “I fear that the storm may snatch me up again / and carry me, groaning heavily, over the fish-filled sea” (5.419–420). On the shore at night: “I fear that I may become the prey and spoil of wild beasts” (5.473). Twice his deliberation ends in a choice (5.360–364, 5.474; see [[kerdos]]), and a goddess brings him through each danger he fears (5.333–353, 5.382–387, 5.427, 5.437, 5.491–493; see [[athena]]). Ino's word should have removed the first: with her veil “there is no fear that you will suffer anything or perish” (5.346–347; see [[leucothea]]), but he fears a trap and keeps to the raft until it breaks (5.356–364; see [[dolos]]). Earlier in the book the formula ὣς φάτο, ῥίγησεν δέ, “So he spoke, and … shuddered”, gives the same start to Calypso at Hermes' message and to Odysseus at Calypso's offer of a raft (5.116, 5.171; see the translation's table of formulas).
