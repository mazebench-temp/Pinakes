---
summary: Orion is named twice in Book 5, as the hunter whom Dawn took for herself and Artemis killed in Ortygia, in Calypso's complaint against the jealous gods (5.121–124), and as the constellation that the Bear watches, among the stars by which Odysseus steers (5.274).
# Ὠρίωνʼ (5.121), the elided accusative, found only here; Ὠρίωνα (5.274) is claimed by the entry.
grc: ["Ὠρίωνʼ"]
---

**The hunter.** Calypso's first example of the gods' jealousy is Orion: “So it was when rosy-fingered Dawn took Orion for herself: / you gods who live at ease begrudged her so long, / until in Ortygia chaste Artemis of the golden throne / came upon him with her gentle arrows and killed him” (5.121–124; see [[dawn-and-orion]], [[eos]], [[artemis]], [[ortygia]] and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]). W. W. Merry notes that Orion, the giant and hunter whom Odysseus will see among the dead (11.572–575), appears even in Homer as a constellation, and reads the story in the sky: it is when, at the end of July, Orion rises in all his splendor with the dawn in the eastern sky, and then pales before the morning light, that he appears as the lover of Eos (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.121).

**The constellation.** As Odysseus steers his raft by night, the Bear “turns in its place and watches Orion” (5.274 = *Iliad* 18.488; see [[the-bear]]). Merry supposes the voyage to fall about the autumn equinox, when Orion would rise in the south-east an hour or two after midnight, and the Bear, its head turned toward him, would seem to be watching the hunter as he comes up (on 5.273). The hunter of Calypso's tale and the star of the steersman are one Orion, and the book names him in both.
