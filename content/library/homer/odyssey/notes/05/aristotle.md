---
summary: The Poetics defends the verse that the Bear “alone” never bathes in Ocean as a metaphor, and fragments of the Homeric Problems ask why Calypso mixes nectar for Hermes, what αὐδήεσσα means of Ino, and why Odysseus refused the immortality that Calypso offered. The History of Animals tells the gull of Hermes' simile from the diving bird of Ino's.
---

- “And ‘she alone has no share’ is by metaphor, for the best known is alone” (*Poetics* 1461a20–21, on 5.275 = *Iliad* 18.489): [[alone-without-a-share]] and [[the-bear]].
- If the gods drink nothing but nectar, why does Calypso “mix” it for Hermes (5.93)? The verb, Aristotle answers, means not only mixing one liquid with another but simply pouring (*Homeric Problems* fr. 393 Gigon, from the scholia on 5.93): [[nectar]].
- Ino, “who was once a mortal with human voice” (5.334): Aristotle, the scholia report, read οὐδήεσσα, “earthly”, for αὐδήεσσα; and of Circe, who has the same epithet, he says that she speaks in human fashion, since the gods speak through signs, birds, victims, sacrifices and dreams (*Homeric Problems* fr. 394 Gigon, from the scholia on 5.334 and 10.136; see the translation's note on 5.334): [[leucothea]] and [[with-human-voice]].
- Why did Odysseus not accept immortality when Calypso offered it? Aristotle answers that Odysseus says so to the Phaeacians to appear more dignified and to make them more eager to send him on, and that he did not take the gift, not because he was not persuaded, but because he did not believe her (*Homeric Problems* fr. 401 Gigon, from the Vienna scholia on 23.337). In this book the offer is not only Odysseus' report: Calypso tells Hermes that she said she would make him immortal and ageless (5.135–136), and she makes the offer again on the last evening, where Odysseus answers only with his longing for home (5.206–224): [[immortality]], [[immortal-and-ageless]] and [[calypso]].
- The λάρος, the gull of Hermes' simile (5.51), and the αἴθυια, the diving bird of Ino's (5.337, 5.353), both lay two or three eggs in the rocks by the sea, the gull in summer and the αἴθυια at the beginning of spring, straight after the solstice (*History of Animals* 5.9; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.51): [[gull]], [[the-gull]] and [[like-a-diving-bird]].
- The σκώψ, one of the owls that roost in the wood around Calypso's cave (5.66), is among the night birds with crooked talons, and “smaller than the γλαύξ” (*History of Animals* 8.3; Merry on 5.66): [[birds]].
