---
summary: It is Odysseus' fate “to see his friends and to reach / his high-roofed house” (5.41–42, 5.114–115), and, Ino tells him, to escape to the land of the Phaeacians (5.345). Calypso and Ino call him κάμμορε, “Ill-fated man” (5.160, 5.339); in the storm he wishes he had died and “met my fate” at Troy, since “now it was fated that I be caught by a wretched death” (5.308, 5.312), and he would have perished “against fate” had not Athena given him presence of mind (5.436–437). Ino “has a share of honor from the gods” (5.335).
grc: [κάμμορε, εἵμαρτο]
---

**His fate.** Zeus' decree for Odysseus ends with his μοῖρα: “For so it is his fate to see his friends and to reach / his high-roofed house and his native land” (5.41–42). ὥς, “in such wise”, takes in both the perils he must meet on the way and the honorable escort of the Phaeacians (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.41). Hermes repeats the verses to Calypso with ἔτι, “still”, after the αἶσα that forbids him to perish on her island (5.113–115; see [[aisa]]), and Ino names the next stage, the land of the Phaeacians, “where it is your fate to escape” (5.345; see [[nostos]] and [[phaeacians]]).

**Ill-fated.** Calypso's first word to him is κάμμορε, “Ill-fated man, do not mourn here any longer” (5.160), and Ino's is the same, “Ill-fated man, why is Poseidon the earth-shaker / so terribly angry with you” (5.339–340; see [[calypso]] and [[leucothea]]). In Homer the word is used only of Odysseus: Eurycleia's “that ill-fated man” (2.351), and later his mother's greeting in the underworld and Athena's on the night before the killing of the suitors (11.216, 20.33).

**A death in the storm.** He wishes he had died at Troy, θανέειν καὶ πότμον ἐπισπεῖν, “and met my fate” (5.308), the phrase of Peisistratus' grief for any mortal “who dies and meets his fate” (4.196), and ends: “but now it was fated that I be caught by a wretched death” (5.312 = *Iliad* 21.281, Achilles in the river; see [[three-times-blessed]]). εἵμαρτο, of the same root as μοῖρα, is “had been fated”, even before he began this voyage (Merry on 5.312).

**Against fate.** When the wave flings him against the rocks, “Then unhappy Odysseus would have perished against fate, / had not gray-eyed Athena given him presence of mind” (5.436–437; see [[athena]]). ὑπὲρ μόρον is the phrase of Zeus in Book 1, of men who have sorrows “against fate” through their own recklessness (1.34), and Merry refers to his note there (on 5.436; see the translation's note on 1.34 and [[who-is-to-blame]]). Here a goddess keeps what is fated from being overrun, as in the Iliad Apollo goes into Troy lest the Danaans sack it “against fate” that day (*Iliad* 21.515–517).

**A share.** μοῖρα is first a portion, and the verb of it is ἔμμορε, “has as a share”. Ino, once a mortal, “now in the open sea has a share of honor from the gods” (θεῶν ἒξ ἔμμορε τιμῆς, 5.335), “enjoyeth her meed of honour from the gods” in Merry's rendering (on 5.335; see [[time]] and [[immortality]]), in the words in which Nestor speaks of the honor that falls to a scepter-bearing king (*Iliad* 1.278). The Bear, says the verse that Aristotle quotes, is ἄμμορος, “alone has no share” in the baths of Oceanus (5.275; see [[alone-without-a-share]]).
