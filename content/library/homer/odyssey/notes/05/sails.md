**Cloth and rigging.** While Odysseus finishes the hull, “Calypso, brilliant among goddesses, brought cloth / to make sails; and he fashioned these well too. / And he made fast in it the braces and halyards and sheets” (5.258–260; see [[the-building-of-the-raft]] and [[calypso]]). φάρεα, “cloth”, is the plural of φᾶρος, the great mantle that Calypso puts on that morning (5.230; see [[cloak]]). W. W. Merry explains the three ropes in his appendix on the Homeric ship. The κάλοι are the halyards, which haul the yard up and let it down, running through a hole high in the mast or a ring; the ὑπέραι are the braces, made fast to the ends of the yard, which trim it to the wind; and the πόδες, Latin *pedes*, are the sheets, ropes at the lower corners of the sail for altering its position, as the braces fix the yard's, and Eustathius explains that they are called “feet” because they are below, opposite the braces above (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix I §16; see [[mast]]). The words ὑπέραι and κάλοι are found only in this verse.

**The sails spread.** “Glad of the wind, brilliant Odysseus spread his sails” (5.269), the warm and harmless wind that Calypso has sent (5.268). The plural ἱστία is used of a single sail, perhaps for the sail with its rigging, as ἅρματα is used for a chariot, or for the strips of canvas of which it was made (Merry, Appendix I §§5, 14).

**The sail lost.** At the first great wave, “far off the sail and the yard fell into the sea” (5.318). σπεῖρον is the word here, the word of the winding-sheet that Penelope weaves for Laertes (2.102; see [[shroud]]) and of the sails stored in the Phaeacians' harbor (6.269; Merry, Appendix I §14).
