summary: Calypso answers Zeus' command with a protest. The gods are hard and jealous beyond all others, who begrudge goddesses their mortal husbands: so they begrudged Dawn her Orion until Artemis killed him, and Demeter her Iasion until Zeus struck him, and so now they begrudge her the man she saved from the sea.

**The protest.** Hermes has told Calypso that Zeus bids her send Odysseus away, “So he spoke, and Calypso, brilliant among goddesses, shuddered” (5.116), and she answers with an accusation against the gods as a body:

{{quote:19761fa6-0a13-5731-bcaa-74165ddb7d13}}

σχέτλιοι, “hard”, is the word of Apollo's protest in the Iliad, “You are hard, you gods, and destructive” (*Iliad* 24.33), and some read δηλήμονες, “destructive”, here too; Merry keeps ζηλήμονες, “jealous”, which the next verse explains: in the Iliad the gods are destructive in permitting the outrage on Hector's body, here they are jealous of mortals favored by goddesses (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.118; see the translation's note on 5.118). He puts the comma after ἀμφαδίην, so that “if one makes a man her dear husband” explains “openly”, and hears irony in it: the gods wink at the amours of goddesses, but will not let them raise a mortal to the honorable place of husband (on 5.120; see the translation's note on 5.120).

**The examples.** Her proofs are two stories of goddesses whose mortal lovers died for it. Dawn took Orion, and the gods “who live at ease begrudged her so long, / until in Ortygia chaste Artemis of the golden throne / came upon him with her gentle arrows and killed him” (5.122–124; see [[dawn-and-orion]], [[eos]], [[artemis]] and [[who-live-at-ease]]); Artemis, Merry notes, kills Orion not as the goddess of sudden death but from jealousy (on 5.124). Demeter lay with Iasion, and Zeus “killed him, striking him with a gleaming thunderbolt” (5.125–128; see [[demeter-and-iasion]], [[iasion]] and [[zeus]]). Then the present case: “And so now again you begrudge me, you gods, that a mortal man should be with me” (5.129), the man she saved astride his keel, cared for, and meant to make “immortal and ageless all his days” (5.130–136; see [[immortal-and-ageless]] and [[calypso]]).

**Jealousy and the mind of Zeus.** The protest does not change the decision. In the same speech Calypso yields, in the verses Hermes used: “But since there is no way that the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus / can be evaded by any other god, or made vain, / let him go to his ruin” (5.137–139; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). When she tells Odysseus of her oath, her own heart, she says, is “not of iron, but full of pity” (5.190–191): Merry hears in αὐτῇ a contrast between her temper and that of the gods who had spitefully ordered his departure (on 5.190). The gods' grudge is her explanation of Zeus' command; the poem has given another, the council at which Athena pleaded for Odysseus and Zeus decided that it was his fate to go home (5.1–42; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]).
