---
summary: Odysseus lays Poseidon's storm on Zeus, as men commonly do (5.303–304), until Ino asks why Poseidon is so angry with him (5.339–340), and then he says it himself (5.423); it is Zeus he thanks for the sight of land (5.408–409). Calypso blames the jealousy of the gods for the loss of her husband (5.118–129), and Hermes lays the disasters of the returns on the Achaeans' sin against Athena (5.108–109).
refs: [5.108-109, 5.118-129, 5.221, 5.303-304, 5.339-340, 5.408-409, 5.423]
---

**Whose storm.** The narrator tells the storm as Poseidon's: he gathers the clouds and throws the sea into confusion, taking his trident in his hands (5.291–296; see [[poseidon]]). Odysseus on the raft sees only the clouds, and lays them on Zeus: “With what clouds he is wreathing the wide sky, / Zeus, and he has thrown the sea into confusion” (5.303–304). The scholia say that he blames Zeus for the storm according to the common belief, not knowing that it is Poseidon's work (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.304). Ino's question tells him the truth, “why is Poseidon the earth-shaker / so terribly angry with you” (5.339–340; see [[leucothea]] and [[cholos]]), and by the time he speaks again, off the cliffs of Scheria, he knows it: “for I know how the famous shaker of the earth is angry with me” (5.423). Yet it is Zeus he credits with the land he sees, “now that he has granted me to see land beyond all hope, / Zeus” (5.408–409; see [[zeus]]).

**The gods' grudge and the men's sin.** Calypso lays her loss on the gods, “hard” and “jealous beyond all others”, who begrudge goddesses their mortal husbands (5.118–129; see [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]). Hermes, telling her why the man is with her, gives the returns a human cause: the Achaeans went home, “but on their homecoming they sinned against Athena, / who roused against them an evil wind and long waves” (5.108–109; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]). Merry holds that Athena's storm is not the one that wrecked Odysseus, which was the work of Zeus, as Calypso says in the same verses and Odysseus will tell (5.131–134; 12.403–425; Merry on 5.108; see the translation's note on 5.105–111 and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). Odysseus, refusing immortality, makes no claim on the gods at all: “And if again some god wrecks me on the wine-dark sea, / I will endure it” (5.221–222; see [[immortality]]).
