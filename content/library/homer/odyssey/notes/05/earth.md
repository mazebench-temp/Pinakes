---
summary: In Book 5 the earth is above all the native land that Odysseus cannot reach (5.15, 5.26, 5.41–42, 5.204, 5.207, 5.220, 5.301), and then the land that he sees from the sea, swims for and at last kisses (5.392–399, 5.408, 5.439, 5.463); Calypso swears by it, and Poseidon's clouds cover “land and sea alike” (5.184, 5.294).
---

**The native land.** The book's first sorrow is that Odysseus “cannot reach his native land” (5.15 = 4.558; see [[dear-native-land]]), and every promise in it is a promise of that land: Zeus bids Athena escort Telemachus so that he may reach his own “quite unharmed” (5.25–26), Zeus decrees that Odysseus shall reach “his high-roofed house and his native land” (5.41–42, 5.114–115; see [[high-roofed]]), and Calypso warns him of the sorrows he must fill up before he reaches it (5.206–207). He answers her that he yearns all his days “to come home and see the day of my homecoming” (5.219–220; see [[day-of-homecoming]] and [[nostos]]). In the storm he remembers her words: “that on the sea, before I reached my native land, / I would fill up the measure of sorrows” (5.301–302).

**Earth as witness.** Calypso swears “Let Earth now be witness to this, and the wide Heaven above, / and the down-flowing water of Styx” (5.184–185 = *Iliad* 15.36–37; see [[sky]], [[styx]] and [[oath]]), as Earth is called on in the oaths of the Iliad (*Iliad* 3.104, 3.278).

**The land seen from the sea.** Poseidon's clouds cover “land and sea alike” (5.294; see [[sea]]). After the storm the land is what Odysseus sees, “near at hand … as he was lifted by a great wave”, as welcome as a father's life to his children, “so welcome did the land and the woods appear to Odysseus” (5.392–398; see [[the-fathers-recovery]]); “now that he has granted me to see land beyond all hope”, he says to himself at the cliffs (5.408), and he swims along the coast “looking toward the land” (5.439; see [[shore]] and [[scheria]]). ἤπειρος, “the land”, set against the sea, is the word of his goal: “eager to set his feet on the land” (5.399), and of Ino's veil, to be thrown back into the sea “far from the land” (5.350; see [[veil]]).

**The kiss.** When the river has brought him in, he withdraws from it, sinks down among the rushes, “and kissed the grain-giving earth” (5.463; see [[the-river-of-scheria]]). ζείδωρος, “grain-giving”, is the epithet of the earth as the giver of men's food (see [[grain-giving]]).
