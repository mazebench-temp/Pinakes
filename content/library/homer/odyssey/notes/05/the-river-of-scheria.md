summary: Swept along the cliffs of Scheria, Odysseus comes to the mouth of a river, knows its god as it flows out, and prays to him as a suppliant; the god stops his stream, holds back the wave and brings him safe into his mouth. Odysseus lets Ino's veil go into the river, which carries it back to her, and, fearing the river's cold breeze at dawn, climbs to the wood above it to sleep.

**The mouth of the river.** Flung back from the rock, Odysseus swims along outside the surf looking for “beaches where the waves strike slanting, and harbors of the sea” (5.440), and finds a river instead: “But when he came to the mouth of a fair-flowing river, / swimming, where the place seemed to him the best, / smooth, free of rocks, and there was shelter from the wind” (5.441–443; see [[fair-flowing]] and [[scheria]]). λεῖος πετράων is “smooth of all rocks”, that is, free of them (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.443). The beaches he was looking for are, in Merry's view, the low jutting horns of shore found especially at the mouths of rivers, where the waves break aslant instead of full on (on 5.418). The last verse returns when he tells Arete how he came there (7.282).

**Knowing the god.** “He knew the god as he flowed forth, and prayed to him in his heart” (5.444): ἔγνω δὲ προρέοντα, “he recognised him (sc. for a god) as he flowed forth” (Merry on 5.444). He knows a god in the current itself, as Calypso knew Hermes at sight (5.77–80; see [[seeing-the-gods]]), but he does not know which god, and his prayer begins “Hear me, lord, whoever you are” (see [[hear-me]]):

{{quote:be3a36a8-d0cd-5dc5-a9c8-cbef0efeb7c2}}

πολύλλιστον, “much prayed for”, is “greatly longed for”, as the night that comes to the beaten Achaeans is τρίλλιστος, “thrice prayed for” (*Iliad* 8.488; see [[thrice-prayed-for]]); a scholium takes it as owning the river's divinity, Odysseus praying to the river as though each had its own δαίμων (Merry on 5.445 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 5.445). The prayer is a suppliant's, from the maxim to the claim: “I claim to be your suppliant” (see [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]] and [[supplication]]).

**The answer.** “So he spoke, and at once the god stopped his stream, and held back the wave, / and made a calm before him, and brought him safe / into the river's mouth” (5.451–453). πρόσθε is in front of the swimmer, as Athena broke the waves “ahead” of him (5.385; Merry on 5.452; see [[athena]]). ἐσάωσεν, “brought him safe”, is the verb of Calypso's rescue of Odysseus astride the keel, “Him I saved” (ἐσάωσα, 5.130). The god of the river is the last of the powers that bring him through the sea, after Ino's veil and Athena's North Wind (5.346–353, 5.382–387; see [[leucothea]] and [[divine-intervention]]).

**The veil and the night.** When his breath comes back he unties Ino's veil “And he let it go into the river that murmurs to the sea, / and a great wave carried it back down the stream” to her hands (5.460–462; see [[murmuring-to-the-sea]]). ἁλιμυρήεις was commonly taken as “running with noise into the sea”; others narrow it to a river at its mouth, met and forced back by the sea water (Merry on 5.460). Then he withdraws from the river and kisses the earth (5.462–463). The river saved him from the sea, but he fears to keep watch by it through the night, for the cold and the dew, “and the breeze from a river blows cold in the early dawn” (5.466–469); so he climbs to the wood, which he finds “near the water” (5.475), and sleeps under the olives (see [[olive]]).
