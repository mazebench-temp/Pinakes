summary: The book has two shores. On Calypso's island Odysseus sits weeping on the shore, on the rocks and the beaches, looking out over the sea (5.81–84, 5.151–158); on Scheria the coast is all reefs, headlands and sheer rock, with no beach where the waves strike slanting, and a great wave throws him on “the rugged shore” (5.400–440).

**The shore of grief.** When Hermes comes to the cave, Odysseus is not within: “he sat weeping on the shore, as he had before, / racking his heart with tears and groans and sorrows” (5.82–83; see [[calypsos-cave]] and [[tears]]). Calypso, when the god has gone, finds him there: “She found him sitting on the shore; and his eyes were never / dry of tears”, and by day “he would sit on the rocks and the beaches” and look out over the barren sea (5.151–158; see [[calypso]] and [[sea]]). ἂμ πέτρῃσι is Aristarchus' reading for ἐν πέτρῃσι (Merry's critical note on 5.156; see the translation's note on 5.156). The shore is where men go apart in grief in the Iliad too: Chryses walks along it and prays to Apollo, and Achilles sits on it and calls on his mother (*Iliad* 1.34–36, 1.348–351). On the island the translation keeps “shore” for ἀκτή and “beaches” for ἠιών (see its table of key words).

**The coast of Scheria.** On the far side of the sea there is no such shore. Odysseus hears the roar of the sea on the reefs, and finds “jutting headlands and reefs and crags”, with no harbors and no roadsteads (5.401–405; see [[scheria]] and [[harbor]]). What he hopes for, if he swims on, is ἠιόνας τε παραπλῆγας λιμένας τε θαλάσσης, “beaches where the waves strike slanting, and harbors of the sea” (5.418 = 5.440). W. W. Merry finds the derivation of ἠιών unclear, perhaps from εἶμι, “go”, of a beach on which one can walk or a line of coast that seems to go forward; comparing the uses of the word, he concludes that ἠιόνες are jutting horns of shore, especially at the mouths of rivers, mostly low, as the shores at the river's mouth that resound in a simile of the Iliad (*Iliad* 17.263–265; see [[the-wave-at-the-rivers-mouth]]) and the long mouth of the whole shore that the Achaean ships fill between the headlands (*Iliad* 14.35–36). παραπλῆγες, then, are the opposite of the ἀντιπλῆγες ἀκταί of Sophocles (*Antigone* 592, as Merry quotes it), headlands that meet the blow of the waves full on: beaches where the seas strike aslant, as on the curving sides of a bay, so that the waves break slantwise and run along instead of being hurled back (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.418).

**The rugged shore.** While he ponders, “a great wave carried him onto the rugged shore” (5.425; see [[rugged]]), τρηχεῖαν ἐπʼ ἀκτήν, and there his skin would have been stripped off and his bones crushed had not Athena put it in his mind to seize the rock (5.426–429; see [[the-octopus]]). When he comes out at last it is at the mouth of a river, “smooth, free of rocks” (5.441–443; see [[rivers]] and [[the-river-of-scheria]]).
