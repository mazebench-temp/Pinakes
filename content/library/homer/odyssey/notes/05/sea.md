---
summary: Book 5 is the book of the sea. Odysseus weeps on Calypso's shore looking out over the barren sea; Hermes crosses it like a gull, and the raft is dragged down into it; then Poseidon throws it into confusion, the great waves break the raft, Ino rises from it and goes back into it, and for two nights and two days Odysseus swims, until the backwash of the coast flings him out again and the sea gushes from his mouth at the river's mouth (5.50–56, 5.81–84, 5.100–101, 5.174–176, 5.261, 5.291–332, 5.333–353, 5.374–457).
# πόντοιο (5.446), λαῖτμα "gulf" (5.409; with θαλάσσης at 5.174, 4.504) and ἁλμυρὸν ὕδωρ "salt water" (5.100), first met
# in the Odyssey here or claimed only now; in the later books they are the sea too (7.35, 9.227, 9.323, 9.486, 15.294).

**The sea that holds him.** On Calypso's island the sea is what Odysseus looks at. He sits on the shore, “racking his heart with tears and groans and sorrows. / He would look out over the barren sea, letting tears fall” (5.83–84 = 5.157–158; see [[barren-sea]], [[shore]] and [[tears]]). The scholia call 5.84 superfluous here, saying that the verse was carried up from 5.158, and Merry's text brackets it (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 5.83–84). He has no ships with oars and no companions “who might take him over the broad back of the sea”, in the words of Athena and of Calypso (5.16–17 = 5.141–142 = 4.559–560; see [[broad-back-of-the-sea]]). The way out is the sea that Hermes complains of: “who of his own will would speed across so much salt water, / boundless?” (5.100–101; see [[untold]] and [[hermes]]), and that Odysseus fears: “when you bid me cross on a raft the great gulf of the sea, / dread and grievous; over it not even balanced ships / cross, swift-sailing, glorying in the wind of Zeus” (5.174–176; see [[raft]] and [[balanced-ships]]). λαῖτμα, “gulf”, is the depth of the open sea, which Odysseus will say, when he has seen the land again, that he has cut through and crossed (5.409; see the translation's table of key words). ἀγαλλόμεναι, “glorying”, makes the ships live things, W. W. Merry notes, that walk the water (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.176). It was in the middle of the wine-dark sea that Zeus shattered his last ship, as Calypso tells Hermes, and on the wine-dark sea, Odysseus answers her, some god may wreck him again (5.131–132, 5.221; see [[wine-dark]] and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]).

**The gods' sea.** Hermes falls from the upper air “upon the sea” and hurries over the wave like a gull “through the dread bosom of the barren sea”, and steps out of “the violet sea” onto the island (5.50–56; see [[upper-air]], [[the-gull]] and [[violet-sea]]). For the gods the sea is a road; for Odysseus it is “the misty sea” over which a raft must carry him (5.164; see [[misty]]). He drags the finished raft “into the shining sea” with levers (5.261; see [[shining-sea]]), and sails for seventeen days, “crossing the sea”, until the land of the Phaeacians looks “like a shield on the misty sea” (5.278–281; see [[scheria]]).

**The sea in the storm.** Poseidon, seeing him, “gathered the clouds and threw the sea into confusion, / taking his trident in his hands”, and with clouds “covered / land and sea alike; and night arose from the sky” (5.291–294; see [[poseidon]] and the translation's table of key words on ταράσσω). Merry finds that the language and the rhythm of the verse suggest the sudden coming of the dark (on 5.294), and quotes Coleridge's line, “At one stride comes the dark”. Odysseus, not knowing whose storm it is, blames Zeus, “and he has thrown the sea into confusion” (5.304; Merry on 5.304; see [[zeus]]). The wave strikes him from above, holds him under, and he comes up and spits out “the bitter brine, that ran streaming in plenty from his head” (5.313–323; see [[bitter]]). The winds carry the raft “over the open sea this way and that” (5.330; see [[thistles-in-the-north-wind]]).

**The sea's goddess.** Ino, once a mortal, “now in the open sea has a share of honor from the gods” (5.335; see [[leucothea]]). ἁλὸς ἐν πελάγεσσι is “in the open sea”, the plural of πέλαγος (see the translation's table of key words). She rises from the sea like a diving bird and plunges back “into the billowing sea”, and a dark wave hides her (5.337, 5.352–353; see [[like-a-diving-bird]] and [[billowing]]); her veil is to be thrown back “into the wine-dark sea / far from the land” (5.349–350; see [[veil]]). Amphitrite breeds its monsters in numbers (5.421–422; see [[sea-beasts]] and [[amphitrite]]), and Poseidon, his work done, drives over it to Aegae (5.380–381; see [[aegae]]).

**The swimmer.** Odysseus plunges headlong into the sea, ἁλὶ κάππεσε (5.374), and for two nights and two days drifts “on the swollen wave” (5.388–389). At the coast the sea is all noise and foam: “he heard the roar of the sea on the reefs”, and “everything was wrapped in the foam of the sea” (5.401–403; see [[shore]]); ἄχνη is the sea spray, already suggested, Merry notes, by ἐρευγόμενον, “belching” (on 5.403). “Nowhere does a way out of the gray sea appear” (5.410; see [[gray-sea]]): Merry renders “nowhere is any escape from the sea clear away”, θύραζε only adding the sense of “out” (on 5.410). “The sea is deep close in”, ἀγχιβαθὴς δὲ θάλασσα (5.413), and if he swims on, the storm may carry him “over the fish-filled sea” (5.420; see [[fish-filled]]). The backwash “flung him far out into the sea” (5.431; see [[the-octopus]]). When at last he reaches the river's mouth, “his dear heart was overcome by the sea. / All his flesh was swollen, and much sea water gushed / up through his mouth and nostrils” (5.454–456; see [[the-river-of-scheria]]). He prays to the river as one “fleeing out of the sea from the rebukes of Poseidon” (5.446), and the river that brings him in is the river “that murmurs to the sea”, ἁλιμυρήεις (5.460; see [[murmuring-to-the-sea]]).
