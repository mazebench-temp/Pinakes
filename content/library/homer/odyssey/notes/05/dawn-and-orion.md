---
summary: Calypso's first example of the gods' jealousy: when Dawn took Orion for herself, the gods who live at ease begrudged her until Artemis killed him in Ortygia with her gentle arrows (5.121–124).
refs: [5.121-124]
passages:
  - 5.121-124 | Dawn and Orion
---

**The charge.** Hermes has delivered Zeus' command, and Calypso shudders and answers with a charge against the gods: “You are hard, you gods, and jealous beyond all others, / you who begrudge goddesses that they should lie with men / openly, if one makes a man her dear husband” (5.118–120; see [[calypso]] and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]). Dawn is her first proof.

**The first proof.** “So it was when rosy-fingered Dawn took Orion for herself” (5.121): ὣς μὲν ὅτʼ opens the first example, ὣς δʼ ὁπότʼ the second, Demeter and Iasion (5.125; see [[demeter-and-iasion]]), and ὣς δʼ αὖ νῦν, “And so now again”, brings Calypso to her own case (5.129). The verb of the charge comes back in each part: ἀγάασθε, ἠγάασθε, ἄγασθε, “begrudge” (5.119, 5.122, 5.129). τόφρα … ἧος, “so long … until”: the gods' grudge lasted until the lover was dead (5.122–123). In both examples he dies, Orion by Artemis' arrows and Iasion by Zeus' thunderbolt, and Calypso, who has saved Odysseus from the sea and meant to make him immortal, sets herself beside the two goddesses (5.129–136; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and [[immortal-and-ageless]]).

**Dawn in the book.** The book began with Dawn rising “from her bed beside noble Tithonus, / to bring light to the immortals and to mortals” (5.1–2; see [[eos]] and [[tithonus]]), the mortal husband whom the gods left to her. In Calypso's story she is “rosy-fingered Dawn” (ῥοδοδάκτυλος Ἠώς, 5.121; see [[rosy-fingered]]), as at every daybreak (5.228), and the gods who begrudge her are θεοὶ ῥεῖα ζώοντες, “you gods who live at ease” (5.122; see [[who-live-at-ease]]).

**Artemis.** χρυσόθρονος Ἄρτεμις ἁγνή, “chaste Artemis of the golden throne” (5.123; see [[artemis]] and [[golden-throned]]), killed Orion “with her gentle arrows” (5.124; see [[gentle-arrows]]). Artemis brings death to women and Apollo to men, but here, Merry says, she kills not as a goddess of death but from jealousy (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.124). The scholia report that some struck out 5.123–124, since nowhere in Homer does Artemis kill a man, and Merry's text reads ἕως μιν for ἧος (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 5.121–124). For Ortygia see [[ortygia]].
