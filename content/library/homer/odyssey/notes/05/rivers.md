---
summary: The river of Book 5 is the unnamed river of Scheria, at whose mouth Odysseus comes ashore: he prays to its god as a suppliant, the god stays his stream and brings him in, the veil goes back to Ino down its current, and by its banks Odysseus fears the frost, the dew and the cold breeze of the dawn (5.441–469).
---

**The river's mouth.** After the cliffs and reefs of the coast, Odysseus comes swimming “to the mouth of a fair-flowing river”, “where the place seemed to him the best, / smooth, free of rocks, and there was shelter from the wind” (5.441–443; see [[fair-flowing]], [[shore]] and [[scheria]]). The river is a god, and Odysseus knows him: “he knew the god as he flowed forth, and prayed to him in his heart” (5.444; see [[the-river-of-scheria]]). His prayer is a suppliant's, “as I now / come to your stream and to your knees, after toiling much” (5.448–449; see [[supplication]]), and the god answers it at once: “at once the god stopped his stream, and held back the wave, / and made a calm before him, and brought him safe / into the river's mouth” (5.451–453). The river's προχοαί, its outpourings, are the mouth where the sea meets the stream, as in the Iliad's simile of the great wave that roars against the stream at a river's mouth (*Iliad* 17.263–265; see [[the-wave-at-the-rivers-mouth]]); here the god quiets both. The rivers of the Iliad are gods too: Achilles fights the Scamander, to which, he taunts, the Trojans throw bulls and live horses (*Iliad* 21.130–132, 21.211–382; see [[achilles-and-the-river]] and [[scamander]]). This one has no name, and Odysseus prays to it as “lord, whoever you are” (5.445).

**The veil goes back.** When he has his breath again, Odysseus unties Ino's veil: “And he let it go into the river that murmurs to the sea, / and a great wave carried it back down the stream, and at once Ino / took it in her own hands” (5.460–462; see [[veil]], [[leucothea]] and [[murmuring-to-the-sea]]). ἁλιμυρήεις is commonly rendered “running with noise into the sea”, W. W. Merry notes, and in the Iliad it is a general epithet of rivers; others narrow it here to a river at its mouth, met and forced back by the sea water (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.460). Then he withdraws from the river and sinks down among the rushes, and kisses the grain-giving earth (5.462–463; see [[earth]]).

**The night by the river.** He fears to spend the night there: “If I keep watch through the comfortless night by the river, / I fear that the cruel frost and the fresh dew together / may overcome my spirit, gasped away in my weakness; / and the breeze from a river blows cold in the early dawn” (5.466–469). ἐν ποταμῷ may be “by the river”, or within its bed and banks (Merry on 5.466). θῆλυς ἐέρση is the “fresh” dew, that nourishes and refreshes, but is no boon to an exhausted man who wants warmth; Merry renders the whole (on 5.467): “I fear lest the cruel frost and fresh dew quench my life exhausted after my swooning”. For the breeze he reports Ameis' comparison of Livy, on the Romans led across the Trebia in winter, *quidquid aurae fluminis appropinquabant, adflabat acrior frigoris vis*, “the nearer they came to the breeze of the river, the keener the cold that blew on them” (Livy 21.54), and Herodotus' remark that the Nile, unlike other rivers, sends out no breeze (on 5.469): Herodotus explains that nothing is likely to blow from very hot places, while a breeze is wont to blow from something cold (Herodotus 2.27). So Odysseus leaves the river and climbs to the shady forest (5.470–475; see [[olive]] and [[trees]]).
