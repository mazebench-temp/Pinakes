summary: Hermes carries Zeus' command to Calypso. He binds on his golden sandals, takes his wand, steps on Pieria and skims the waves like a gull to the far-off island; he stops to gaze at the cave and its garden, is known at once by the nymph, eats ambrosia and drinks nectar, and tells her, against his own will, that she must let Odysseus go, warning her to fear the wrath of Zeus.

**Our messenger in all else.** Zeus turns from Athena “and said to Hermes, his dear son, face to face: / ‘Hermes, since you for your part are our messenger in all else’” (5.28–29; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]] and [[zeus]]). The second half of the first verse is the one with which he sends Hermes to Priam in the Iliad (*Iliad* 24.333). ἀντίον ηὔδα here is not an answer but an address in another's presence (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.28; see the translation's note on 5.28). αὖτε puts the weight on σύ, “thou for thy part”, and τά τʼ ἄλλα περ is “in all else whatsoever” (Merry on 5.29). Merry notes that Hermes is called here the general messenger of the gods, where in the Iliad Iris for the most part fills that office (“Iris, who is the messenger among the immortal gods”, *Iliad* 15.144), and that Iris is never named in the Odyssey. He reports the old explanation from a false etymology, Ἶρις from ἔρις, “strife”, that Iris carried the messages of war and Hermes those of peace, and the scholia's, that the warlike Iliad uses the terrifying Iris and the fabulous Odyssey Hermes, though the Iliad too sends him once, to bring Priam safe with the ransom. From the *Hymn to Demeter*, where Zeus sends Iris first and Hermes after her, when the message has failed (*Hymn to Demeter* 314–340), Merry infers that Iris is the common go-between and Hermes the god chosen for errands that need careful handling: the warning to Aegisthus (1.38), the release of Odysseus, the warning against Circe (10.277; Merry on 5.29; see [[iris]] and the general article).

**The flight.** Hermes arms for the journey in the verses of the Iliad's errand (5.43–49 = *Iliad* 24.339–345; see the translation's note on 5.43–49): “At once he bound under his feet his fine sandals, / ambrosial, golden, which carried him over the waters / and over the boundless earth as fast as the blasts of the wind” (5.44–46), the verses of Athena's sandals in Book 1 (1.96–98; see [[sandals]]), “and he took the wand with which he bewitches the eyes of men, / of whomever he will, and again wakes others who are sleeping” (5.47–48; see [[wand]]). Merry finds the wand more fitting in the Iliad, where Hermes pours sleep on the Achaean sentries (*Iliad* 24.445–446; Merry on 5.43–49). The scholia say the verses were carried over here wrongly; others answered that such things belong to the gods, as Poseidon takes his trident with him to the Ethiopians (Merry's critical note). πέτετο, “flew”, is used of any rapid movement, not only of birds (Merry on 5.49). “Stepping on Pieria, he fell from the upper air upon the sea” (5.50), on the road Hera takes from Olympus at *Iliad* 14.225–229 (Merry on 5.50; see [[pieria]]); then he skims the waves “like a bird, a gull”, and “like it, Hermes rode over the many waves” (5.51–54; see [[the-gull]]). The last verse, which the scholia and Eustathius thought added without need, makes him like the bird in his swoop, not in his form (Merry on 5.54; see the translation's note on 5.54). It is the only place in the book where he is called Ἑρμῆς; elsewhere he is Ἑρμείας (5.28, 5.29, 5.85, 5.87, 5.196).

**The god who gazes.** The narrator ends his description of the cave, its wood, its birds, its vine, its springs and its meadows with a verdict, “There even an immortal, if he came, / would gaze at the sight and delight his mind”, and the next verse makes it true: “There the guide, the slayer of Argus, stood and gazed” (5.73–75; see [[calypsos-cave]] and [[guide]]). The general clause prepares for Hermes (Merry on 5.73–74). Only when he has gazed at everything does he go in (5.76–77).

**Known at sight.** Calypso knows him the moment she looks him in the face, “for the gods are not unknown to one another, / the immortals, not even if one lives in a house far away” (5.79–80; see [[seeing-the-gods]] and the translation's note on 5.77–78). The scholia say she knew him by a divine power, not because she had seen him before, and Merry takes the second verse as Calypso's own case, living on her distant island (on 5.79). She seats him on a shining throne and greets him as a guest who seldom comes: “Why, Hermes of the golden wand, have you come to me, / respected and dear? Before now you have not come often” (5.87–88; see [[throne]] and [[xenia]]). χρυσόρραπις, “of the golden wand”, is his title in the Odyssey (5.87; again 10.277, 10.331) and in the *Hymn to Demeter*, χρυσόρραπιν Ἀργειφόντην (335; see [[of-the-golden-wand]]). She sets ambrosia before him and mixes red nectar, “And the guide, the slayer of Argus, drank and ate” (5.92–94; see [[ambrosia]] and [[nectar]]).

**The unwilling messenger.** He tells the message only when he has eaten, and only because he is asked: “You, a goddess, ask me, a god, why I have come; and I will tell you / my story truly; for you bid me” (5.97–98), words added, Merry explains, because the message is unwelcome (on 5.98). The god complains of the journey like a traveler, and what he misses on the way is the gods' due from men, sacrifices and choice hecatombs (see [[hecatomb]] and [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). His account of the man Calypso holds is the most wretched of all who fought at Troy, with the storm of Athena on the Achaeans' homecoming (5.105–111; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]] and the translation's note on 5.105–111), and the order is short: “This man he bade you now send away with all speed” (5.112), for it is his fate to reach home (5.113–115). Zeus' own words to Hermes had more: no escort of gods or men, the raft, the twentieth day, Scheria and the Phaeacians (5.32–40). Hermes says nothing of them, and it is Calypso who, having no ships to send him in, bids Odysseus build the raft (5.140–142, 5.161–164; see [[messenger-scene]] and [[raft]]).

**The warning.** When she has called the gods jealous and yielded (5.118–144; see [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]), Hermes leaves her a warning: “Send him away now, then, and have regard for the wrath of Zeus, / lest in time to come he grow angry with you in resentment” (5.146–147). ἐποπίζεο is to have regard for ὄπις, the regard due to the gods (Merry on 5.146; see the translation's note on 5.146 and [[menis]]). “So he spoke, and the mighty slayer of Argus went away” (5.148), and that evening Odysseus sits on “the throne from which had risen / Hermes” (5.195–196).

**His titles.** He is διάκτορος ἀργεϊφόντης, “the guide, the slayer of Argus”, four times in the book (5.43, 5.75, 5.94, 5.145), κρατὺς ἀργεϊφόντης, “the mighty slayer of Argus”, twice (5.49, 5.148), and Ἑρμεία χρυσόρραπι once (5.87; see [[guide]], [[argeiphontes]] and [[of-the-golden-wand]]). Zeus calls him his son, υἱὸν φίλον (5.28), as at *Iliad* 24.333; Apollo addresses him as Διὸς υἱέ, “son of Zeus” (8.335), and Eumaeus sets aside a share of meat for the nymphs and for Hermes, “son of Maia” (14.435).
