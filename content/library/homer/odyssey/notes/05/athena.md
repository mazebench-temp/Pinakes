summary: Athena pleads for Odysseus at the second council and is reminded that the plan of vengeance was her own. Then she does nothing until Poseidon has driven home to Aegae; at once she binds the other winds and rouses the North Wind, twice saves Odysseus on the rocks of Scheria by a thought she puts in his mind, and at the end of the book pours sleep on his eyes.

**Her concern.** “And Athena told them of the many sorrows of Odysseus, / remembering them; for he was her concern, there in the house of the nymph” (5.5–6; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]] and [[calypso]]). Her plea is built of verses spoken earlier in the poem by Mentor, Proteus and Medon (5.8–20; see the translation's note on 5.7–20), and Zeus answers that the plan of vengeance on the suitors was hers: “Was it not you yourself who devised this plan, / that Odysseus should come and take vengeance on those men?” He gives her Telemachus to bring home: “escort him skillfully, for you are able” (5.23–25; see [[zeus]] and [[telemachus]]). Merry points out that she had said nothing of vengeance at the first council (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.23), and that in this council she says nothing either of her own proposal there, to send Hermes to Calypso (on 5.7).

**Her storm.** In Hermes' account of the returns, the Achaeans “on their homecoming … sinned against Athena, / who roused against them an evil wind and long waves” (5.108–109; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]), as Nestor told it (3.132–135). Merry: Hermes gives a general sketch of the Greeks' disasters, without special reference to Odysseus, of whom Athena was always the champion and protector, and her storm had nothing to do with his shipwreck, which was the work of Zeus (on 5.108; see the translation's note on 5.105–111).

**After Poseidon has gone.** Through the storm she takes no part. Poseidon raises it, breaks the raft, taunts Odysseus and drives his horses home to Aegae (5.282–381; see [[poseidon]] and [[aegae]]), and only then does she act:

{{quote:adbfb558-4c99-58d3-8c41-7e1ad04d15a8}}

ἄλλʼ ἐνόησεν, “thought of another thing”, is the verse with which she turns the action in Book 2 and in Book 4 (2.382, 2.393, 4.795; see [[thought-of-another-thing]]). εὐνηθῆναι, “lie down to rest”, puts the winds to bed, and πρὸ δὲ κύματʼ ἔαξεν is “beat down the waves in front of him”: the clash of the winds had raised a cross sea in which no swimmer could live (Merry on 5.384, 5.385; see [[boreas]]). The poem says why she waited only later. On the road to the city of the Phaeacians she does not yet appear to Odysseus face to face, for she respects her father's brother, who rages against him until he reaches his own land (6.329–331), and on Ithaca she tells Odysseus that she would not fight Poseidon, her father's brother, angry for his son's blinding (13.341–343).

**Twice on the rocks.** On the coast of Scheria she saves him twice, and both times with a thought, not a hand. Thrown on the rugged shore, “There his skin would have been stripped off and his bones crushed together, / had not the gray-eyed goddess Athena put it in his mind” (5.426–427): he seizes the rock and clings to it until the wave goes by (5.428–429). The indicative λάβε shows that the thought was acted on (Merry on 5.427). When the backwash tears him from the rock, “Then unhappy Odysseus would have perished against fate, / had not gray-eyed Athena given him presence of mind” (5.436–437), and he swims out beyond the breakers and along the coast (5.438–440; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[divine-rescue]]). ἐπιφροσύνη is “prudence”; one variant repeats the verse of 5.427 (Merry on 5.437; see the translation's note on 5.437). ὑπὲρ μόρον, “against fate”, is the phrase of Zeus' complaint at the first council (1.34; Merry on 5.436; see [[moira]]). The swimming and the clinging are his own: the goddess gives the thought (see [[divine-intervention]]). He does not know it. In the next book he prays to her outside the city of the Phaeacians, “now at least hear me, since before you never heard me / when I was being wrecked, when the famous shaker of the earth wrecked me” (νῦν δή πέρ μευ ἄκουσον, ἐπεὶ πάρος οὔ ποτʼ ἄκουσας / ῥαιομένου, 6.325–326, a plain rendering; see [[seeing-the-gods]]).

**Sleep.** The book ends with her. When Odysseus has covered himself with leaves like a brand in the ashes, “Athena / poured sleep on his eyes, so that it might free him as quickly as possible / from his grievous weariness, enfolding his dear eyelids” (5.491–493; see [[hypnos]]). ὕπνος is the subject of παύσειε, as the masculine ἀμφικαλύψας shows (Merry on 5.492; see the translation's note on 5.492–493). It is the service she does Penelope four times, “until sweet / sleep was cast upon her eyelids by gray-eyed Athena” (1.363–364; again 16.451, 19.604, 21.358), and the next book opens with Odysseus asleep and Athena going to the city of the Phaeacians (6.1–3).
