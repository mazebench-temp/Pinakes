---
summary: Euripides tells of the Ino of this book before she became a goddess, the mother who, maddened by the gods and driven from her house by Hera, leapt into the sea with her two sons, and of her son Palaemon as a guardian of ships; and his captive Trojan women mourn that they will no longer whirl the κερκίς at the loom, as Calypso does.
---

- Ino, maddened by the gods when Hera drove her from her house, the one woman of old who laid hands on her own children: she fell into the brine, stretching her foot over the sea's headland, and perished with her two sons (*Medea* 1282–1289); and a herdsman's prayer to the gods of the sea, “O son of the sea goddess Leucothea, guardian of ships, lord Palaemon, be gracious to us” (*Iphigenia among the Taurians* 270–271; on 5.333–335): [[leucothea]].
- The chorus of captive Trojan women, mourning with Hecuba, will no longer “whirl the κερκίς at the looms of Ida” (*Trojan Women* 199–200; Merry cites it as 198), the use from which Merry argues that Calypso's golden κερκίς (5.62) is the shuttle (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.62; see the translation's note on 5.62): [[loom]] and [[weaving]].
