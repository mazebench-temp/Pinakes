summary: Calypso tells Hermes that she meant to make Odysseus “immortal and ageless all his days” (5.135–136), and on the last evening offers it again, if he will stay with her (5.206–213). He grants that she is immortal and ageless and his wife mortal, and chooses home (5.215–224). The book also names the goddesses' mortal lovers, Tithonus, Orion and Iasion, and Ino, a mortal who has become a goddess of the sea (5.1, 5.121–128, 5.333–335).

**The offer.** Calypso tells Hermes that she saved Odysseus when he was astride the keel, “Him I loved and cared for, and I said / that I would make him immortal and ageless all his days” (5.135–136; see [[immortal-and-ageless]]). On the last evening she makes the offer a condition: if he knew in his mind how many sorrows it is his lot to fill up before he reaches his native land, he would stay and keep this house with her, “and be immortal, for all your longing to see / your wife” (5.206–210; see [[algos]]). She rests her claim on her looks: she is in no way inferior to his wife in form or stature, “since it is in no way fitting / for mortal women to vie with immortals in form and looks” (5.211–213; see [[eidos]]).

**The choice.**

{{quote:f61bdf60-6f54-5400-abab-390c33b21729}}

He grants the comparison and rests nothing on it: she is “immortal and ageless”, Penelope mortal (5.218). Merry notes that Calypso lays all the stress on Penelope's beauty, while Odysseus has the tact to speak only of his wife's wisdom, περίφρων, “prudent”, and in telling his longing speaks only of his home (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.216; see [[penelope]] and [[periphron]]). What he wants is “to come home and see the day of my homecoming” (5.220; see [[nostos]] and [[day-of-homecoming]]), and he will bear whatever a god sends on the sea, for “already I have suffered very much and toiled much / on the waves and in war; let this too be added to them” (5.221–224; “let this too come into the list of my woes”, Merry on 5.224; see [[much-enduring]]). The offer is told twice more in the poem, by Odysseus to Arete and in his tale to Penelope, each time with the words that she never persuaded the heart in his breast (7.256–258, 23.335–337).

**Mortal food.** At the meal before she speaks, the nymph sets before him “all kinds of food, / to eat and to drink, such as mortal men eat”, while her slave women set ambrosia and nectar beside her (5.196–199; see [[ambrosia]] and [[nectar]]). The food of the gods is what makes them bloodless and immortal in the Iliad's account (*Iliad* 5.341–342; see [[ichor]]). Merry reports Hayman's remark that now, with no hope of keeping him, she feeds him on mortal food (on 5.136; see the translation's note on 5.136).

**The goddesses' lovers.** The book begins with Dawn rising “from her bed beside noble Tithonus” (5.1; see [[tithonus]]), and Calypso's complaint names two lovers whom the gods would not let a goddess keep, Orion, whom Artemis killed, and Iasion, whom Zeus struck with his thunderbolt (5.121–128; see [[dawn-and-orion]], [[demeter-and-iasion]] and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]). Merry hears irony in her words: the gods wink at the amours of goddesses, but will not endure that they raise a mortal to the honorable place of husband (on 5.120).

**A mortal made a goddess.** Ino is the one mortal in the book who has crossed over: she “was once a mortal with human voice, / but now in the open sea has a share of honor from the gods” (5.334–335; see [[leucothea]] and the translation's note on 5.334). She lends Odysseus her immortal veil, κρήδεμνον … ἄμβροτον, and takes it back when he has reached the land (5.346–347, 5.459–462; see [[veil]]).
