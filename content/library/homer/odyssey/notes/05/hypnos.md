summary: Hermes' wand “wakes others who are sleeping” (5.48); on Calypso's island Odysseus “would sleep beside her, under compulsion” (5.154); on the raft “sleep did not fall on his eyelids” as he watched the stars (5.271); on the shore of Scheria he fears to fall asleep in the thicket (5.470–473), until Athena “poured sleep on his eyes” in his bed of leaves (5.491–493).
grc: [ὑπνώοντας, ἰαύεσκεν, καταδράθω]
en: [sleeping, fall asleep]
---

**Sleep and its lack.** Hermes takes up the wand “with which he bewitches the eyes of men, / of whomever he will, and again wakes others who are sleeping” (5.47–48 = *Iliad* 24.343–344; see [[wand]] and [[hermes]]); Merry finds the wand's power more fitting in the Iliad, where Hermes had to lull the guards to sleep (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.43–49; see the translation's note on 5.43–49). On Calypso's island Odysseus “would sleep beside her, under compulsion, / in the hollow caves, unwilling beside the willing” (5.154–155; see [[with-an-unwilling-heart]] and [[calypso]]). On the raft he does not sleep: he steers, and “sleep did not fall on his eyelids / as he watched the Pleiades and Boötes, who sets late, / and the Bear” (5.270–273; see [[pleiades]] and [[the-bear]]).

**Sleep on the shore.** Come ashore, he weighs where to spend the night: by the river, where the frost and the dew may overcome him, or up the slope in the thick bushes, where, “if sweet sleep comes over me”, he may become the prey and spoil of wild beasts (5.465–473; see [[sweet-sleep]]). The optative μεθείη marks a further hope beyond falling asleep, that the chill and weariness may leave him (Merry on 5.471; see the translation's note on 5.471). He chooses the wood, heaps up a bed of leaves under two bushes of olive and wild olive, and covers himself like a brand kept in the ashes (5.474–491; see [[olive]] and [[the-brand-in-the-ashes]]), “and Athena / poured sleep on his eyes, so that it might free him as quickly as possible / from his grievous weariness, enfolding his dear eyelids” (5.491–493; see [[athena]]). ὕπνος is the subject of παύσειε, as the masculine ἀμφικαλύψας shows (Merry on 5.492). The next book begins with him asleep there, “overcome by sleep and weariness” (6.1–2).
