summary: The book is about a man without a ship. Odysseus has no ships with oars and no companions, Calypso has none to give him, and the swift ship that brought him was shattered by Zeus' thunderbolt; Zeus sends him home on a raft as broad as a freight ship, which he sets against the balanced ships that would not cross that sea, and only the Phaeacians will send him home in a ship (5.16–17, 5.37, 5.131–132, 5.141–142, 5.174–176, 5.249–250).
# φορτίδος, "a freight ship" (5.250; again 9.323), first met here.
grc: [φορτίδος]
en: [freight ship]
---

**No ship.** Athena's plea and Calypso's refusal turn on the same verses: he “has no ships with oars and no companions / who might take him over the broad back of the sea” (5.16–17 = 4.559–560), and Calypso, bidden to send him away, says “I have no ships with oars and no companions” (5.141–142; see [[oars]] and [[calypso]]). The ship that brought him to her was “his swift ship”, which Zeus struck with a gleaming thunderbolt and shattered in the middle of the wine-dark sea, and she saved him astride its keel (5.130–132; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and [[thunderbolt]]). The other ships of the book are the suitors' ship, lying in wait for Telemachus, which Zeus means to go back again with its purpose foiled (5.25–27; see the translation's note on 5.27 and [[the-suitors]]), and the ship of the Phaeacians, who “will send him in a ship to his dear native land” (5.37; see [[phaeacians]]).

**Ships and the raft.** Odysseus measures Calypso's command against real ships: on a raft across the great gulf of the sea, “over it not even balanced ships / cross, swift-sailing, glorying in the wind of Zeus” (5.174–176; see [[raft]], [[balanced-ships]] and [[swift-sailing-ships]]). ἀγαλλόμεναι, “glorying”, suggests the common impersonation of the ship that seems to “walk the water like a thing of life” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.176), and the contrast is between good ships properly built and such a one as Odysseus might make for himself (Merry on 5.175). The raft he builds is measured by a ship: “As wide as the floor of a ship that a man marks out, / a broad freight ship, a man well skilled in carpentry, / so wide did Odysseus make his broad raft” (5.249–251; see [[the-building-of-the-raft]]). The freight ship, φορτίς, from φόρτος, “cargo”, is broader in the beam than a ship made for speed (Merry, Appendix I §4); the Cyclops' club will be measured by the mast of one (9.322–323).

**No harbor.** When Odysseus comes to the coast of Scheria, “there were no harbors to hold ships, nor roadsteads” (5.404; see [[harbor]]); ἐπιωγαί are not harbors but roadsteads, where ships might lie under the lee of the land (Merry on 5.404).
