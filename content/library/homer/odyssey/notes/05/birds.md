summary: The birds of Book 5 are the birds of the sea. Long-winged birds roost in the wood around Calypso's cave, owls and hawks and long-tongued crows of the sea (5.65–67); Hermes skims the waves like a gull (5.51–54), and Ino rises from the sea and goes back into it like a diving bird (5.337, 5.353).

# found only here in the Odyssey; κορώνῃσιν (12.418, 14.308), the men in the sea like sea crows, is to be added with its book.

**The birds of the cave.** Around Calypso's cave grows a wood, “And there long-winged birds would roost, / owls and hawks and long-tongued crows / of the sea, whose concern is the works of the sea” (5.65–67; see [[trees]], [[calypsos-cave]] and [[long-winged]]). Three kinds are named. The ἴρηκες are hawks (see [[hawk]]). The σκῶπες, named only here in Homer, are owls of some kind: Eustathius knew a reading κῶπες (Merry's critical note on 5.66). W. W. Merry quotes Pliny, who says that Homer names the scops as a kind of bird, that he cannot picture the antic movements told of them as they lie in wait, and that the birds themselves are no longer known (*Natural History* 10.49). Merry derives the name not from σκώπτω, “mock”, as of a bird that mimics others, but from σκέπτομαι, “look”, a “staring” bird, as γλαῦξ, the owl, is akin to λεύσσω, “see”; he suggests “horned owl” for want of evidence (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.66). Aristotle counts the σκώψ among the night birds with crooked talons, with the γλαύξ and others, “and the σκώψ is smaller than the γλαύξ” (*History of Animals* 8.3).

The κορῶναι εἰνάλιαι, “crows of the sea”, Merry would render “cormorants”, whose dark plumage may have given them the popular name of sea crows. There is nothing out of place, he says, in making them roost in trees, since cormorants nest in mangroves in the southern seas; but there is nearly as much uncertainty about these birds as about the σκῶπες: the scholia identify them with the αἴθυιαι, the diving birds, and Hesychius with the λάροι, the gulls. τανύγλωσσοι, “long-tongued”, probably refers to their cry, as if the bird were caught at the moment of its screech, with its tongue stretched out (on 5.66). The men of Odysseus' last ship, thrown into the sea by Zeus' thunderbolt, will be carried on the waves around the ship “like crows” (κορώνῃσιν ἴκελοι, 12.418, a plain rendering). The crows' concern is θαλάσσια ἔργα, “the works of the sea”, which Merry takes of the pursuit of fish, comparing πολεμήια ἔργα, “the works of war” (12.116), and on the same verse he reports Nitzsch's comparison of the fishermen in Hesiod who “work the gray sea” (*Theogony* 440).

**The birds of the similes.** The gods of this book move like birds of the sea. Hermes, coming down from Pieria, skims the waves λάρῳ ὄρνιθι ἐοικώς, “like a bird, a gull” (5.51; see [[the-gull]] and [[gull]]), the general word first and then the kind, as in the vultures of the Iliad's oak (*Iliad* 7.59; see [[like-vultures]]). Ino rises from the sea to the broken raft and plunges back into it αἰθυίῃ ἐικυῖα, “like a diving bird” (5.337, 5.353; see [[like-a-diving-bird]]). Merry keeps the two birds apart: the λάρος does not dive deep, and the αἴθυια “must be a bird that dives” (on 5.51).
