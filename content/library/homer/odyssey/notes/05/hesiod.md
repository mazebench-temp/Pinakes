---
summary: Hesiod's Theogony tells more of the figures of this book. Calypso is a daughter of Oceanus and bore Odysseus two sons; Styx, by whose water she swears, is the eldest of Oceanus' daughters and the great oath of the gods; Demeter bore Plutus to Iasion in a thrice-plowed fallow in Crete; Ino is a daughter of Cadmus and Harmonia; Dawn bore Memnon to Tithonus, and the winds to Astraeus. The Works and Days ends the sailing season when the Pleiades, fleeing Orion, set in the misty sea.
---

- **Calypso.** “Lovely Calypso” is one of the daughters of Oceanus and Tethys (*Theogony* 359), and at the end of the poem “Calypso, brilliant among goddesses”, with the Odyssey's formula for her, bears Odysseus two sons, Nausithous and Nausinous (*Theogony* 1017–1018). Homer calls her the daughter of Atlas (1.52, 7.245) and gives her no children: [[calypso]] and [[brilliant-among-goddesses]].
- **Styx.** Calypso swears by “the down-flowing water of Styx, which is the greatest / and most dread oath for the blessed gods” (5.185–186). Hesiod's Styx is the foremost and the eldest of the daughters of Oceanus (*Theogony* 361, 776–777); she came first to Olympus with her children to fight for Zeus against the Titans, and he made her the great oath of the gods (*Theogony* 383–403). Her cold water drips from a high rock, and Iris brings it from far off in a golden jug when a god must swear; a god who swears falsely by it lies a year without breath or voice, ἀνάπνευστος καὶ ἄναυδος, and for nine years more is kept from the gods' councils and feasts (*Theogony* 775–806). Odysseus, come out of the sea, lies ἄπνευστος καὶ ἄναυδος, “breathless and speechless” (5.456): [[styx]] and [[oath]].
- **Iasion.** “Demeter, brilliant among goddesses, bore Plutus, joined in lovely love with the hero Iasion in a thrice-plowed fallow, in the rich land of Crete” (*Theogony* 969–971): the field is the field of Calypso's verse, νειῷ ἔνι τριπόλῳ (5.127), and the son is Wealth, who goes over all the earth and the broad back of the sea and makes rich the man into whose hands he comes (*Theogony* 972–974). The text prints Ἰασίωνʼ ἥρωι; Merry cites the verse with the form Ἰάσιος (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.125): [[iasion]], [[demeter]] and [[demeter-and-iasion]].
- **Ino.** Harmonia bore to Cadmus Ino, Semele, Agave, Autonoe and Polydorus in Thebes (*Theogony* 975–978); Homer's Ino is “the daughter of Cadmus” (5.333): [[leucothea]] and [[cadmus]].
- **Dawn and the winds.** Dawn bore to Tithonus Memnon, king of the Ethiopians, and Emathion (*Theogony* 984–985), and to Astraeus the winds, “the clearing West Wind, the North Wind swift on his way, and the South Wind” (*Theogony* 378–380). The book opens as Dawn rises from Tithonus' bed (5.1–2), and its storm brings these three winds with the East Wind (5.295–296): [[eos]], [[tithonus]], [[memnon]], [[zephyrus]], [[boreas]], [[notus]] and [[eurus]].
- **The Pleiades.** Odysseus steers watching the Pleiades (5.272). For Hesiod their setting ends the sailing season: “when the Pleiades, fleeing the mighty strength of Orion, fall into the misty sea, then the blasts of winds of every kind rage; and then keep your ships no longer on the wine-dark sea” (*Works and Days* 619–622; Merry on 5.272), with the misty sea and the winds of every kind of this book (5.164, 5.293, 5.305). Their rising begins the harvest and their setting the plowing (*Works and Days* 383–384): [[pleiades]] and [[orion]].
