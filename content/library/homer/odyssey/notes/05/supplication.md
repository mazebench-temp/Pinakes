summary: Odysseus comes as a suppliant to the god of the river of Scheria: “I come to you, much prayed for” (5.445), “as I now / come to your stream and to your knees, after toiling much” (5.448–449), “But pity me, lord; I claim to be your suppliant” (5.450). The god stops his stream and brings him safe into its mouth (5.451–453).
grc: [ἱκέτης, "γούναθʼ ἱκάνω"]
en: [suppliant]
passages:
  - 5.441-453 | The suppliant of the river
---

Odysseus' first supplication in the poem is made to a god, and in words alone: he cannot clasp a river's knees, and he names them. “I come to you, much prayed for”, he begins, after the opening of a prayer, “Hear me, lord, whoever you are” (5.445; see [[prayer]] and the translation's note on 5.445); then the maxim on which he rests his claim, that even to the immortal gods a wanderer is to be respected (5.447–448; see [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]]), and the claim itself, ἱκέτης δέ τοι εὔχομαι εἶναι, “I claim to be your suppliant” (5.450; see [[euchomai]] and [[pity]]). To come to the knees is the suppliant's phrase: Telemachus came to Nestor's and to Menelaus' knees for news of his father (τὰ σὰ γούναθʼ ἱκάνομαι, 3.92, 4.322, as Thetis to Hephaestus, *Iliad* 18.457), and this whole verse returns, with “your husband” for “your stream”, when Odysseus clasps the knees of Arete in the Phaeacian palace (7.147; see [[gounata]]). The god answers as a host would answer: he stops his stream, holds back the wave, makes a calm before him and brings him safe into the river's mouth (5.451–453; see [[the-river-of-scheria]]). Eumaeus will use the same claim for the stranger when he puts him in Telemachus' hands, “he claims to be your suppliant” (16.67).
