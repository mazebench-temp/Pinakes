summary: Zeus sends Hermes to tell Calypso “the homecoming of stout-hearted Odysseus” (5.31), on a raft and without escort of gods or men. Odysseus sits on her shore “as he mourned for his homecoming” (5.153), and sets “the day of my homecoming” above immortality (5.220). In the storm Ino bids him “strive to come / to the land of the Phaeacians” (5.344–345), νόστος of reaching a place; Hermes recalls the Achaeans who sinned against Athena “on their homecoming” (5.108).

**The counsel of Zeus.** Zeus answers Athena at the second council by sending Hermes with the verses she proposed at the first, “our unerring counsel, / the homecoming of stout-hearted Odysseus, that he may return” (5.30–31 = 1.86–87; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). νόστον stands in apposition with βουλήν, and ὥς κε νέηται spells it out (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.31; see [[boule]]). This homecoming has its conditions: “with the escort neither of gods nor of mortal men” but on a raft (5.32–33), θεῶν πομπῇ being such an escort as Telemachus had (Merry on 5.32, comparing 2.417), and to Scheria, where the Phaeacians will honor him like a god and send him home in a ship with more treasure than he would have carried from Troy (5.34–40; see [[raft]], [[scheria]] and [[phaeacians]]). “For so it is his fate to see his friends and to reach / his high-roofed house and his native land” (5.41–42, again 5.114–115; see [[moira]]), after the verses in which Proteus told Menelaus that it was not yet his fate to reach his home (4.475–476). Telemachus' return is the other half of the plan. The suitors are eager to kill him “as he comes home” (5.18–19 = 4.700–701), and Athena is to bring him to his native land unharmed, “and the suitors in their ship may go back again” (5.25–27; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]] and the translation's note on 5.27).

**The returns.** Hermes sums up the war: the men who fought around the city of Priam sacked it in the tenth year and went home, “but on their homecoming they sinned against Athena” (5.105–109; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]] and the translation's note on 5.105–111). Merry notes that the going home is qualified by what follows, as in the false tale of 14.240–242, where the Achaeans sack the city of Priam in the tenth year and go home with their ships, “and a god scattered the Achaeans” (on 5.107).

**Mourned and chosen.** On Calypso's shore Odysseus' eyes are never dry, “and his sweet life was ebbing away / as he mourned for his homecoming, since the nymph no longer pleased him” (5.152–153; see [[lament]] and [[tears]]). Merry glosses οὐκέτι: she had found favor in his eyes as the one who saved him from the wreck, and that feeling had passed into homesickness (on 5.153). Calypso asks whether he really wishes to go home at once, “home to your dear native land” (οἶκόνδε, 5.204), and offers him immortality if he will stay; he answers, “But even so I wish and yearn all my days / to come home and see the day of my homecoming” (5.219–220; see [[immortality]] and [[day-of-homecoming]]). The proem's man “wanting his homecoming and his wife” (1.13) here chooses it against a goddess.

**Coming to a strange land.** In the storm Ino bids him leave the raft, “and swimming with your hands strive to come / to the land of the Phaeacians, where it is your fate to escape” (5.344–345; see [[leucothea]]). Merry renders “strive to get home on the Phaeacian shore”, literally “grasp your return”: γαίης depends on νόστου, as in νόστον Ἀχαιίδος (23.68), and νόστος is used here of coming to a place, without the idea of returning, though the notion of getting home, of coming to rest, is probably not absent (on 5.344–345; see the translation's note on 5.344–345). He had compared the phrase when he joined νόστου with “over the broad back of the sea” at 3.142 (on 3.142).
