summary: On his way back from the Ethiopians, Poseidon sees Odysseus on his raft from the mountains of the Solymi. Angry that the gods have changed their purpose while he was away, though he knows that it is Odysseus' lot to escape in the land of the Phaeacians, he raises the storm with his trident, rouses the wave that breaks the raft, taunts Odysseus and drives his horses home to Aegae. Ino and Odysseus himself name his anger.

**From the mountains of the Solymi.** The god who was away at the first council comes back late in the book: “But the powerful earth-shaker, returning from the Ethiopians, / saw him from far away, from the mountains of the Solymi; for he came into his sight / sailing over the sea” (5.282–284; see [[ethiopians]], [[solymi]] and the general article). He had gone to the Ethiopians to receive a sacrifice (1.22–26; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.282), and his absence let the gods decide on Odysseus' homecoming at both councils (see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). “And he grew all the more angry in his heart”: his smouldering anger blazed up afresh when he saw Odysseus near the Phaeacian land, and so almost out of his reach (Merry on 5.284). Then “he shook his head and spoke to his own heart” (5.285), the verse of Zeus watching Hector in Achilles' armor and pitying Achilles' horses (*Iliad* 17.200, 17.442; see [[shook-his-head]]):

{{quote:081769c5-f081-58ee-8e7e-5fe02b54be92}}

He knows what Zeus has decreed: it is Odysseus' αἶσα, his lot, to escape in the land of the Phaeacians (see [[aisa]] and the translation's note on 5.289). His anger works within that limit, as Zeus foretold at the first council, when he said that Poseidon would let go his anger, for he could not strive alone against all the immortals (1.77–79). ἅδην ἐλάαν κακότητος is “drive him to his heart's content along the road of misery”, a lively phrase in the mouth of the god who drives his horses over the sea (Merry on 5.290, comparing *Iliad* 19.423; see the translation's note on 5.290).

**The storm.** “So saying, he gathered the clouds and threw the sea into confusion, / taking his trident in his hands” (5.291–292). The trident is named three times in Homer, always in his hands: here, when he leads the waves against the Achaean wall (*Iliad* 12.27–28), and when he splits the rock of Gyrae under Ajax, son of Oileus (4.506–507). The next verses, “and with clouds he covered / land and sea alike; and night arose from the sky” (5.293–294), are used again of the storms that Zeus sends on Odysseus' ships after the raid on the Cicones and at Thrinacia (9.67–69, 12.313–315), and the gathering of clouds is the work of the cloud-gatherer (see [[cloud-gatherer]]). Odysseus, on the raft, gives the storm to Zeus (5.303–305; see [[zeus]]). All four winds fall on the sea together (5.295–296; see [[eurus]], [[notus]], [[zephyrus]] and [[boreas]]).

**The wave.** When Ino has given Odysseus her veil and he is still deciding to stay on the raft, “Poseidon the earth-shaker roused a great wave against him, / dread and grievous, arching over him, and it struck him” (5.366–367), and the beams are scattered like a heap of dry husks in the wind (5.368–370; see [[winnowing]] and [[raft]]).

**The taunt and the drive home.** “And the powerful earth-shaker saw him” as he plunged into the sea with the veil under his chest, and again he shook his head and spoke to his own heart, with Odysseus before his eyes: “So now, after suffering many evils, wander over the sea, / until you come among men whom Zeus has nurtured. / But even so I do not expect you to make light of your misery” (5.375–379). It is a threat in sarcastic words, as if Odysseus would not be satisfied with anything short of the extremity of suffering (Merry on 5.379; see the translation's note on 5.379). “So he spoke, and lashed the fine-maned horses, / and came to Aegae, where his glorious house is” (5.380–381; see [[fine-maned]] and [[aegae]]), the golden house in the depths of the sea from which he drives out over the waves in the Iliad (*Iliad* 13.21–31). Merry takes this Aegae for the town of Achaea, joined with Helice in the cult of Poseidon at *Iliad* 8.203 (on 5.381). Only when he has gone does Athena act (5.382–387; see [[athena]]).

**His anger named.** Others name his anger twice, with the verb ὀδύσσομαι that the poem sets beside Odysseus' name (see [[cholos]]). Ino asks, “why is Poseidon the earth-shaker / so terribly angry with you (ὠδύσατʼ), that he plants so many evils for you? / Yet he will not destroy you, though he rages greatly” (5.339–341; see [[leucothea]]); ἐκπάγλως is from the root of ἐκπλήσσω (Merry on 5.340). And Odysseus, who had blamed Zeus for the storm, knows the god by the time he reaches the rocks of Scheria: “for I know how the famous shaker of the earth is angry with me” (ὀδώδυσται, 5.423). To the river he calls himself one “fleeing out of the sea from the rebukes of Poseidon” (5.446; see [[the-river-of-scheria]]).

**His titles.** He is κρείων ἐνοσίχθων, “the powerful earth-shaker”, when he sees Odysseus (5.282, 5.375), Ποσειδάων ἐνοσίχθων, “Poseidon the earth-shaker”, in Ino's question and in the narrator's verse of the wave (5.339, 5.366), and κλυτὸς ἐννοσίγαιος, “the famous shaker of the earth”, in Odysseus' mouth (5.423; see [[earth-shaker]], [[powerful]] and [[shaker-of-the-earth]]).
