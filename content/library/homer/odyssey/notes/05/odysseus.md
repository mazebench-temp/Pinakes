summary: The first book in which Odysseus himself appears. The gods decree his homecoming, and Hermes finds him weeping on Calypso's shore; he suspects her when she bids him build a raft, makes her swear, and on the last evening chooses his home and his mortal wife over immortality. He builds the raft in four days and sails for seventeen, steering by the stars; on the eighteenth Poseidon wrecks him, and with Ino's veil, Athena's help and the river god's pity he comes ashore on Scheria after two nights and two days in the sea, and sleeps under the olives (5.5–493).

**The decree.** At the second council Athena tells again “the many sorrows of Odysseus”, “for he was her concern” (5.5–6), in verses spoken before by Mentor, Proteus and Medon (5.8–20; see the translation's note on 5.7–20 and [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). Zeus reminds her that the plan was her own, “that Odysseus should come and take vengeance on those men” (5.24; see [[the-suitors]]), and decrees his return: on a raft, with the escort neither of gods nor of men, in twenty days to deep-soiled Scheria, where the Phaeacians will honor him like a god and send him home with more bronze, gold and clothing than he would have brought from Troy (5.31–40; see [[raft]], [[scheria]] and [[phaeacians]]). “For so it is his fate to see his friends and to reach / his high-roofed house and his native land” (5.41–42 = 5.114–115; see [[moira]]): ὥς, “in such wise”, takes in both the perils of the voyage and the honorable escort from Phaeacia (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.41). To Calypso, Hermes calls him “a man more wretched than all the others” (5.105), not than all mankind, Merry explains, but than all the heroes who fought around the walls of Troy (on 5.106).

**On the shore.** Hermes does not find him in the cave: “he sat weeping on the shore, as he had before” (5.82). When Calypso goes to him, the narrator tells how his days and nights have passed:

{{quote:5af09c77-2da9-5aec-bdd7-7552b98c4e75}}

οὐκέτι, “no longer”: as his savior from shipwreck she had found favor in his eyes, but that feeling has passed and homesickness has taken its place (Merry on 5.153). ἀνάγκῃ is “even perforce”, if he would not come willingly (on 5.154), and παρʼ οὐκ ἐθέλων ἐθελούσῃ, “unwilling beside the willing”, sets the contrasted words side by side, as ἐθέλων ἐθέλουσαν does of Aegisthus and Clytemnestra (3.272; Merry on 5.155). These are the tears Proteus saw (4.556), and the longing Athena told of at the first council, when he longed to see even the smoke leaping up from his own land, and to die (1.57–59; see [[tears]], [[nostos]] and [[calypso]]).

**The oath.** His first words in the poem are a suspicion. Calypso has bidden him build a raft and promised to send him home with a fair wind (5.160–170), and he shudders:

{{quote:7aa0f87c-2f75-5c87-b355-3b4f496c0815}}

He sets good ships properly built against such a craft as he might make for himself (Merry on 5.175; see [[balanced-ships]]), and he is “cautious to a fault”, since she had said that she would send him away “with a ready heart” (Merry on 5.177; 5.161). She smiles, calls him “a rogue, and no fool”, and swears by Earth, Heaven and the water of Styx (5.180–191; see [[calypso]] and [[oath]]). He will ask Circe for the same oath in the same words, on Hermes' advice (10.343–344, 10.299–300), and his mistrust comes back in the sea, when Ino bids him leave the raft: “may it not be that someone is weaving a trap for me again” (5.356). He was suspicious of Calypso, and now again he mistrusts Leucothea (Merry on 5.356; see [[dolos]] and [[leucothea]]).

**The choice.** On the last evening Calypso addresses him with the Iliad's verse of address, “Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices” (5.203; *Iliad* 2.173 and often; see [[son-of-laertes]]), here used for the first time in the Odyssey, and asks whether he still wishes to go: if he knew the sorrows in store for him he would stay and be immortal, and she is no worse than his wife in form or stature (5.203–213). He answers:

{{quote:f61bdf60-6f54-5400-abab-390c33b21729}}

Calypso lays all the stress on Penelope's beauty; Odysseus has the tact to speak only of her wisdom, περίφρων, “prudent”, and in telling of his longing he speaks only of his home (Merry on 5.216; see [[penelope]] and [[periphron]]). ταλαπενθέα θυμόν, “a heart … that bears up under sorrow”, is found only here in Homer (5.222), and μετὰ καὶ τόδε τοῖσι γενέσθω, “let this too be added to them”, sets the new woe beside the old (Merry on 5.224, 5.155). At the end of the poem he tells Penelope how Calypso promised to make him immortal and ageless all his days, but never persuaded the heart in his breast (23.333–337, a plain rendering; see [[immortal-and-ageless]] and [[immortality]]).

**The builder.** In four days he builds the raft with the tools Calypso gives him. He fells twenty trees and hews them with the axe, “and smoothed them skillfully and made them straight to the chalk line” (5.245), bores and fits them and hammers the raft together with pegs and fastenings, as wide as the floor of a broad freight ship that a man well skilled in carpentry marks out; he sets up the deck on the close-set ribs, finishes with long side planks, makes mast and yard and steering oar, fences it with osier withes, fashions sails and ropes, and drags it down to the sea with levers (5.243–262; see [[the-building-of-the-raft]] and [[the-chalk-line]]). The words of the chalk line come back when he tells Penelope how he made their bed from the trunk of an olive: he smoothed it well and skillfully with the bronze, made it straight to the chalk line, and bored it all with an auger (23.196–198, a plain rendering; see [[bed]]).

**The steersman.** “Glad of the wind, brilliant Odysseus spread his sails” (5.269), and he steers “with skill with the steering oar”, never sleeping, watching the Pleiades, Boötes and the Bear, which Calypso had bidden him keep on his left hand (5.270–277; see [[pleiades]], [[bootes]], [[the-bear]] and [[orion]]). For seventeen days he sails, and on the eighteenth the shadowy mountains of the Phaeacians' land appear, “like a shield on the misty sea” (5.278–281; see the translation's note on 5.281).

**The storm.** Poseidon, returning from the Ethiopians, sees him and raises the storm (5.282–296; see [[poseidon]]). “And then Odysseus' knees and dear heart were loosened” (5.297; see [[loosened-his-limbs]]), and four times in the book he speaks “to his own proud heart” (5.298, 5.355, 5.407, 5.464; see [[deeply-troubled]] and [[decision-scene]]). In the first speech he fears that Calypso's words are coming true, and blames Zeus for the clouds and the winds, not knowing that the storm is Poseidon's, as men commonly lay a storm on Zeus (5.299–305; the scholia, in Merry on 5.304; see [[zeus]]). Then:

{{quote:e966c437-7141-5a60-9d14-729a47b40013}}

The day is the fight over the body of Achilles, which the shade of Agamemnon recalls in the underworld (24.36–42); Merry adds that Ajax carried off the body while Odysseus held off the Trojans (on 5.309; see [[the-fight-over-the-body-of-achilles]] and [[achilles]]). κλέος ἦγον is “would have spread my fame”, or, as others take it, “would have brought back tidings of me” (Merry on 5.311; see [[kleos]]). It is the death his son wished for him in Book 1: had he fallen among his companions at Troy, the Achaeans would have made him a tomb, and he would have won great fame for his son (1.236–240; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]] and [[three-times-blessed]]).

**In the sea.** The wave throws him from the raft and breaks the mast, and Calypso's garments hold him under; he comes up spitting brine, and does not forget the raft (5.313–326). Ino calls him “Ill-fated man”, as Calypso had (5.339, 5.160): in Homer κάμμορος is said of Odysseus alone, by his son before (2.351), and later by his mother and by Athena (11.216, 20.33). She tells him whose anger pursues him, the earth-shaker's, and gives him her immortal veil (5.339–350; see [[leucothea]] and [[veil]]). He resolves to stay on the raft while its beams hold together (5.356–364), until Poseidon's wave scatters them; then he bestrides a single beam “as though riding a horse”, strips off Calypso's garments, stretches the veil under his chest and plunges into the sea (5.365–375; see [[the-rider-of-four-horses]]). The art of riding is alluded to elsewhere in Homer only in a simile of the Iliad, and even there the rider is a performer leaping from horse to horse (*Iliad* 15.679–684; Merry on 5.371). Poseidon taunts him, and drives home to Aegae (5.375–381).

**The cliffs.** Athena binds the other winds and rouses the North Wind (5.382–387); after two nights and two days he sees the land from the crest of a wave, as welcome as a father's recovery to his children (5.388–399; see [[the-fathers-recovery]]). But the coast is cliffs and reefs, and in his third speech he weighs the jagged rock, the storm that may carry him off again and the sea monster a god may send, “for I know how the famous shaker of the earth is angry with me” (5.408–423; see [[cholos]] and [[sea-beasts]]): ὀδώδυσται is the verb from which his grandfather gave him his name (19.406–409), and now, since Ino's warning, he knows whose anger it is. A wave casts him on the rock; at Athena's prompting he clings to it, and the backwash strips the skin from his hands, as pebbles cling to the suckers of an octopus dragged from its den (5.424–435; see [[the-octopus]]). “Then unhappy Odysseus would have perished against fate, / had not gray-eyed Athena given him presence of mind” (5.436–437; see [[athena]] and [[moira]]).

**The suppliant.** Swimming along outside the surf, he comes to the mouth of a fair-flowing river, and “he knew the god as he flowed forth” (5.444): he recognized him for a god (Merry on 5.444). He prays to him in his heart:

{{quote:be3a36a8-d0cd-5dc5-a9c8-cbef0efeb7c2}}

The god stops his stream, makes a calm before him and brings him safe into the river's mouth (5.451–453; see [[the-river-of-scheria]] and [[supplication]]). γούνατʼ ἔκαμψε is the picture of a man exhausted, his arms dropping and his knees bending under him (Merry on 5.453). He lies breathless and speechless, swollen with the sea; when his spirit gathers again he lets the veil go into the river that murmurs to the sea, and a great wave carries it back down the stream to Ino's hands; then he sinks down among the rushes and kisses the grain-giving earth (5.454–463).

**The bed of leaves.** His last speech opens with the words he spoke when he was left alone in battle, “Ah me, what will become of me?” (5.465, as *Iliad* 11.404, the only other place in Homer). He fears the frost and the dew by the river and the beasts of the wood, and “this seemed to him the better way”: the wood (5.465–475). Under two bushes grown from one spot, one of wild olive and one of olive, so thick that neither wind nor sun nor rain could pass through them, he heaps up a bed of leaves (5.476–485; see [[olive]]). “Much-enduring brilliant Odysseus saw it and rejoiced” (5.486), and he covers himself as a lonely man hides a brand in the black ashes to keep the seed of fire: he keeps his spark of life under the leaves (5.487–491; Merry on 5.488; see [[the-brand-in-the-ashes]]). Athena pours sleep on his eyes (5.491–493; see [[hypnos]]).

**His epithets.** The formula of the book is πολύτλας δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς, “much-enduring brilliant Odysseus” (5.171, 5.354, 5.486), used here for the first time in the Odyssey, as he first speaks. With it come πολύμητις, “of many wiles” (5.214); πολυμήχανος, “of many devices”, with Λαερτιάδης, “son of Laertes” (5.203); διογενής, “sprung from Zeus” (5.203, 5.387); μεγαλήτωρ, “great-hearted” (5.81, 5.149, 5.233); ταλασίφρων, “stout-hearted” (5.31); θεῖος, “divine” (5.11, 5.198); δῖος, “brilliant” (5.269); and δύστηνος, “unhappy” (5.436). See [[much-enduring]], [[of-many-wiles]], [[of-many-devices]], [[son-of-laertes]], [[sprung-from-zeus]], [[great-hearted]], [[stout-hearted]], [[divine]] and [[brilliant]].
