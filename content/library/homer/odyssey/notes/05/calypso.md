summary: Hermes finds Calypso singing at her loom in her cave and receives him as a guest; told the command of Zeus, she calls the gods jealous of goddesses who take mortal husbands, but yields. She bids Odysseus build a raft, swears by Earth, Heaven and the water of Styx that she means him no harm, and on the last evening offers him immortality and sets her beauty against his wife's. Then she gives him the tools and the cloth, bathes and clothes him, stocks his raft and sends him off with a fair wind, the Bear on his left hand (5.14–277).

**In the halls of the nymph.** At the second council Athena speaks of Odysseus “there in the house of the nymph” (5.6), and repeats the verses in which Proteus told Menelaus where he was, “in the halls of the nymph Calypso, who by force / holds him there” (5.14–17 = 4.557–560; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). Zeus sends Hermes to “tell the nymph of the lovely tresses our unerring counsel” (5.30), the verse Athena proposed at the first council (1.86; see [[hermes]] and [[with-lovely-tresses]]): Odysseus is to go home on a raft, “with the escort neither of gods nor of mortal men” (5.32–33; see [[raft]]).

**At her loom.** Hermes finds her within her great cave. A fire of cedar and thyine wood burns on the hearth, and its scent spreads over the island, “and she within, singing with a lovely voice, / walked to and fro at the loom and wove with a golden shuttle” (5.59–62; see [[fire]], [[loom]] and [[weaving]]). The κερκίς is the shuttle rather than the rod that beats the threads close (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.62). Circe is found in the same way, singing with a lovely voice as she walks to and fro at her great immortal loom (10.221–222; see [[circe]]). Around the cave are the wood, the roosting birds, the vine, the four springs and the meadows at which even an immortal would gaze (5.63–75; see [[calypsos-cave]]).

**The welcome.** She knows Hermes as soon as she looks him in the face (5.77–78), “for the gods are not unknown to one another, / the immortals, not even if one lives in a house far away” (5.79–80). The scholia say that she knew him not by having seen him before but by a divine power, and Merry takes the last words as a description of her own case, living in her distant isle (on 5.79; see [[the-gods-are-not-unknown-to-one-another]] and [[ogygia]]). She seats him on a shining, glossy throne and greets him in the words with which Thetis is greeted in the house of Hephaestus: “Why, Hermes of the golden wand, have you come to me, / respected and dear? Before now you have not come often” (5.87–88, as *Iliad* 18.385–386, 18.424–425). She promises to do what he asks, “if I can accomplish it and if it is a thing that can be accomplished” (5.89–90 = *Iliad* 14.195–196, 18.426–427), and sets a table of ambrosia and mixes red nectar (5.92–93; see [[visit-scene]], [[of-the-golden-wand]], [[xenia]], [[ambrosia]] and [[nectar]]).

**The jealousy of the gods.** Hermes tells his errand: Zeus says that there is with her a man more wretched than all the others who fought at Troy, and bids her send him away with all speed, since it is his fate to see his friends and reach his home (5.97–115). She shudders, and answers:

{{quote:19761fa6-0a13-5731-bcaa-74165ddb7d13}}

Merry keeps ζηλήμονες, “jealous”, against the variant δηλήμονες of the Iliad's verse (*Iliad* 24.33): there the gods were destructive in permitting the outrage on Hector's body; here they are jealous of mortals favored by goddesses, as the next verse explains (on 5.118; see the translation's note on 5.118). He hears irony in her words: the gods wink at the amours of goddesses, but will not endure that they raise a mortal to the honorable position of ἀκοίτης, husband (on 5.120). Her examples are Dawn, whose Orion Artemis killed in Ortygia, and Demeter, whose Iasion Zeus struck with his thunderbolt (5.121–128; see [[dawn-and-orion]], [[demeter-and-iasion]], [[mythological-paradigm]] and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]). “And so now again you begrudge me, you gods, that a mortal man should be with me” (5.129). She saved him astride the keel, alone, when Zeus struck and shattered his ship with a thunderbolt (5.130–134; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]); her last two verses there are the ones Hermes has just used after the storm that Athena raised against the Achaeans, where Merry would strike them out, since the wreck was the work of Zeus (5.110–111; Merry on 5.108; see the translation's note on 5.105–111). “Him I loved and cared for, and I said / that I would make him immortal and ageless all his days” (5.135–136; see [[immortal-and-ageless]]).

**She yields.** Since there is no way that the mind of Zeus can be evaded or made vain (5.137–138 = 5.103–104; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]), “let him go to his ruin” over the barren sea; but she cannot send him herself, for she has “no ships with oars and no companions” (5.139–142), the words in which Athena had told of his plight (5.16–17). πέμψω expresses her inability rather than her refusal to obey (Merry on 5.140). She will give him counsel “with a ready heart” and not hide it (5.143–144). Hermes bids her “have regard for the wrath of Zeus” (5.146; see [[menis]]), and goes.

**The oath.** She finds Odysseus weeping on the shore (5.151–158; see [[odysseus]]). She says nothing of Hermes or of Zeus' command: she bids him stop mourning, “for now with a ready heart I will send you away” (5.160–161), and tells him to cut beams and build a broad raft, which she will stock with bread, water and red wine and send off with a fair wind, “if the gods are willing, who hold the wide sky, / who are stronger than I am both to devise and to fulfill” (5.162–170; see [[provisions]]). When he tells the story to Arete he will not know whether she sent him at a message from Zeus or because her own mind had changed (7.261–263). He shudders and suspects some other plan, and will not set foot on the raft unless she swears (5.171–179). She smiles, strokes him with her hand and calls him by name (5.180–181; see [[smiling-at-him]] and [[called-him-by-name]]), as Athena will smile at him and stroke him when he tries a false tale on her in Ithaca (13.287–295). “Truly you are a rogue, and no fool” (5.182): Merry hears a sportive tone, as she laughs at the prudent way in which he takes care of himself; full of craft himself, he suspects a crafty intention in everyone else (on 5.182). Then she swears:

{{quote:b0d30078-c674-5e77-b1ec-f0eeda0b7bf9}}

The first three verses are Hera's oath to Zeus (*Iliad* 15.36–38; see [[styx]], [[oath]] and [[oath-formulas]]), and in the last two she sets her own temper against that of the gods who have ordered his departure (Merry on 5.190; see [[pity]]).

**The last evening.** In the cave Odysseus sits on the throne from which Hermes rose, and the nymph sets before him the food “such as mortal men eat”, while her slave women set ambrosia and nectar beside her (5.194–199; see [[feast-type-scene]]). Merry reports Hayman's remark that now, with no hope of keeping her lover, she feeds him with mortal food, though θεῷ ὥς, the narrator's word for the care he had in her house (8.453), may mean no more than that he was royally treated (on 5.136; see the translation's note on 5.136); she herself still takes nectar and ambrosia (on 5.199). Then she speaks:

{{quote:36ba93b0-7376-5c72-aa20-f8aa827f10f2}}

σὺ δὲ χαῖρε καὶ ἔμπης, “Then farewell to you, even so”: her first anger may have wished his ruin, and now tenderer feelings come back on her (Merry on 5.205); σὺν ἐμοί goes with φυλάσσοις, “help me to keep” this house (on 5.208). Merry points to these verses, from 5.208 on, for the kind of “gentle and wheedling talk” with which, as Athena told the gods, she bewitched him (on 1.56; 1.56–57). She stakes her claim on form and stature, and he grants it and answers that he longs for home (5.215–224; see [[odysseus]] and [[penelope]]). Then “the sun went down and darkness came on”, and in the inner part of the hollow cave the two of them “took their pleasure in love, staying by each other” (5.225–227; see [[philotes]]).

**Her gifts.** At dawn she puts on a great silver-white mantle, a golden girdle and a head covering, in the verses of Circe's robing (5.230–232 = 10.543–545; see [[dressing-scene]], [[girdle]] and [[veil]]). “And then she planned the escort for great-hearted Odysseus” (5.233), the πομπή he had doubted (5.173). She gives him a bronze axe sharpened on both sides, with an olive-wood handle, and a polished adze, leads him to the far edge of the island where the tall trees grow, and goes home (5.234–242); later she brings augers, and cloth to make sails (5.246, 5.258–259; see [[axe]] and [[the-building-of-the-raft]]). On the fifth day “brilliant Calypso sent him from the island” (5.263): τῷ δʼ ἄρα πέμπτῳ πέμπʼ is a play of sound (Merry on 4.754). She bathes him and clothes him in fragrant garments, puts on board a skin of dark wine, a great skin of water, provisions and many meats, and sends “a fair wind, harmless and warm” (5.264–268), as she had promised (5.165–167; see [[provisions]] and [[the-fair-wind]]). She has told him how to steer, keeping the Bear on his left hand as he crossed the sea (5.276–277; see [[the-bear]]): Merry supposes him to be steering generally eastward (on 5.273).

**After the parting.** Her words and her gifts go with him into the storm. When Poseidon's waves rise he fears “that everything the goddess said was true”, that on the sea he would fill up the measure of sorrows before he reached his native land (5.300–302, from 5.206–207); and “the garments that brilliant Calypso had given him” weigh him down in the sea, until he strips them off to swim with Ino's veil (5.321, 5.372; see [[leucothea]]).

**Her epithets.** Nine times she is Καλυψώ, δῖα θεάων, “Calypso, brilliant among goddesses” (5.78–276), twice δῖα θεάων alone (5.159, 5.192), and three times δῖα Καλυψώ, “brilliant Calypso” (5.263, 5.321, 5.372); she is νύμφη ἐυπλόκαμος, “the nymph of the lovely tresses” (5.30, 5.57–58), and πότνια νύμφη, “the lady nymph” (5.149), and Odysseus addresses her as πότνα θεά, “Lady goddess” (5.215). See [[brilliant-among-goddesses]], [[nymphs]] and [[queenly]].
