---
summary: The North Wind, “born of the clear sky”, is one of the four winds of Poseidon's storm; in the simile of the storm it carries thistles over the plain in late summer; and when Poseidon has gone, Athena binds the other winds and rouses the swift North Wind to carry Odysseus toward the Phaeacians.
---

**In the storm.** When Poseidon has gathered the clouds, “The East Wind and the South Wind clashed together, and the stormy West Wind / and the North Wind, born of the clear sky, rolling up a great wave” (5.295–296; see [[poseidon]], [[eurus]], [[notus]] and [[zephyrus]]). σὺν … ἔπεσον is “dashed together”, as the winds are μισγομένων, “clashing together”, when the mast breaks (5.317; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.295). αἰθρηγενέτης, “born of the clear sky”, is passive, of a land wind that comes down from the heights, though the scholia explained it as bringing fair weather, or cold; the Iliad's form is αἰθρηγενής (15.171, 19.358), and Rhianus and Aristophanes read αἰθρηγενέης here (Merry on 5.296 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 5.296 and [[born-of-the-clear-sky]]).

**The thistles.** The broken raft is carried over the sea “As when the North Wind of late summer carries thistles / over the plain, and they cling thick together” (5.328–329; see [[thistles-in-the-north-wind]]), and then the winds play with it among themselves: “now the South Wind would toss it to the North Wind to carry” (5.331). ὀπωρινός is the season of ὀπώρα, from the rising of the Dog Star to the setting of the Pleiades (Merry on 5.328; see the translation's note on 5.328–329); the North Wind of late summer dries the orchard in a simile of the Iliad (*Iliad* 21.346–347; see [[the-north-wind-dries-the-orchard]]).

**Athena's wind.** After Poseidon has driven home, Athena binds up the paths of the other winds and bids them lie down to rest, “and she roused the swift North Wind, and broke the waves ahead, / until he should come among the Phaeacians, lovers of the oar” (5.385–386; see [[athena]] and [[phaeacians]]). For two nights and two days Odysseus drifts on the swollen wave, and on the third day the wind falls and there is a calm (5.388–392). The wind that helped to raise the storm is the one wind left to carry him through it, as in the Iliad the breath of the North Wind revives the fainting Sarpedon (*Iliad* 5.697–698).
