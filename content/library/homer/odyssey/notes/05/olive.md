# φυλίης "of wild olive" (5.477), found only here; ἐλάινον "of olive wood" (5.236), which is also the olive stake of the
# Cyclops (9.382).

**The handle of the axe.** For the raft Calypso gives Odysseus “a great axe that fitted his hands, / of bronze, sharpened on both sides; and in it / a beautiful handle of olive wood, well fitted” (5.234–236; see [[axe]] and [[the-building-of-the-raft]]). Peisander's axe in the Iliad has the same: “a fine / axe of good bronze, on a handle of olive wood” (*Iliad* 13.611–612). The Cyclops' club, which Odysseus will make into a stake, is a trunk of green olive (9.320, 9.382).

**The two bushes.** Cast up at the mouth of the river, Odysseus goes to the forest and finds it near the water “in a place open to view; and he crept under two bushes / that grew from the same spot, one of wild olive, one of olive” (5.476–477; see [[trees]]). ἐν περιφαινομένῳ is “in a clearing”, ground with an open space around it, as Circe's house stands in a place with a view all round (10.211; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.476; see [[in-a-place-with-a-view]]). ἐξ ὁμόθεν, “from the same spot”, Merry would even render “from the same root”, since Phaeacia was a land of marvel (on 5.477). The two plants are φυλίη and ἐλαίη. φυλίη occurs only here; some commentators, Merry reports, took it for a kind of evergreen thorn, still called φυλίκη on Corfu, but it is generally and best taken, with Eustathius, the scholia and Dioscorides, as the wild olive, ἀγριέλαιος; Hesychius adds the explanations “a kind of fig” and “a tree like the holm oak” (on 5.477; see the translation's note on 5.476–477). The scholia report that the commentaries had γεγαῶτας, and Merry takes πεφυῶτας for a correction by Aristarchus (Merry's critical note on 5.477).

**The shelter.** The bushes are so thick that “neither the wet might of the blowing winds could pass, / nor did the shining sun ever strike them with its rays, / nor could the rain pass right through them; so thick / they grew, intertwined with one another” (5.478–481). The same three verses describe the thicket on Parnassus where the great boar lay that gave Odysseus his scar, and the same half-verse its litter of leaves (19.439–443; see [[the-scar-of-odysseus]]). Under the olives Odysseus heaps up a bed of the leaves, “enough to protect two men or three / in the winter season, however hard it might rage”, and covers himself with them as a man hides a brand in the black ashes (5.482–491; see [[bed]] and [[the-brand-in-the-ashes]]). On the shore of Ithaca he will sit with Athena at the foot of another olive, at the head of the harbor of Phorcys (13.372–373), and his marriage bed is built on the trunk of an olive (23.183–204).
