---
summary: On the morning of the building Odysseus puts on a cloak and a tunic, and Calypso a great silver-white mantle, in the verses with which Circe will dress (5.229–230 = 10.543); the cloth for the sails is φάρεα, the plural of the mantle's word (5.258).
---

**The cloak named first.** “At once Odysseus put on a cloak and a tunic” (5.229; see [[tunic]] and [[dressing-scene]]). The order looks unnatural, since the tunic must go on before the cloak, but W. W. Merry finds it natural: the cloak is named first because it strikes the eye more than the undergarment (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.229, comparing 3.467 and 10.365; see the translation's note on 5.229). In the same way the bath is named after the clothing when Calypso sends him off, “when she had clothed him in fragrant garments and bathed him” (5.264; Merry on 5.264; see [[bathing]]).

**The goddess' mantle.** “and the nymph herself put on a great silver-white mantle, / fine and lovely” (5.230–231; see [[calypso]] and [[silver-white]]). The verses dress Circe in the same words on the morning when Odysseus leaves her for the house of Hades (10.543–545), and they go on to the girdle and the head covering (see [[girdle]] and [[veil]]). φᾶρος is the great mantle of kings and goddesses (see the entry). Its plural names cloth of another use: Calypso “brought cloth / to make sails” (φάρεʼ, 5.258–259; see [[sails]] and the translation's row φάρεα).

**Garments that weigh him down.** Calypso had promised to clothe him (5.167), and on the fifth day she sends him off in fragrant garments (5.264). In the storm “the garments weighed him down that brilliant Calypso had given him” (5.321), and before he swims he strips them off, as Ino bade him (5.343, 5.372; see [[leucothea]]). Odysseus later tells Arete that Calypso clothed him in immortal garments, and that through his seven years with her he wet the immortal clothes she gave him with his tears (7.259–260, 7.265, a plain rendering).
