**The mortal wife.** Calypso does not name her. Odysseus would stay and be immortal, she says, “for all your longing to see / your wife, for whom you yearn always, all your days”, and she claims to be in no way inferior to her in form or stature, “since it is in no way fitting / for mortal women to vie with immortals in form and looks” (5.209–213; see [[calypso]]). Odysseus names her, with her epithet:

{{quote:f61bdf60-6f54-5400-abab-390c33b21729}}

Calypso lays all the stress on Penelope's beauty; Odysseus has the tact to speak only of the wisdom of his wife, περίφρων, “prudent”, and when he tells of his homesickness he speaks only of his home and says no more of Penelope (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.216; see [[periphron]] and [[odysseus]]). ἀκιδνοτέρη, “less”, is of uncertain meaning, “weaker” or “meaner” in the scholia, and μέγεθος, “size”, always comes into the Greek idea of beauty (Merry on 5.217; see the translation's note on 5.216–217). The suitor Eurymachus will praise her for both, her form and stature and the mind within her (18.248–249).

**Do not be angry.** μή μοι τόδε χώεο, “do not be angry with me for this”, which Odysseus says to the goddess (5.215), is found once more in Homer, in Penelope's own words to him when she has tested him with the bed: let him not be angry that she did not welcome him as soon as she saw him (23.213–214; Merry on 5.215 compares the verse). In the same speech she blames the gods, who begrudged the two of them to stay by each other and enjoy their youth (23.210–212): ἀγάσαντο is the verb of Calypso's complaint against the gods (5.119, 5.122, 5.129), and παρʼ ἀλλήλοισι μένοντε repeats the words of the last night in the cave, when Odysseus and Calypso took their pleasure in love, “staying by each other” (5.227). He tells her at last of Calypso's offer, which never persuaded his heart (23.333–337; see [[immortal-and-ageless]]).
