---
summary: About to drown, Odysseus wishes he had died at Troy, for then he would have had his funeral rites and the Achaeans would have spread his fame; “but now it was fated that I be caught by a wretched death” (5.308–312).
refs: [5.308-312]
---

Telemachus' grief in Book 1 was that his father had no tomb at Troy to give him fame (1.236–241); in Book 5 Odysseus says it of himself. The storm has broken over the raft, and he calls the Danaans who died at Troy blessed: would that he too had died on the day the Trojans hurled their spears at him around the dead son of Peleus, “Then I would have had my funeral rites, and the Achaeans would have spread my fame; / but now it was fated that I be caught by a wretched death” (5.308–312; see [[three-times-blessed]] and [[the-fight-over-the-body-of-achilles]]). The κτέρεα and the κλέος go together: the rites the Achaeans would have paid him, and the fame they would have carried home, or the report of him they would have brought (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.311; see [[kleos]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). The man lost at sea has neither, and no mound. The shade of Agamemnon will tell the shade of Achilles of the funeral that Odysseus envies: the washing and the lament, the burning, and the great mound heaped on a jutting headland by the Hellespont, “so that it might be seen far off from the sea by men, / both those who live now and those who will be hereafter” (24.43–84, a plain rendering; see [[achilles]] and [[tomb]]).
