summary: The gods keep the rule of the house among themselves: Calypso seats Hermes on a throne, greets him in the words with which the goddesses welcome Thetis in the Iliad, and sets ambrosia and nectar before him before she hears his errand (5.85–96). Her hospitality to Odysseus ends with mortal food, a bath, garments, provisions and a fair wind, and Zeus foretells the welcome of the Phaeacians, who will honor him like a god and send him home with gifts (5.36–38, 5.194–201, 5.263–268).

**A god's welcome.** Hermes comes to the cave, and Calypso knows him at sight (5.77–80; see [[the-gods-are-not-unknown-to-one-another]]). She seats him on a shining throne before she says a word, and her greeting is the one with which Charis and Hephaestus receive Thetis in the Iliad (*Iliad* 18.385–387, 18.424–427; see [[visit-scene]] and [[throne]]):

{{quote:78a3775d-3ded-5d5a-bb9b-951456cda2fb}}

αἰδοῖός τε φίλος τε is “an honoured and welcome guest” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.88). The last verse, ξείνια, “good cheer”, is Charis' invitation (*Iliad* 18.387), and it does not fit here: it is wanting in the best manuscripts, and Hermes is already seated (5.86). Merry thinks it interpolated from the Iliad, unless the guest is bidden to move from the reception room to the dining room (Merry's critical note on 5.91; see the translation's note on 5.91).

**Food before the errand.** The order is the order of every welcome in the poem: the guest eats first and speaks after. Calypso sets the table with ambrosia and mixes red nectar, and only “when he had dined and satisfied his heart with food, / then at last he answered her with words and said” (5.92–96; see [[feast-type-scene]], [[table]], [[ambrosia]] and [[nectar]]). So Telemachus promised the stranger in Book 1 that he would tell his need when he had tasted the meal, and Nestor waited to question his guests until they had eaten their fill (1.123–124, 3.69–70). Hermes' answer has the courtesy of the welcome in it: κέλεαι γάρ, “for you bid me”, is added because the message is unwelcome, and he would not tell it if he were not bidden (Merry on 5.98). He has come unwillingly over the salt water, where there is no city of men to offer the gods sacrifices (5.99–102).

**Calypso's guest.** Odysseus has been her guest for seven years (7.259), saved when he came astride the keel, loved and cared for (5.130–136; see [[calypso]]). On the last evening he sits on the throne from which Hermes rose, and she sets before him “all kinds of food, / to eat and to drink, such as mortal men eat”, while her slave women set ambrosia and nectar beside her (5.195–199). Merry reports Hayman's remark that now that Calypso has no hope of keeping him she feeds him with mortal food, where until then, as Odysseus' bath among the Phaeacians recalls, he had been cared for constantly θεῷ ὥς, “like a god” (8.453); though θεῷ ὥς may mean no more than that he was royally treated (Merry on 5.136). On the fifth day she sends him away as a host sends a guest, bathed and clothed in fragrant garments, with wine, water and provisions on board, and a fair wind behind him (5.263–268; see [[bathing]], [[provisions]] and [[guest-gifts]]).

**The welcome to come.** Zeus foretells the hospitality that will end the wanderings: the Phaeacians “will honor him with all their hearts like a god, / and will send him in a ship to his dear native land, / giving him bronze and gold in plenty, and clothing”, more than he would have brought from Troy (5.36–40; see [[phaeacians]]). The first host he meets on Scheria is a river, and he comes to it as a suppliant, for a wanderer is to be respected even by the gods (5.445–450; see [[the-river-of-scheria]] and [[supplication]]).
