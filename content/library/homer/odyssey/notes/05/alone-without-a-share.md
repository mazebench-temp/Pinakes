---
summary: The Bear that alone never bathes in Ocean is the star by which Odysseus steers from Calypso's island, keeping it on his left hand as she bade him.
passages:
  - 5.270-277 | Odysseus steers by the stars
---

The three verses on the Bear (5.273–275) are those of the shield of Achilles (*Iliad* 18.487–489), and the translation keeps their English; the text here prints ἄμαξαν, “wagon”, where the Iliad has the name Ἄμαξα (see the translation's note on 5.273–275 and [[the-bear]]). On the shield the stars are a picture; here they are a course. Odysseus sits at the steering oar without sleeping, watching the Pleiades, Boötes and the Bear, “For this was the one Calypso, brilliant among goddesses, had bidden him / keep on his left hand as he crossed the sea” (5.276–277). The Bear never sets and is in the sky all night, and so the verse that the critics attacked also tells why it is the steersman's guide. With the Bear on his left hand, Merry explains, Odysseus is sailing east. He supposes the season to be about the autumn equinox: near midnight Boötes would be setting in the north-west and the Pleiades rising in the south-east, with Orion an hour or two after them, so that the Bear, its head turned toward the rising Orion, seems to the poet to be watching the hunter (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.273; see [[pleiades]] and [[orion]]).

**The critics.** Merry grants that the Bear is not in fact the only constellation that never sets, and quotes Aristotle's answer, that “alone” is said by metaphor, “for the best known is alone” (*Poetics* 1461a20–21). Strabo's defense, that Homer means by the Bear the whole arctic circle (*Geography* 1.1.6), is refuted, Merry thinks, by these very verses, which make the Bear turn to face Orion; Strabo himself speaks of the Bear “which he also calls the Wagon and says watches Orion”. Merry reports that Heliodorus, quoted in Apollonius' Homeric Lexicon, admitted that Homer said it through ignorance, and that Sir G. C. Lewis thought the Great Bear the only group of stars in the northern sky that had been made into a constellation in Homer's time; and that Callimachus gave the verse a myth, Tethys refusing Callisto, the Bear, a refuge in her waters out of regard for Hera (Merry on 5.275). Lewis's answer is close to the one Strabo gives to another charge, that Homer knew one Bear instead of two: the other, Strabo says, had probably not yet been made a constellation, and came to the Greeks only after the Phoenicians had marked it out and used it for sailing (*Geography* 1.1.6; see [[strabo]]).
