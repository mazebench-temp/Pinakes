---
summary: Menelaus rebukes his attendant, “You were no fool, Eteoneus, son of Boethous, / before now; but now you babble folly like a child” (4.31–32); Eidothea asks Menelaus, “Are you so utterly a fool, stranger” (4.371); and Penelope fears for a son who is νήπιος, “a child, knowing nothing well either of toils or of assemblies” (4.818).
---

νήπιος is the child and the man who does not see like a grown man. Eteoneus is called a child for asking whether to send the strangers away (4.31–32; see [[eteoneus]] and [[xenia]]); Eidothea calls Menelaus a fool for staying on Pharos, and Merry renders “Art thou an utter fool, sir stranger, and spiritless? or choosest thou thus to be reckless, and takest pleasure in sorrow?” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.371). Penelope's son is νήπιος in years and in experience, “knowing nothing well either of toils or of assemblies” (4.818; see [[mothers-and-sons]] and [[ponos]]).
