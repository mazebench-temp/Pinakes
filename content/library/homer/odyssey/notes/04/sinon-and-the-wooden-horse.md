**The answer to Helen.** Menelaus agrees that all she has said is fair and right, and adds that in all his travels he has never seen a heart like that of stout-hearted Odysseus (4.266–270). Then he begins his own tale with her verse, οἷον καὶ τόδʼ ἔρεξε καὶ ἔτλη καρτερὸς ἀνήρ, “What a thing this too was that the strong man did and endured” (4.271 = 4.242; see [[odysseus-enters-troy-as-a-beggar]]). Her story was of Odysseus in Troy and of her own help to him; his is of Odysseus in the horse, and of Helen at its side.

**The horse.** ἵππῳ ἔνι ξεστῷ, “in the polished horse” (4.272), is the hollow ambush, κοῖλον λόχον (4.277), as Demodocus will call it when he sings how the Achaeans poured out of it, “leaving the hollow ambush” (8.515, a plain rendering; see [[ambush]]). The verse of the men inside, Ἀργείων Τρώεσσι φόνον καὶ κῆρα φέροντες, “of the Argives, bringing slaughter and doom to the Trojans” (4.273), is repeated in his song (8.513).

**A god's prompting.** “Some god must have bidden you, / a god who meant to hold out glory to the Trojans” (4.274–275; see [[daimon]] and [[kydos]]). κελευσέμεναι is an aorist infinitive, Merry says, and the god is needed to excuse her: she came from curiosity, not to betray the men, but had she tempted them to answer, the Trojans would have won the glory of the victory (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.274). He observes that the act is hard to square with the remorse she has just professed, unless as a heedless longing to hear the voices of her own people once more (on 4.264). “And godlike Deiphobus followed you as you went” (4.276; see [[deiphobus]] and [[godlike]]): some rejected the verse already in antiquity, Merry thinks because it seemed to point to the later legend that Deiphobus married Helen after the death of Paris (Merry's critical note on 4.276; his note on the verse cites Euripides, *Trojan Women* 959–960).

**The voices.** “Three times you walked around the hollow ambush, feeling it all over, / and you called out by name the best of the Danaans, / making your voice like the voices of the wives of all the Argives” (4.277–279; see [[danaans]] and [[argives]]). ἀλόχοισιν, “the wives”, is a short way of saying “the voices of the wives”, and Aristarchus wrote the verb εἴσκουσʼ (Merry on 4.279 and his critical note). Such mimicry, Merry thinks, points to a special inspiration, like that of the Delian maidens, who “know how to imitate the voices and the clatter of all men”, so that each would say he was speaking himself (on 4.264; *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* 162–164, a plain rendering).

**Odysseus restrains them.** Menelaus, Diomedes and Odysseus sat in the middle of the horse (4.280–281; see [[diomedes]]). The two of them were eager to go out or to answer at once from inside, αἶψʼ ὑπακοῦσαι, “instantly to answer” (Merry on 4.283), “but Odysseus held us back and checked us, eager though we were” (4.284). In the eleventh book Odysseus tells the shade of Achilles that he was in charge of the horse, to open and to close the ambush, and that Neoptolemus alone never paled or wept inside it (11.523–532, a plain rendering; see [[neoptolemus]]).

**Anticlus.** “Then all the other sons of the Achaeans were silent, / and Anticlus alone wanted to answer you / with words; but Odysseus pressed upon his mouth with his hands, / unceasingly, with his strong hands, and saved all the Achaeans, / and held him so until Pallas Athena led you away” (4.285–289; see [[anticlus]], [[sons-of-the-achaeans]], [[silence]] and [[athena]]). Hardly any copy had the five verses, and Aristarchus rejected them because the poet never mentions Anticlus in the Iliad; a scholium says that Anticlus comes from the Cycle (Merry's critical note and his note on 4.285; see the translation's note on 4.285–289). Merry adds that 4.285 and 4.286 agree ill with 4.282, that Pallas Athena agrees ill with the nameless god of 4.275, and τόφρα δʼ ἔχʼ, “held him so”, with ἔσχεθεν, “checked us” (4.284); Nitzsch would keep the verses, since “and saved all the Achaeans” is the great deed that the opening verse promised, and Merry prints them in brackets (on 4.285). ἐπὶ μάστακα χερσί, “upon his mouth with his hands” (4.287), returns only once in Homer, when Eurycleia tells Penelope that Odysseus took her by the mouth with his hands and would not let her speak, after she had known him by his scar (23.75–77, a plain rendering; see [[odysseus-recognized-by-his-nurse]]).

**Telemachus' answer.** The two stories are told to the son, and he takes them as praise of his father that comes too late: “it is all the harder; for none of this kept grim destruction from him, / not even if the heart within him had been of iron” (4.292–293; see [[telemachus]]).
