---
summary: Argos is the homeland far from which the men perished at Troy, the realm where Menelaus would have given Odysseus a city, and the land where Menelaus is not fated to die; in Penelope's lament, twice, Odysseus' fame is wide “through Hellas and the heart of Argos” (4.99, 4.174–177, 4.562, 4.726, 4.816).
---

**Horse-pasturing Argos.** Menelaus would be content with a third of his wealth if the men were safe “who perished then / in wide Troy, far from horse-pasturing Argos” (4.97–99; see [[troy]] and [[horse-pasturing]]): Argos is the home of the whole army, as in the Iliad, where the Achaeans hope to return to it (*Iliad* 2.115). Proteus uses the same epithet of Menelaus' own home: “it is not decreed / to die and meet your fate in horse-pasturing Argos”, for the immortals will send him to the Elysian plain (4.561–563; see [[elysium]] and [[menelaus]]).

**A city in Argos.** “And I would have given him a city in Argos to dwell in, and built him a house, / bringing him from Ithaca with his possessions and his son / and all his people, emptying one of the cities / that lie round about and are ruled by me myself” (4.174–177; see [[odysseus]] and [[ithaca]]). The Argos of this wish is Menelaus' own realm in the Peloponnese, and the city would be one of his. Merry describes the cities “that lie round about” as the towns of dependent neighbors, whose people would be found homes elsewhere, for ἐξαλαπάξας means emptying a city of its people, not pulling it down (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.177). He takes the offer for a courtesy of a kind never meant to be claimed, like the seven cities among Agamemnon's gifts to Achilles (*Iliad* 9.149–153; Merry on 4.174; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]); Nitzsch, whom he quotes, found the idea of kingship in it more Oriental than Achaean. Merry sets the dative Ἄργεϊ, “in Argos”, beside Αἰγύπτῳ, “in Egypt” (4.351; on 4.352; see [[egypt]]).

**The heart of Argos.** Penelope's lament, when Medon has told her of the plot, recalls her husband, “a brave man, whose fame is wide through Hellas and the heart of Argos” (4.726), and she says the same verse to the phantom of her sister (4.816; see [[penelope]] and [[iphthime]]). It is the verse of the first book (1.344), a phrase for the whole of Greece (see [[hellas]]); the scholia called it superfluous here (see the translation's note on 4.726).
