summary: Telemachus asks Menelaus to send them to bed, and the slave women set beds beneath the colonnade; the guests sleep in the porch, Menelaus in the inner part of the house with Helen beside him, and at dawn he rises from his bed (4.294–307). Menelaus says of the suitors that they wished to lie in the bed of a fierce-hearted man, and likens them to fawns laid in a lion's lair (4.333–340); Penelope reproaches her women for not rousing her from her bed (4.730).

**Beds for the guests.** After Helen's and Menelaus' stories Telemachus asks, “But come, send us to bed, so that now / we may lie down and take our fill of sweet sleep” (4.294–295), and Helen bids the slave women “set beds beneath the colonnade” with blankets, rugs and woolly cloaks; they go out with torches and spread them, and a herald leads the strangers out (4.296–301; see [[blankets]], [[cloak]], [[torches]] and [[heralds]]). The verses are those of the beds Achilles had made for Priam and his herald (*Iliad* 24.643–648; see [[feast-type-scene]]). δέμνια, always plural in Homer, properly means the bedstead, W. W. Merry notes, but θέμεναι, “set”, shows that something portable is meant here (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.297).

**The master's bed.** The guests sleep in the porch, “but the son of Atreus slept in the inner part of the high house, / and beside him lay Helen of the long robes, brilliant among women” (4.304–305; see [[house]], [[menelaus]] and [[helen]]), as Nestor slept with his wife beside him in Book 3 (3.402–403). It is this marriage that Proteus makes the ground of Menelaus' fate: he will not die in Argos but go to the Elysian plain, “because you have Helen as your wife” (4.569; see [[elysium]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]). At dawn “Menelaus, good at the war cry, rose from his bed” (4.307), as Nestor rose from his bed in Book 3 (3.405).

**The bed of a fierce-hearted man.** When Telemachus has told him of the suitors, Menelaus answers: “Ah, truly it was in the bed of a fierce-hearted man / that they wished to lie, weaklings as they are” (4.333–334; see [[the-suitors]]). The bed is Odysseus' marriage bed, and the simile that follows turns it into a lion's lair, εὐνή again, in which a hind has laid her fawns to sleep, to their death when he comes home (4.335–340; see [[the-hind-in-the-lions-lair]] and [[lion]]).

**Penelope's bed.** Penelope reproaches her women that none of them thought “to rouse me from my bed” when her son went on board the ship (4.729–731; see [[handmaids]]). That night she lies in her upper room without food, and sleep comes to her in the middle of her pondering (4.787–794; see [[upper-room]]).
