---
summary: The sea of Book 4 is the sea of Menelaus' tale and of the suitors' ambush: the broad back of the sea that the travelers cross, the island in the sea of many waves where the gods held Menelaus, the Old Man who knows its depths and comes out of it at midday with his seals, the great gulf that swallowed the Locrian Ajax, and the strait where the suitors lie in wait (4.313, 4.354–362, 4.385–406, 4.499–511, 4.669–671, 4.842–847).
# πόντου (4.436), the sea from which Eidothea brings the sealskins.
grc: [πόντου]
---

**The sea between.** Menelaus asks what need has brought Telemachus to Lacedaemon “over the broad back of the sea” (4.313), and the winds he lacked on Pharos are “the winds that are the escorts / of ships over the broad back of the sea” (4.361–362; see [[broad-back-of-the-sea]] and [[pharos]]). The island lies “in the sea of many waves” (4.354; see [[of-many-waves]]). The way home is a way “over the fish-filled sea” (4.381, 4.390, 4.424, 4.470; see [[fish-filled]]), sailed “over the wine-dark sea” (4.474; see [[wine-dark]]), and the way back to Egypt is over “the misty sea” (4.482; see [[misty]]). Odysseus, Proteus says, has no ships and no companions “who might take him over the broad back of the sea” (4.560; see [[calypso]]).

**The sea of the Old Man.** Proteus “knows the depths / of the whole sea”, as Atlas does in the first book (4.385–386; 1.52–53; see [[proteus]] and [[atlas]]). At midday he comes out of the sea under the breath of the West Wind, “hidden in the dark ripple” (4.400–402; see [[the-ripple-of-the-west-wind]]), and his seals come up “out of the gray sea, / breathing out the bitter smell of the deep sea” (4.405–406; see [[seals]], [[gray-sea]] and [[deep-sea]]). His daughter and he leave the same way: “So saying, she plunged beneath the billowing sea” (4.425), and he too (4.570; see [[eidothea]] and [[billowing]]). Eidothea fetches the sealskins from the sea, “having plunged into the broad bosom of the sea” (4.435–436).

**The sea that kills.** The Locrian Ajax boasted that against the will of the gods he had escaped “the great gulf of the sea” (4.504), and when Poseidon split the rock, the piece that bore him “carried him down into the boundless billowing sea. / So he perished there, when he had drunk the salt water” (4.510–511; see [[the-death-of-ajax-son-of-oileus]], [[gyrae]] and [[poseidon]]). Merry defends the last verse, which the scholia called cheap and Nitzsch rejected, for its grim irony: one moment the boaster sits secure on the rock, the next he swallows salt water and dies (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.510–511; see the translation's note on 4.511).

**The sea of the ambush.** In Ithaca Penelope cannot see why her son should have gone on board ships, “which are horses of the sea / for men, and cross over the wide waters” (4.708–709; see [[ships]]), and she fears for him “either in the land of the people where he has gone, or on the sea” (4.821). The suitors' ship goes out “over the watery ways” to “a rocky island in the middle of the sea”, and waits there (4.842–847; see [[watery-ways]], [[asteris]] and [[the-suitors]]).
