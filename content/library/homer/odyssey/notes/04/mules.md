---
summary: The mules of Book 4 are the young of Noemon's twelve mares in Elis, “hard-working mules, / unbroken”, still at the teat; he needs his ship back to cross over and break one in (4.634–637).
---

**Mules at the teat.** Noemon, son of Phronius, who lent Telemachus his ship, asks Antinous when it will be back, since he needs it “to cross over to Elis with its wide dancing floors, where I have horses, / twelve mares, and at their teats hard-working mules, / unbroken; one of these I would drive off and break in” (4.634–637; see [[noemon-son-of-phronius]], [[elis]] and [[horses]]). ὑπό is “at the teat”, under the mares (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.636): the mules are foals, born of the mares, and not yet broken to work, ἀδμῆτες, though they are already ταλαεργοί, “hard-working”, an epithet that Homer gives only to mules (see [[unbroken]] and [[hard-working]]). In the chariot race of the Iliad the second prize is such a mare, “six years old, unbroken, carrying a mule foal in her womb” (*Iliad* 23.265–266).

**Mules for Ithaca.** Merry observes that Ithaca, as Telemachus tells Menelaus (4.605–608), was no place for breeding horses or mules, so that Noemon kept his paddock in Elis, but that the mule, the beast of the mountains, as he reads its other name οὐρεύς, was well suited to such a country (on 4.635; see [[ithaca]]). The question is harmless, but it tells the suitors that Telemachus has really gone (4.638–640; see [[the-suitors]]). The same verse names the mares and mules that Iphitus had lost when he met Odysseus and gave him his bow (21.22–23).
