summary: Lament for the living as well as the dead. Menelaus comforts his heart with lament for the men lost at Troy, “for satiety of chilling lament comes quickly” (4.102–103), and his words rouse the longing for lament in all at Sparta (4.113, 4.183); in Helen's tale the Trojan women wail for the men Odysseus killed (4.259). In Ithaca Penelope laments on the threshold of her chamber among her whimpering women (4.716–741), until Eurycleia lulls her lament (4.758) and Athena's phantom comes to end it (4.800–801).
# ὀδύρομαι "mourn" (4.100, 4.104, 4.110, 4.194, 4.740, 4.800, 4.819, 4.828), whose ὀδύρεται an Iliad note claims; the
# translation keeps "mourn" apart from γοάω "lament".
grc: [γόῳ, γόοιο, γοόωσαν, ἐκώκυον, "ὀδυρ*"]
en: [lament, wailed]
---

**The pleasure of lament.** Menelaus mourns the men who died at Troy “often, as I sit in our halls— / at one moment I comfort my heart with lament, and at another / I stop; for satiety of chilling lament comes quickly—” (4.101–103; see [[satiety-of-all-things]]). Merry, following Wolf, takes the two verses as a parenthesis between ἀλλʼ ἔμπης and τῶν πάντων (4.100, 4.104), and renders “one moment, 'tis true, I ease my heart with lamentation, and then again I cease, for quickly comes surfeit of freezing grief” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.100). τέρπομαι with γόος is the comfort of mourning (see the translation's table of key words), the comfort Achilles refuses until he has entered the mouth of bloody war (*Iliad* 19.312–313); κρυερὸς γόος, “chilling lament”, is the lament Achilles tells Priam has no profit in it (*Iliad* 24.524). The same evening Menelaus' words rouse the longing for lament in Telemachus, and then in them all (4.113, 4.183; see [[the-longing-for-lament]] and [[tears]]); Peisistratus asks for an end to it after supper, and Menelaus agrees (4.193–215; see [[the-only-prize-of-miserable-mortals]]).

**The Trojan women.** In Helen's tale Odysseus kills many Trojans on his way back from the city, “Then the other Trojan women wailed shrilly; but my heart / rejoiced” (4.259–260; see [[odysseus-enters-troy-as-a-beggar]]). κωκύω is the women's cry of mourning: Thetis wails aloud when she hears her son groan for Patroclus, and Hecuba when she sees her son dragged (*Iliad* 18.35–37, 22.405–407).

**Penelope's lament.** At Medon's news Penelope cannot bear to sit on a chair, but sits on the threshold of her chamber, lamenting piteously, while the slave women whimper around her, young and old (4.716–720; see [[handmaids]]); then, “lamenting without ceasing”, she speaks among them of her husband and her son (4.721–741; see [[penelope]] and [[mothers-and-sons]]). οἴκτρʼ ὀλοφυρομένη, “lamenting piteously”: the same words, in the plural, are said in Book 24 of the daughters of the Old Man of the Sea around the body of Achilles (24.59; see [[pity]]). Her lament is for a son who is alive; she would send old Dolius to Laertes, in the hope that he may come out and mourn before the people (ὀδύρεται, 4.735–741; see [[laertes]]). Eurycleia's confession and counsel “lulled her lament, and checked her eyes from lament” (4.758), and Athena sends the phantom to make Penelope, “mourning and lamenting”, cease from it (4.800–801; see [[eidolon]] and [[iphthime]]).
