summary: Menelaus' tale of Proteus is the center of the book. Held on Pharos for want of hecatombs, he learns from Eidothea that her father, the unerring Old Man of the Sea, comes out of the water at midday to sleep among his seals. Hidden under sealskins, he and three companions seize the old man and hold him while he becomes a lion, a serpent, a leopard, a boar, water and a tree. Then Proteus tells him to go back to the river Aegyptus and sacrifice, tells the deaths of Ajax and Agamemnon and the captivity of Odysseus, and foretells Menelaus' own end in the Elysian plain.

**What the Old Man of the Sea told me.** Telemachus begs Menelaus to tell him truly whatever he knows of his father (4.322–331), and Menelaus answers with an oath of candor and a source: “but what the unerring Old Man of the Sea told me, / of that I will not conceal a word from you, or hide it” (4.349–350; see [[menelaus]] and [[telemachus]]). The whole tale of Egypt that follows (4.351–586) is told to bring out one sentence of the old man's, the news of Odysseus on Calypso's island (4.555–560), and Telemachus repeats the verses to his mother when he is home (17.138–146). Merry supposes that in the story of Proteus we have the poet's adaptation of “some well-known sailors' yarns” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.389; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]).

**Immortal Proteus of Egypt.** Menelaus has been held on Pharos for twenty days, and his men are fishing with hooks for hunger, when a goddess meets him alone (4.351–370; see [[pharos]] and [[eidothea]]). She tells him where his help lies:

{{quote:b6be6fb2-b8d3-599c-b9d7-349b00106fc0}}

πωλεῖται, “often comes”, is said of a habit, and τις, “an”, makes the title a description before it is a name. The old man will tell Menelaus “the way and the measures of your path” and his homecoming, and also “whatever evil and good has been done in your halls” while he has been away (4.389–393; see [[nostos]]). Menelaus' answer, “for a god is hard for a mortal man to overpower” (4.397; see [[a-god-is-hard-for-a-mortal-to-overpower]]), is the reason for the ambush. He has also said that “the gods know all things” (4.379, again 4.468). Merry takes this for a courteous hyperbole, or the theoretical view of the gods, which the poems do not bear out: Proteus, the sea god, knows all the depths of the sea, but is quite witless of the trick devised against him (on 4.379; see [[the-gods-know-all-things]] and [[not-even-the-gods-know-everything]]).

**The wiles of the old man.** His daughter tells his habits. When the sun stands at midday he comes out of the sea “at the breath of the West Wind, hidden in the dark ripple”, and lies down in the hollow caves with his seals sleeping around him (4.400–406; see [[zephyrus]], [[hollow-caves]] and [[seals]]). He will count them first and go his rounds, and when he has counted them off by fives he will lie down in their midst, “like a herdsman among his flocks of sheep” (4.411–413). πεμπάσσεται is properly to count by fives, on the fingers, though Merry thinks the word had already lost that sense, since men in Homer count by tens (on 4.412; see the translation's note on 4.412 and [[numbers-and-counting]]). Merry renders ὀλοφώια, “wiles” (4.410), “sorcerer's arts” (on 4.410). The men must hold him whatever he becomes, and let him go only when he speaks to them in the shape in which they saw him lie down (4.415–424).

**The ambush.** At dawn Menelaus takes three companions to the shore, where Eidothea has scooped out beds in the sand and brought the skins of four seals, “freshly flayed; for she was devising a trick against her father” (4.433–440; see [[dolos]]). The stench of the seals would have made it the most dread of ambushes, had she not put ambrosia under each man's nose (4.441–446; see [[ambrosia]] and [[ambush]]). At midday the old man comes, finds his seals, and counts the men first among his beasts, “and had no thought / in his heart that there was a trick” (4.450–453).

**The changes.** Then they seize him:

{{quote:3beecca9-25e6-5347-8b98-d338b5ab6404}}

His daughter had foretold “all the things that upon the earth / creep, and water, and wondrous fire” (4.417–418). The tale gives four beasts, then water and a tree, and no fire; she spoke of what he could do, and Menelaus tells what he did. Merry: “there was no doubt of his power to take various shapes”, and ἑρπετά is used in the widest sense, of all that moves upon the earth (on 4.417–418). He notes that the power of taking many forms belongs especially to sea and river gods, Nereus, Glaucus and Achelous, and quotes Ovid, in whom Proteus himself advises Peleus to hold Thetis fast through her changes (on 4.419; see [[shape-changing]], [[lion]], [[serpent]], [[leopard]] and [[boar]]). δολίης … τέχνης, “his crafty art” (4.455), is the phrase of Aegisthus' scheme against Agamemnon later in the same speech (δολίην … τέχνην, 4.529; see [[aegisthus]]).

**The questions.** When he grows weary, the old man questions Menelaus in words: “Which of the gods, son of Atreus, helped you to devise this plan” (4.462–463). Menelaus will not be misled (παρατροπέων, 4.465: see the translation's note) and asks what he asked the daughter, which god binds him from his path and how he will go home (4.468–470 = 4.379–381). The answers come one by one. Menelaus should have sacrificed to Zeus and the other gods before he embarked; he will not see his home until he has gone back to “the water of Aegyptus, the river fallen from Zeus”, and offered hecatombs (4.472–480; see [[egypt]] and [[hecatomb]]). Asked of the Achaeans, he warns Menelaus that he will not be long without tears (4.492–494), and tells of the two leaders who perished on the way home and of the one still alive and held back on the wide sea (4.495–498): Ajax, son of Oileus, drowned by Poseidon at the Gyraean rock for his boast (4.499–511; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[poseidon]]), and Agamemnon, saved from the sea by Hera and killed at Aegisthus' feast (4.512–537; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). When Menelaus has wept his fill, the old man tells him to hurry home, for he may find Aegisthus alive, or Orestes may have killed him first (4.543–547; see [[orestes]]). The third man is Odysseus, held by Calypso in her halls (4.555–560; see [[odysseus]] and [[calypso]]). Last comes Menelaus' own end, not death in Argos but the Elysian plain (4.561–569; see [[elysium]] and [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]). Then “he plunged beneath the billowing sea” (4.570), in the verse of his daughter's going (4.425). Menelaus does as he is told: he sails back to the river, sacrifices, heaps up a mound for Agamemnon, and the immortals give him a fair wind home (4.581–586; see [[prophecy]]).
