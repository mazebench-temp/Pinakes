---
summary: After the company has wept for Odysseus and Antilochus, Helen casts the drug into the wine and bids the guests feast and take delight in stories; then she and Menelaus tell of Odysseus at Troy, and no one weeps again that night (4.183–295).
---

**The tears before.** Menelaus' grief for Odysseus “roused the longing for lament” in all of them: Helen, Telemachus and Menelaus wept, and Peisistratus remembered his brother Antilochus (4.183–188; see [[the-longing-for-lament]], [[lament]] and [[antilochus]]). Peisistratus asks that they not weep after supper, though weeping for the dead is “the only prize for miserable mortals” (4.193–198), and Menelaus agrees: “let us leave off the weeping that came before, / and let us remember supper again” (4.212–213; see [[feast]]). Asphalion pours water over their hands and they eat (4.216–218; see [[asphalion]]).

**The drug.** Then Helen “thought of another thing” and cast the drug into the wine from which they were drinking (4.219–220; see [[helen]] and [[thought-of-another-thing]]). Its virtue is told in the conditional of a single day: not even if his mother and father lay dead, not even if his brother or his son were killed before his eyes, would a man who drank it let a tear fall (4.222–226). The cases are the griefs the evening has just heard of, a father lost and a brother killed (4.110–114, 4.186–202). When she has cast it in and bidden them pour the wine, she speaks again: “now feast, sitting in the halls, / and take delight in stories; for I will tell you what is fitting” (4.233–239; see [[mythos]]).

**The stories after.** Over the drugged wine Helen tells how Odysseus came into Troy disguised as a beggar and how she alone knew him, and Menelaus how Odysseus held back the Achaeans in the Horse when she called to them (4.240–289; see [[odysseus]]). Neither story is without grief: Helen speaks of her blind folly and the home she left (4.259–264), and Telemachus answers that it is all the harder, since none of this kept grim destruction from his father (4.291–293). But no one weeps, and the guests ask to go to bed (4.294–295). The drug is not mentioned again. The next morning Menelaus' tale of Proteus brings him to the news of his brother's murder, and he tells how he wept on the sand and had no wish to live (4.538–541; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]).
