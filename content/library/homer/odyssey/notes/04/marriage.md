**Two weddings.** Menelaus gives “a wedding feast to his many kinsmen / for his son and his noble daughter” (4.3–4): Hermione goes to the son of Achilles, as he promised at Troy, “and now the gods were bringing their wedding to pass”, and a bride is brought from Sparta for Megapenthes (4.5–12; see [[the-wedding-at-sparta]], [[hermione-daughter-of-helen]] and [[megapenthes]]). ἤγετο, of a bridegroom bringing home his wife, is used here of the father getting a wife for his son (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.10).

**The son-in-law of Zeus.** Proteus tells Menelaus that he will not die in Argos but be sent to the Elysian plain, “because you have Helen as your wife, and in their eyes you are the son-in-law of Zeus” (4.569; see [[elysium]], [[helen]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]). His marriage is the reason given for his end in the Elysian plain.

**A marriage that will not be.** In Ithaca a suitor says, “Surely a marriage for us the much-wooed queen / is preparing, and does not know that slaughter has been made ready for her son” (4.770–771; see [[the-suitors]], [[penelope]] and [[much-wooed]]). In Books 1 and 2 γάμος was the marriage the suitors pressed on Penelope (1.249, 1.277, 2.97, 2.196, 2.205).

**Wives.** The book names its wives with the Iliad's words: Agamemnon died “by the guile of his ruinous wife” (ἀλόχοιο, 4.92; see [[clytemnestra]]); Alcandre is “the wife of Polybus” (δάμαρ, 4.126) and as “his wife” gave Helen gifts (ἄλοχος, 4.130; see [[alcandre]]); Polydamna is “the wife of Thon” (παράκοιτις, 4.228; see [[polydamna]]); at the Horse Helen made her voice “like the voices of the wives of all the Argives” (ἀλόχοισιν, 4.279; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]]); the banqueters' “wives with lovely veils” send bread (ἄλοχοι, 4.623); and Iphthime is the sister “whom Eumelus had married” (ὄπυιε, 4.798; see [[iphthime]] and [[eumelus]]).
