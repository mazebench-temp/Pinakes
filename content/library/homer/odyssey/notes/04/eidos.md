---
summary: Hermione “had the form of golden Aphrodite” (4.14), and Helen remembers leaving a husband who lacked nothing, “in mind or in form” (4.264). The book's likenesses are told with other verbs: the son like the father (4.141–150), Odysseus like a beggar (4.244–250), Helen's voice like the wives' (4.279), Mentor or a god like him (4.654), and the phantom like Iphthime (4.796).
---

**Form.** εἶδος is the beauty of Helen's daughter, Hermione, ἣ εἶδος ἔχε χρυσέης Ἀφροδίτης, “who had the form of golden Aphrodite” (4.14; see [[hermione-daughter-of-helen]] and [[aphrodite]]), and with φρένες it makes the whole man: Helen's husband “lacked nothing, in mind or in form” (4.264; see [[phrenes]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]).

**Likenesses.** The book is full of likeness, told with ἔοικα, ἐίσκω, ἴσκω and ἴκελος rather than with εἴδομαι. Helen has never seen anyone so like another (ἐοικότα) as this young man is like the son of Odysseus, and Menelaus agrees, “as you liken them” (ἐίσκεις, 4.141–148; Merry on 4.148 traces ἐίσκω from “make like” to “regard as like” and so “believe”, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886; see [[fathers-and-sons]]). Odysseus made himself like a servant and a beggar (ἐοικώς, ἤισκε, ἴκελος, 4.245–249), Helen made her voice like the wives' (ἴσκουσʼ, 4.279), Noemon saw Mentor “or a god, who was like him in every way” (ἐῴκει, 4.654), and Athena's phantom “was like a woman in form” (ἤικτο, 4.796; see [[eidolon]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). Proteus does not take a likeness but becomes each thing in turn (γίγνετο, 4.456–458; see [[proteus]]).
