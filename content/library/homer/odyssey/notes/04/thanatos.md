summary: The book turns on whether Odysseus is alive or dead (4.110, 4.833–837). Peisistratus grants tears to “any mortal who dies” (4.196) and weeps for his dead brother (4.199), Helen's drug holds back tears even for a mother and father dead (4.224), and Menelaus, whose brother was killed at his homecoming (4.512–537), is “not decreed / to die” in Argos (4.561–562). Penelope fears her son's death (4.789) and Eurycleia hopes that Athena may save him “even from death” (4.753).

**Alive or dead.** Menelaus mourns Odysseus because “we know nothing, / whether he is alive or dead” (4.109–110), and the question runs through the book. Menelaus asks Proteus for the third man, “who is still alive and held back on the wide sea, / or dead” (4.552–553), and learns that he lives on Calypso's island (4.555–560; see [[calypso]]). Penelope asks the phantom the same of her husband, whether he still looks on the light of the sun, “or is already dead and in the house of Hades” (4.833–834), and is refused: “whether he is alive or dead; and it is a bad thing to speak empty wind” (4.837; see [[it-is-bad-to-speak-empty-wind]]).

**The dead and the deathless.** Peisistratus' brother Antilochus “is dead, by no means the worst / of the Argives” (4.199–200), and Helen's drug would stop a man's tears “not even if his mother and his father lay dead” (4.224; see [[tears]]). Agamemnon was killed at his own feast (4.534–537; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). Menelaus himself will not die: “But for you, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, it is not decreed / to die and meet your fate in horse-pasturing Argos” (4.561–562; see [[thesphaton]] and [[elysium]]). In Ithaca Penelope says that had she known of the voyage, Telemachus would have stayed, “or he would have left me dead in the halls” (4.734), and she lies wondering “whether her noble son would escape death” (4.789; see [[mothers-and-sons]]); Eurycleia trusts that Athena “might save him even from death” (4.753).
