summary: Menelaus' house is full of gifts given by hosts abroad: silver bathtubs, tripods and gold from Polybus of Egyptian Thebes, and for Helen a golden distaff and a silver work basket from his wife (4.125–132). Menelaus promises Telemachus three horses, a chariot and a goblet; Telemachus asks for a treasure he can carry, and Menelaus changes the gift for the finest thing in his house, the silver bowl that the king of the Sidonians gave him when he was his guest (4.587–619). The good cheer Menelaus ate in other men's houses is ξεινήια too (4.33).
# The gifts of Polybus and Alcandre (4.128–132), the gifts promised (4.589–592), Telemachus' request (4.600–602) and the
# gift changed (4.612–619).
refs: [4.128-132, 4.589-592, 4.600-602, 4.612-619]
passages:
  - 4.125-132 | The gifts from Egyptian Thebes
  - 4.587-619 | The gifts for Telemachus
---

**Gifts from Egypt.** Helen's silver basket “had been given her / by Alcandre, the wife of Polybus, who lived in Thebes / in Egypt”, and the giver's husband gave Menelaus “two silver bathtubs / and two tripods, and ten talents of gold; / and apart from these his wife gave Helen beautiful gifts” (4.125–130; see [[polybus-of-thebes]], [[alcandre]], [[thebes-in-egypt]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). Husband and wife give to husband and wife, each to each. The gifts of Menelaus' wanderings are the wealth of his house, and he has brought them home from Cyprus, Phoenicia and Egypt (4.81–90; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]] and [[possessions]]).

**The gifts offered.** When he has told his tale, Menelaus asks Telemachus to stay eleven or twelve days, “and then I will send you on your way well, and give you splendid gifts, / three horses and a well-polished chariot; and then / I will give you a fine goblet, so that you may pour libations to the gods, / the immortals, remembering me all your days” (4.589–592; see [[horses]], [[chariot]] and [[libation]]). The goblet is a gift of remembrance: the guest will think of his host whenever he pours.

**The gift Telemachus asks for.** Telemachus answers that he would gladly stay a year listening to Menelaus' stories, but his companions are waiting at Pylos; and he asks for another gift: “And whatever gift you would give me, let it be a treasure” (4.600).

κειμήλιον is a treasure laid up in the house, as the stranger in Book 1 was offered a gift that “will be a treasure for you / from me, such as dear guest-friends give to guest-friends” (1.311–313; see [[possessions]] and [[xenia]]); W. W. Merry renders Telemachus' words “let the gift which you may give me be something to treasure up”, and the horses he will leave Menelaus “to adorn your royal stable” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.601). The line is Athena's answer to that offer in Book 1, “And the gift that your dear heart bids you give me” (1.316), turned into a request. ἄγαλμα, “a delight” (4.602), is the word of the gilded heifer that is to please Athena (3.438) and of the ivory cheekpiece in the Iliad's simile that lies in a chamber “as a delight for a king” (*Iliad* 4.144; see the translation's row ἄγαλμα).

**The gift changed.** Menelaus smiles and strokes him with his hand: “You are of good blood, dear child, by the way you speak; / Well then, I will change these gifts for you; for I can” (4.609–612). μεταστήσω, “I will exchange”, has this sense only here (Merry on 4.612; see the translation's row μεθίστημι). From the treasures that lie stored in his house he gives the finest and most precious, a mixing bowl of silver rimmed with gold, the work of Hephaestus, which Phaedimus, king of the Sidonians, gave him when his house sheltered him on his way home (4.613–619; see [[mixing-bowl]] and [[phaedimus]]). A gift that was itself a guest-gift passes on to a new guest, and its history goes with it. In Book 15 Menelaus keeps his word, and Helen adds a robe of her own making for Telemachus' bride (15.113–129, a plain rendering).
