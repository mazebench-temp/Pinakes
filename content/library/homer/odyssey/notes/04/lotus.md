---
summary: Menelaus' wide plain at Sparta has “much lotus” in it, with galingale, wheat, spelt and barley, the pasture of a country for horses, as Telemachus says when he declines the gift of three horses (4.602–604).
---

**Much lotus.** Telemachus will not take horses to Ithaca, and he gives Menelaus the reason in the land itself: “for you are lord of a plain / that is wide, in which there is much lotus, and galingale, / and wheat and spelt and broad-eared white barley” (4.602–604; see [[horses]], [[galingale]], [[wheat]], [[spelt]] and [[barley]]). ᾧ ἔνι μὲν λωτὸς πολύς is the only place in the Odyssey where the lotus is the plant of the fields. W. W. Merry calls it a sort of trefoil or clover, not to be confused with the lotus of the Lotus-eaters, and reports Sprengel's identification of it, in his history of botany, as the *Lotus corniculatus* of Linnaeus (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.603; see the translation's note on 4.603). It is the Iliad's lotus, the fodder that Achilles' idle horses munch beside their chariots (*Iliad* 2.775–777).

The plain is a pasture for horses, which Ithaca is not: there are no wide courses there and no meadow (4.605–608; see [[ithaca]] and [[horse-pasturing]]). Merry thinks of the flat land of Messenia or of the Eurotas valley (on 4.602).
