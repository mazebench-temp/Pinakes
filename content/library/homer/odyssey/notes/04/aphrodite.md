# Unnamed: "when she led me there away from my dear native land" (4.262).
refs: [4.262]
---

**The form of golden Aphrodite.** The book opens on Menelaus' double wedding, and Helen's only child is likened to the goddess: “Hermione, who had the form of golden Aphrodite” (4.14; see [[hermione-daughter-of-helen]], [[the-wedding-at-sparta]] and [[golden]]). εἶδος, “form”, is the word of looks (see [[eidos]]). The comparison is the Iliad's for the most beautiful women: Briseis comes to mourn Patroclus “like golden Aphrodite” (*Iliad* 19.282), and Cassandra, “like golden Aphrodite”, is the first to see her father bringing Hector's body home (*Iliad* 24.699). Later in the Odyssey Penelope herself comes from her chamber Ἀρτέμιδι ἰκέλη ἠὲ χρυσέῃ Ἀφροδίτῃ, like Artemis or golden Aphrodite (17.37, 19.54), and Helen in this book enters “like Artemis of the golden distaff” (4.122; see [[artemis]]).

**The blind folly that Aphrodite gave.** At the end of her tale of Odysseus in Troy Helen says that when the Trojan women wailed over the men he had killed, her own heart rejoiced, since it had already turned toward home, “and I groaned over the blind folly that Aphrodite / gave me, when she led me there away from my dear native land, / leaving behind my child and my bridal chamber and my husband, / who lacked nothing, in mind or in form” (4.261–264; see [[helen]] and [[ate]]). Merry sees in the passage how the poet joins good understanding with morality and ignorance with crime: sin appears rather as a blinding of the mind than as a perversion of the will, and the blindness, ἄτη, may be the direct work of a god, as Agamemnon says that Zeus took away his wits; here it is sent by Aphrodite (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.261, comparing *Iliad* 19.86–88 and 19.137). He reports Bothe's reading νοσφισσαμένη, which would throw the whole blame on the goddess, and keeps the accusative (on 4.263). In the Iliad it is Aphrodite who leads Helen from the wall to Paris' chamber, and Helen who resists and then follows (*Iliad* 3.383–420; see [[the-judgment-of-paris]]). Menelaus, answering, does not blame her: a god must have bidden her come to the Wooden Horse (4.274–275; see [[daimon]]).
