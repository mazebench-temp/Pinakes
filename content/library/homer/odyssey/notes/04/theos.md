---
summary: The gods in Menelaus' tale hold him in Egypt for hecatombs not offered (4.351–353) and are hard for a mortal to overpower (4.397); “the gods know all things” (4.379, 4.468), and Zeus “gives good and evil; for he can do all things” (4.237). Men are measured against them: Menelaus goes out “like a god to look upon” (4.310), and Noemon saw “Mentor, or a god” (4.654).
---

**What the gods do.** Helen's maxim gives Zeus the giving of good and evil (4.236–237; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]), and Menelaus lays Odysseus' lost homecoming on “the god himself” (4.181; see [[who-is-to-blame]]). In his tale the gods held him on Pharos because he had not offered them perfect hecatombs, “and the gods always wished their commands to be remembered” (4.351–353, a verse Zenodotus rejected; see the translation's note on 4.353 and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]); he appeals to Eidothea and to Proteus, “for the gods know all things” (4.379, 4.468; see [[the-gods-know-all-things]]), and tells why the old man must be caught by a goddess's plan: “a god is hard for a mortal man to overpower” (4.397; see [[a-god-is-hard-for-a-mortal-to-overpower]]). The gods turned the wind for Agamemnon (4.520) and gave Menelaus a fair one home (4.585–586).

**Like a god.** Peisistratus delights in Menelaus' voice “as a god's” (4.160), Menelaus goes out of his chamber “like a god to look upon” (4.310), and Noemon saw “Mentor, or a god, who was like him in every way” (4.654; see [[truth-and-deception]]). Medon does not know “whether some god roused” Telemachus to go to Pylos (4.712), and Penelope asks the phantom whether it is a god (4.831). The phantom answers for the gods who live at ease: they do not let her weep, “for he is no offender against the gods” (4.805–807; see [[eidolon]]).
