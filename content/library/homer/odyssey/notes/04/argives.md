---
summary: The Argives of this book are the army of the war, as Menelaus, Helen and Peisistratus remember it: Odysseus, whom Menelaus would have honored above all other Argives (4.171–172); Antilochus, by no means the worst of them (4.199–200); the camp to which Odysseus went back from Troy with his intelligence (4.258); and the best of the Argives in the wooden horse, whose wives' voices Helen imitated (4.272–279).
---

The name is used five times, always of the Achaeans who fought at Troy (see [[achaeans]] and [[danaans]]). Menelaus had meant to welcome Odysseus “above all other / Argives” and to give him a city in Argos (4.171–174; see [[odysseus]] and [[argos]]). Peisistratus calls his brother Antilochus “by no means the worst / of the Argives” (4.199–200; see [[antilochus]]). In Helen's story Odysseus, after his spying, “went back among the Argives, and brought back much intelligence” (4.258; see [[odysseus-enters-troy-as-a-beggar]]); Ἀργείους, the accusative, is used only here in the Odyssey. In Menelaus' story the men in the horse are “all the best / of the Argives, bringing slaughter and doom to the Trojans”, a verse Demodocus will sing again (4.272–273 = 8.512–513), and Helen calls them by name “making your voice like the voices of the wives of all the Argives” (4.279; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]]). The adjective is Helen's own title in the book, Ἀργείη Ἑλένη, “Argive Helen” (4.184, 4.296; see [[argive-helen]]).
