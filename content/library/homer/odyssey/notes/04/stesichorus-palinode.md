In the evening at Sparta Helen tells how she alone knew Odysseus when he came into Troy as a beggar (see [[odysseus-enters-troy-as-a-beggar]]), and how, when he went back and the other Trojan women wailed, she rejoiced, because her heart was already turned toward home:

{{quote:35314b76-b702-5059-be4c-32a39cf0c8ef}}

Menelaus answers with a tale of her at the Wooden Horse, inside Troy, where she called the chieftains by name (4.266–289; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and [[helen]]). Stesichorus' palinode, as Plato quotes it, says the opposite: “That story is not true. / You did not go on the well-benched ships, / you did not come to the citadel of Troy” (*Phaedrus* 243a–b). The Helen of this book does not deny the voyage. She calls it ἄτη, “blind folly”, and lays it on Aphrodite (see [[ate]] and [[aphrodite]]); the palinode clears her by denying that she went at all.

The land to which the later versions sent her is the land of this book's other tales. Herodotus' priests kept her in Egypt with King Proteus, and Euripides joined their story to Stesichorus' phantom, setting the real Helen in Proteus' house while the phantom went to Troy; Menelaus, wandering home, finds her there, as in this book he is held in Egypt on his way home (4.351–352; see [[helen-in-egypt]] and [[euripides-helen]]).
