summary: At Sparta the son is known by the father. Helen sees Odysseus in Telemachus, and Menelaus the feet and hands, the glances and the hair (4.141–150); Peisistratus speaks for the son whose father is gone (4.164–167), and Menelaus praises him as his father's son, as Nestor's sons are Nestor's (4.204–211). Telemachus has come for a report of his father (4.317), the suitors plot his death on his way home from the search (4.669–672), and in Ithaca the house hopes for the offspring of the son of Arceisius (4.754–757).

**Known by his father.** Menelaus' welcome begins with a guess: the strangers' lineage is not lost in them, “since base men would not beget such sons as you” (4.62–64; see [[genee]]). When Telemachus weeps at the name of Odysseus, Menelaus ponders whether to let him speak of his father himself (4.113–119), and Helen comes and settles it: she has never seen anyone so like another, man or woman, “as this man is like the son of great-hearted Odysseus, / Telemachus” (4.141–144; see [[helen]] and [[sebas]]). Merry quotes Athenaeus that women are quick to detect the likeness of children to their parents, and sets Helen's readiness against the slowness of Menelaus (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.141). Menelaus agrees: “for such were his feet, and such his hands, / and the glances of his eyes, and his head and the hair above” (4.149–150). In Book 1 Athena saw the likeness in his head and his fine eyes, and in Book 3 Nestor heard it in his words (1.208–209, 3.122–125).

**The son without a father.** Peisistratus speaks for the young man who is too modest to speak for himself: “For a son whose father is gone has many sorrows / in his halls, when there are no others to be his helpers” (4.164–165; see [[peisistratus]] and [[algos]]). Telemachus asks only for a report of his father and that Menelaus remember, for his sake, any word or deed his father fulfilled at Troy (4.317–331; see [[telemachus]]). In the Iliad Andromache's lament gives the fatherless child's lot (*Iliad* 22.490–498; see [[the-day-of-orphanhood]]).

**Nestor's sons.** Menelaus praises Peisistratus' speech as his father's: “for your father too is such a man, and so you speak wisely”, and Zeus has granted Nestor to grow old in ease, with sons of good sense who are the best with the spear (4.204–211; see [[easily-known-is-the-offspring]] and [[nestor]]). He tells Telemachus the same when he asks for a gift he can carry: “You are of good blood, dear child, by the way you speak” (4.611). Peisistratus' own grief is for a brother, Antilochus (4.186–202; see [[kasignetos]]).

**Other fathers.** Megapenthes is Menelaus' son by a slave woman, for the gods gave Helen no more children after Hermione (4.10–14; see [[megapenthes]]). Proteus' daughter takes pity on Menelaus and devises a trick against her own father (4.364–366, 4.437; see [[eidothea]] and [[proteus]]), and in the tale of the returns there is the son who avenges: Orestes may have killed Aegisthus before Menelaus comes home (4.546–547; see [[orestes]]).

**The son's danger.** In Ithaca Antinous learns that “the young boy is gone”, and prays that Zeus destroy his strength “before he reaches the measure of youth”; he will lie in ambush for him, “so that he may sail miserably in search of his father” (4.665–672; see [[antinous]] and [[the-suitors]]). Medon tells Penelope that he has gone in search of news of his father, or of what fate he has met (4.701, 4.713–714). She would send to Laertes, who may mourn before the people that are eager “to destroy his offspring, the offspring of godlike Odysseus” (4.738–741; see [[laertes]]), and Eurycleia answers that the offspring of the son of Arceisius is not altogether hateful to the gods: someone will still be left to hold the house (4.754–757; see [[arceisius]] and [[mothers-and-sons]]).
