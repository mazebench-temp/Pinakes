**Sensible Penelope.** Menelaus, mourning Odysseus, thinks of the three who mourn him at home, “the old man Laertes and sensible Penelope / and Telemachus” (4.111–112; see [[sensible]]). In Ithaca she is περίφρων Πηνελόπεια, “prudent Penelope”, when she lies fasting and when she speaks to the phantom (4.787, 4.808, 4.830; see [[periphron]]).

**The news.** “Nor was Penelope long without hearing / of the plans that the suitors were brooding deep in their minds” (4.675–676): the herald Medon overheard them and comes to tell her (4.677–679; see [[medon-the-herald]]). She takes him for their messenger and curses the suitors: may this be their last meal here (4.681–685). Merry explains the tangled wish as Eustathius did: she should have begun “O that they had never wooed me”, but hurries to the thought uppermost in her mind, “may this be their last meal” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.684). Then she turns on them, in the second person, with what they heard as children from their fathers:

{{quote:69ebc11a-4436-5e5d-8bb1-57b6219f2fbd}}

Medon answers that a greater evil is planned: they mean to kill Telemachus on his way home from Pylos and Lacedaemon (4.697–702). “So he spoke, and her knees and dear heart were loosened where she was, / and for a long time speechlessness took her, and her two eyes / filled with tears, and her strong voice was checked” (4.703–705; see [[tears]]): θαλερὴ … φωνή is “the flow of her voice” (Merry on 4.705). Her first question is why her son had any need to board the swift ships, “which are horses of the sea / for men” (4.707–709; see [[ships]]).

**The lament.** She cannot bear to sit on any of the chairs in the house, but sits on the threshold of her chamber, lamenting, among all her women, young and old (4.716–720; see [[lament]] and [[handmaids]]). The number of the household's women is given in a later book as fifty (Merry on 4.720, citing 22.421). Her lament is for husband and son together: “I who first lost my brave, lion-hearted husband, / who excelled among the Danaans in every kind of prowess, / a brave man, whose fame is wide through Hellas and the heart of Argos; / and now the storm winds have snatched away my beloved son / from the halls without report, and I did not hear of his setting out” (4.724–728; see [[lion-hearted]], [[kleos]] and [[mothers-and-sons]]). ἀκλέα, “without report”, means that no tidings of him have been left behind (Merry on 4.728). She reproaches her women, σχέτλιαι, “Hard-hearted women”, who knew and did not wake her: had she known, he would have stayed, or left her dead in the halls (4.729–734). The double κε of 4.733 is found only here in Homer (Merry on 4.733). She would send old Dolius to Laertes (4.735–741; see [[dolius]] and [[laertes]]).

**The nurse.** Eurycleia confesses that she gave Telemachus his provisions and swore not to tell her for twelve days, and counsels her to bathe, go up with her women and pray to Athena, and not to trouble the old man (4.742–757; see [[eurycleia]]). “So she spoke, and lulled her lament, and checked her eyes from lament” (4.758). Penelope does as she is told:

{{quote:85432772-48e4-57c3-982f-beecb5cf952f}}

The barley grains were not burned, as Nitzsch thought, but poured from the basket like wine from a cup, so that the offering was rather a libation (Merry on 4.761; see [[barley-grains]] and [[prayer]]). Merry compares Virgil's echo of the prayer's condition, “if ever my father Hyrtacus brought gifts to your altars for me” (on 4.763, citing *Aeneid* 9.404). Her cry is the ὀλολυγή the women raise at the sacrifice in Pylos (4.767; 3.450), and the suitors in the hall take it for a sign that “the much-wooed queen / is preparing” a marriage for them (4.768–771; see [[the-suitors]] and [[much-wooed]]).

**The dream.** She lies in her upper room “without food, tasting neither food nor drink” (4.788; see [[fasting]]), pondering whether her son will escape death, as a lion ponders in fear when men draw their crafty circle around him (4.789–793): the point of the likeness, Eustathius saw, is not the lion's courage but his fear and helplessness, and the hunters' ring is the suitors' design (Merry on 4.791; see [[lion]]). Sleep comes upon her, and Athena sends a phantom in the likeness of her sister Iphthime, who tells her that her son will come home, for Athena goes with him (4.795–829; see [[iphthime]], [[eidolon]] and [[athena]]). Penelope, slumbering at the gates of dreams, calls it “sister” (4.810), repeats her lament (4.814–816 = 4.724–726) and asks of her husband, whether he lives or is in the house of Hades; it will not say (4.831–837; see [[it-is-bad-to-speak-empty-wind]]). She starts up from sleep, κούρη Ἰκαρίοιο, “the daughter of Icarius”, and “her dear heart was warmed, / so clear was the dream that had rushed upon her in the dead of night” (4.839–841; see [[daughter-of-icarius]] and [[dream]]). The dream, though “dim” to the senses, was clear to the mind (Merry on 4.841).
