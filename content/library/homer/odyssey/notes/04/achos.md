summary: Menelaus grieves for all the men lost at Troy, but for Odysseus “always, grief not to be forgotten” (4.100–110); he is grieved on the sand at Proteus' news (4.549, 4.553). Antinous speaks “in distress” and fury (4.661), grief that destroys the heart is poured about Penelope (4.716), and the phantom tells her that the gods do not let her weep or grieve (4.806).
grc: [ἀχεύων, ἀχνυμένῳ, ἀκάχησθαι]
en: [grieve, grieved]
---

**Grief not to be forgotten.** Menelaus mourns and grieves for all the men lost at Troy, “grieved though I am”, but for none so much as for Odysseus: “for him it was to be / that he himself should have sorrows, and for me, always, grief not to be forgotten / for him” (4.100–109; see [[algos]] and [[odysseus]]). Merry takes the genitive κείνου as “sorrow for him”, as Agamemnon's ἄχος σέθεν for the wounded Menelaus (*Iliad* 4.169) (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.108). ἄλαστος, “not to be forgotten”, is the grief that has no forgetting; that evening Helen mixes into the wine a drug that brings forgetfulness of every ill (4.220–221; see [[nepenthe]]).

**On the sand.** When Proteus has told him of his brother's death, Menelaus' heart is broken and he weeps; at the old man's counsel his heart and spirit are warmed again, “grieved though I was”, and he asks for the third man, “I want to hear, grieved though I am” (4.538–553; see [[tears]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). The last verse was generally rejected; Eustathius defended it as the doubt of a man confused by grief (see the translation's note on 4.553).

**In Ithaca.** Antinous rises among the suitors ἀχνύμενος, “in distress; and his heart, dark on every side, was filled / with mighty fury” (4.661–662), the verses of Agamemnon's anger in the first assembly of the Iliad (*Iliad* 1.103–104; see [[antinous]] and [[menos]]). At Medon's news “grief that destroys the heart was poured about” Penelope (ἄχος ἀμφεχύθη θυμοφθόρον, 4.716; see [[lament]]), and Athena's phantom comes to tell her that the gods do not let her “weep or grieve, since he will yet come home” (4.805–806; see [[eidolon]]).
