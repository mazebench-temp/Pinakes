---
summary: Helen comes into the hall “like Artemis of the golden distaff”, and her handmaid Phylo sets beside her a silver basket with her golden distaff lying across it, a gift of Alcandre of Egyptian Thebes (4.121–135).
---

Helen comes out of her fragrant chamber “like Artemis of the golden distaff” (4.122; see [[of-the-golden-distaff]] and [[artemis]]), and her three handmaids bring her chair, a rug and her silver work basket (4.123–125; see [[handmaids]] and [[basket]]). The distaff was a gift from Egypt: Polybus of Thebes gave Menelaus silver bathtubs, tripods and gold, and “apart from these his wife gave Helen beautiful gifts: / she granted her a golden distaff and a basket on wheels / of silver” (4.130–132; see [[alcandre]], [[polybus-of-thebes]] and [[guest-gifts]]). Phylo sets the basket beside her, full of finely spun yarn, “and across it / lay the distaff, laden with violet-dark wool” (4.134–135). The basket held the dressed yarn ready for the weaver, W. W. Merry explains, and the distaff carried wool that had yet to be spun; Eustathius took τετάνυστο to mean that it did not stand upright but lay across the basket from rim to rim (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.132–135). Merry adds that Helen was famous as a workwoman, and that she appears here once more “devoted to domestic duties” (on 4.122 and 4.132–135). In the Iliad she was weaving a great purple web of the battles of Trojans and Achaeans when Iris found her (*Iliad* 3.125–128; see [[weaving]]). She sits in her chair with the distaff beside her and questions her husband about the strangers (4.136–146).
