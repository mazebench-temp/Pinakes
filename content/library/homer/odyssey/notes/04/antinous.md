summary: Noemon's question tells Antinous that Telemachus has sailed in the ship he lent him. In a fury Antinous asks for a swift ship and twenty companions to lie in wait for him in the strait between Ithaca and Samos, and the suitors approve; in the evening he silences their boasting, chooses twenty men, launches the ship and waits for dark (4.625–674, 4.773–786).

**The question.** The suitors are throwing the discus and the javelin before the hall of Odysseus, “And Antinous was sitting there, and godlike Eurymachus, / leaders of the suitors, and in excellence they were by far the best” (4.628–629; see [[eurymachus]] and [[the-suitors]]). The words from ἀρετῇ give the reason why they lead (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.629). Noemon, son of Phronius, asks Antinous when Telemachus will be back from Pylos, since he needs the ship he lent him (4.630–637; see [[noemon-son-of-phronius]]), and the suitors are amazed, for they thought the boy was on his farms (4.638–640). Antinous asks when he went and who went with him, “Chosen men of Ithaca, or his own / hired men and slaves? He could do even that” (4.643–644), and whether he took the ship by force or was given it (4.645–647). Merry takes the young men of 4.643 to be divided into the chosen men and Telemachus' own servants, as the scholia punctuate (on 4.643–644), and the hired men and slaves to be enough, if he pleased, to man a ship (on 4.644).

**The plan.** When Noemon has gone, the spirit of both leaders is indignant (4.658; Merry on 4.658), and Antinous speaks “in distress; and his heart, dark on every side, was filled / with mighty fury, and his eyes were like blazing fire” (4.661–662; see [[eyes-like-fire]]), the verses of Agamemnon's anger at Calchas (*Iliad* 1.103–104). The journey is “a great deed … overbearingly accomplished” (4.663), the boy will be a mischief to them hereafter, and may Zeus “destroy his strength before he reaches the measure of youth” (4.667–668), Aristarchus' reading for the common “before he becomes a bane to us” (Merry's critical note on 4.668 and on 4.668; see the translation's note on 4.668). The first verse is Telemachus' own request in the assembly of Ithaca, “But come, give me a swift ship and twenty companions” (2.212): the suitor answers the son's voyage with a voyage of his own. ἰόντα is only “on his way”, the direction left to the context (Merry on 4.670; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]], [[ithaca]] and [[samos]]). “So he spoke, and they all approved and bade him do it” (4.673).

**The ship.** When Penelope's cry rings through the house and the suitors boast that the queen is preparing their marriage, Antinous checks them: “Strange men, avoid overbearing talk, / all of it alike, lest someone report it even inside” (4.774–775; see [[strange-one]] and [[overbearing]]). Let them rise in silence and do what pleased them all (4.776–777); Merry sets the silence against their din (on 4.776). “So saying, he chose twenty of the best men” (4.778), and they launch the ship, set mast and sail, anchor her out in the water, ready to sail, and take their supper ashore, waiting for evening (4.779–786; Merry on 4.785). In the night they sail out to lie in wait for Telemachus at Asteris (4.842–847; see [[asteris]]).

**His father.** He is twice Εὐπείθεος υἱός, “son of Eupeithes” (4.641, 4.660; see [[eupeithes]]): the second verse, “And Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke among them”, comes again when he speaks among the suitors in later books (16.363, 18.42, 20.270, 21.140; see the translation's table of formulas).
