---
summary: Helen is home with Menelaus. At a double wedding in their house (4.3–14) she tells how she longed for “my husband, who lacked nothing, in mind or in form” (4.263–264), he tells how she tried the men in the Horse with the voices of their wives (4.274–289), and they sleep side by side (4.304–305); Proteus promises him the Elysian plain “because you have Helen as your wife” (4.569). Agamemnon was killed “by the guile of his ruinous wife” (4.92), and in Ithaca Penelope mourns the husband she lost (4.724–726).

**Weddings at Sparta.** Telemachus arrives on the day Menelaus is sending his daughter Hermione to the son of Achilles, promised at Troy, and bringing the daughter of Alector from Sparta for his son Megapenthes (4.3–14; see [[the-wedding-at-sparta]], [[hermione-daughter-of-helen]] and [[megapenthes]]). The book's other wives are glimpsed in passing: Alcandre, wife of Polybus of Egyptian Thebes, who gave Helen her golden distaff and silver basket (4.125–132), Polydamna, wife of Thon, who gave her the drug (4.228), the wives with lovely veils who send bread to the banquet (4.623), and Iphthime, “whom Eumelus had married” (4.797–798; see [[iphthime]]).

**Two tales at the table.** Helen's tale ends in remorse: she groaned over the blind folly that Aphrodite gave her, “leaving behind my child and my bridal chamber and my husband, / who lacked nothing, in mind or in form” (4.261–264; see [[ate]] and [[helen]]). Merry finds her clever enough to flatter the vanity every man has, and Menelaus pleased (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.264). He answers that all she has said is “fair and right” (4.266), and tells a tale of his own: she came to the Horse with Deiphobus, walked around it three times, and called the best of the Danaans by name, “making your voice like the voices of the wives of all the Argives” (4.274–279; see [[deiphobus]] and [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]]). Menelaus lays her coming on a god who meant to give the Trojans glory (4.274–275; see [[daimon]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]), a very favorable view of her conduct, as Eustathius remarked; for her mimicry Merry compares the Delian maidens of the Hymn to Apollo, who can imitate the voices of all men (on 4.264; Hymn to Apollo 162–164). Then the night: “but the son of Atreus slept in the inner part of the high house, / and beside him lay Helen of the long robes, brilliant among women” (4.304–305).

**The son-in-law of Zeus.** Proteus tells Menelaus that he will not die in Argos but be sent to the Elysian plain, “because you have Helen as your wife, and in their eyes you are the son-in-law of Zeus” (4.561–569; see [[elysium]] and [[in-laws]]). σφιν is an ethical dative, “in their eyes”: the gods count him Zeus' son-in-law (see the translation's note on 4.569). His fortune comes through his wife.

**The other house of Atreus.** Menelaus' wealth gives him no joy, since another man killed his brother “in secret, unawares, by the guile of his ruinous wife” (4.91–93; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]] and [[dolos]]). In Proteus' account the wife is not named again, and Aegisthus kills Agamemnon at his feast; Merry notes that the later accounts in Books 11 and 24 give Clytemnestra the greater part (on 4.536; see the translation's note on 4.536 and [[clytemnestra]]).

**Penelope's husband.** In Ithaca Penelope laments that she first lost “my brave, lion-hearted husband, / who excelled among the Danaans in every kind of prowess” (4.724–725, again to the phantom, 4.814–815; see [[posis]] and [[arete]]), and the suitors, in their hall, say that the much-wooed queen is preparing a marriage for them (4.770–771; see [[the-suitors]]).
