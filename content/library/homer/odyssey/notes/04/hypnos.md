summary: The thought of Odysseus makes “sleep and food hateful” to Menelaus (4.105); Telemachus asks to “take our fill of sweet sleep” (4.294–295). On Pharos Proteus “lies down to sleep” among his seals at midday (4.403–405), and the men in the sealskins could hardly lie down beside them (4.443). Penelope falls asleep pondering her son's fate, and the phantom finds her “slumbering very sweetly at the gates of dreams” (4.793–809).

**At Sparta.** Menelaus mourns for Odysseus, “who makes sleep and food hateful to me / when I remember him” (4.105–106); Merry takes ἀπεχθαίρει as causative, “makes hateful”, with Eustathius' gloss μισητὸν ποιεῖ (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.105). After the tales Telemachus asks to be sent to bed, “so that now / we may lie down and take our fill of sweet sleep” (4.294–295; see [[sweet-sleep]]), and the guests sleep in the porch, Menelaus and Helen in the inner room (4.302–305; see [[bed]]).

**On Pharos.** Proteus comes out of the sea at midday, “and when he has come out he lies down to sleep in the hollow caves”, and his seals sleep around him (4.400–405; see [[seals]] and [[proteus]]). Menelaus and his men must catch him asleep (4.414, 4.421), and the stench made the ambush dreadful: “for who would lie down to sleep beside a beast of the sea?” (4.443). In the lion simile the hind “has lulled her newborn suckling fawns to sleep” in the lion's lair (4.335–336; see [[the-hind-in-the-lions-lair]]).

**Penelope's sleep.** Fasting and pondering, Penelope is overtaken by sweet sleep, “and she slept, leaning back, and all her limbs were loosened” (4.793–794); the phantom stands above her head and asks, “Are you asleep, Penelope, grieving at heart?” (4.804; see [[dream-scene]]), and she answers “slumbering very sweetly at the gates of dreams” (4.809; see [[onar]]). The dream gone, “she started up out of sleep” (4.839).
