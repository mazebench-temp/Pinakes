summary: Menelaus' possessions: no mortal can rival Zeus, whose house and possessions are immortal, but Menelaus has no joy of his own, gathered in eight years of wandering while his brother was murdered (4.78–93). He would have brought Odysseus from Ithaca with his possessions (4.175); Telemachus asks that his gift be a treasure, and Menelaus gives him the finest of the treasures stored in his house (4.600, 4.613). In Ithaca Penelope accuses the suitors of wasting the wealth of Telemachus (4.686–687).
# κειμήλια (4.613; also 2.75) and κτῆσιν (4.687), first claimed here.
grc: [κειμήλια, κτῆσιν]
en: [treasures, wealth]
---

**No joy in wealth.** Menelaus answers Telemachus' whisper about his house: “Dear children, truly no mortal could rival Zeus; / for his house and his possessions are immortal”, and among men there may or may not be one who can rival him in possessions, gathered in eight years of wandering; “so it is with no joy that I am lord of these possessions” (4.78–93; see [[house]], [[zeus]] and [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). Egyptian Thebes, where Polybus lived, is the place “where the most treasures lie stored in the houses” (4.127), the words Achilles used of Thebes in Egypt (*Iliad* 9.381–382; see [[thebes-in-egypt]]).

**Treasures.** Telemachus asks that his gift be a κειμήλιον, “a treasure”, and Menelaus gives him the finest and most precious of the gifts that lie stored as κειμήλια in his house (4.600, 4.613–614; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[mixing-bowl]]).

**The wealth of Telemachus.** In Ithaca Penelope turns on the suitors, “you who gather here constantly and waste much substance, / the wealth of wise-hearted Telemachus” (4.686–687; see [[the-suitors]]), and Telemachus tells Menelaus that his house is being eaten up (4.318).
