summary: The book is full of meals. At Sparta the strangers arrive in the middle of a wedding feast, are fed apart, sup again after the weeping and drink Helen's drugged wine over her stories, and at the close banqueters come to the king's house (4.3–19, 4.47–68, 4.212–239, 4.620–624). In Menelaus' tale Aegisthus kills Agamemnon at a feast, and Proteus speaks of a funeral feast (4.531–535, 4.547); in Ithaca Penelope wishes that the suitors' meal might be their last, and they take their supper on the shore before the ambush (4.683–686, 4.786).

**The wedding feast.** Menelaus is found “giving a wedding feast to his many kinsmen” (δαινύντα γάμον, 4.3), the phrase of Orestes' funeral feast in Book 3, δαίνυ τάφον (3.309; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.3 and 3.309). “So they were feasting throughout the great high-roofed house, / the neighbors and kinsmen of glorious Menelaus, / delighting in it”, with a singer and two tumblers (4.15–19; see [[the-wedding-at-sparta]] and [[tumblers]]). It was not the wedding day itself, Merry thinks, but the end of the festivities (on 4.3).

**The strangers' meal.** Menelaus bids Eteoneus “bring them on into the house to feast” (θοινηθῆναι, 4.36, its only use in Homer; see [[xenia]]), and the two are fed apart from the wedding guests in the verses of the Odyssey's welcome, with water, bread, dainties, meat and golden cups, and the chine of an ox from Menelaus' own portion (4.52–66; see [[water-for-the-hands]] and [[feast-type-scene]]). Menelaus calls it δεῖπνον, “the meal”: Merry takes δεῖπνον for the principal midday meal, rightly used here of the travelers' first regular meal of the day though the day was far spent; the evening meal is δόρπον, as at 4.213, and δεῖπνον is used again of the meal taken by travelers setting out and by herdsmen before they leave home for the day (15.79, 15.397; Merry on 4.61).

**Supper again.** After the weeping for Odysseus and Antilochus, Peisistratus asks to stop, for he takes no delight in mourning “after supper” (μεταδόρπιος, 4.194; see the translation's note on 4.194), and Menelaus agrees: “let us remember supper again” (4.213). Asphalion pours water over their hands and they eat (4.216–218), and Helen casts her drug into the wine and bids them “now feast, sitting in the halls, / and take delight in stories” (4.238–239; see [[nepenthe]]). Merry fills in Peisistratus' broken reasoning: for him there is no pleasure in weeping after supper, the morning will come and serve for weeping, and he does not grudge tears to the dead (on 4.193).

**The banqueters.** When Menelaus and Telemachus have finished speaking of the gifts, “the banqueters went to the house of the divine king. / They drove sheep, and brought wine that makes men strong, / and their wives with lovely veils sent them bread” (4.621–623). δαιτυμόνες, “banqueters”, is the Odyssey's word for the guests at a feast, of the Phaeacians in Alcinous' hall and of the suitors (7.102, 8.66, 22.12). The verses are suspect: the guests bring their own food, as to a shared feast (ἔρανος), which is another thing from Menelaus' wedding feast, and most editors since Wolf reject them; ἔπεμπον implies that the wives were not present; and περὶ δεῖπνον πένεσθαι, “busy about the meal”, is a construction found nowhere else, where πένεσθαι takes a direct object, as in δαῖτα πένεσθαι (Merry on 4.621–624; see the translation's note on 4.620–624).

**A feast for a murder.** In Proteus' account of Agamemnon's homecoming, Aegisthus sets twenty men in ambush “and on the other side bade them prepare a feast”, goes to invite Agamemnon, “and killed him / when he had feasted him, as one kills an ox at the manger” (4.530–535; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]] and [[aegisthus]]). καλέων, “to invite”, is to bid him to the feast, and agrees with δειπνίσσας (Merry on 4.532). Proteus tells Menelaus that he will find Aegisthus alive, or Orestes will have killed him first, “and you may come in time for the funeral feast” (4.546–547; see [[orestes]]); the scholia explain τάφος as the meal at the burial, and Merry compares Orestes' funeral feast in Book 3 (on 4.547; 3.309).

**The suitors' meals.** When Medon comes to her, Penelope asks whether the suitors have sent him to bid the slave women stop their work “and prepare a feast for them”, and wishes that they may “take their meal here now for the very last time” (4.681–685; see [[medon-the-herald]], [[penelope]] and [[the-suitors]]). Merry explains the broken sentence as two wishes run together, that they had never wooed her, and that this might be their last meal (on 4.684). The suitors who sail to lie in wait for Telemachus anchor their ship, step ashore, “and there they took their supper, and waited for evening to come” (4.785–786). On Pharos Menelaus and his companions made ready their supper by the sea on the night before the ambush of the seals and again after it (4.429, 4.574; see [[proteus]]).
