**Stories with the drug.** Helen casts her drug into the wine and bids the men feast “and take delight in stories; for I will tell you what is fitting” (4.239; see [[helen]]). She cannot tell or name all the trials of stout-hearted Odysseus (4.240–241; see [[stout-hearted]]); μυθήσομαι is a subjunctive, parallel to ὀνομήνω, and Odysseus uses the same turn in the underworld when he cannot name all whom Neoptolemus killed (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.240, 4.242; 11.517). ἀλλʼ οἷον τόδʼ ἔρεξε, “but what a thing this was”: Merry prefers the scholia's view that οἷον is an exclamation, and renders it “To think what a deed he did and dared there!” (on 4.242). Menelaus begins his own story with the same verse (4.271; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]]).

**The disguise.** αὐτόν μιν, “himself”, stands for the reflexive that Homer does not have as one word (Merry on 4.244). For the self-inflicted blows Merry sets beside Odysseus two men in Herodotus: Zopyrus, who mutilated himself and went over to the Babylonians as a deserter to win Babylon for Darius, and Pisistratus, who wounded himself to win a bodyguard from the Athenians (on 4.245; Herodotus 3.154, 1.59.4; see [[pisistratus]] and [[herodotus]]). The spying, he says, falls in the time when the wooden horse was being made: Odysseus wanted to measure the gates, to see whether the horse could pass, and a scholium says that he sought Helen's help for the Greeks (on 4.245). The disguise is told twice, οἰκῆι ἐοικώς, “like a servant” (4.245), and δέκτῃ, “a beggar” (4.248). Aristarchus took δέκτης for a beggar, and the cyclic poet read it as a name, Dectes; Merry suggests that the scholium means the Little Iliad (see the translation's note on 4.248, Merry's critical note and [[little-iliad]]). Friedländer, followed by Bekker and Nauck, struck out the words from εὐρυάγυιαν (4.246) to Τρώων πόλιν (4.249), so that the text runs ἀνδρῶν δυσμενέων κατέδυ πόλιν· οἱ δʼ ἀβάκησαν, because a servant and a beggar cannot both stand (Merry on 4.246 and his critical note). ὃς οὐδὲν τοῖος ἔην, “he who was nothing of the kind”, means anything but a beggar: Odysseus, Aristarchus explained, was the most glorious and splendid of men (Merry on 4.248). The Trojans ἀβάκησαν, “suspected nothing”: Merry reports Döderlein's derivation from ἀ- and βάζω, “speak”, with a sense that passes from speechlessness to ignorance, and the gloss of Apollonius and the scholia, ἠγνόησαν, “they did not know” (on 4.249).

**Helen alone.** “But I alone knew him for what he was” (4.250): ἀνέγνων is the verb of recognition, and τοῖον ἐόντα, “to be the man he was”, a Greek in disguise (Merry on 4.250; see [[anagnorisis]]). She questioned him, and he evaded her κερδοσύνῃ, “by craft” (4.251; see [[kerdos]]). Only when she had bathed him, anointed him and clothed him, and sworn a mighty oath not to reveal him before he reached the ships, did he tell her “the whole plan of the Achaeans” (4.252–256; see [[bathing]] and [[oath]]). Eustathius, as Merry reports, says that she bathed him on purpose, to prove him a liar, when he denied who he was, by stripping off his rags; the oath binds her only until he is back in the camp; and νόον, “the plan”, is the plan of the wooden horse (Merry on 4.252, 4.254, 4.256). He went back among the Argives “and brought back much intelligence” (4.258): ἄγειν, “bring back”, treats the knowledge as plunder (Merry on 4.258; see [[spies-and-scouts]]).

**The scene to come.** Disguised as an old beggar by Athena, Odysseus will come into his own house, will be washed by his old nurse, who knows him by his scar, and will seize her by the throat and bid her keep silent (13.429–438, 19.467–490, plain renderings; see [[odysseus-recognized-by-his-nurse]] and [[eurycleia]]). Helen's tale tells the same pattern in Troy: the beggar, the bath, the recognition, the promise of silence.

**Helen's joy.** “Then the other Trojan women wailed shrilly; but my heart / rejoiced, since already my heart was turned to going / back home, and I groaned over the blind folly that Aphrodite / gave me” (4.259–262; see [[trojan-women]], [[ate]] and [[aphrodite]]). Merry quotes Nitzsch for Helen's remorse in the Iliad (on 4.260, citing *Iliad* 3.139, 6.349 and 24.761 and the verses that follow them), and takes ἄτη as a blinding of the mind sent by a god, here by Aphrodite (on 4.261). She ends by praising the husband she left, “who lacked nothing, in mind or in form” (4.264), and Merry thinks her clever enough to flatter him (on 4.264). Menelaus answers with a story of his own, in which Helen came to the horse and nearly drew the hidden chieftains into answering her (4.266–289; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]]).
