summary: The book of tears at Sparta. Telemachus lets a tear fall at his father's name and hides it behind his cloak (4.113–116); all four weep, and Peisistratus asks that the weeping stop until morning (4.183–215); Helen's drug keeps a man from tears for a day (4.222–226). In Menelaus' tale Agamemnon weeps for joy at his homecoming (4.523), Menelaus weeps on the sand at his brother's death (4.539–544), and Odysseus sheds big tears on Calypso's island (4.556); in Ithaca Penelope's eyes fill with tears (4.705).

**The tear behind the cloak.** At the name of his father Telemachus lets a tear fall to the ground and holds his purple cloak up before his eyes with both hands (4.113–116), and Menelaus knows him by it: “he let a bitter tear fall from beneath his brows, / holding up his purple cloak before his eyes” (4.153–154; see [[telemachus]], [[cloak]] and [[the-longing-for-lament]]). It is his father's gesture: at the Phaeacian feast Odysseus draws his great purple mantle over his head, ashamed to be seen shedding tears beneath his brows (*Odyssey* 8.83–86).

**Weeping, and an end to it.** Menelaus' words roused in them all the longing for lament: Helen wept, Telemachus and Menelaus wept, and the son of Nestor did not keep his eyes free of tears, remembering his brother (4.183–188; see [[antilochus]]). Peisistratus grants tears to the dead as their only prize, but takes no delight in mourning after supper (4.193–202; see [[the-only-prize-of-miserable-mortals]]), and Menelaus agrees, “So let us leave off the weeping that came before” (4.212). Helen's drug goes further: whoever drank it “would not let a tear fall down his cheeks for that day, / not even if his mother and his father lay dead”, nor if his brother or his son were cut down before his eyes (4.222–226; see [[nepenthe]] and [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]).

**Tears in the tale.** Proteus warns Menelaus that he will not be long ἄκλαυτον, “without tears”, when he has heard all (4.494). Agamemnon, home at last, kisses his native land, “and many / hot tears poured from him, since he was glad to see his land” (4.522–523; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]); his brother, hearing of his death, weeps on the sand, rolling on the ground, until he has had his fill of weeping, and the Old Man of the Sea bids him stop, “since we shall find that nothing comes of it” (4.538–544; see the translation's table of key words on ἄνυσις). Odysseus is seen “on an island shedding big tears” (4.556; see [[calypso]]).

**Penelope.** At Medon's news her eyes fill with tears and her strong voice is checked (4.704–705), and Eurycleia, who kept the voyage secret, says she did it “so that you might not mar your fair skin with weeping” (4.749). Athena sends the phantom to make her cease “from her weeping and her tearful lament” (4.800–801; see [[tearful]]), and it tells her that the gods do not let her weep or grieve (4.805–806; see [[lament]], [[achos]] and [[mothers-and-sons]]).
