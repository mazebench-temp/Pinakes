**Herodotus' two passages.** After the Iliad's verses on the robes from Sidon, Herodotus goes on: Homer “mentions it also in the Odyssey, in these verses”, and quotes the lines on Helen's drugs, then “these others that Menelaus says to Telemachus” (*Histories* 2.116.4–5, plain renderings):

“In these verses he shows that he knew of Alexander's wandering to Egypt; for Syria borders on Egypt, and the Phoenicians, whose city Sidon is, live in Syria” (*Histories* 2.116.6). His text of both passages is word for word the one printed here; he quotes the first through “many good and many grim, growing intermixed” (4.230), and stops the second before the verse that Zenodotus rejected, “and the gods always wished their commands to be remembered” (4.353; see the translation's note on 4.353). The Odyssey's verses put Helen's drugs and Menelaus' delay in Egypt, and Herodotus takes them as signs that Homer knew the Egyptian story and let it go (*Histories* 2.116.1; see [[herodotus]] and [[nepenthe]]).

**Thonis.** In Herodotus' story the warden of the Canopic mouth of the Nile, who arrests Alexander and sends word to Proteus at Memphis, is called Thonis (Θῶνις, *Histories* 2.113.3–2.115.1). In this book Polydamna, who gave Helen her drugs, is “the wife of Thon” (Θῶνος παράκοιτις, 4.228). Strabo knew of an old city Thonis on the coast toward Canopus, named after the king who received Menelaus and Helen as guests (*Geography* 17.1.16; see [[thon]] and [[polydamna]]).

**Proteus.** Herodotus' Proteus is a man of Memphis who became king of Egypt. His precinct lies south of the temple of Hephaestus, Phoenicians from Tyre live around it, and in it stands a shrine of “the foreign Aphrodite”, whom Herodotus took to be Helen (*Histories* 2.112). This book's Proteus is “immortal Proteus of Egypt”, the Old Man of the Sea and the servant of Poseidon, whom Menelaus seizes on Pharos among his seals (4.384–386, 4.450–461; see [[proteus]]).

**Menelaus in Egypt.** The priests told Herodotus that after Troy Menelaus sailed up to Memphis, met with great hospitality, and got back Helen unharmed and all his goods. But when he wished to sail away, calms held him for a long time, and he did an unholy thing: he took two children of the country and sacrificed them, and when it became known he fled with his ships toward Libya, hated and pursued (*Histories* 2.119). In this book the gods keep Menelaus on Pharos twenty days without a fair wind, because he had not offered them perfect hecatombs, and Proteus sends him back to the river of Egypt to offer them before he can go home (4.351–362, 4.472–480, 4.581–586; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). The two accounts share the delay, the rich gifts of the Egyptians (4.125–132) and Libya (4.85); Herodotus' Menelaus wins his passage by a crime, Homer's by the sacrifices he owes.

**Helen at Troy.** Herodotus thought that Homer chose the story of Helen at Troy as the one fitter for epic (*Histories* 2.116.1). This book has her there twice. In her own tale she alone knew Odysseus when he slipped into the city disguised as a beggar, bathed him and kept his secret, and rejoiced when he went back, for her heart was already turned toward home (4.240–264; see [[odysseus-enters-troy-as-a-beggar]]); in Menelaus' tale she comes to the Wooden Horse with Deiphobus and calls the chieftains by name in the voices of their wives (4.266–289; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and [[helen]]). Euripides kept both stories, the phantom at Troy and the real Helen in Egypt (see [[euripides-helen]] and [[stesichorus-palinode]]).
