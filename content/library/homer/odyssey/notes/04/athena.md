summary: Athena is absent from Sparta and present at the end of the book in Ithaca. In the tales of Troy she led Helen away from the Wooden Horse, and Ajax, son of Oileus, died hated by her. In Ithaca Noemon has seen “Mentor, or a god” sail with Telemachus; Eurycleia sends Penelope to pray to her, Penelope prays with barley grains, “and the goddess heard her prayer”, and that night Athena sends a phantom in the likeness of Penelope's sister to tell her that Telemachus has Pallas Athena for his escort.

**In the tales of Troy.** Menelaus ends his tale of the Wooden Horse, where Helen walked round the hollow ambush calling the chieftains in their wives' voices, with Odysseus holding Anticlus' mouth shut “until Pallas Athena led you away” (4.289; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and [[anticlus]]). A god must have bidden Helen come, he says, “a god who meant to hold out glory to the Trojans” (4.274–275; see [[daimon]]); another god took her away. On the five verses of Anticlus, which Aristarchus rejected, see the translation's note on 4.285–289. In Proteus' account of the returns, Ajax, son of Oileus, “would have escaped his doom, hated though he was by Athena”, had he not boasted that he had escaped the sea against the will of the gods (4.502–504; see [[the-death-of-ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). Merry: the Locrian Ajax was shipwrecked under the curse of Athena (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.499). The poem's first song was of “the grim homecoming that Pallas Athena laid on them from Troy” (1.327), and Nestor laid the strife of the sons of Atreus to her wrath (3.135; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]). Merry takes it that Hera saved Agamemnon from the storm raised by Athena (on 4.513), the evil wind and long waves that she sent against the Achaeans who had sinned against her (5.108–109; see [[hera]]).

**The prayer of the wish.** “If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo”, Menelaus begins his wish that Odysseus might meet the suitors as he was when he wrestled Philomeleides on Lesbos (4.341–346; see [[if-only-father-zeus]] and [[odysseus-wrestles-philomeleides]]). Merry quotes Gladstone: Pallas and Apollo alone are invoked with Zeus in this formula, which gives a peculiar solemnity to some paramount desire; Nitzsch would limit it to wishes beyond the speaker's hope (on 4.341; see [[apollo]] and [[zeus]]).

**Mentor, or a god.** In Ithaca Noemon tells the suitors that he saw Mentor going on board as the leader of Telemachus' crew, “Mentor, or a god, who was like him in every way”, and that he saw brilliant Mentor in Ithaca the next morning (4.653–656; see [[noemon-son-of-phronius]] and [[mentor]]). The guess is right: Athena went on board in Mentor's likeness and sat in the stern beside Telemachus (2.399–401, 2.416–418; see [[in-the-likeness-of-mentor]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). Medon does not know “whether some god roused him” to go to Pylos (4.712); the audience knows that she did (1.88–89).

**Penelope's prayer.** When the herald has told Penelope of the ambush, Eurycleia confesses that she helped Telemachus go, and counsels: bathe, put on clean clothes, go up to the upper room with your women, “and pray to Athena, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus; / for then she might save him even from death” (4.750–753; see [[eurycleia]]). The barley grains in the basket are not burned: they are poured out as wine is poured from its cup, more like a libation (Merry on 4.761, against Nitzsch, who took it for a smoke-offering of holy barley; see [[barley-grains]]). The invocation, “Hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone”, is Diomedes' before his great day in the Iliad (*Iliad* 5.115) and Odysseus' in Athena's grove outside the town of the Phaeacians (6.324); Athena is supplicated here, Merry notes, in the character in which she appears in the Iliad, as the war-goddess (on 4.762; see [[atrytone]], [[child-of-aegis-bearing-zeus]] and [[hear-me]]). The claim is Chryses' to Apollo, “or if ever I burned for you the fat thigh-pieces” (*Iliad* 1.40; see [[thigh-pieces]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]), here made on a husband's behalf for a son. ὀλόλυξε, “she raised the cry”, is the women's cry at a prayer or a sacrifice (3.450; *Iliad* 6.301; Merry on 4.767; see [[ololyge]]). When the Trojan women raise it to Athena in the Iliad, she refuses: “So she spoke in prayer, but Pallas Athena shook her head” (*Iliad* 6.311). Here “the goddess heard her prayer” (4.767; see [[prayer-type-scene]]), and the suitors, hearing the cry, take it for a sign that the queen is preparing a marriage (4.768–771; Merry on 4.768).

**The escort.** The answer comes that night. “Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena thought of another thing” (4.795; see [[thought-of-another-thing]] and [[gray-eyed]]): she makes a phantom in the likeness of Penelope's sister Iphthime and sends it to stop her weeping (4.796–801; see [[eidolon]] and [[iphthime]]). It tells her that the gods will not let her grieve, since her son will come home (4.805–807), and names the god of her prayer: “for such an escort goes with him, one whom other men too / have prayed to stand beside them, for she has the power, / Pallas Athena; and she pities you in your mourning; / and she has sent me now to tell you this” (4.826–829). πομπός, “escort”, is the word of Hermes sent with Priam, “for such an escort will go with you, the slayer of Argus” (*Iliad* 24.182), and παρεστάμεναι, “to stand beside”, is the verb of Nestor's wonder at Troy, where Pallas Athena “openly stood beside” Odysseus (3.222). Of Odysseus she lets the phantom say nothing (4.836–837; see [[it-is-bad-to-speak-empty-wind]]).
