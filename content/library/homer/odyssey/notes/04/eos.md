summary: Dawn begins the sixth day of the poem, the morning of Menelaus' tale, and twice brings a day in the tale itself, on Pharos. Peisistratus remembers his brother Antilochus, whom “the splendid son of shining Dawn” killed at Troy, and the next morning, he says, will come for talk.

**The son of Dawn.** When Menelaus' grief for Odysseus has set them all weeping, Peisistratus weeps for his own brother: “for in his heart he remembered noble Antilochus, / whom the splendid son of shining Dawn had killed” (4.187–188; see [[antilochus]] and [[peisistratus]]). The killer is not named. He is [[Memnon]], king of the eastern Ethiopians, whom Achilles killed to avenge Antilochus, as Merry explains, citing Pindar (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.188; see [[ethiopians]] and [[nestor]]). The Odyssey names him once, when Odysseus tells Achilles in the underworld that Eurypylus was the handsomest man he saw “after brilliant Memnon” (μετὰ Μέμνονα δῖον, 11.522). φαεινή, “shining”, is Dawn's epithet here (see [[phaeinos]]).

**There will be dawn as well.** Peisistratus ends his speech with a reason to stop weeping: “for I, for my part, / take no delight in mourning after supper; there will be early-born / Dawn as well” (4.193–195; see [[early-born]] and the translation's note on 4.194). The morning will bring its own time for grief and for talk, and Menelaus agrees: “there will be talk at dawn too / for Telemachus and me” (4.214–215).

**Three dawns.** ἦμος δʼ ἠριγένεια φάνη ῥοδοδάκτυλος Ἠώς, “And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered”, comes three times in the book (see [[dawn-line]] and [[rosy-fingered]]). The first opens the sixth day of the poem: Menelaus rises from his bed, puts on his clothes and sword, and goes to sit beside Telemachus (4.306–311; see [[menelaus]] and [[dressing-scene]]). The other two are dawns of the tale he tells. On Pharos Eidothea promises to lead him to the seals “when dawn appears” (4.407), and at the first dawn he goes along the shore, imploring the gods, with three companions (4.431–434; see [[eidothea]] and [[proteus]]); at the second, after the old man's prophecy and a night on the shore, they drag the ships down into the shining sea and sail back to the river Aegyptus (4.576–582; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). The book has no dawn in Ithaca. The scenes there fall on the fourth day of the poem, before the day in Sparta, and end at night, with Penelope's dream and the suitors' ship sailing out to lie in ambush (4.625–847; see the translation's note on 4.655–656).
