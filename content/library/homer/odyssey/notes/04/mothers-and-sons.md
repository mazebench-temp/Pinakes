---
summary: Penelope learns that her son has sailed: “now the storm winds have snatched away my beloved son / from the halls without report” (4.727–728). Eurycleia kept it from her under oath, she prays to Athena to save him, and she lies fasting until the phantom of her sister tells her that he will come home (4.742–841). At Sparta Helen remembers the child she left (4.263).
refs: [4.263, 4.325, 4.703-710, 4.727-734, 4.746-749, 4.765, 4.770-771, 4.787-794, 4.806-807, 4.817-823]

**The son gone without a word.** Medon tells Penelope that the suitors mean to kill her son on his way home from Pylos and Sparta (4.697–702; see [[medon-the-herald]]). She did not know he had gone. Her knees and dear heart are loosened, speechlessness takes her, and when she can speak she asks why he had to board a ship, “Was it so that not even his name should be left among men?” (4.703–710; see [[kleos]]). Among her women she laments a husband lost and now a son: “now the storm winds have snatched away my beloved son / from the halls without report, and I did not hear of his setting out” (4.727–728). Had she known, he would have stayed, “or he would have left me dead in the halls” (4.732–734; see [[lament]] and [[penelope]]). Her words for the son are Telemachus' for his father in Book 1: “But now the Harpies have snatched him away without report” (νῦν δέ μιν ἀκλειῶς ἅρπυιαι ἀνηρείψαντο, 1.241; see the translation's note on 1.241); in Book 20 storm winds and Harpies carry off the daughters of Pandareus (20.66, 20.77).

**The nurse's oath.** Eurycleia, his nurse, confesses that she gave him the bread and wine, and that he took a great oath from her not to tell his mother before the twelfth day, or until she missed him herself, “so that you might not mar your fair skin with weeping” (4.742–749; see [[eurycleia]] and [[oath]]). She sends Penelope to pray, and Penelope asks Athena, by the thigh-pieces Odysseus burned to her, to “save my dear son” (4.759–766; see [[athena]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). In the hall the suitors say that the much-wooed queen is preparing a marriage for them, and “does not know that slaughter has been made ready for her son” (4.768–771; see [[the-suitors]]).

**For him I mourn even more.** Fasting in her upper room, she ponders whether her son will escape death, like a lion encircled by men (4.787–794; see [[fasting]] and [[lion]]). To the phantom of her sister she says that she mourns the son even more than the husband, “a child, knowing nothing well either of toils or of assemblies”, and fears for him on land and on the sea (4.817–823; see [[nepios]] and [[iphthime]]); the phantom has already told her that he will come home, “for he is no offender against the gods” (4.806–807). Her fear is the counterpart of Telemachus' grief at Sparta for his father (see [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**Other mothers.** Telemachus' father, as he says to Menelaus, was born to misery: “for beyond all men his mother bore him to misery” (4.325, = 3.95). Helen, in her remorse, remembers the bridal chamber, the husband and the child she left when Aphrodite led her to Troy (4.261–264; see [[helen]] and [[hermione-daughter-of-helen]]); and Megapenthes is Menelaus' son by a slave woman, since Helen bore no child after Hermione (4.10–14; see [[megapenthes]]).
