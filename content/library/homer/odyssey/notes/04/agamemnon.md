summary: Agamemnon is the brother whom Menelaus mourns among his riches, killed in secret while he wandered (4.90–92). Proteus tells his end: Hera saved him on the voyage home, he landed and kissed his native soil, and Aegisthus, warned by a lookout, invited him to a feast and killed him “as one kills an ox at the manger” (4.512–537). In Egypt Menelaus heaps up a mound for him, so that his fame might be unquenchable (4.584).

**The brother.** Menelaus answers Telemachus' wonder at his house with the cost of it: “While I, gathering much substance in those lands, / was wandering, meanwhile another man killed my brother / in secret, unawares, by the guile of his ruinous wife; / so it is with no joy that I am lord of these possessions” (4.90–93; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]] and [[possessions]]). He names neither the killer nor the brother. ἀνωιστί, “unawares”, from οἴω, “think”, is like Latin *necopinato* (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.92). The wife's guile, δόλῳ οὐλομένης ἀλόχοιο, is Menelaus' charge; in Proteus' account she has no part (see [[clytemnestra]] and [[dolos]]).

**Saved by Hera.** “But your brother escaped the dooms and got away from them / in his hollow ships; for queen Hera saved him” (4.512–513; see [[hera]] and [[queenly]]). σὸς δὲ … ἀδελφεός, “your brother”, answers Αἴας μέν, “Ajax”, at the head of the account (Merry on 4.512; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]), and Hera saved him from the storm that Athena raised against the Achaeans on their way home (Merry on 4.513; 5.108–109). In the Iliad Hera names Argos, Sparta and wide-streeted Mycenae as the three cities dearest to her (*Iliad* 4.51–52; see [[mycenae]]).

**The landing.**

{{quote:bc5ab78b-ee66-5092-89f5-53a66980066e}}

The storm near Malea carried him “to the far edge of the land” where Thyestes had lived and Aegisthus lived now (4.514–518; see [[malea]], [[thyestes]] and the translation's note on 4.514–520). When the gods turned the wind back he came home rejoicing; for the custom of kissing one's native soil Merry refers to Livy (on 4.522, citing Livy 1.56). Odysseus will kiss the grain-giving earth of Ithaca when he knows it again (13.353–354, a plain rendering), and the shade of Agamemnon will warn him to bring his ship home in secret, since women can no longer be trusted (11.454–456; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).

**The feast.** A lookout posted by Aegisthus had watched for a year “lest he should pass by unseen, and remember his furious courage” (4.527; see [[furious]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]). Aegisthus “went to invite Agamemnon, shepherd of the people, / with horses and chariot, pondering shameful things; / and he led him up, unaware of his destruction, and killed him / when he had feasted him, as one kills an ox at the manger” (4.532–535; see [[aegisthus]], [[shepherd-of-the-people]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). ἀνήγαγε is “brought him up” from the shore to the city (Merry on 4.534). Ἀτρεΐδεω, “of the son of Atreus” (4.536), is the only time in the book that the patronymic means Agamemnon: everywhere else the son of Atreus is Menelaus, the brother who lived (4.51, 4.185, 4.304, and in the addresses of 4.156, 4.190, 4.235, 4.291, 4.316, 4.492 and 4.594), as is the Ἀτρέος υἱέ, “son of Atreus”, of Proteus' questions (4.462, 4.543; see [[son-of-atreus]], [[atreus]] and [[menelaus]]).

**The mound.** Menelaus learns the news on the sand of Pharos and weeps until he has had his fill (4.538–541). When he has gone back to the river of Egypt and appeased the gods, “I heaped up a tomb for Agamemnon, so that his fame might be unquenchable” (4.584; see [[tomb]], [[kleos]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). A scholium calls the mound a cenotaph, an empty tomb, and says that Menelaus wrote on a stone Agamemnon's name, the cause of his death, and where and how he suffered it, but Merry answers, after Lowe, that this was no age of inscriptions and a simple mound was enough, and he takes the passage as a witness to the ancient custom of raising cairns and barrows to keep a memory alive where there was no written record (on 4.584). In the last book the shade of Agamemnon describes the great mound that the army heaped for Achilles on a headland, to be seen from the sea by men now living and to come (24.80–84, a plain rendering; see [[achilles]]).
