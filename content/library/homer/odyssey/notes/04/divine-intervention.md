summary: In Sparta the gods act only in men's tales: a god brings Helen to the Wooden Horse and Athena leads her away; the gods hold Menelaus in Egypt, a goddess pities him and teaches him to catch a god, Poseidon saves and drowns Ajax, Hera saves Agamemnon, and the immortals send Menelaus home and will send him to the Elysian plain. In Ithaca the goddess acts herself: she hears Penelope's prayer and sends her a phantom in a dream.

**In the tales.** No god comes to Sparta in this book; the gods' work is told. Menelaus says that the god himself must have begrudged him and Odysseus their life together, and made Odysseus alone a man without a homecoming (4.181–182; see [[theos]]). Helen says that Zeus gives good and evil to one and to another, for he can do all things (4.236–237; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]). Menelaus explains her visit to the Wooden Horse by a god: “some god must have bidden you, / a god who meant to hold out glory to the Trojans”, and ends with another, “until Pallas Athena led you away” (4.274–275, 4.289; see [[daimon]] and [[athena]]). Merry takes it that Helen came from curiosity, not meaning to betray the heroes, and that had she drawn them out the Trojans would have won the glory of victory: hence the god (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.274).

**The tale of Egypt.** Menelaus' tale of his homecoming is a chain of divine acts. The gods hold him back in Egypt for the hecatombs he owes them (4.351–353, 4.360); “one of the gods pitied me and saved me”, the daughter of Proteus (4.364–366; see [[eidothea]]), who saves the ambush again with ambrosia (4.444–446; see [[ambrosia]]); and the god whom she teaches him to catch, once he is caught, asks “Which of the gods … helped you to devise this plan” (4.462; see [[proteus]]). The old man's account of the returns is of gods too: Poseidon saves Ajax from the sea and drowns him for his boast, and Hera saves Agamemnon (4.500–513; see [[poseidon]] and [[hera]]); the gods turn the wind back, and he comes home to be murdered (4.520; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). When Menelaus has made his sacrifice and “ended the anger of the gods who live forever”, the immortals give him a fair wind (4.583–586), and they will send him at last to the Elysian plain (4.561–564; see [[elysium]]).

**In Ithaca.** The herald does not know “whether some god roused him”, or whether Telemachus' own heart urged him to go to Pylos (4.712–713; see [[medon-the-herald]]); the audience knows that Athena put might and daring in his heart in Book 1 (1.88–89, 1.320–321). Penelope prays to Athena, “and the goddess heard her prayer” (4.767); that night Athena “thought of another thing” and sent the phantom of Iphthime to tell her that her son has the goddess for his escort (4.795–829; see [[eidolon]] and [[dream]]). The same night the suitors sail out to lie in wait for him (4.842–847).

**Weddings and children.** The book's first lines put the gods in a house as well: “now the gods were bringing their wedding to pass”, and “to Helen the gods brought no more offspring to light” (4.7, 4.12; see [[olympians]]).
