summary: Mortals meet gods in Book 4 and know them only in part. On Pharos Menelaus knows Eidothea for a goddess but not which, until she names her father; in Ithaca Noemon has seen “Mentor, or a god”, and Penelope asks the phantom of her sister whether it is a god. The god Proteus, for his part, does not see the men hidden among his seals.

**Whoever you are of the goddesses.** The goddess who meets Menelaus alone on Pharos does not say who she is, and he answers her “whoever you are of the goddesses” (4.376; see [[eidothea]]). He knows that she is a goddess, but not which; he learns it only when she speaks of “immortal Proteus of Egypt” and adds, “and they say that he is my father, who begot me” (4.385–387; see [[proteus]]).

**The god who does not see.** The capture of Proteus turns the theme about. Menelaus fears that the old man may “see me first, or know beforehand, and avoid me” (4.396), and the goddess hides him under a sealskin. When the old man comes at midday he counts the men first among his beasts, “and had no thought / in his heart that there was a trick” (4.452–453): Merry sets this against “the gods know all things” (on 4.379; see [[the-gods-know-all-things]]). Caught, Proteus sees at once that a god must be behind it, and asks, “Which of the gods, son of Atreus, helped you to devise this plan” (4.462).

**Mentor, or a god.** In Ithaca Noemon has seen the leader of Telemachus' crew go on board, “Mentor, or a god, who was like him in every way”, and yet saw brilliant Mentor in Ithaca the next morning: “But at this I marvel” (4.653–656; see [[mentor]], [[athena]] and [[in-the-likeness-of-mentor]]). He has the right answer among his two guesses and cannot choose between them. The suitors do not ask which it was.

**A god's voice.** Penelope, asleep, takes the phantom that Athena sends for her sister Iphthime, who has never visited her before (4.810–811). When it tells her that Pallas Athena has sent it, she answers, “If you are a god and have heard a god's voice” (4.831), and asks for news of her husband (see [[eidolon]] and [[iphthime]]). She is nearer the truth than before, but not at it: the phantom is neither her sister nor a god, but a likeness that a god has made.
