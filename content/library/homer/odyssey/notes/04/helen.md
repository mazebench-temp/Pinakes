summary: Helen comes from her chamber like Artemis, with the gifts of Egyptian Thebes, and at once knows Telemachus by his likeness to his father; she weeps with the others, casts the Egyptian drug into the wine, and tells how she alone knew Odysseus when he came into Troy disguised as a beggar. Menelaus answers with the night of the Horse, when she called the chieftains in their wives' voices. Proteus says that Menelaus will go to the Elysian plain because he has her as his wife (4.121–305, 4.569).

**Like Artemis.** While Menelaus ponders, “Helen came out of her fragrant high-roofed chamber, / like Artemis of the golden distaff” (4.121–122; see [[artemis]] and [[of-the-golden-distaff]]). Her three women bring a chair, a rug and the silver work basket on wheels, and across it lies the golden distaff; both were gifts of Alcandre, the wife of Polybus of Egyptian Thebes (4.123–135; see [[adreste]], [[alcippe]], [[phylo]], [[alcandre]] and [[polybus-of-thebes]]). The epithet of Artemis answers to the distaff she carries, though Merry, from the epithets it keeps in the Iliad, would render it “with golden arrows” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.122; see the translation's note on 4.122). The scholia report that Rhianus and Aristophanes read Ἑλένης for Ἑλένῃ at 4.12 (Merry's critical note on 4.12).

**She knows the son.** She speaks before her husband has made up his mind:

{{quote:b895960e-cc85-5c44-ab59-5e74c9908140}}

ψεύσομαι, ἦ ἔτυμον ἐρέω; is not a question whether she is mistaken: Merry, from the Iliad's verse, takes the choice to be between speaking the truth and saying nothing (on 4.140; see the translation's note on 4.140). Women are quick to see the likeness of children to their parents, as Athenaeus remarks (5.190d), and Merry sets the readiness of Helen against the natural slowness and indecision of Menelaus, whom the Spartan girls of Theocritus' wedding song tease for going to sleep so early (on 4.141, quoting Athenaeus and Theocritus 18.9). The name she gives herself, κυνῶπις, “dog-faced” (4.145), is the word of her self-reproach in the Iliad (*Iliad* 3.180; see [[dog-face]]), and the war is “for my sake” (see [[the-abduction-of-helen]]). When Menelaus' wish for Odysseus rouses the longing for lament, Ἀργείη Ἑλένη, Διὸς ἐκγεγαυῖα, “Argive Helen wept, child of Zeus” (4.184; see [[argive-helen]], [[daughter-of-zeus]] and [[the-longing-for-lament]]).

**The drug and the tale.** Then “Helen, child of Zeus, thought of another thing”: she casts into the wine the drug against grief that Polydamna, the wife of Thon, gave her in Egypt (4.219–232; see [[nepenthe]], [[polydamna]] and [[thought-of-another-thing]]), and bids them feast and take delight in stories, for Zeus gives good and evil now to one and now to another (4.235–239; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]). Her story is of Odysseus in Troy, marred with blows and in rags, whom she alone knew; she bathed and anointed him, and swore not to betray him until he was back at the ships (4.240–258; see [[odysseus-enters-troy-as-a-beggar]] and [[odysseus]]). The disguised stranger had his bath from Helen herself, and she promised only not to tell before he was safe, Merry notes (on 4.252, 4.254). When he had killed many Trojans and gone back, the Trojan women wailed, but she rejoiced:

{{quote:35314b76-b702-5059-be4c-32a39cf0c8ef}}

Merry finds in the passage the poet's way of joining good understanding with right conduct: Helen's sin is a blinding of the mind, ἄτη, sent by Aphrodite (on 4.261; see [[ate]] and [[aphrodite]]), and her heart had turned to home, as in the Iliad's passages of her remorse that Nitzsch gathers (*Iliad* 3.139 and after, 3.383–444, 6.349 and after, 24.761 and after, as Merry reports him on 4.260). The last verse, that she left a husband who lacked nothing in mind or form, is a flattery that pleases him, Merry says, as his answer shows (on 4.264).

**At the Horse.** Menelaus answers with another story of Odysseus, in which Helen does not come off so well: some god must have bidden her, he says, who meant to give the Trojans glory, when she came to the Horse with Deiphobus, walked round it three times feeling it, and called the best of the Danaans by name, “making your voice like the voices of the wives of all the Argives” (4.274–279; see [[deiphobus]] and [[daimon]]). Odysseus held the others back and closed Anticlus' mouth with his hands until Pallas Athena led her away (4.280–289; see [[anticlus]]). Merry, after Eustathius, takes the god for Menelaus' generous account of her act, and thinks she came to the Horse from curiosity, not to betray the heroes; her power of mimicry reminds him of the Delian maidens, who know how to imitate the voices of all men (on 4.264, 4.274; *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* 162–164). The later legend that Deiphobus married her after the death of Paris is in two verses of Euripides' *Trojan Women*, bracketed in the text (959–960; Merry on 4.276; see [[euripides]]).

**Night, and the promise.** Telemachus asks for bed, and Ἀργείη Ἑλένη bids the slave women make up beds in the colonnade (4.296–301; see [[bed]]); she lies down beside her husband in the inner room, πὰρ δʼ Ἑλένη τανύπεπλος ἐλέξατο, δῖα γυναικῶν, “and beside him lay Helen of the long robes, brilliant among women” (4.305; see [[long-robed]] and [[brilliant]]). She is not seen again in the book, but she is in Proteus' prophecy: Menelaus will be sent to the Elysian plain, “because you have Helen as your wife, and in their eyes you are the son-in-law of Zeus” (4.569; see [[menelaus]] and [[elysium]]).
