**At Sparta.** Telemachus tells his errand with the suitors first: “the house is full of hostile men, who are always / slaughtering my thronging sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns, / the suitors of my mother, overweening in their arrogance” (4.319–321; see [[hostile-men]] and [[hybris]]). Menelaus is “deeply troubled”: “Ah, truly it was in the bed of a fierce-hearted man / that they wished to lie, weaklings as they are” (4.333–334), and he likens them to the fawns a hind lays in a lion's lair, on whom the lion comes back to bring a shameful doom (4.335–340; see [[the-hind-in-the-lions-lair]]). If only Odysseus would come among them such as he was in Lesbos, “they would all be swift-fated, and bitter would their wedding be” (4.346; see [[odysseus-wrestles-philomeleides]]).

**At their games.** In Ithaca “the suitors, in front of the hall of Odysseus, / amused themselves throwing the discus and the javelin / on the leveled ground, where they used to, in their arrogance” (4.625–627; see [[discus]]), verses that come again in Book 17 (17.167–169) and echo the Myrmidons at their games beside the ships while Achilles sulks (*Iliad* 2.774). The words ὅθι περ πάρος, “where they used to”, are a parenthesis (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.627). Noemon's question tells them that Telemachus has gone to Pylos, which they had not believed (4.630–640; see [[noemon-son-of-phronius]]); Antinous and Eurymachus make them sit down together and stop their games (4.659), and Antinous asks for a ship and twenty men to lie in wait for the boy in the strait between Ithaca and Samos. “So he spoke, and they all approved and bade him do it” (4.673; see [[antinous]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).

**Overheard.** They brood their plans deep in their minds, but the herald Medon hears them from outside the courtyard (4.676–678; see [[medon-the-herald]]). Penelope curses them as men who gather to waste her son's substance, forgetting the king who never wronged any of their fathers: “But your spirit and your shameful deeds / are plain to see, and there is no thanks afterward for good deeds done” (4.694–695; see [[penelope]] and [[no-thanks-for-good-deeds]]). She prays Athena to “ward off the suitors, wickedly overweening” (4.766; see [[overweening]]).

ὁμάδησαν marks the sudden clamor when they heard Penelope's cry and mistook its meaning, not the usual noise of their feasting (Merry on 4.768). Antinous silences them, lest someone report their talk inside, and chooses twenty of the best men; they launch the ship, fit it out, and wait for evening (4.773–786). Penelope, fasting in her upper room, ponders whether her son will escape death “or be brought down by the overbearing suitors” (4.790; see [[overbearing]]), and the phantom of her sister cannot deny that “many enemies are plotting against him” (4.822; see [[iphthime]]).

**The ambush.** “But the suitors embarked and sailed over the watery ways, / pondering in their minds sheer slaughter for Telemachus” (4.842–843), and at the rocky island of Asteris, between Ithaca and Samos, “there the Achaeans waited for him, lying in ambush” (4.844–847; see [[asteris]]). The poem leaves them there until Telemachus comes home by another way in Book 15.
