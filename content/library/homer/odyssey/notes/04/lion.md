---
summary: Three lions in Book 4. Menelaus likens Odysseus to a strong lion who comes home to his lair and kills the fawns that a hind has laid there, as Odysseus will kill the suitors (4.333–340); Proteus, seized on the shore of Pharos, turns first into “a bearded lion” (4.456); and Penelope, sleepless with fear for her son, is likened to a lion that ponders in a ring of hunters (4.791–793).
---

**The lion comes home.** When Telemachus has told how the suitors are eating up his house, Menelaus answers that they wished to lie in the bed of a fierce-hearted man, weaklings as they are (4.333–334; see [[fierce-hearted]]), and gives the simile of the hind that lays her fawns to sleep “in the thicket of a strong lion”, κρατεροῖο λέοντος (4.335; see [[the-hind-in-the-lions-lair]] and [[deer]]). The lion is not shown hunting. He comes back to his own lair, ἑὴν … εὐνήν, and finds the fawns in it (4.338), as Odysseus, Menelaus foretells, will come home and find the suitors in his house (4.340; see [[odysseus]] and [[the-suitors]]). Telemachus repeats the words to his mother when he is home (17.124–131).

**A bearded lion.** When Menelaus and his men seize Proteus, “first of all he became a bearded lion, / and then a serpent, and a leopard, and a great boar” (4.456–457; see [[proteus]]). λέων … ἠυγένειος is the only bearded lion of the Odyssey; the Iliad's three are λὶς ἠϋγένειος, with the rarer word for the beast (*Iliad* 15.275, 17.109, 18.318). Merry suspects that the epithet is a lengthened form of εὐγενής, “noble”, rather than a word for the beard (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.456; see the translation's note on 4.456 and [[bearded]]). The lion is the first of the changes, and the men hold it fast with the rest (4.459; see [[serpent]], [[leopard]] and [[boar]]).

**Penelope's lion.** At the end of the book Penelope, who has learned of the ambush laid for her son, lies fasting in her upper room, “And as much as a lion ponders in a throng of men / in fear, when they draw their crafty circle around him, / so much was she pondering” until sleep comes (4.791–793; see [[the-lion-encircled]] and [[penelope]]). Merry, after Eustathius, notes that she is likened not to a bold lion but to a frightened one (on 4.791). The book thus has two lions of Ithaca: the husband who will come home to kill, and the wife who lies hemmed in by the same men. In her laments Penelope twice calls Odysseus θυμολέων, “lion-hearted” (4.724, 4.814; see [[lion-hearted]]).
