summary: The gods held Menelaus in Egypt “since I had not offered them perfect hecatombs; / and the gods always wished their commands to be remembered” (4.352–353); Proteus sends him back to the river Aegyptus to offer them, and the gods give him a fair wind home (4.472–480, 4.581–586). Penelope prays to Athena by the thigh-pieces Odysseus burned to her, “remember these things now for me, and save my dear son” (4.763–765), the words in which Telemachus asked Menelaus to remember his father's services (4.328–331).

**The hecatombs not offered.** Menelaus begins his tale with the cause of his delay: the gods held him in Egypt, eager though he was to come home, because he had not offered them τεληέσσας ἑκατόμβας (4.351–352; see [[hecatomb]]). Merry takes τεληέσσας not as “perfect” victims but, like other epithets in -εις, as “effective” or “acceptable” sacrifices, which win an accomplishment (τέλος) from the gods (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.352). The next verse, “and the gods always wished their commands to be remembered”, Zenodotus rejected, asking what commands there had been; Merry calls it a saying added by a later rhapsode (see the translation's note on 4.353). Proteus tells him the same: he ought to have offered fine sacrifices to Zeus and the other gods before he embarked, and he will not see his home until he goes back to the river and offers holy hecatombs; “and then the gods will grant you the journey you have in mind” (4.472–480; see [[proteus]]). He does so, “And when I had ended the anger of the gods who live forever”, the immortals gave him a fair wind (4.581–586; see [[cholos]] and [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]).

**Remember these things now.** Penelope's prayer rests on a debt. With barley grains in a basket she prays to Athena: “if ever Odysseus of many wiles in his halls / burned to you the fat thigh-pieces of ox or sheep, / remember these things now for me, and save my dear son” (4.762–765; see [[athena]], [[prayer]] and [[thigh-pieces]]), and the goddess hears her (4.767). Telemachus had asked Menelaus in the same form, a man's service in the place of a sacrifice: “if ever my brave father Odysseus / promised you any word or deed and fulfilled it / in the land of the Trojans, where you Achaeans suffered woes, / remember these things now for me, and tell me truly” (4.328–331; see [[supplication]]). Chryses opens his prayer in the Iliad with the same appeal to past offerings (*Iliad* 1.39–41).

**Libations, remembering me.** Menelaus offers Telemachus a fine goblet, “so that you may pour libations to the gods, / the immortals, remembering me all your days” (4.591–592; see [[libation]] and [[xenia]]): the gift will bind guest and host each time the guest gives the gods their share.
