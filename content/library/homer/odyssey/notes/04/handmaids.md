**At Sparta.** The slave women wash and anoint the strangers, a handmaid brings the water for their hands, and “the revered housekeeper” brings bread and dainties (4.49–56; see [[bathing]] and [[water-for-the-hands]]). Helen does not come in alone: “with her came Adreste and set for her a well-made chair, / and Alcippe brought a rug of soft wool, / and Phylo brought a silver basket” (4.123–125; see [[adreste]], [[alcippe]], [[phylo]] and [[basket]]), and “the handmaid Phylo” sets the basket beside her (4.133). The scholia report that some read ἅμα δρήστη, “with her a servant”, for ἅμʼ Ἀδρήστη (W. W. Merry's critical note on 4.123). At bedtime Helen bids the slave women set the beds beneath the colonnade, “and the women went out from the hall with torches in their hands, / and spread the beds” (4.296–301; see [[bed]] and [[torches]]), as Achilles' women made up beds for Priam (*Iliad* 24.643–648).

**In Ithaca.** When Medon has told her of the plot, Penelope sits on the threshold of her chamber, “lamenting piteously; and around her the slave women whimpered, / all who were in the house, young and old” (4.719–720; see [[penelope]] and [[threshold]]). Merry recalls that the poem later gives their number as fifty (22.421; *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.720). She turns on them: “Hard-hearted women, not even you, any of you, thought in your minds / to rouse me from my bed, though you knew it well in your hearts, / when he went on board the black hollow ship” (4.729–731). Merry renders σχέτλιαι “hard-hearted maidens” (on 4.729). She asks that someone call old Dolius, “my slave, whom my father gave me when I came here”, to tell Laertes (4.735–738; see [[dolius]]). It is the nurse who answers, “the dear nurse Eurycleia”: she knew it all, gave Telemachus his provisions and swore not to tell (4.742–749; see [[eurycleia]]). Then Penelope bathes and goes up to her upper room “with her handmaids” to pray (4.750–761; see [[upper-room]] and [[prayer]]).
