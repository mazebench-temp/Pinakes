**The flocks of Libya.** Menelaus ends the list of the lands of his wandering with Libya, “where the lambs are horned at once. / For the flocks bear young three times in the full year's round” (4.85–86; see [[lambs]], [[libya]] and [[the-horned-lambs-of-libya]]). There neither master nor shepherd lacks cheese, meat or sweet milk, “but the flocks always yield unfailing milk to drink” (4.87–89). μῆλα are the small livestock, sheep and goats together (see the Book 1 note). τελεσφόρον εἰς ἐνιαυτόν, “in the full year's round”, is the only phrase in which Homer uses the adjective; Merry takes it as a fixed epithet of the year, “the maturing year”, the period that brings the seasons and the crops round to completion (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.86). ἐπηετανόν, “unfailing”, was explained of old as “lasting out the year”, and by Curtius, after Döderlein, as “perpetual” (Merry on 4.89; see the translation's note on 4.89). Merry reports in his critical note that Bekker moved 4.86 to follow 4.89, to give the reason for the constant milk.

The marvel is fertility. Merry sets beside it the report, in the collection of marvels handed down under Aristotle's name, that among the Umbrians the flocks bear three times a year, and Virgil's Italy, where the flocks are twice with young, *bis gravidae pecudes* (on 4.85; [Aristotle], *On Marvellous Things Heard* 80; Virgil, *Georgics* 2.150). In the Odyssey's picture of the land of a just king the flocks bear young without fail, with wheat and barley and trees heavy with fruit (19.111–114, a plain rendering; see [[wheat]] and [[barley]]).

**Proteus' flock.** The old man of the sea lies down among his seals “like a herdsman among his flocks of sheep” (νομεὺς ὣς πώεσι μήλων, 4.413), and Menelaus' men take him by lying in his flock under sealskins (4.435–453; see [[seals]] and [[proteus]]). A πῶυ is a flock of sheep (see the general article).

**Sheep for the feast.** At Sparta the banqueters come to the king's house, “They drove sheep, and brought wine that makes men strong, / and their wives with lovely veils sent them bread” (4.622–623; see [[feast]]). Most editors since Wolf reject the lines as a patch, since a shared feast to which the guests bring their own food is another thing from the wedding feast of the book's opening, and Merry rejects Eustathius' view that they describe what was happening in Ithaca (on 4.621–624; see the translation's note on 4.620–624). In Ithaca the suitors are always “slaughtering my thronging sheep”, Telemachus tells Menelaus, in the verse of the first book (4.320 = 1.92; see [[the-suitors]] and the Book 1 note).

**Ox or sheep.** The suitors, learning that Telemachus has sailed, are amazed, for they thought he was on the farm, “with the flocks or with the swineherd” (4.640; see [[boar]]). Penelope, praying for him, asks Athena to remember whether Odysseus ever “burned to you the fat thigh-pieces of ox or sheep” (ἢ βοὸς ἢ ὄϊος, 4.764; see [[thigh-pieces]], [[cattle]] and [[prayer]]). The verse is the one in Nestor's prayer to Zeus at the ships, ἢ βοὸς ἢ οἰὸς κατὰ πίονα μηρία καίων (*Iliad* 15.373), with ἔκηε, “burned”, for καίων, “burning”.
