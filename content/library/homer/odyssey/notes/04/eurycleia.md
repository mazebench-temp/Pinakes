summary: When Penelope laments that no one told her of her son's going, the old nurse confesses that she knew, gave him his provisions and swore to say nothing for twelve days; she counsels her to bathe, go up with her women and pray to Athena, and not to trouble old Laertes, for the gods do not hate the line of Arceisius (4.742–758).

**The confession.** “Then the dear nurse Eurycleia spoke to her in turn” (4.742; see [[penelope]]): “Dear bride, kill me with the pitiless bronze / or let me be in the hall; but I will not hide my word from you” (4.743–744). σὺ μὲν ἄρ με κατάκτανε … ἢ ἔα means “whether you slay me or spare me, I will not hide what I have to say” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.743), and νύμφα, “bride”, is her name for the queen, a vocative with its final vowel short, as when Iris calls Helen νύμφα φίλη in the Iliad (Merry on 4.743, comparing *Iliad* 3.130). She tells what Book 2 showed: “I knew all this, and I gave him all that he bade me, / bread and sweet wine; and he took a great oath from me / not to tell you before the twelfth day had come, / or you yourself missed him and heard that he had gone, / so that you might not mar your fair skin with weeping” (4.745–749), the oath and the provisions of the storeroom, in the words Telemachus used (2.349–380; see [[telemachus]] and [[oath]]).

**The counsel.** Her advice turns the lament into a rite: “But bathe, and put clean clothes on your body, / and go up to your upper room with your handmaids / and pray to Athena, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus; / for then she might save him even from death” (4.750–753; see [[athena]], [[bathing]] and [[prayer]]). She would spare Laertes the news: “And do not afflict an afflicted old man; for I do not think / that to the blessed gods the offspring of the son of Arceisius / is altogether hateful; someone will still be left, I think, to hold / the high-roofed house and the rich fields far away” (4.754–757; see [[laertes]] and [[arceisius]]). “So she spoke, and lulled her lament, and checked her eyes from lament” (4.758; see [[lament]]), and Penelope bathes, goes up and prays, word for word as the nurse bade her (4.759–767).
