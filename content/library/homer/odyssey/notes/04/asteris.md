summary: The book ends at Asteris. The suitors sail out over the watery ways, and in the harbors of the little rocky island “between Ithaca and rocky Samos” they wait in ambush for Telemachus (4.842–847).

  - 4.842-847 | The ambush at Asteris

{{quote:4b0e8d02-8725-5572-9a2b-b95964be7a64}}

Antinous had asked for a swift ship and twenty companions, “so that I may lie in ambush for him on his way and watch for him / in the strait between Ithaca and rocky Samos” (4.669–671; see [[antinous]], [[the-suitors]] and [[samos]]); the island is where the plan is carried out. It is a place made for an ambush: small and rocky, out in the strait, with harbors where ships can lie, ναύλοχοι, “with an entrance on either side”, ἀμφίδυμοι, so that a ship lying there can come out either way (4.846–847; see [[harbor]]). The description is built like the Iliad's of Poseidon's stable under the sea, “There is a wide cave in the depths of the deep sea, / between Tenedos and rocky Imbros; / there Poseidon the earth-shaker halted his horses” (*Iliad* 13.32–34): a place set between two islands, and then what happens there. The men in the ship are “the Achaeans” in the last verse of the book, the name of the whole people (see [[achaeans]]).

The poem leaves them there and comes back to them in the fifteenth and sixteenth books, when Athena warns Telemachus of the ambush in the strait (15.28–29) and the suitors' ship comes home without him (16.351–357; see [[telemachus]]). Merry did not accept the identification of Asteris with the modern rock of Daskalio (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.846; see the translation's note on 4.846).
