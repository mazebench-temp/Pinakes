summary: Gold and silver fill the Sparta scenes: the golden pitcher, silver basin and golden cups of the welcome (4.53–58), the gold and silver among the splendors of Menelaus' halls (4.72–73), the Egyptian gifts, two silver bathtubs, ten talents of gold, a golden distaff and a silver basket rimmed with gold (4.125–132), and the parting gift, a mixing bowl all of silver with its rims finished with gold (4.615–616). In Menelaus' tale Aegisthus pays his lookout two talents of gold (4.525–526).

**A king's metals.** The guests wash from a golden pitcher over a silver basin and drink from golden cups (4.53, 4.58; see [[basin-and-pitcher]] and [[cup]]), and when they have eaten Telemachus whispers his wonder at “the flash of bronze throughout the echoing halls, / and of gold and amber and silver and ivory” (4.72–73; see [[house]], [[amber]], [[ivory]] and [[bronze]]). The comparison he draws, with the court of Olympian Zeus, Menelaus turns aside: no mortal could rival Zeus, and he himself has no joy in his possessions, gathered in eight years of wandering while his brother was murdered (4.74–93; see [[possessions]]).

**Gifts from Egypt.** The wealth came from abroad. Polybus of Thebes in Egypt gave Menelaus “two silver bathtubs / and two tripods, and ten talents of gold”, and his wife Alcandre gave Helen a golden distaff and a silver basket on wheels, “and its rims were finished with gold” (4.125–132; see [[polybus-of-thebes]], [[alcandre]], [[distaff]], [[basket]], [[tripod]] and [[talent]]). The ten talents of gold are a measure of the gifts the Iliad counts: Agamemnon offered Achilles “seven tripods untouched by fire, and ten talents of gold” (*Iliad* 9.122; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]).

**The silver bowl.** In place of the horses Telemachus will not take, Menelaus gives him a mixing bowl that “of silver / it is all, and its rims are finished with gold, / the work of Hephaestus” (4.615–617; see [[mixing-bowl]] and [[hephaestus]]). κεκράανται, “are finished” (4.616), is the word used of the golden rim of Helen's basket, κεκράαντο, “were finished” (4.132): W. W. Merry defends it, from κραίνω, “complete”, against the reading κεκέραστο, “are mixed”, since the poet speaks of a rim of gold added as a finish, not of the metal mixed (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.132). The Iliad's finest silver bowl was Sidonian work, which Achilles set as a prize at Patroclus' games (*Iliad* 23.740–747); this one was given to Menelaus by the king of the Sidonians (4.617–618; see [[phaedimus]] and [[sidonians]]).

**Gold for a murder.** Aegisthus posted a lookout to watch for Agamemnon's landing “and promised him as wages / two talents of gold” (4.525–526; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]).
