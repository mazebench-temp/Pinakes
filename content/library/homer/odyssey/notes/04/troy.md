---
summary: In Sparta Troy is the place of the past: where Menelaus promised his daughter to the son of Achilles (4.6), where the men perished for whom he would give two thirds of his wealth (4.97–99), where the Achaeans came for Helen's sake (4.145–146), the hostile city that Odysseus entered as a beggar, and the city of the wooden horse (4.240–289), and the point from which the returns began (4.488).
# Unnamed: "the wide-streeted city of hostile men" (4.246) and "the city of the Trojans" (4.249).
refs: [4.246, 4.249]
---

**The war's place.** The book's first mention of Troy is a promise: “for in Troy he had first promised and nodded his assent / that he would give her” (4.6–7), the betrothal of Hermione to the son of Achilles that the wedding at Sparta now fulfills (see [[hermione-daughter-of-helen]] and [[neoptolemus]]). Menelaus would live with a third of his wealth if the men were safe “who perished then / in wide Troy, far from horse-pasturing Argos” (4.98–99; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]), and Helen recalls the war as hers, “when for my sake, dog-faced as I am, you Achaeans / came to Troy, bent on bold war” (4.145–146; see [[helen]]).

**The city.** In Helen's story Odysseus “slipped into the wide-streeted city of hostile men” and “into the city of the Trojans” disguised as a beggar (4.246–249; see [[odysseus-enters-troy-as-a-beggar]] and [[wide-streeted]]), and in Menelaus' story Helen comes to the wooden horse with Deiphobus (4.271–289; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]]); the sack itself is not told (see [[the-fall-of-troy]]). The returns begin from it: Menelaus asks Proteus about the Achaeans “whom Nestor and I left behind when we went from Troy” (4.488), with Τροίηθεν ἰόντες, the verse-end of the homecomings (see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]).
