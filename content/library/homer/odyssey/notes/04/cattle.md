---
summary: The cattle of Book 4 are the fat chine of an ox that Menelaus gives his guests from his own portion (4.65–66), the shambling cattle that the suitors slaughter (4.320), the ox killed at the manger to which Agamemnon at Aegisthus' feast is likened (4.535), and the thigh-pieces of ox or sheep in Penelope's prayer (4.764).
---

**The chine of honor.** When the guests have bathed and sat down, Menelaus “set before them the fat chine of an ox, / lifting the roast meat in his hands, which they had set before himself as his portion of honor” (4.65–66; see [[geras]], [[feast]] and [[xenia]]). The chine is the honored portion: Agamemnon honors Ajax with the long chine (*Iliad* 7.321), and Eumaeus honors Odysseus in the same words (14.437). Merry, who gathers these places with Achilles' meal for the envoys (*Iliad* 9.207) and the portion of chine that Odysseus sends to the singer Demodocus (8.474–476), finds here the custom by which the kings of Sparta were given their portion from the public table; Menelaus, having eaten, gives his guests what remains of his own (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.65). γέρα is a plural of amplification, not made to agree with νῶτα (on 4.66).

**The suitors' slaughter.** In Telemachus' complaint the suitors are always “slaughtering my thronging sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns” (4.320), the verse of the first book (1.92; see the Book 1 note, [[shambling]] and [[with-curving-horns]]).

**An ox at the manger.** Proteus tells how Aegisthus brought Agamemnon up from the shore, “and he led him up, unaware of his destruction, and killed him / when he had feasted him, as one kills an ox at the manger” (4.534–535; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]], [[aegisthus]] and [[agamemnon]]). The ox at its manger is fed and does not suspect; the king is feasted and killed. Agamemnon's shade tells his own death in the same verse (11.411 = 4.535). Merry notes that ὥς τίς τε may stand for ὥς τέ τις, as in a lion simile of the Iliad (*Iliad* 17.61), and that κατέκτανε is the gnomic aorist (on 4.535). In the Iliad's similes cattle die under the axe and at the altar, and in the jaws of lions (see [[the-ox-and-the-axe]] and [[the-roped-ox]]); here the beast dies where it eats.

**Ox or sheep.** Penelope asks Athena to remember whether Odysseus ever “burned to you the fat thigh-pieces of ox or sheep” (4.764), in the words of Nestor's prayer at the ships (*Iliad* 15.373; see [[sheep]] and [[thigh-pieces]]).
