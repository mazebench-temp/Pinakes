summary: The book is set in two great houses. Menelaus' high-roofed house at Sparta gleams as of the sun or of the moon, and Telemachus whispers that the court of Zeus must be like it; Menelaus answers that no mortal can rival Zeus, whose house is immortal, and that he has no joy of his wealth (4.20–112). The guests sleep in the porch, the master in the inner part (4.296–305). In Ithaca the suitors plot inside the house of Odysseus while the herald listens from the courtyard; Penelope sinks down on the threshold of her chamber, goes up to her upper room to pray, and the phantom slips into her chamber past the bolt (4.625–841).

**The house like the sun.** The travelers stop in the doorway, πρόθυρα, which W. W. Merry takes for the entrance from outside into the court (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.20; see [[door]]). The attendants lean the chariot against “the gleaming walls”, ἐνώπια παμφανόωντα, a verse of Hera's chariot in the Iliad (4.42 = *Iliad* 8.435; see [[chariot]]); Merry, after Nitzsch and the scholia, takes them for the inner faces of the gateway of the court, shining with the sun that came through the open gate and was reflected from its pavement (on 4.42). Then the strangers are led into “the divine house”:

{{quote:04a83a7d-2fdf-5cd6-8bfb-235ad77482f6}}

The same verse of the gleam is said of the house of Alcinous (7.84). Merry distinguishes δόμος from δῶμα here: δῶμα is the whole of the inhabited buildings, apart from the court, and δόμος in its proper use the hall, though both can mean simply “abode” (on 4.43). They marvel κατὰ δῶμα, at one thing and another throughout the house, and then take their fill of looking, ὁρώμενοι, the leisurely gaze after the first glance (Merry on 4.44, 4.47).

**The court of Zeus.** When they have bathed and eaten, Telemachus whispers to Peisistratus:

{{quote:1fd864a2-dac3-54c9-a2e6-b5f3716601e7}}

The bronze, gold, amber, silver and ivory are the brightness of a king's house (see [[gold-and-silver]], [[amber]] and [[ivory]]). αὐλή in its proper sense is the court, and Merry supposes that Telemachus, after the splendors of the δῶμα, comes back to the court as he saw it on first entering, unless the word is used loosely for the dwelling itself, as Merry finds it in the Iliad and in the later “court of Zeus” of Aeschylus' *Prometheus Bound* (on 4.74, citing *Iliad* 24.452 and *Prometheus Bound* 122); Seleucus read τοιαῦτα δόμοις ἐν κτήματα κεῖται, avoiding the word (Merry's critical note, from Athenaeus; see the translation's note on 4.74). Menelaus overhears, and corrects the comparison: “Dear children, truly no mortal could rival Zeus; / for his house and his possessions are immortal” (4.78–79; see [[zeus]] and [[possessions]]). His own wealth he gathered in eight years of wandering, while another man killed his brother, “so it is with no joy that I am lord of these possessions” (4.81–93; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). He lost a house “that was very well appointed and held many good things” (4.95–96), which Merry takes of his house as it was before the war (on 4.94), and he would gladly live with a third of what he has if the men who died at Troy were safe (4.97–99).

**The house at night.** Helen comes out of her fragrant high-roofed chamber (4.121; see [[helen]]). At bedtime the slave women set beds “beneath the colonnade” (ὑπʼ αἰθούσῃ), the guests sleep “in the porch of the house”, and Menelaus “in the inner part of the high house”, with Helen beside him (4.296–305; see [[bed]]). The verses are those of Priam's night in Achilles' hut and of Nestor's house (4.297–302, as *Iliad* 24.644–647, 24.673; 4.304 = 3.402), and Merry compares the pleonasm ἐν προδόμῳ δόμου, “in the porch of the house”, with βοῶν ἐπιβουκόλος, “the herdsman of the cattle” (on 4.302; 3.422). The next morning Menelaus goes out of his chamber and sits beside Telemachus (4.310–311).

**Other houses.** Menelaus would have built Odysseus a house in Argos (4.174; see [[xenia]]). In Egyptian Thebes “the most treasures lie stored in the houses” (4.127; see [[thebes-in-egypt]]), and the king of the Sidonians sheltered Menelaus in his house (4.618; see [[phaedimus]]). Aegisthus lived in the house where Thyestes had lived before, and the companions of Agamemnon and the men of Aegisthus were killed “in the halls” (4.517–518, 4.537; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]); Odysseus weeps in the halls of Calypso (4.557; see [[calypso]]).

**The house of Odysseus.** “My house is being eaten up”, Telemachus tells Menelaus (ἐσθίεταί μοι οἶκος, 4.318), the farms impoverished by the food sent in daily for the suitors (Merry on 4.318; see [[the-suitors]]). In Ithaca the suitors play in front of the hall, and go into the house to plot; the herald Medon hears their counsels “as he stood outside the courtyard, while they inside were weaving their plan” (4.625, 4.674, 4.677–678; see [[medon-the-herald]] and the translation's note on 4.678). Penelope cannot bear to sit on a chair, “though there were many chairs in the house, / but she sat on the threshold of her chamber, worked with much toil” (4.717–718; see [[threshold]]); she goes up to her upper room to pray, and lies there without food while the suitors raise a din “throughout the shadowy halls” (4.750–768, 4.787; see [[upper-room]]). Eurycleia comforts her that someone will be left “to hold / the high-roofed house and the rich fields far away” (4.756–757; see [[eurycleia]]). At night the phantom goes into her chamber “past the strap of the bolt” and slips away “past the bolt of the doorpost” (4.802, 4.838; see [[eidolon]] and [[door]]).
