# Unnamed: the wish and the memory (4.341-342, 4.344-346).

**The wish.** Telemachus has told Menelaus of the suitors who devour his house (4.318–321; see [[the-suitors]]). Menelaus answers in anger: they wished to lie in the bed of a fierce-hearted man, weaklings as they are, and Odysseus will come upon them as a lion comes back to its lair and finds the fawns a hind has left there (4.333–340; see [[the-hind-in-the-lions-lair]] and [[lion]]). Then he turns the threat into a prayer, αἲ γάρ, Ζεῦ τε πάτερ καὶ Ἀθηναίη καὶ Ἄπολλον, “If only, father Zeus and Athena and Apollo” (4.341), the formula, Merry says after Gladstone, of a wish uttered with special solemnity, which Nitzsch would limit to wishes beyond the speaker's hope (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.341; see [[zeus]], [[athena]] and [[apollo]]).

**The memory.** The wish is shaped by a memory: τοῖος ἐών, οἷός ποτʼ ἐυκτιμένῃ ἐνὶ Λέσβῳ, “such as he was once in well-built Lesbos” (4.342; see [[lesbos]] and [[well-built]]). One scholium's lemma reads ἐν Ἀρίσβῃ, “in Arisbe”, for ἐνὶ Λέσβῳ (Merry's critical note on 4.342). ἐξ ἔριδος, “out of strife”, is “in a match” or “after a challenge” (Merry on 4.343; see [[eris]]), and “all the Achaeans rejoiced” when he threw his man down (4.344; see [[achaeans]] and [[philomeleides]]). The match was a thing to be watched, like Odysseus' bout with Ajax at the games for Patroclus, which ended in a draw (*Iliad* 23.700–739).

**Swift-fated.** The last two verses are the wish of Athena, in the likeness of Mentes, in the first book: “if only Odysseus, such as that, would engage the suitors: / they would all be swift-fated, and bitter would their wedding be” (4.345–346 = 1.265–266). There the memory was of Odysseus in Mentes' house, on his way back from Ephyra, where he had gone for a man-slaying drug for his arrows (1.255–264; see [[odysseus-seeks-poison]]); here it is of the wrestler. Telemachus repeats Menelaus' whole speech to his mother when he comes home, and the wish with it (17.124–137).
