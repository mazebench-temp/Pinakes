---
summary: Menelaus lays Odysseus' lost homecoming on “the god himself”, who begrudged it (4.181–182), and Helen's coming to the Horse on “some god” who meant to give the Trojans glory (4.274–275); Helen blames Aphrodite for her blind folly (4.261–262). Menelaus knows that the gods held him in Egypt for hecatombs not offered (4.351–353, 4.377–378); Ajax perishes for his own boast (4.502–511). In Ithaca Medon cannot tell whether a god roused Telemachus or his own heart (4.712–713).

**A god begrudged it.** Menelaus would have given Odysseus a city in Argos and lived beside him until death, “But the god himself, it seems, was to begrudge us this, / who has made that unhappy man alone without a homecoming” (4.174–182; see [[nostos]]). ἀγάσσεσθαι is to look at something great with admiration or with envy, and here the envy: Merry gathers the gods' jealousy in Homer, Calypso's complaint that the gods are jealous beyond all others (ζηλήμονες ἔξοχον ἄλλων, 5.118–120) and Poseidon's grudge against the Phaeacians for their safe escorts (8.564–566), and compares Herodotus' saying that the divine is altogether jealous (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.181; Herodotus 1.32.1; see [[theos]]).

**Helen's folly and the god at the Horse.** Helen groans over the blind folly “that Aphrodite / gave me” when she led her from home (4.261–262; see [[ate]] and [[aphrodite]]). Merry takes the passage to show how the poet joins good understanding to right conduct and ignorance to crime: wrong is a blinding of the mind rather than a perversion of the will, and such blindness may be the work of a god, as Agamemnon pleads in the Iliad (*Iliad* 19.86–89, 19.137; Merry on 4.261). Menelaus' tale gives her a god too: “some god must have bidden you, / a god who meant to hold out glory to the Trojans” (4.274–275; see [[daimon]] and [[kydos]]). For Merry she came from curiosity, not to betray the men, and only a god's prompting explains it (on 4.274).

**Menelaus' own fault.** On Pharos Menelaus tells Eidothea that he must have sinned against the immortals (μέλλω ... ἀλιτέσθαι, “assuredly I must have offended the gods”, Merry on 4.377), and Proteus names the fault, the sacrifices not offered before he sailed (4.351–353, 4.472–480; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Ajax, son of Oileus, is saved from the sea by Poseidon and would have escaped his doom, hated though he was by Athena, had he not boasted that he had escaped against the will of the gods; Merry calls the boast his second sin, which brings the catastrophe (4.499–511; Merry on 4.502; see [[the-death-of-ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).

**A god or his own heart.** Medon does not know “whether some god roused him, or whether his own / heart was eager to go to Pylos” (4.712–713; see [[medon-the-herald]] and [[telemachus]]); it was both, for Athena put the voyage in Telemachus' mind in Book 1. Penelope says that “the Olympian has given me sorrows beyond / all the women who were born and raised with me” (4.722–723; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]), and the phantom answers for her son: “he is no offender against the gods” (4.807).
