summary: Ships carry the book's journeys and its plot. Menelaus brought his wealth home in his ships, and on Pharos they wait on the sand until he can sail back to the river; in Ithaca Telemachus' voyage comes out through the ship he borrowed from Noemon, Penelope calls ships “horses of the sea”, and the suitors launch a ship to lie in wait for him (4.82, 4.356–359, 4.577–582, 4.634–656, 4.707–709, 4.778–786, 4.842).
# ἁλὸς ἵπποι, "horses of the sea" (4.708), the ships of Penelope's metaphor: horses' Book 4 note excepts the line.
grc: ["ἁλὸς ἵπποι"]
en: [horses of the sea]
---

**Menelaus' ships.** “I brought them home in my ships”, Menelaus says of his wealth (4.82; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). Pharos is measured by a ship's run, “as far off as a hollow ship covers in a whole day”, and its harbor is one “from which men put their balanced ships / out into the sea, when they have drawn dark water” (4.356–359; see [[pharos]], [[hollow-ships]] and [[balanced-ships]]); ἀπὸ … βάλλουσιν, “put … out”, is probably the pushing off with a pole that Odysseus does from the Cyclops' shore (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.358; 9.487). Eidothea bids him take three of the best of his companions “beside your well-benched ships” (4.409; see [[well-benched]]). When the gods' will is known, the ships are launched with the verses of the sailing scene: “first of all we dragged the ships down into the shining sea, / and set the masts and the sails in the balanced ships, / and they went on board themselves, and sat down at the rowing benches; / and sitting in order they struck the gray sea with their oars” (4.577–580; see [[sailing-type-scene]] and [[rowing-benches]]), and he halts them at the river (4.581–582; see [[aegyptus-river]]). In Proteus' answers Ajax is brought down “among his long-oared ships”, Agamemnon escapes “in his hollow ships”, and Odysseus has “no ships with oars and no companions” (4.499, 4.513, 4.559; see [[long-oared]]).

**The borrowed ship.** In Ithaca the voyage is found out through the ship. Noemon needs it back: “He went off with a ship of mine” (4.634; see [[noemon-son-of-phronius]]); Antinous asks whether Telemachus took it by force or was given it, and Noemon answers, “I gave it to him myself, willingly” (4.645–649; see [[antinous]]). Penelope, told of the plot, cannot see why her son should go to sea: “He had no need / to go on board swift-sailing ships, which are horses of the sea / for men, and cross over the wide waters” (4.707–709; see [[penelope]] and [[swift-sailing-ships]]). The ἵπποι are almost “chariots”, as where the word means team and car together, and Merry compares Aeschylus' Prometheus, who invented “the sea-wandering, linen-winged chariots of sailors”, and Catullus' Argo, “the chariot flying with the light breeze” (Merry on 4.708; Aeschylus, *Prometheus Bound* 467–468; Catullus 64.9; see [[horses]] and [[chariot]]).

**The suitors' ship.** Antinous asks for “a swift ship and twenty companions” (4.669), and the suitors launch it with care: they drag it down into deep water, set the mast and sails, fit the oars in their leather straps, take their arms on board, and anchor it out in the water until evening (4.778–786; see [[the-suitors]] and [[mast]]). ἐν νοτίῳ, “out in the water”, is a ship lying afloat, moored to the shore by the stern and to the anchor stones by the bow, ready to sail (Merry on 4.785; see the translation's note on 4.785). At nightfall they embark and sail for Asteris (4.842–847; see [[asteris]]).
