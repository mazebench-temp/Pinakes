summary: In Book 4 Zeus is the measure of Menelaus' wealth and the giver of every fortune. Telemachus thinks the court of Olympian Zeus must be like his house, and Menelaus answers that no mortal could rival Zeus. Menelaus' welcome and his praise of Nestor's fortune, Helen's words before her tale of Troy and Penelope's lament all turn on what Zeus gives and withholds; Helen is his daughter, and through her Menelaus is his son-in-law and will be sent to the Elysian plain.

**The house of Zeus.** Telemachus, marveling at the bronze, gold, amber, silver and ivory of Menelaus' halls, whispers to Peisistratus: “Surely such is the court of Olympian Zeus within, / so many are these things, untold; awe holds me as I look at them” (4.74–75; see [[house]], [[gold-and-silver]] and [[awe-holds-me]]). On αὐλή, “court”, and Seleucus' reading without it, see the translation's note on 4.74. Menelaus overhears and corrects him: “Dear children, truly no mortal could rival Zeus; / for his house and his possessions are immortal” (4.78–79). Merry notes that ἀνδρῶν, “among men”, at the head of the next verse, is set against Ζηνί: among men there may be one or none to rival him (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.80; see [[possessions]]). The comparison and its correction frame the wealth gathered in eight years of wandering, which Menelaus at once says he holds without joy (4.80–99; see [[menelaus]]).

**What Zeus gives.** The book is full of mortals who speak of what Zeus gives. Menelaus rebukes Eteoneus for asking whether to receive the strangers: “Surely the two of us ate much good cheer / of other men before we came here, if ever Zeus / might give us rest from misery, hereafter at least” (4.33–35; see [[xenia]]). Many were the hospitalities they enjoyed, Merry explains, and so they must not fail to extend hospitality to others (on 4.34). He would have welcomed Odysseus above all the Argives, “if he had granted the two of us a homecoming over the sea / with our swift ships, far-seeing Olympian Zeus” (4.172–173; see [[far-seeing]] and [[olympian]]). He praises Nestor's good fortune: “Easily known is the offspring of a man for whom the son of Cronus / spins prosperity at his marriage and at his birth” (4.207–208; see [[easily-known-is-the-offspring]], [[olbos]] and [[son-of-cronus]]). Merry, taking γεινομένῳ as “at his birth”, compares Hesiod's Fates, who give mortals at their birth good and evil to have (on 4.208; *Theogony* 218–219). Helen, before she tells her tale of Troy, says it outright: “though the god, now to one and now to another, / Zeus, gives good and evil; for he can do all things” (4.236–237; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]] and the general article). Proteus tells Menelaus that he ought to have sacrificed to Zeus and the other gods before he embarked (4.472–474; see [[proteus]]).

**In Ithaca.** The suitors' leader and the queen speak of Zeus too. Antinous prays that Zeus may destroy Telemachus' strength “before he reaches the measure of youth” (4.667–668; on the reading see the translation's note on 4.668; see [[antinous]]); Medon prays that the son of Cronus may not bring the suitors' plot to pass (4.699; see [[medon-the-herald]]); and Penelope begins her lament, “the Olympian has given me sorrows beyond / all the women who were born and raised with me” (4.722–723; see [[penelope]]). Athena, to whom Eurycleia sends her to pray, is the “daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus” (4.752), and in the prayer the “child of aegis-bearing Zeus” (4.762; see [[aegis-bearing]] and [[athena]]).

**His daughter and his son-in-law.** Helen is Διὸς ἐκγεγαυῖα, “child of Zeus” (4.184, 4.219), and Διὸς θυγάτηρ, “the daughter of Zeus”, who had cunning drugs from Egypt (4.227; see [[helen]] and [[nepenthe]]). That parentage is the reason Proteus gives for Menelaus' end: the immortals will send him to the Elysian plain, “because you have Helen as your wife, and in their eyes you are the son-in-law of Zeus” (4.569; see [[elysium]] and [[in-laws]]). Menelaus' guests are of the lineage of Zeus as well. To his attendant “they are like the lineage of great Zeus” (4.27; see [[eteoneus]]), and to Menelaus they are of the lineage of “kings whom Zeus has nurtured” (4.63–64; see [[zeus-nurtured]]). The river of Egypt to which Menelaus must go back is διιπετής, “fallen from Zeus”, fed, Eustathius thought, by rain (4.477, 4.581; Merry on 4.477; see [[fallen-from-zeus]] and the translation's note on 4.477, 4.581).
