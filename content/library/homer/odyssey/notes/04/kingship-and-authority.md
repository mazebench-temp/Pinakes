---
summary: Menelaus is a king whose wealth gives him no joy (4.93), who would have emptied one of the cities he rules to settle Odysseus near him (4.174–177). In Ithaca Penelope remembers Odysseus as a king who did no man wrong, “though that is the way of divine kings: / one man among mortals they hate, another they love” (4.691–692).
refs: [4.62-64, 4.93, 4.174-177, 4.314, 4.602, 4.617-618, 4.629, 4.687-693]
---

**A king and his cities.** Menelaus, king of a house like the court of Zeus, is “lord of these possessions” with no joy, since his brother was killed while he gathered them (4.90–93; see [[anax]] and [[olbos]]). He would have given Odysseus a city in Argos and built him a house, “emptying one of the cities / that lie round about and are ruled by me myself” (4.174–177). Merry takes the cities as the homes of perioeci, whose people would be given homes elsewhere, and ἐξαλαπάξας as the removal of the people, not the pulling down of the houses (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.177; see the translation's table of key words). Nitzsch found the offer earnest and the conception of kingly power more Oriental than Achaean; Merry answers that such offers, like Agamemnon's seven cities for Achilles (*Iliad* 9.149–153), are “assurances of high consideration” never meant to be claimed (on 4.174). He asks Telemachus whether his errand is a public matter or his own (δήμιον ἦ ἴδιον, 4.314; see [[demos]]); he is lord of a wide plain fit for horses (4.602), and his mixing bowl was the gift of Phaedimus, king of the Sidonians (4.617–618; see [[phaedimus]] and [[basileus]]).

**The kings whom Zeus nurtured.** Menelaus sees in the two young men the lineage of “kings whom Zeus has nurtured, / scepter-bearing kings, since base men would not beget such sons as you” (4.63–64; see [[kings]], [[zeus-nurtured]] and [[genee]]).

**The way of divine kings.** Penelope tells Medon that the suitors never heard from their fathers what Odysseus was among them: he did nothing excessive to anyone and said nothing excessive among the people, “though that is the way of divine kings: / one man among mortals they hate, another they love”; he never did anything reckless to a man (4.687–693; see [[themis]] and [[atasthalie]]). Merry renders “as is indeed the common way with kings” (on 4.690). The suitors repay him with waste, and there is no thanks for good deeds (4.694–695; see [[no-thanks-for-good-deeds]]), as Mentor said in the assembly of Book 2, that no one remembers Odysseus, “and he was kind, like a father” (2.230–234). The suitors' own leaders, Antinous and Eurymachus, are ἀρχοί, “leaders”, and “in excellence … by far the best” (4.629; see [[hegemon]] and [[arete]]).
