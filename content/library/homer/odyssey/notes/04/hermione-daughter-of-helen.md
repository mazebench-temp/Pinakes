**Promised at Troy.** Telemachus finds Menelaus giving a wedding feast “for his son and his noble daughter in his house” (4.4; see [[the-wedding-at-sparta]]). “The daughter he was sending to the son of Achilles, breaker of men; / for in Troy he had first promised and nodded his assent / that he would give her, and now the gods were bringing their wedding to pass. / So he was sending her there with horses and chariots to go / to the renowned city of the Myrmidons, over whom he ruled” (4.5–9; see [[neoptolemus]] and [[myrmidons]]). The son of Achilles is the king there, of Phthia (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.9), and the tenses suggest to Merry that her departure was still being prepared (on 4.3). She is named only at the end of the lines that explain her half-brother's birth: Helen bore no child after her, “Hermione, who had the form of golden Aphrodite” (4.12–14; see [[megapenthes]], [[helen]] and [[aphrodite]]).

**The child left behind.** Telling how she knew Odysseus in Troy, Helen groans over the blind folly Aphrodite gave her when she led her away from her native land, “leaving behind my child and my bridal chamber and my husband” (4.263; see [[ate]]), as in the Iliad she wishes she had died before she followed Paris, “leaving my bridal chamber and my kinsmen, / and my darling daughter” (*Iliad* 3.174–175). From the Iliad's word τηλυγέτην, “darling”, Merry argues that Hermione was already growing into womanhood when her mother fled (on 4.11).
