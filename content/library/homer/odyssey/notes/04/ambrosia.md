**Against the stench.** Eidothea has hidden Menelaus and three companions under the skins of freshly flayed seals, on the beach where her father's seals come out of the sea to sleep (4.435–440; see [[eidothea]] and [[seals]]). The ambush is nearly undone by the smell:

{{quote:583d2019-2b1a-5431-82a2-6f226013240f}}

ὀλοώτατος ὀδμή, “the most baneful smell”, is set against ἡδὺ μάλα πνείουσαν, “very sweet in its fragrance”, and the ambrosia undoes what the seals have done: it ὄλεσσε, “killed”, the smell, the verb from which ὀλοώτατος is formed. ἐφράσατο μέγʼ ὄνειαρ, “devised a great blessing”, gives the goddess the credit, and the men wait all the morning “with enduring heart” (4.447; see [[ambush]]).

**The gods' substance.** Merry, after Buttmann, takes ἀμβροσίη for immortality itself: as the gods anoint themselves with beauty, κάλλεϊ … ἀμβροσίῳ (18.192–193), so they feed on immortality, and because the root meaning is so general the word is easily applied to different substances (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.445). He gathers its uses: it is the food that sustains immortality, which Calypso eats while she gives Odysseus the bread of men (5.196–199); Achilles is fed with nectar and ambrosia and is not made immortal (*Iliad* 19.352–354); Hera cleanses herself with it (*Iliad* 14.170), Apollo anoints Sarpedon with it (*Iliad* 16.680), and it keeps the corpse of Patroclus from decay, where it seems a distinct essence or perfume (*Iliad* 19.38–39). He reports Bergk's view that nectar was at first the only food of the gods, and that the distinction of nectar as drink and ambrosia as food is plainer in the Odyssey than in the Iliad (on 4.445; see [[nectar]]). ἀμβροσίη is the feminine of the adjective, used as a noun, and needs no word for “food” understood with it (Merry on 4.445). Here it is used for its smell. Thetis dripped it through the nostrils of the dead Patroclus “so that his flesh might be sound” (*Iliad* 19.38–39; see the note on *Iliad* 19); Eidothea sets it under the noses of the living.

**The ambrosial night.** The adjective comes twice in the same tale, in the verse “we made ready our supper, and the ambrosial night came on” (4.429, 4.574; see [[ambrosial]]). Merry, after Buttmann, gives it the general sense “immortal”, so “sacred”, and finds in it, when it is said of night, a tacit reference to the refreshment of sleep (on 4.429).
