---
summary: Helen comes out of her chamber “like Artemis of the golden distaff” (4.122), and her handmaids bring her own golden distaff and silver basket.
---

The epithet's only use in the Odyssey is a simile, and the scene that follows makes the distaff real: Phylo sets beside Helen the silver basket on wheels, a gift from Egyptian Thebes, with “the distaff, laden with violet-dark wool” across it (4.122–135; [[helen]], [[artemis]], [[phylo]]). Merry gives the other places of the epithet, both beside κελαδεινή (*Iliad* 16.183, 20.70), and holds that the company of epithets there requires “with golden arrows”, since a distaff would confuse the picture of the huntress (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.122); the translation keeps “of the golden distaff” here, beside Helen's own distaff (see the translation's note on 4.122).
