---
summary: In Menelaus' tale “the ambrosial night came on” twice on Pharos, after the meeting with Eidothea and after the prophecy (4.429, 4.574).
grc: ["ἀμβροσίη νύξ"]
---

Merry, after Buttmann, takes ἀμβρόσιος as “immortal”, in the most general sense “sacred”, as partaking of a divine nature, and notes that when it is said of night there is always a tacit reference to the refreshment of sleep, as in Priam's “through the ambrosial night, when other mortals are asleep” (*Iliad* 24.363; *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.429). Here the verse of supper and nightfall leads to sleep on the shore (4.429–430, 4.574–575; [[sleep]]). The ambrosia that Eidothea sets under the men's noses is the noun (4.445; [[ambrosia]]).
