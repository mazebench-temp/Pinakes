---
summary: Twice the West Wind blows in Menelaus' tale of Proteus. The old man comes out of the sea at midday “at the breath of the West Wind, hidden in the dark ripple”, and in the Elysian plain Oceanus always sends up the breezes of the West Wind, blowing shrill, to refresh men.
---

**The dark ripple.** Eidothea tells Menelaus when her father will come: “When the sun stands astride the middle of the sky, / then the unerring Old Man of the Sea comes out of the sea / at the breath of the West Wind, hidden in the dark ripple” (4.400–402; see [[proteus]] and [[eidothea]]). Merry explains μελαίνῃ φρικί, “the dark ripple”, by the Iliad's simile, “As the ripple of the West Wind spreads over the sea / when it has just risen, and the sea grows dark beneath it” (*Iliad* 7.63–64; see [[the-ripple-of-the-west-wind]]), where the grammarians explain φρίξ as the first wave that rises out of a calm, the first gentle breath of the West Wind on the sea. He reports that Catullus imitated the passage and that Virgil caught the darkness of μελαίνῃ, and sums it up: “The ruffled surface of the water veils the sea-god as he rises” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.402). The wind of the Iliad's battle similes is here the breeze of noon (see the general article).

**The breezes of the Elysian plain.** In the plain where life is easiest there is no snow, no severe winter and no rain, “but always the breezes of the West Wind, blowing shrill, / Oceanus sends up to refresh men” (4.567–568; see [[elysium]] and [[oceanus]]). ἀῆται, “breezes”, are “blasts” in the Iliad, as when Hera rouses the blasts of grievous winds over the sea (*Iliad* 14.254; see the translation's key-word row); λιγύ, “shrill”, is the clear sound of the wind (see [[shrill]]). Merry finds here “the same refreshing breeze” that brings Proteus out of the water at midday, and that ripens the fruits in the orchard of Alcinous (on 4.567, comparing 4.402 and 7.119). Its presence shows that the Elysian plain belongs to the land of the living (on 4.567). The *Problems* ascribed to Aristotle quote the verse in another form, ἀλλʼ αἰεὶ ζεφύροιο διαπνείουσιν ἀῆται, and ask why the west wind is thought to bring fair weather and to be the sweetest of the winds (*Problems* 26.31; see the translation's note on 4.567 and [[elysium|the Elysian plain]]).
