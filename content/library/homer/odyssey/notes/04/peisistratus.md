summary: Peisistratus comes with Telemachus to Sparta and speaks for him: he confirms that the young man is Odysseus' son, too modest to speak first, and that Nestor sent him as his escort. When Menelaus' grief rouses the longing for lament, he weeps for his brother Antilochus, whom he never saw, and asks that the weeping stop until morning; Menelaus praises him as his father's son (4.20–215, 4.302–303).

**At Sparta.** He drove Telemachus from Pylos (3.481–497), and the two stop in Menelaus' doorway, “the hero Telemachus and the splendid son of Nestor” (4.21; see [[telemachus]], [[splendid]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). It is to him that Telemachus whispers his wonder at the house, “Take note, son of Nestor, dear to my heart” (4.71; see [[house]]).

**For Telemachus.** When Helen and Menelaus have seen the likeness, Peisistratus speaks:

{{quote:14f80393-2aff-5274-94be-d4167f650bf3}}

σαόφρων is “modest” here (see the translation's key words), and ἐπεσβολίας ἀναφαίνειν, “making a show of forward talk”, the boldness a young man shows when he speaks before his elders (see [[aidos]]). The scholia report that Rhianus' text lacked 4.158–160 and that five lines were athetized as superfluous and unbecoming to a young man; Ameis takes the athetesis to concern 4.163–167 (the critical note of Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.158–160; see the translation's note on 4.158–167). Nestor's purpose, “so that you might give him counsel in some word or some deed” (4.163), is Peisistratus' account of the visit, and the lot of a son “whose father is gone”, with “no others to be his helpers” (4.164–165), is Telemachus' own (see [[nestor]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**Tears for a brother.** When Menelaus' wish for Odysseus sets them all weeping, “nor did the son of Nestor keep his eyes free of tears; / for in his heart he remembered noble Antilochus, / whom the splendid son of shining Dawn had killed” (4.186–188; see [[antilochus]], [[eos]] and [[tears]]). The son of Dawn is [[Memnon]], king of the Eastern Ethiopians, whom Achilles killed in revenge for Antilochus (Merry on 4.188). Peisistratus begins with his father's praise of Menelaus' wisdom (4.190–192), a verse Aristarchus rejected and Merry keeps, taking οἷσιν ἐνὶ μεγάροισι with Nestor's saying, “in his own halls” (Merry on 4.192; see the translation's note on 4.192), and asks that the weeping wait for the morning:

{{quote:8eb56cad-f1ee-561c-92e0-54e34009ebcf}}

Merry supplies the thoughts the sentence leaves out: let them stop weeping, since there is no pleasure in weeping after supper, and the morning will serve for it; not that he grudges tears to the dead (on 4.193). The two verses on the prize of the dead are a parenthesis (on 4.198; see [[the-only-prize-of-miserable-mortals]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). μεταδόρπιος is “after supper”, the evening meal (on 4.194; see the translation's note on 4.194). Then the brother: “For my brother too is dead, by no means the worst / of the Argives; you may have known him; for I, for my part, / never met him or saw him” (4.199–201). Peisistratus, the youngest of the family, may not have been born when Antilochus sailed for Troy (Merry on 4.200).

**His father's son.** Menelaus answers that he has said all that a wise man would say and do, even an older one, “for your father too is such a man, and so you speak wisely” (4.204–206; see [[wise]] and [[easily-known-is-the-offspring]]), and blesses Nestor, whom Zeus has granted to grow old in ease in his halls, with sons of good sense and the best with the spear (4.207–211; see [[olbos]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). Then the two young men sleep in the porch of the house (4.302–303), and Peisistratus is not mentioned again in the book.
