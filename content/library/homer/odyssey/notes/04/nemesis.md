---
summary: Telemachus “is indignant in his heart / at making a show of forward talk” before Menelaus (4.158–159), and Peisistratus is “not at all indignant / at weeping for any mortal who dies” (4.195–196).
---

**The young man's restraint.** Peisistratus explains why Telemachus has said nothing: he is modest, “and he is indignant in his heart / at making a show of forward talk, having come here for the first time, / before you, whose voice the two of us delight in as a god's” (4.158–160; see [[aidos]] and [[peisistratus]]). The scholia report that some athetized these lines as unfit for a young man to speak (see the translation's note on 4.158–167).

**Not grudging tears.** Peisistratus asks for an end to weeping after supper, but does not grudge tears to the dead: νεμεσσῶμαί γε μὲν οὐδὲν / κλαίειν (4.195–196), which Merry renders “am displeased”, “grudge”, “forbid”, comparing Telemachus' words to his mother, τὸ μὲν οὔ σε νεμεσσῶμαι κεχολῶσθαι (18.227; *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.195; see [[the-only-prize-of-miserable-mortals]]). The translation keeps νεμεσάω, “be indignant”, apart from ἀγάσσεσθαι, “begrudge”, the god's grudging of Odysseus' homecoming (4.181; see the translation's table of key words and [[who-is-to-blame]]).
