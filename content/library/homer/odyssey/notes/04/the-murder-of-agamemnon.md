summary: Menelaus speaks of the murder first as the shadow on his wealth, his brother killed in secret “by the guile of his ruinous wife” (4.90–92). Then he tells what Proteus told him, the homecoming and the killing that Nestor left untold: a storm near Malea, the landing, the lookout posted by Aegisthus, the ambush of twenty men and the feast at which Agamemnon was killed “as one kills an ox at the manger”, with all his companions (4.512–537). Proteus adds that Orestes may already have taken vengeance (4.546–547), and Menelaus heaps up a mound for his brother (4.584).

**The third telling.** Zeus told the murder as an example to the gods (1.32–43), and Nestor told the seduction, the reign of Aegisthus and the vengeance of Orestes (3.254–312). Neither told the homecoming itself or the killing. Menelaus has them from a god: he asks the Old Man of the Sea for “an exact account” of the Achaeans who came home and those who perished (4.486–490; see [[give-me-an-exact-account]] and [[proteus]]), and is warned that he will not be long without tears (4.492–494). The shade of Agamemnon will tell it once more, to Odysseus in the underworld (11.405–434).

**The way home.** Where Ajax perished, Agamemnon escaped, for Hera saved him (4.512–513; see [[agamemnon]] and [[the-death-of-ajax-son-of-oileus]]). Then, as he was about to reach the steep mountain of Malea, a storm wind carried him “to the far edge of the land, where Thyestes had his dwelling / before, but at that time Aegisthus, son of Thyestes, dwelt there” (4.514–518; see [[malea]] and [[thyestes]]). How a storm off Malea could bring him there was an old difficulty. Nitzsch held 4.514–516 an interpolation, and Bothe would put 4.519–520 directly after 4.516; Merry imagines the storm of 5.108–109 driving the king far to the south, and a second one, as he rounded Malea to sail up the coast of Argolis, carrying him to the end of the Argolic promontory, where the wind shifted and brought him to his own port; and he notes that the difficulty disappears if, as the geographer Andron said, Thyestes and Aegisthus after him had their home on Cythera (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.514; see [[cythera]] and the translation's note on 4.514–520). When the gods turned the wind back, he set foot on his native land, kissed it and wept for joy (4.519–523).

**The lookout.**

{{quote:1cbfe659-9edb-53b9-ba3e-5ca6d6677ef0}}

Merry construes ὑπέσχετο with μισθόν, “promised as wage”, the two talents of gold (on 4.525), and refers both pronouns of 4.526–527 to the lookout: he watched “lest he should pass by unseen”, landing and reaching Mycenae unannounced (on 4.526). For εἰς ἐνιαυτόν, “for a year”, he compares the first words of Aeschylus' watchman on the roof of the Atreidae, who prays for release from “the length of a year-long watch” (on 4.526; *Agamemnon* 1–2): there the watchman waits for the beacon from Troy, at the bidding of the queen (see [[aeschylus]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]). The verse that sends the lookout “to bring the news to the shepherd of the people” (4.528) is, but for one word, the verse in which Eteoneus went to tell Menelaus of his guests (4.24); here the shepherd of the people is Aegisthus, and three verses later Agamemnon (4.532; see [[shepherd-of-the-people]]).

**The ambush and the feast.** ἑτέρωθι, “on the other side”, is the other side of the palace: the ambush and the feast were both in the halls, ἐν μεγάροισιν (4.537; Merry on 4.531). καλέων is to invite him to the feast, as δειπνίσσας, “when he had feasted him”, shows (Merry on 4.532); βῆ, “went”, is down to the shore, and ἀνήγαγε, “led him up”, up from it to the city (Merry on 4.533, 4.534). The killing is told in the verse of the shade's own account, ὥς τίς τε κατέκτανε βοῦν ἐπὶ φάτνῃ, “as one kills an ox at the manger” (4.535 = 11.411), where the aorist is gnomic (Merry on 4.535). None of Agamemnon's companions survived, nor any of Aegisthus' men (4.536–537). In Proteus' account Clytemnestra does nothing; Merry finds it inconsistent with the later form of the story in the eleventh and twenty-fourth books, where she plays so great a part, and notes that the tragedians made the bath the scene of the murder (on 4.536; see the translation's note on 4.536, [[clytemnestra]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon-in-tragedy]]). Menelaus himself, before the tale, had laid the death to “the guile of his ruinous wife” (4.92).

**Twenty men.** Aegisthus “chose twenty of the best men in the land / and set an ambush” (4.530–531; see [[ambush]]). Within the same book the suitors do the same against Telemachus: Antinous asks for a swift ship and twenty companions to lie in wait in the strait, and “So saying, he chose twenty of the best men” (4.669–671, 4.778; see [[antinous]], [[the-suitors]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). The verse of Aegisthus' choice, κρινάμενος κατὰ δῆμον ἐείκοσι φῶτας ἀρίστους (4.530), returns as ἐκρίνατʼ ἐείκοσι φῶτας ἀρίστους (4.778): the son is to be ambushed on his way home as the king was.

**The news.** “So he spoke, and my dear heart was broken, / and I wept as I sat on the sand, and my heart / no longer wished to live and see the light of the sun” (4.538–540). Menelaus rolls on the ground in his grief, κυλινδόμενος (4.541), as Odysseus will, in the same verses, when Circe tells him that he must go to the house of Hades (10.496–499), and as Priam rolls in the dung for Hector (*Iliad* 22.414, 24.640); Merry refers to Plato's censure of such want of self-control in the heroes (on 4.541), where Socrates would not have the poets show Priam “rolling in the dung” (Plato, *Republic* 388a–b; see [[plato]]).

**Orestes.** The old man bids him stop weeping and hurry home: “For either you will find him alive, or Orestes / will have killed him before you, and you may come in time for the funeral feast” (4.546–547; see [[orestes]] and [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]]). Merry takes κεν … κτεῖνεν of what has probably already happened and κεν … ἀντιβολήσαις of what probably will (on 4.546), and the scholia explain τάφου as the meal at the burial, as at 3.309 (Merry on 4.547). Nestor has already told the outcome: Menelaus came home on the very day of the feast for Aegisthus (3.309–312; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]).

**In the poem.** Proteus tells the murder inside the story of Menelaus' own return, and Menelaus tells it to the son of the man whose homecoming is still to come. The landing on the native soil, the kiss and the tears, the enemy lying in wait at home, and the feast in the halls are all to be met again in Odysseus' return, and this book has already set the suitors' ambush beside Aegisthus' (see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).
