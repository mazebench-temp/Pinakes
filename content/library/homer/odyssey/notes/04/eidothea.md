summary: On Pharos, where the gods hold Menelaus twenty days without a wind, Eidothea pities him, chides him for sitting idle, and tells him how to catch her father Proteus. At dawn she brings the skins of four seals, hides Menelaus and three companions in beds scooped in the sand, and puts ambrosia under their noses against the stench of the seals.

**One of the gods.** The gods hold Menelaus on Pharos, a day's sail off Egypt, because he has not offered them perfect hecatombs, and for twenty days no wind blows (4.351–362; see [[pharos]] and [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). Then help comes from the sea:

{{quote:19e62516-e048-5a4d-bb46-c141463dec98}}

καί νύ κεν … εἰ μή, “would have … had not”, is the narrator's turn that brings a story to the edge of another outcome and names the god who turned it back, here in Menelaus' own mouth (see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[divine-rescue]]). ὀλοφύρατο, “pitied”, is the verb of lament, and θυμὸν ὄρινα, “it was her heart above all that I stirred”, makes her pity his doing: his plight moved her most of all the gods (see [[pity]]). She is named once, at the head of the next verse, after her father and his title. On the name and the reading of Zenodotus, Εὐρυνόμη, see the general article and the translation's note on 4.366.

**Her rebuke.** She finds him alone, οἴῳ ἔρροντι, “as I went my ill-starred way”, while his men are fishing round the island with curved hooks (4.367–369), and her first words chide him: “Are you so utterly a fool, stranger, and feeble-minded, / or do you give up of your own will, and take delight in suffering sorrows” (4.371–372; see [[nepios]]). Menelaus answers “whoever you are of the goddesses” that he is held against his will and must have sinned against the immortals, and asks her, since “the gods know all things”, which of them binds him from his path and how he will go home (4.376–381). Merry translates her rebuke: “Art thou an utter fool, sir stranger, and spiritless? or choosest thou thus to be reckless, and takest pleasure in sorrow?” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.371).

**Her counsel.** “Brilliant among goddesses” (4.382, 4.398; see [[brilliant-among-goddesses]]), she answers twice in the same verse, “Well then, stranger, I will tell you very exactly” (4.383, 4.399), the verse of Telemachus' answer to Athena in Book 1 (1.214). First she tells who her father is and what he will tell, if Menelaus can catch him in ambush (4.383–393; see [[proteus]]). Menelaus asks her to devise the ambush herself, “for a god is hard for a mortal man to overpower” (4.395–397); αὐτή is emphatic, “do thou thyself contrive it” (Merry on 4.395). She then tells the old man's habits and his wiles, how the men must hold him through all his changes, and when they may let him go (4.400–424). “So saying, she plunged beneath the billowing sea” (4.425), the verse with which her father leaves Menelaus (4.570).

**The sealskins and the ambrosia.** At dawn, while Menelaus goes along the shore with three companions, imploring the gods, she has plunged into the sea and brought out the skins of four seals, “freshly flayed; for she was devising a trick against her father” (4.435–437; see [[dolos]] and [[seals]]). The δέ gives the reason, “for she was plotting”: “'twas a trap she was devising” (Merry on 4.437). She scoops out beds in the sand, shallow holes to lie in “like a hare's 'form'” (Merry on 4.438), lays the men down in order and throws a skin over each (4.438–440). The stench would have made it the most dread of ambushes, “But she herself saved us, and devised a great blessing” (4.444): she brings ambrosia and puts it under each man's nose, and it kills the smell of the beast (4.445–446; see [[ambrosia]]). She saved Menelaus once by her pity (ἐσάωσε, 4.364) and saves him again here (ἐσάωσε, 4.444). After this she is not named again. When her father comes out of the sea at midday, counts his seals and lies down among them, the trick does its work without her (4.450–453; see [[ambush]]).
