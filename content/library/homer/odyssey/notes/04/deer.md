---
summary: The hind of Menelaus' simile, who lays her newborn suckling fawns to sleep in a strong lion's lair and goes out to graze on the slopes, so that the lion, coming home, brings a shameful doom on them: so Odysseus will on the suitors (4.335–340).
---

**The hind and her fawns.** The only deer of the book are in Menelaus' simile (4.335–339; see [[the-hind-in-the-lions-lair]] and [[lion]]). ἔλαφος is the hind, as the feminine participles show, and her young are νεβροί, “fawns”, νεηγενέας γαλαθηνούς, “newborn” and “suckling”, two words found only here and where Telemachus repeats the verses (4.336; 17.127). She lulls them to sleep and goes out to graze: κνημοὺς ἐξερέῃσι καὶ ἄγκεα ποιήεντα / βοσκομένη, “and goes out to search the mountain slopes and the grassy valleys / for pasture” (4.337–338; see [[grassy]]). The lion comes back while she is away.

**Who are the deer?** The fawns are the suitors, whom Menelaus has just called ἀνάλκιδες, “weaklings” (4.334), the word Poseidon uses in the Iliad of the deer that stray through the forest “without courage” (*Iliad* 13.104; see [[timid-deer]]). Merry adds, with a doubt, “perhaps the presumptuous ἔλαφος” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.335). Eustathius, whom Merry quotes, took the hinds for the suitors' parents, weaklings who let their sons be in Odysseus' house, but also remarked that the poet gives the hind twins so as not to liken so many suitors to one fawn; Merry prefers the second remark and takes “both of them”, ἀμφοτέροισι (4.339), of the two fawns (on 4.339; see the translation's note on 4.339). In the Iliad the deer is the coward's beast: Achilles tells Agamemnon that he has “the heart of a deer” (*Iliad* 1.225; see [[dog-eyes-deer-heart]]). Here the timid beast is also the one that walks into the wrong lair.
