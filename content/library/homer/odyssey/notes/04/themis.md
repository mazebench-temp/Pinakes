---
summary: Penelope remembers Odysseus as a king who did nothing excessive to anyone, “though that is the way of divine kings: / one man among mortals they hate, another they love” (ἥ τʼ ἐστὶ δίκη θείων βασιλήων, 4.691–692).
grc: [δίκη]
en: [the way of divine kings]
---

δίκη here is “custom”, the way things are with a kind of men. Merry renders the clause “as is indeed the common way with kings”, and gathers the other places where the word has this sense (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.690–691): it is the way of mortals when one dies, of slaves, of the suitors of old, of the gods who hold Olympus, of a man long away from his country, and of old men (11.218, 14.59, 18.275, 19.43, 19.168, 24.255). The way of kings is to hate one man and to love another; Odysseus kept to no such way, and did nothing ἐξαίσιον, nothing beyond what is due, to anyone (4.690–693; see [[aisa]], [[atasthalie]] and [[kingship-and-authority]]). Penelope draws the conclusion against the suitors: there is no thanks afterward for good deeds (4.694–695; see [[no-thanks-for-good-deeds]]).
