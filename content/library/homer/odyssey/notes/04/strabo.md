---
summary: Strabo reads this book as a geographer. He weighs the readings of “hollow Lacedaemon”, defends Menelaus' wanderings to the Ethiopians, the Sidonians and the Erembi, explains Pharos “in the open sea” and its day's sail as a traveler's tale grown from a real change of the coast, takes Proteus and Helen's drugs for the poet's myth-making, finds the city of Thon near Canopus, quotes the lambs of Libya for the wisdom of travel, sets the Elysian plain in the far west, and asks whether Asteris is still as Homer describes it.
---

- **Lacedaemon.** Some write κητώεσσαν, others καιετάεσσαν: κητώεσσαν is taken either from κήτη, “sea beasts”, or as “great”, which seems the more likely; καιετάεσσαν either as “full of calamint” or from the rifts made by earthquakes, called καιετοί, for Laconia is easily shaken (*Geography* 8.5.7, on 4.1; see the translation's note on 4.1): [[lacedaemon]].
- **The wanderings of Menelaus.** Which Ethiopians he reached sailing from Egypt, which Sidonians, and who the Erembi are, questions on which Aristonicus, a grammarian of Strabo's own time, collected the views of many in his work on the wanderings of Menelaus; Crates' voyage from Gades to India, and the routes by the isthmus or a canal; Menelaus' wealth as the spoils or gifts of kings, the Sidonians named beside the Phoenicians for their craft and for the bowl of the Sidonian king (4.615–619); and the Erembi taken most plausibly for the Arabs, though Strabo would not change the old text as Zeno did, writing Ἄραβάς τε (*Geography* 1.2.31–35, on 4.81–85): [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]], [[sidonians]], [[erembi]] and [[ethiopians]].
- **Pharos.** Homer was not ignorant in calling Pharos an island in the open sea a day's sail from Egypt: Menelaus, like every traveler, boasted, and the poet, knowing how the Nile's floods and silt had been adding land toward the island, multiplied the distance for the sake of the marvelous (*Geography* 1.2.23, 1.2.30, 1.3.17, on 4.354–359); in Strabo's day it is a long islet close to the mainland, making a harbor with two mouths (*Geography* 17.1.6): [[pharos]].
- **Proteus and the drugs.** “Nor are the tales of Proteus and the Pygmies, or the powers of the drugs”, signs of ignorance: the poets invent such things for pleasure and delight (*Geography* 1.2.30, on 4.219–232 and 4.384–570): [[proteus]] and [[nepenthe]].
- **Thonis.** An old city Thonis near Canopus, named after the king who received Menelaus and Helen, and the verse on Polydamna, the wife of Thon (*Geography* 17.1.16, quoting 4.228): [[nepenthe]] and [[helen-in-egypt]].
- **The lambs of Libya.** Menelaus' wanderings, to Libya “where the lambs are horned at once”, quoted for the knowledge of lands, animals and plants that makes the travelers among the heroes the wisest (*Geography* 1.1.16, quoting 4.83–86): [[the-horned-lambs-of-libya]].
- **The Elysian plain.** Homer, who had heard from the Phoenicians of the wealth and the mild air of Iberia, set the Elysian plain in the far west, where the west wind blows, and the later poets' Isles of the Blessed are shown off Maurusia (*Geography* 1.1.4–5, 1.2.31, 3.2.13, quoting 4.563–568): [[elysium-and-the-isles-of-the-blessed]] and [[elysium]].
- **Asteris.** The island has changed and now has not even a good anchorage (*Geography* 1.3.18, quoting 4.844–847); Demetrius of Scepsis says that it does not remain as the poet describes it, with harbors that have an entrance on either side, but Apollodorus that it does, and has a small town, Alalcomenae, on its isthmus (*Geography* 10.2.16, on 4.846–847): [[asteris]].
