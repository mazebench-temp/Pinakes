summary: Athena makes a phantom in the likeness of Penelope's sister Iphthime and sends it to her chamber while she sleeps. The “dim phantom” tells her that her son will come home, for Pallas Athena goes with him as his escort; it will not say whether Odysseus is alive or dead, and slips away past the bolt into the blasts of the winds.

**The phantom of Iphthime.** Penelope has fallen asleep at last, fasting, pondering whether her son will escape the suitors (4.787–794), when the goddess acts:

{{quote:44fe0297-647c-5724-adf8-995967841fd3}}

δέμας δʼ ἤικτο γυναικί, “it was like a woman in form”, is said elsewhere in the Odyssey of Athena herself, when she comes to Odysseus in a woman's likeness (13.288, 16.157, 20.31); here the likeness goes without the goddess. Iphthime is the daughter of Icarius and the wife of Eumelus, who lives in Pherae (4.797–798; see [[iphthime]], [[icarius]], [[eumelus]] and [[pherae]]); on whether the name is an epithet, see the translation's note on 4.797. Merry: “Notice that in Homer the dream is not merely the creation of the sleeper's mind, but is represented as having a real existence apart from him” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.796; see [[dream]]). It comes into the chamber “past the strap of the bolt” and stands above her head (4.802–803; see [[dream-scene]] and [[door]]).

**What it says.** It speaks first, as the dreams of the Iliad do, of her sleep, “Are you asleep, Penelope, grieving at heart?”, and gives its message: the gods who live at ease will not let her weep, since “he will yet come home, / your son; for he is no offender against the gods” (4.804–807; see [[who-live-at-ease]] and the translation's key-word row on εὕδεις). Penelope takes it for her sister, who has not visited before because she lives so far away (4.810–811), and pours out her grief again (4.812–823). The phantom answers with the name of the god: “for such an escort goes with him, … / Pallas Athena; and she pities you in your mourning; / and she has sent me now to tell you this” (4.826–829; see [[athena]]). “If you are a god and have heard a god's voice”, Penelope says, then tell me of my husband (4.831–834): Merry takes θεοῖό τε ἔκλυες αὐδῆς as “art sent here at the bidding of some god”, as Hermes, himself a god, might be sent by Zeus (on 4.831). The phantom will not say: “Of him I will not tell you from beginning to end, / whether he is alive or dead; and it is a bad thing to speak empty wind” (4.836–837; see [[it-is-bad-to-speak-empty-wind]], [[anemolios]] and [[hades]]).

**The dim phantom.** Each answer is introduced by the same verse, “Answering her, the dim phantom said” (4.824, 4.835). ἀμαυρός, “dim”, Curtius derived from ἀ-, “not”, and the root of μαρμαίρω, “glitter”; others take it of the glimmering, wavering look of a dream (Merry on 4.824). The phantom leaves as it came, “past the bolt of the doorpost / into the blasts of the winds” (4.838–839), through the slit in which the strap of the bolt worked (Merry on 4.838). Penelope wakes with her heart warmed, “so clear was the dream that had rushed upon her in the dead of night” (4.839–841). There is nothing in ἐναργές, “clear”, Merry says, against ἀμαυρόν: the dream, dim to the bodily sense, had a clear meaning to the mind (on 4.841; see [[dream]]).
