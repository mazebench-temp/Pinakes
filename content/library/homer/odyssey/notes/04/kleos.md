---
summary: Menelaus heaps a tomb for Agamemnon in Egypt “so that his fame might be unquenchable” (4.584); Penelope mourns a husband “whose fame is wide through Hellas and the heart of Argos” (4.726, 4.816) and a son snatched from the halls “without report” (4.728).
grc: [ἀκλέα]
en: [without report]
---

**Unquenchable fame.** Menelaus could not bury his brother. When he had appeased the gods at the river Aegyptus he heaped a mound for him, ἵνʼ ἄσβεστον κλέος εἴη, “so that his fame might be unquenchable” (4.583–584; see [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). ἄσβεστος, “unquenchable”, is the word of fire, of the gods' laughter and of the war cry (*Iliad* 16.123, 1.599, 11.50); Odysseus will pray for such fame for Alcinous, if he sends him home (7.331–333).

**Wide fame and no report.** Penelope twice names the husband she lost, ἐσθλόν, τοῦ κλέος εὐρὺ καθʼ Ἑλλάδα καὶ μέσον Ἄργος, “a brave man, whose fame is wide through Hellas and the heart of Argos” (4.726, again to the phantom, 4.816), in the words she used of him to the singer in Book 1 (1.344; on 4.726 see the translation's note). Her son is gone ἀκλέα, “without report” (4.728), as Telemachus said of his father in Book 1, ἀκλειῶς (1.241; see the translation's note on 1.241 and [[mothers-and-sons]]); she asks whether he went so that not even his name should be left among men (4.710). Telemachus, for his part, has come to Sparta for a κληηδών of his father, “some report” (4.317; see [[fathers-and-sons]]).
