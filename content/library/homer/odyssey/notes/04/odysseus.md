summary: Odysseus is absent and everywhere in the book: Menelaus mourns him above all the dead at Troy and would have given him a city in Argos; Helen tells how he slipped into Troy as a beggar and she alone knew him, Menelaus how he held the Achaeans silent in the Horse; Telemachus is known by his likeness to him; and Proteus tells Menelaus that he is alive, held by Calypso on her island. In Ithaca Penelope remembers him as a king who never wronged anyone (4.104–841).

**The man most mourned.** Menelaus grieves for all the men lost at Troy, but for none so much “as for one, who makes sleep and food hateful to me / when I remember him, since no one of the Achaeans toiled as much / as Odysseus toiled and endured” (4.105–107; see [[menelaus]] and [[achos]]). ἀπεχθαίρει, “makes hateful”, is causative (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.105). He does not know whether Odysseus is alive or dead, and his father, his wife and his son mourn him (4.109–112; see [[laertes]], [[penelope]] and [[telemachus]]). He would have given him a city in Argos and brought him with his people from Ithaca, so that they might meet often until death; “But the god himself, it seems, was to begrudge us this, / who has made that unhappy man alone without a homecoming” (4.169–182). The son is known by the father's feet and hands, the glances of his eyes and his hair (4.141–150; see [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**Two tales of Troy.** Over Helen's drugged wine both husband and wife tell what “the strong man did and endured” (4.242, 4.271), each in nearly the same verse. Helen tells how he marred himself with blows, threw rags over his shoulders and went into Troy as a beggar, “he who was nothing of the kind by the ships of the Achaeans”, and how she alone knew him, bathed and anointed him and swore not to reveal him; he killed many Trojans and went back with much intelligence (4.240–258; see [[odysseus-enters-troy-as-a-beggar]] and [[helen]]). ὃς οὐδὲν τοῖος ἔην is “he who was anything but a beggar”, as Aristarchus took it (Merry on 4.248). Menelaus answers with the night of the Horse, “where we were sitting, all the best / of the Argives” (4.272–273), when Helen called the chieftains in their wives' voices: “but Odysseus held us back and checked us, eager though we were”, and he pressed his strong hands on the mouth of Anticlus until Athena led her away (4.284–289; see [[anticlus]] and [[deiphobus]]). His title in both tales is ταλασίφρων, “stout-hearted” (4.241, 4.270; see [[stout-hearted]]). Telemachus' answer is that none of it saved him: “not even if the heart within him had been of iron” (4.292–293).

**The wrestler.** At dawn, when he hears of the suitors, Menelaus prays that Odysseus may come among them such as he was at Lesbos, when he stood up and threw Philomeleides:

{{quote:433cb27a-1645-5c36-b446-9219162ca416}}

The simile before it has already said what will happen: the lion comes back to his lair and brings a shameful doom on the fawns, “so Odysseus will bring a shameful doom on those men” (4.335–340; see [[the-hind-in-the-lions-lair]] and [[odysseus-wrestles-philomeleides]]).

**Alive.** Proteus tells the fates of the leaders: two perished on the way home, and “one, still alive somewhere, is held back on the wide sea” (4.496–498). Menelaus asks him to name the third man (4.551–553), and he answers:

{{quote:ed55951d-69f9-5546-b98b-1e2ef70265f6}}

Athena told the gods as much in their council (1.48–59), and in the likeness of Mentes told Telemachus that his father was alive, held on a sea-washed island (1.196–199), but Nestor knew nothing of him (3.184–185); the news Menelaus brings is the first report of one who has seen him, τὸν δʼ ἴδον, “him I saw” (4.556; see [[calypso]], [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).

**The just king.** In Ithaca Penelope, taking the herald Medon for the suitors' messenger, reminds the suitors of the man whose house they waste:

{{quote:69ebc11a-4436-5e5d-8bb1-57b6219f2fbd}}

Merry renders it, “in that he neither did anything unfair to any one, nor spoke anything unfair, as is indeed the common way with kings”: the ordinary king, while he may befriend one man, is pretty sure to spite another (on 4.690). δίκη is “custom”, “the way” of kings (on 4.691), and ἀτάσθαλον, “reckless”, answers ἐξαίσιον, “excessive” (on 4.693; see [[kingship-and-authority]] and [[atasthalie]]). It is the praise Mentor gave him in the assembly of Ithaca, “and he was kind, like a father” (2.234; see [[mentor]]). His wife laments him as πόσιν ἐσθλὸν … θυμολέοντα, “my brave, lion-hearted husband”, “whose fame is wide through Hellas and the heart of Argos” (4.724–726 = 4.814–816; see [[lion-hearted]] and [[kleos]]), and prays to Athena by the thigh-pieces “Odysseus of many wiles” burned to her (4.763–765; see [[of-many-wiles]]). The phantom of her sister will not tell her whether he lives (4.836–837; see [[iphthime]]).
