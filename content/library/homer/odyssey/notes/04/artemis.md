---
summary: Helen comes out of her fragrant chamber “like Artemis of the golden distaff”, and her maids bring her own golden distaff and silver basket.
---

**Like Artemis.** While Menelaus is wondering whether to question the weeping stranger, “Helen came out of her fragrant high-roofed chamber, / like Artemis of the golden distaff” (4.121–122; see [[helen]]). The comparison is the Odyssey's for a woman of grace and stature: Odysseus likens Nausicaa to Artemis (6.151), and Penelope comes from her chamber like Artemis or golden Aphrodite (17.37, 19.54; see [[aphrodite]]). χρυσηλάκατος, “of the golden distaff”, is the Iliad's epithet of the goddess (*Iliad* 16.183, 20.70; see [[of-the-golden-distaff]]), and here it stands beside the golden distaff that Alcandre gave Helen in Egyptian Thebes, which her maid Phylo lays across her silver basket a few verses later (4.130–135; see [[distaff]] and [[phylo]]). Merry thought that in the Iliad, where Artemis is “clamorous” and “of the showering arrows”, the company of epithets requires “with golden arrows”, since the distaff would confuse the picture of the huntress; the gold, he adds, need be no more than ornament, as of her golden reins (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.122). The translation keeps “of the golden distaff” here and in the Iliad (see the translation's note on 4.122).
