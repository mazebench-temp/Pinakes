summary: The strangers bathe in polished tubs as soon as they have gazed at the house, and slave women wash and anoint them (4.47–50); Menelaus' own silver bathtubs were a gift from Egyptian Thebes (4.128). Helen tells how she bathed Odysseus when he came into Troy disguised as a beggar, and only then did he tell her the plan of the Achaeans (4.252–256). In Ithaca Eurycleia bids Penelope bathe and pray, and she does (4.750, 4.759).

**The guests' bath.** “But when they had taken their fill of looking with their eyes, / they stepped into polished bathtubs and bathed. / And when the slave women had washed them and anointed them with oil, / and put woolly cloaks and tunics around them, / they sat down on chairs beside Menelaus” (4.47–51; see [[handmaids]], [[cloak]] and [[feast-type-scene]]). The verse of the tubs is the bath of Odysseus and Diomedes when they come back from their night raid (*Iliad* 10.576), the only bath of living men in the Iliad, and the next verse, with τούς for τόν, is the washing of Hector's body by Achilles' slave women (*Iliad* 24.587; see [[the-ransom-of-hector]]). In the Odyssey the bath belongs to the welcome. Telemachus was bathed by Nestor's daughter at Pylos (3.464–469), and he and Theoclymenus will bathe in the same verses in Ithaca (17.87–89). W. W. Merry notes that the cloak is named before the tunic, though it is put on last, because the outer garment is the one that catches the eye (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.50).

**Silver bathtubs.** Among the gifts that Polybus of Egyptian Thebes gave Menelaus were “two silver bathtubs / and two tripods, and ten talents of gold” (4.128–129; see [[polybus-of-thebes]], [[guest-gifts]] and [[gold-and-silver]]).

**Helen's bath for Odysseus.** In Helen's tale Odysseus slipped into Troy marred with blows and dressed in rags like a beggar, and she alone knew him; by craft he evaded her questions. “But when I was bathing him and anointing him with oil, / and had put clothes on him, and sworn a mighty oath / not to reveal Odysseus among the Trojans / before he had reached the swift ships and the huts, / then at last he told me the whole plan of the Achaeans” (4.252–256; see [[helen]] and [[odysseus-enters-troy-as-a-beggar]]). The bath is the hospitality the house gives a stranger, and Helen gives it herself; Eustathius, as Merry reports him, thought she did it on purpose, so that when the rags were stripped off she could prove who he was though he denied it (on 4.252). The tale foreshadows the bath in which Eurycleia, washing the feet of the beggar in Odysseus' own house, knows him by his scar (19.386–394; see [[odysseus-recognized-by-his-nurse]]).

**Penelope's bath.** When Penelope has heard of the plot against her son, Eurycleia tells her: “But bathe, and put clean clothes on your body, / and go up to your upper room with your handmaids / and pray to Athena” (4.750–752), and she does so in the same words (4.759–761; see [[penelope]], [[eurycleia]] and [[prayer]]). ὑδρηναμένη, from ὕδωρ, “water”, is the washing before prayer, and the verse returns when Penelope goes up to pray after Telemachus' homecoming (17.48, 17.58).
