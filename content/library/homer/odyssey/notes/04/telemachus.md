summary: At Sparta Telemachus marvels at Menelaus' house, weeps behind his cloak at his father's name, and is known by his likeness to Odysseus; at dawn he tells his errand and begs for the truth, hears the tale of Proteus and the news that his father is held by Calypso, and refuses the horses Menelaus offers, since Ithaca is a land for goats. In Ithaca the suitors learn that he has sailed and send Antinous with twenty men to ambush him on his way home, and Penelope, who did not know he had gone, laments and prays for him (4.1–847).

**The house.** The two young men drive into Lacedaemon at nightfall and stop at the door, “the hero Telemachus and the splendid son of Nestor” (4.21; again 4.303; see [[peisistratus]] and [[heros]]). After the bath and the meal he puts his head close to Peisistratus and whispers his wonder at the flash of bronze, gold, amber, silver and ivory: “Surely such is the court of Olympian Zeus within” (4.69–75; see [[house]], [[amber]] and [[awe-holds-me]]). Menelaus hears him, and turns the comparison aside (4.78–81; see [[menelaus]]).

**The tears.** When Menelaus says that he mourns Odysseus above all the dead, and that “sensible Penelope / and Telemachus” mourn him at home, “in him he roused the longing for lament for his father; / and he let a tear fall from his eyelids to the ground when he heard of his father, / holding up his purple cloak before his eyes / with both his hands” (4.111–116; see [[the-longing-for-lament]], [[tears]] and [[cloak]]). It is the gesture of his father among the Phaeacians, who draws his great purple mantle over his head and hides his tears when Demodocus sings of Troy (8.83–86). Menelaus sees it and does not know whether to let him speak first (4.116–119).

**Known by his father.** Helen comes in and names him at once, from the likeness: “as this man is like the son of great-hearted Odysseus, / Telemachus, whom he left newly born in his house” (4.143–144; see [[helen]]). Menelaus agrees, “for such were his feet, and such his hands, / and the glances of his eyes, and his head and the hair above” (4.149–150), a verse that Virgil reproduces, Merry notes, shortening it, *sic oculos, sic ille manus, sic ora ferebat* (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.149, citing *Aeneid* 3.490; see [[fathers-and-sons]]). Peisistratus answers for him: “this is truly his son, as you say; / but he is modest” (4.157–158), unwilling to make a show of forward talk on his first visit (see [[aidos]]). He weeps again with the others when Menelaus speaks of the homecoming Odysseus was denied (4.185).

**The answer to the tales.** After Helen's story and Menelaus', he does not praise his father: “it is all the harder; for none of this kept grim destruction from him, / not even if the heart within him had been of iron” (4.292–293), and he asks for bed (4.294–295). ἄλγιον is “all the harder” (Merry on 4.292).

**The errand.** At dawn Menelaus sits beside him and asks what need has brought him, “Is it a public matter or your own?” (4.312–314): δήμιον is public business, as in the assembly of Ithaca (Merry on 4.314, comparing 2.32). He has come for some report of his father (4.317), and he tells first of the suitors, “My house is being eaten up, and my rich tilled fields are ruined” (4.318), the farms drained by the food sent in daily to the suitors (Merry on 4.318; see [[the-suitors]]), and ends in the supplication he made to Nestor, verse for verse (4.322–331 = 3.92–101; see [[nestor]] and [[supplication]]): “do not soften anything out of respect for me, or out of pity” (4.326). He learns from the tale of Proteus what Nestor could not tell him, that his father is alive, held by force in the halls of the nymph Calypso (4.555–560; see [[calypso]] and [[odysseus]]).

**The horses refused.** Menelaus asks him to stay eleven or twelve days and offers three horses and a chariot (4.587–592), and Telemachus answers with courtesy and sense:

{{quote:a0c65b44-fcc0-592f-bcd1-79af5ac4ff38}}

He would stay a year, listening to Menelaus' stories, but his companions are waiting in Pylos (4.594–599; see [[pylos]]). Yet by the reckoning of the later books he stays a month at Sparta, for he is not seen again until Athena comes to send him home in Book 15 (Merry on 4.594; see the translation's note on 4.594). Merry compares Horace's lines on Ithaca as no place for horses, with neither level courses nor rich grass (on 4.605, citing *Epistles* 1.7.41; see [[ithaca]] and [[goats]]). Menelaus smiles, strokes him with his hand and says, “You are of good blood, dear child, by the way you speak” (4.611), and gives him the silver mixing bowl instead (4.612–619; see [[phaedimus]]).

**Plotted against.** In Ithaca the suitors learn of his voyage only when Noemon asks Antinous when Telemachus will be back with the ship he lent him (4.630–637; see [[noemon-son-of-phronius]]). They had thought him on his farms, with the flocks or the swineherd (4.638–640). “Ah, truly a great deed has been overbearingly accomplished / by Telemachus, this journey; and we said it would never come to pass” (4.663–664), says Antinous, who calls him νέος πάϊς, “the young boy” (4.665), and asks for a ship and twenty men “so that I may lie in ambush for him on his way and watch for him / in the strait between Ithaca and rocky Samos” (4.670–671; see [[antinous]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). The herald Medon tells Penelope that they mean to kill him “as he comes home” (4.700–701; see [[medon-the-herald]]), and Eurycleia confesses that she gave him his provisions and swore to say nothing for twelve days (4.745–749; see [[eurycleia]]). His mother laments that the storm winds have snatched him away “without report” (4.727–728), “a child, knowing nothing well either of toils or of assemblies” (4.818), and to her he is δαΐφρων, “wise-hearted”, the master whose wealth the suitors waste (4.687; see [[wise-hearted]]). The phantom of Iphthime tells her that he will yet come home, “for he is no offender against the gods” (4.806–807; see [[iphthime]]). The book ends with the suitors waiting for him in ambush at Asteris (4.842–847; see [[asteris]]).

**His names.** Τηλέμαχος πεπνυμένος, “wise Telemachus”, answers Menelaus “face to face” three times (4.290, 4.315, 4.593; see [[wise]]), and Menelaus calls him Τηλέμαχʼ ἥρως, “hero Telemachus” (4.312).
