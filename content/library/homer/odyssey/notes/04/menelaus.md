summary: Menelaus receives Telemachus and Peisistratus on the day of his children's double wedding, rebukes his attendant for doubting whether to take them in, and tells how he gathered his wealth in eight years of wandering while his brother was murdered; he mourns Odysseus above all the dead and knows his son by his likeness. At dawn he tells what Proteus told him on Pharos, the returns of the Achaeans and his own end in the Elysian plain, and he offers Telemachus horses and, when they are refused, a silver mixing bowl (4.1–624).

**The host.** Telemachus finds him giving a wedding feast to his kinsmen, sending his daughter Hermione to the son of Achilles and bringing home a bride for Megapenthes, his son by a slave woman (4.3–14; see [[the-wedding-at-sparta]], [[hermione-daughter-of-helen]] and [[megapenthes]]). When his attendant asks whether to unyoke the strangers' horses or send them on to another host, Menelaus answers “deeply troubled” and calls it a child's folly: “Surely the two of us ate much good cheer / of other men before we came here” (4.30–34; see [[eteoneus]] and [[xenia]]). He bids his guests eat before he asks who they are, since their lineage shows in them (4.60–64), and sets before them with his own hands the fat chine that was his portion of honor (4.65–66; see [[feast-type-scene]]).

**The wealth and the grief.** Telemachus whispers to Peisistratus that the house must be like the court of Olympian Zeus (4.71–75), and Menelaus, overhearing, answers that no mortal could rival Zeus, whose house and possessions are immortal; among men there may be one who will rival him, or none (4.78–81). His wealth is the fruit of his wandering: “For after much suffering and much wandering / I brought them home in my ships, and came back in the eighth year” (4.81–82), by way of Cyprus, Phoenicia, Egypt, the Ethiopians, Sidonians, Erembi and Libya (4.83–85; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]] and [[possessions]]). While he gathered it, “another man killed my brother / in secret, unawares, by the guile of his ruinous wife; / so it is with no joy that I am lord of these possessions” (4.91–93; see [[agamemnon]], [[clytemnestra]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). He would live with a third part of it, if the men who died in Troy were safe (4.97–99), and of them all he mourns one most:

{{quote:ab6eaa35-629f-5f5c-8442-bc6cc8895580}}

καὶ πατέρων … εἰσίν (4.94–95) is a parenthesis, Merry explains, and the house that Menelaus lost, “very well appointed” (4.96), is the house as it was before the war (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.94); ἄχος … κείνου is “sorrow for him” (on 4.108; see [[achos]]).

**The son.** Telemachus weeps behind his cloak at his father's name, and Menelaus ponders whether to let him speak of his father first or to question him (4.113–119; see [[telemachus]]). Before he has decided, Helen comes in and names the likeness, and he agrees: “I too see it so now, lady, as you liken them; / for such were his feet, and such his hands, / and the glances of his eyes, and his head and the hair above” (4.148–150). Merry sets Helen's readiness against the natural slowness and indecision of Menelaus, and notes the weight of νῦν, “now”, after his pondering (on 4.141, 4.148; see [[helen]]). When Peisistratus has confirmed it, Menelaus says what he would have done had Zeus brought Odysseus home: given him a city in Argos, emptying one of the cities round about that he rules, so that they might meet often until death (4.169–180). Nitzsch, Merry reports, would not take the offer as a jest or a mere fancy of friendship, though its conception of kingly power is more Oriental than Achaean (on 4.174); ἐξαλαπάξας means emptying the city of its people, not pulling it down (on 4.177; see the translation's key words). “But the god himself, it seems, was to begrudge us this, / who has made that unhappy man alone without a homecoming” (4.181–182), and at his words all four weep (4.183–186; see [[the-longing-for-lament]]).

**Nestor's son.** To Peisistratus' tears for his brother and his plea not to mourn after supper Menelaus answers with praise of Nestor's house, “Easily known is the offspring of a man for whom the son of Cronus / spins prosperity at his marriage and at his birth” (4.207–208; see [[peisistratus]], [[nestor]] and [[easily-known-is-the-offspring]]), and calls for water and the meal again (4.212–215). After Helen's story he tells his own, of the Horse: he and the son of Tydeus were eager to answer her voice, but Odysseus held them back (4.266–289; see [[odysseus]] and [[deiphobus]]). He sleeps in the inner part of the house, with Helen beside him (4.304–305).

**The tale of Proteus.** At dawn he asks Telemachus his errand (4.312–314), and when he hears of the suitors he likens them to the fawns a hind has laid in a lion's lair (4.333–340; see [[the-hind-in-the-lions-lair]]) and wishes Odysseus were such as he was when he wrestled Philomeleides in Lesbos (4.341–346; see [[odysseus-wrestles-philomeleides]]). Then he tells what “the unerring Old Man of the Sea told me” (4.349): the gods held him on Pharos for twenty days because he had not offered them perfect hecatombs (4.351–362; see [[pharos]]); Eidothea pitied him and taught him how to catch her father (4.363–424; see [[eidothea]]); he and three companions lay under sealskins and held Proteus through all his changes (4.425–461; see [[proteus]]); and the old man told him to go back to the river Aegyptus and sacrifice, and told him the fates of Ajax, son of Oileus, of Agamemnon and of Odysseus (4.472–560; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]). At the news of his brother he wept on the sand, “and my heart / no longer wished to live and see the light of the sun” (4.539–540). Then the prophecy of his own end:

{{quote:6ea2dae7-6e78-531a-bf09-233ca31d9b84}}

οὕνεκα, “because”, goes back to πέμψουσιν, “will send”; ἔχεις is “you have to wife”, and σφιν “in their eyes”: the gods count him the son-in-law of Zeus (Merry on 4.569; see the translation's note on 4.569). Favored heroes, Rhadamanthys the son of Zeus and Menelaus his son-in-law, are carried there alive, to a place on the surface of the earth, not in Hades (Merry on 4.563; see [[elysium]] and [[rhadamanthus]]). He went back to Egypt, offered the hecatombs, heaped up a tomb for Agamemnon “so that his fame might be unquenchable”, and came home with a fair wind (4.581–586; see [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[egypt]]).

**The gifts.** He asks Telemachus to stay eleven or twelve days and offers him three horses, a chariot and a goblet (4.587–592; see [[guest-gifts]]). Telemachus will not take horses to Ithaca, a land for goats, and Menelaus “smiled, / and stroked him with his hand”: “You are of good blood, dear child, by the way you speak” (4.609–611; see [[ithaca]]). He changes the gift for the finest treasure in his house, the silver mixing bowl that the Sidonian king gave him (4.612–619; see [[phaedimus]] and [[mixing-bowl]]). The plain he rules, with its lotus, galingale, wheat, spelt and white barley (4.602–604), may be the Eurotas valley or the flat land of Messenia (Merry on 4.602; see [[lacedaemon]]).

**His names.** He is ξανθὸς Μενέλαος, “fair-haired Menelaus”, in the verses that bring in his speeches (4.30, 4.59, 4.76, 4.147, 4.168, 4.203, 4.265, 4.332; see [[fair-haired]]), βοὴν ἀγαθὸς Μενέλαος, “Menelaus, good at the war cry”, when he rises at dawn and when he smiles at Telemachus (4.307, 4.609; see [[good-at-the-war-cry]]), and Μενελάου κυδαλίμοιο, “glorious Menelaus”, in the phrases for his house, his kinsmen and his attendants (4.2, 4.16, 4.23, 4.46, 4.217; see [[kydalimos]]). He is addressed as διοτρεφές, “nurtured by Zeus” (4.26, 4.138, 4.156, 4.235, 4.291, 4.316, 4.391, 4.561; see [[zeus-nurtured]]), and called Ἀτρεΐδης, “son of Atreus” (4.51, 4.156, 4.185, 4.190, 4.235, 4.291, 4.304, 4.316, 4.492, 4.594; see [[son-of-atreus]]).
