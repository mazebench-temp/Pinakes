summary: On Pharos, where the gods hold Menelaus twenty days without a wind, his companions are always wandering about the island, “fishing / with curved hooks, and hunger wore at their bellies” (4.368–369).
---

**Fishing for hunger.** The gods keep Menelaus on Pharos for twenty days, and his provisions and the strength of his men are nearly spent (4.360–363; see [[pharos]] and [[provisions]]). Eidothea finds him alone, “for they were always wandering about the island, fishing / with curved hooks, and hunger wore at their bellies” (4.368–369; see [[eidothea]]). ἰχθυάασκον is the iterative of ἰχθυάω, “fish”, and with αἰεί it marks a daily round. Merry notes that the second clause gives the reason with δέ, where later Greek would say γάρ, “for hunger wore at their bellies” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.368). The fishing is a sign of need. Odysseus' men on the island of the Sun do the same when their ship's provisions are spent, hunting fish and birds with curved hooks, and the verse comes back whole (12.329–332; 12.332 = 4.369): γναμπτοῖς ἀγκίστροισιν, ἔτειρε δὲ γαστέρα λιμός. γναμπτός is “curved, bent” (see [[supple]]), and ἄγκιστρον, “hook”, occurs in Homer only in these two verses.

**Heroes and fish.** Merry gathers the evidence that the Homeric age ranked fish with the beasts of prey, eaters of the dead (*Iliad* 21.201–204, 24.82), and reports Eustathius' remark on this verse, that it was not right for the heroes to fish except under such need (on 4.368). He also reports that Athenaeus answered the comic poet Eubulus, who asked where in Homer any Achaean eats fish, by pointing to the net of Sarpedon's simile (*Iliad* 5.487), and that Eubulus was putting into verse the observation of Plato's Socrates that Homer does not feast his heroes on fish (Athenaeus 1.46, as Merry reports it; Plato, *Republic* 3.404b–c; see the general article).

**The fish-filled sea.** Menelaus asks Eidothea and then her father how he shall go home “over the fish-filled sea” (4.381, 4.470), and Eidothea twice gives back his words (4.390, 4.424); the storm wind carries Agamemnon over the same sea (4.516; see [[fish-filled]]).
