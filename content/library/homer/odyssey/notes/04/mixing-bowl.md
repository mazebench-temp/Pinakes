---
summary: Two bowls. Helen's drug works on the man who drinks it “once it was mixed in the mixing bowl” (4.222); and in place of the horses Menelaus gives Telemachus the finest treasure in his house, a mixing bowl all of silver with its rims finished with gold, the work of Hephaestus, which Phaedimus, king of the Sidonians, gave him when he stayed in his house (4.613–619).
---

**The drugged bowl.** “Whoever swallowed it down, once it was mixed in the mixing bowl, / would not let a tear fall down his cheeks for that day” (4.222–223; see [[nepenthe]] and [[wine]]). The bowl is the place where wine becomes drink for the company: the suitors feared that Telemachus might bring back drugs from Ephyra “and put them in the mixing bowl, and destroy us all” (2.329–330), and Helen does what they feared, but her drug takes away grief.

**The gift.** When Telemachus has refused the horses, Menelaus changes the gift:

{{quote:d4456eaf-c7e5-5c29-abee-e2388ee6860b}}

The bowl is the best of the κειμήλια, the treasures laid up in his house (see [[possessions]] and [[guest-gifts]]), and its history is part of its worth: a king gave it to Menelaus when “his house sheltered me / as I came there on my way home” (4.618–619; see [[phaedimus]] and [[sidonians]]). Merry compares ἀμφεκάλυψε, “sheltered”, with the same verb of the Horse that the city would take within it (8.511) and with κεύθειν of a house that hides a guest (6.303), and takes νοστήσαντα as no more than “on my homeward voyage” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.618, 4.619). The Iliad's finest silver bowl was Sidonian work too, the prize for the footrace at Patroclus' games:

{{quote:8a94f5a4-7b52-530d-aefc-309fd91a94e7}}

The verses of the gift return in Book 15, when Menelaus keeps his word: he puts a two-handled cup in Telemachus' hand, and Megapenthes sets the shining silver bowl before him (15.113–123, plain renderings; see [[megapenthes]] and [[cup]]).
