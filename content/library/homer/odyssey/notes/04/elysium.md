---
summary: Proteus' last prophecy to Menelaus. It is not decreed for him to die in Argos: because he has Helen as his wife and is the son-in-law of Zeus, the immortals will send him to the Elysian plain at the limits of the earth, where fair-haired Rhadamanthys is and life is easiest for men, without snow, winter or rain, refreshed always by the West Wind that Oceanus sends up.
# Unnamed: "where life is easiest for men", its weather and its breezes (4.565-568).

**The prophecy.** When Proteus has told of Odysseus on Calypso's island, he turns to Menelaus himself:

{{quote:6ea2dae7-6e78-531a-bf09-233ca31d9b84}}

θέσφατον, “decreed”, is what the gods have spoken (see [[thesphaton]]). θανέειν καὶ πότμον ἐπισπεῖν, “to die and meet your fate”, is the phrase Peisistratus used the evening before of every mortal, “any mortal who dies and meets his fate” (4.196), and Helenus' assurance to Hector, “it is not yet your fate to die and meet your end” (*Iliad* 7.52), has the same shape. Menelaus is promised not a later death but none. The immortals will “send” him (πέμψουσιν, 4.564), the verb of an escort home, to πείρατα γαίης, “the limits of the earth” (see the general article and [[proteus]]).

**The easiest life.** The place is described by what it lacks. There is no snow, no severe winter and no rain (4.566), as on Olympus, which is never shaken by winds or wet by rain, and where no snow comes near (6.42–45; see [[olympus]]). ῥηίστη βιοτή, “life is easiest”, is the life of the gods, θεοὶ ῥεῖα ζώοντες, “the gods who live at ease”, as the phantom calls them to Penelope at the end of this book (4.805; see [[who-live-at-ease]]). Instead of storms there is the West Wind, λιγὺ πνείοντος, “blowing shrill” (4.567; see [[shrill]]), whose breezes Oceanus sends up ἀναψύχειν, “to refresh” men (4.568), the verb used of Diomedes cooling his wound in the Iliad (*Iliad* 5.795). It is “the same refreshing breeze”, Merry notes, that brings Proteus out of the water at midday (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.567, comparing 4.402; see [[zephyrus]] and [[oceanus]]). On the form of the verse in the *Problems* ascribed to Aristotle, see the general article and the translation's note on 4.567.

**Fair-haired Rhadamanthys.** ξανθὸς Ῥαδάμανθυς is the only inhabitant named (4.564; see [[rhadamanthus]] and [[fair-haired]]), with the epithet of Menelaus himself, ξανθὸς Μενέλαος. Merry takes the two together: favored heroes, Rhadamanthys the son of Zeus and Menelaus his son-in-law, are carried alive to Elysium and are found there with their bodies (on 4.563).

**The son-in-law of Zeus.** The reason closes the prophecy: “because you have Helen as your wife, and in their eyes you are the son-in-law of Zeus” (4.569; see [[menelaus]], [[helen]], [[zeus]] and [[in-laws]]). σφιν is an ethical dative, “in their eyes”: the gods recognize him as such (Merry on 4.569; see the translation's note on 4.569). Merry reports from the scholia that some read φίλος ἐσσί, “you are dear”, and that the verse was missing in some copies because of the pronoun (critical note on 4.569). The gift comes through Helen, “child of Zeus” (4.184, 4.219), whose drug and tale of Troy filled the evening before. Menelaus says nothing of the promise and goes on with his tale (4.570–586; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]).
