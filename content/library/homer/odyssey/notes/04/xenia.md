summary: At Sparta the welcome is questioned at the door. Eteoneus asks whether to receive the strangers or send them on, and Menelaus rebukes him, remembering how much hospitality he and Helen had from other men on their way home; the strangers are unyoked, bathed and fed before anyone asks who they are, and in the end no one asks, for Helen knows the son by his likeness (4.20–154). Menelaus would have made Odysseus welcome above all the Argives, and he sends Telemachus away with a guest's gift (4.169–182, 4.587–619).

**At the door.** The two young men stop with their horses in the doorway, and Menelaus' attendant goes in to ask what to do with them (4.20–25; see [[eteoneus]]):

{{quote:84e8d088-711e-5b57-bf2e-bec1bc183928}}

The choice he offers, to receive them or to pass them on to another house, is the one a host may not make. φιλέω of a guest is “make welcome” (see the translation's row φιλέω), and the word comes back when Menelaus speaks of the welcome he meant to give Odysseus (4.171). W. W. Merry reads the doubt as a sign that the house was still busy with the wedding, and reports a scholium that the treachery of Paris, once a guest in that house, had made it wary of strangers (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.29; see [[the-wedding-at-sparta]] and [[paris]]).

**The guest who was a guest himself.** Menelaus answers “deeply troubled”:

{{quote:dd795513-b76f-57cf-b19e-9c5029d52f85}}

ξεινήια, the “good cheer” of other men's houses, is the food a host sets before his guest, as Diocles set it before the two travelers at Pherae (3.490; see [[guest-gifts]]). The weight of the sentence, Merry says, falls on ξεινήια πολλὰ φαγόντε, “many were the hospitalities that we enjoyed before we settled down here”, and therefore they must not fail to give the same to others (on 4.34). Menelaus' wanderings have taught him what the guest needs: he wandered for eight years and gathered his wealth in those lands, and one of the gifts he will give Telemachus is the bowl that the king of the Sidonians gave him “when his house sheltered me / as I came there on my way home” (4.81–90, 4.617–619; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]] and [[mixing-bowl]]).

**Food first, questions after.** The attendants unyoke and feed the horses and lead the men in; they gaze at the house, bathe, put on cloaks and tunics, and sit down on chairs beside Menelaus, and the guest's meal is served to them in the verses of the Odyssey's welcome (4.37–58; see [[house]], [[bathing]], [[water-for-the-hands]] and [[feast-type-scene]]). Menelaus greets them, δεικνύμενος, “welcoming” them (Merry on 4.59, referring to his note on 3.41), and states the rule: “Take some of the food, and welcome, both of you! And then, / when you have tasted the meal, we will ask who you are / among men” (4.60–62). Without waiting for an answer he guesses at their birth: they are of the lineage of kings, “since base men would not beget such sons as you” (4.62–64). Then he gives them his own portion of honor, the fat chine of an ox (4.65–66). Merry cites the chine as the portion of honor elsewhere, the piece Odysseus sends to Demodocus (8.475) and the chine with which Eumaeus honors his guest (14.437), and thinks that Menelaus, having already dined, gives the strangers what was left of the portion that had been set before him (on 4.65). The order is the one Telemachus kept with the stranger in Book 1 and Nestor with his guests in Book 3 (1.123–124, 3.69–70). At Sparta the question is never asked. Telemachus weeps at his father's name, Helen comes in and knows him by his likeness to Odysseus, and Peisistratus confirms it (4.113–167; see [[telemachus]], [[helen]] and [[peisistratus]]).

**The welcome that was never given.** When he knows whose son he has in his house, Menelaus tells what he had meant to do for the father: “I thought that when he came I would make him welcome above all other / Argives”, and he would have given him a city in Argos to dwell in, with his possessions, his son and all his people, emptying one of the cities round about that he ruled, so that the two of them might have met often until death parted them (4.169–180; see [[odysseus]] and [[argos]]). ἐξαλαπάξας here means moving the people out, not pulling the houses down (Merry on 4.177; see the translation's row ἐξαλαπάζω).

**The stranger on Pharos.** In his own story Menelaus is the stranger. Eidothea addresses him as ξεῖνε, “stranger”, when she finds him wandering alone on the island, and twice more before she gives him her counsel (4.371, 4.383, 4.399; see [[eidothea]] and [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]).

**The parting.** Menelaus asks Telemachus to stay eleven or twelve days, and then he will send him on his way well and give him splendid gifts (4.587–592). Telemachus would gladly stay a year, but his companions are waiting in Pylos; he asks for a gift he can keep as a treasure, and will not take the horses (4.594–608), and Menelaus changes the gift for the best thing in his house (4.612–619; see [[guest-gifts]]). In Ithaca, in the same book, the suitors eat in another man's house unasked, and Penelope wishes that their meal there might be the last (4.681–695; see [[the-suitors]] and [[feast]]).
