summary: “old Nestor” used to praise Menelaus' wisdom in his halls (4.190–192) and has been granted to grow old in ease (4.209–210); Laertes, “an afflicted old man”, is to be spared more grief (4.754). Memory moves the book: Menelaus remembers Odysseus (4.106, 4.151), Peisistratus his brother (4.187), and Telemachus and Penelope ask a man and a goddess, “remember these things now for me” (4.331, 4.765).
refs: [4.106, 4.151, 4.187-192, 4.209-210, 4.331, 4.353, 4.592, 4.754, 4.765]

**Old men.** Peisistratus quotes his father: “that you were wise beyond all mortals / old Nestor used to say, whenever we made mention of you / in his halls” (4.190–192; see [[nestor]]); Aristarchus rejected the last verse, and Merry keeps it, taking οἷσιν ἐνὶ μεγάροισι with Nestor's saying (see the translation's note on 4.192). Menelaus makes Nestor's old age in ease, with good sons about him, the sign of a fortunate man (4.209–211; see [[easily-known-is-the-offspring]]). In Ithaca Laertes is the old man to be spared: Eurycleia begs Penelope, “do not afflict an afflicted old man” (4.754; see [[laertes]] and [[geron]]).

**Remembering.** Odysseus makes sleep and food hateful to Menelaus “when I remember him” (4.105–106), and he was speaking of him, “remembering”, when Telemachus wept (4.151; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.151, joins ἀμφʼ Ὀδυσῆι with μυθεόμην and lets μεμνημένος stand by itself). Peisistratus remembers his brother Antilochus (4.187; see [[antilochus]]). Telemachus asks Menelaus, “remember these things now for me”, any word or deed his father fulfilled for him (4.328–331), and Penelope asks Athena the same in the same words, by the sacrifices Odysseus made her (4.763–765; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). The gods, too, “always wished their commands to be remembered” (4.353), and Menelaus' gift is to keep him in a guest's memory, a goblet for libations, “remembering me all your days” (4.591–592; see [[xenia]]).
