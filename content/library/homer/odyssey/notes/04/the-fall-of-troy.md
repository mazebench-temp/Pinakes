summary: The book does not tell the sack, but Helen and Menelaus tell two stories of the city's last days: Odysseus spying in Troy as a beggar and learning the plan that would take it (4.240–264), and the night in the wooden horse, where the best of the Argives sat “bringing slaughter and doom to the Trojans” (4.271–289).
# Unnamed: "the whole plan of the Achaeans" (4.256), and the men in the horse "bringing slaughter and doom to the
# Trojans" (4.272-273).
refs: [4.256, 4.272-273]
---

Both stories end before the sack. In Helen's, Odysseus, disguised as a beggar, told her “the whole plan of the Achaeans” (4.256), and νόον, “the plan”, Merry takes to be the plan of the wooden horse (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.256; see [[odysseus-enters-troy-as-a-beggar]]). In Menelaus', the chieftains sit in the horse, “bringing slaughter and doom to the Trojans”, while Helen, with Deiphobus following her, tries them with their wives' voices (4.271–289; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]]). Demodocus will sing the sack itself among the Phaeacians: the Achaeans pouring out of the horse, and Odysseus going with Menelaus to the house of Deiphobus (8.514–520, a plain rendering; see [[deiphobus]]). Helen's joy while the Trojan women wailed for the men Odysseus had killed, since her heart had already turned toward home (4.257–262), and Menelaus' wish that the men who perished at Troy were safe (4.97–99; see [[troy]]) are the book's other glances at the end of the war and its cost.
