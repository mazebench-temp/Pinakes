summary: Menelaus would have settled Odysseus near him had Zeus granted them both a homecoming, but a god has made Odysseus “alone without a homecoming” (4.171–182). On Pharos he asks the gods for his own (4.381, 4.424, 4.470) and hears from Proteus of the returns: two leaders perished “in their homecoming” (4.497), and Agamemnon reached home to be killed (4.519–537). In Ithaca the suitors lie in wait for Telemachus' return (4.669–672, 4.700–701), and the phantom tells Penelope that her son “will yet come home” (4.806).

**Without a homecoming.** Menelaus' own return took eight years of wandering (4.81–92; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). He would have welcomed Odysseus above all the Argives, “if he had granted the two of us a homecoming over the sea / with our swift ships, far-seeing Olympian Zeus”, but the god “has made that unhappy man alone without a homecoming” (4.171–182; see [[who-is-to-blame]]). ἀνόστιμος comes only here in Homer; at the end of the book the phantom uses its opposite of Telemachus, νόστιμός ἐστι, “he will yet come home” (4.806; see the translation's table of key words).

**The returns.** On Pharos Menelaus asks Eidothea, and then Proteus, which god holds him back, “and tell me of my homecoming, how I shall go over the fish-filled sea” (4.381, 4.390, 4.424, 4.470; see [[the-gods-know-all-things]]); the answer is the hecatombs at the river Aegyptus (4.472–480; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Proteus then tells the returns of the others: only two of the leaders perished ἐν νόστῳ, “in their homecoming” (4.496–497), Ajax at sea and Agamemnon at home, when “a safe homecoming appeared” and he set foot on his native land rejoicing (4.499–537; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). Menelaus went home when he had done the rites, ταῦτα τελευτήσας νεόμην (4.585), and he alone is promised more than a return, the Elysian plain (4.561–569).

**Telemachus' return.** Helen's heart had turned to going back home before Troy fell (4.260–261). In Ithaca Noemon asks when Telemachus “will come back” from Pylos (νεῖτʼ, 4.633), and the suitors set an ambush for his return (4.669–672, 4.842–847); Medon tells Penelope that they mean to kill him “as he comes home” (4.700–701; see [[mothers-and-sons]]). νέεσθαι at 4.8, where Menelaus sends Hermione to the son of Achilles, is “to go”, not a homecoming.
