summary: The gods as a body are what men speak of in Book 4. They bring weddings to pass and withhold children, hold Menelaus in Egypt for the hecatombs he owes them and send him home when he has paid, know all things, and are hard to overpower; Menelaus' gift of a goblet is for pouring libations to them, and the gods who live at ease will not let Penelope weep.

**Weddings and children.** The book begins with what the gods give and withhold in a house. Menelaus had promised his daughter at Troy, “and now the gods were bringing their wedding to pass” (4.7), but “to Helen the gods brought no more offspring to light, / once she had first borne a lovely child” (4.12–13; see [[the-wedding-at-sparta]] and [[hermione-daughter-of-helen]]).

**The gods' commands.** In Menelaus' tale the gods as a body hold him back: “In Egypt, eager as I was to come home, the gods / still held me back, since I had not offered them perfect hecatombs; / and the gods always wished their commands to be remembered” (4.351–353), the verses Herodotus quotes (2.116.5; see [[hecatomb]] and [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). Zenodotus rejected the third verse, asking what commands there had been, and Merry takes it for a saying added by a later rhapsode (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 4.353; see the translation's note on 4.353). “There the gods kept me twenty days” (4.360), and Menelaus guesses that he has sinned against “the immortals, who hold the wide sky”, and that “the gods know all things” (4.378–379; see [[the-gods-know-all-things]] and [[wide-sky]]). Proteus confirms it: he should have sacrificed to “Zeus and the other gods” before he embarked, and must go back to the river and offer holy hecatombs “to the immortal gods, who hold the wide sky; / and then the gods will grant you the journey you have in mind” (4.472, 4.478–480; see [[sacrifice]]). He does, “and when I had ended the anger of the gods who live forever”, the immortals give him a fair wind home (4.583–586; see [[gods-who-live-forever]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Ajax died because he boasted that he had escaped the sea “against the will of the gods” (4.504; see [[the-death-of-ajax-son-of-oileus]]), and when Agamemnon came near home “the gods turned the wind back” (4.520).

**Libations and comfort.** Menelaus means his parting gift, a goblet, to keep him in Telemachus' mind whenever he pours libations “to the gods, / the immortals” (4.591–592; see [[libation]]). In Ithaca Eurycleia comforts Penelope that the son of Arceisius' offspring is not altogether hateful “to the blessed gods” (4.754–756; see [[blessed-gods]]), and the phantom tells her that “The gods who live at ease” will not let her weep or grieve, since her son “is no offender against the gods” (4.805–807; see [[who-live-at-ease]] and [[immortals]]).
