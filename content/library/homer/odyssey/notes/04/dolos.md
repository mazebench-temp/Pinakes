summary: Agamemnon was killed “by the guile of his ruinous wife” (4.92). On Pharos Eidothea devises “a trick against her father” (4.437), who has “no thought / in his heart that there was a trick” (4.452–453) and then tries his own “crafty art” (4.455); in Proteus' tale Aegisthus devises “a crafty scheme” (4.529), and in Ithaca Penelope ponders like a lion when men draw “their crafty circle” around him (4.792).

**The guile of a wife.** Menelaus' wealth gives him no joy, since another man killed his brother “in secret, unawares, by the guile of his ruinous wife” (4.91–93; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]). In Proteus' account the trick is Aegisthus': he posts a lookout, chooses twenty men for an ambush, and invites Agamemnon to a feast, δολίην ἐφράσσατο τέχνην, “devised a crafty scheme” (4.524–535; see [[schemer]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]).

**Trick against trick.** Eidothea brings four freshly flayed sealskins, “for she was devising a trick against her father” (4.437), and Proteus counts the hidden men among his seals, “and had no thought / in his heart that there was a trick” (4.452–453; see [[proteus]] and [[eidothea]]). Seized, “nor did the old man forget his crafty art” (δολίης ... τέχνης, 4.455), and became a lion, a serpent, a leopard, a boar, water and a tree (4.456–458); Eidothea had called his ways ὀλοφώια, “wiles” (4.410), and he is ὀλοφώια εἰδώς, “skilled in wiles” (4.460). Merry renders the word “sorcerer's arts” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.410). See [[the-gods-know-all-things]].

**The crafty circle.** Penelope, fasting in her upper room, ponders “as much as a lion ponders in a throng of men / in fear, when they draw their crafty circle around him” (4.791–792; see [[lion]] and [[mothers-and-sons]]). In Helen's tale Odysseus' disguise in Troy is called κερδοσύνη, “craft” (4.251; see [[kerdos]]).
