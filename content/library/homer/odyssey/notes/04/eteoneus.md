**At the door.** Telemachus and Peisistratus stop with their horses in the doorway of the house, “and powerful Eteoneus came forward and saw them, / the ready attendant of glorious Menelaus” (4.22–23; see [[powerful]] and [[ready-attendants]]). He goes through the house “to bring the news to the shepherd of the people” (4.24), the verse of the lookout who brings Aegisthus word of Agamemnon's landing, with πρός for διά (4.528; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]), and, with Penelope's name at its end, of the herald Medon (4.679; see [[medon-the-herald]]). His question is the doorkeeper's:

{{quote:84e8d088-711e-5b57-bf2e-bec1bc183928}}

The strangers are like γενεῇ Διός, “the lineage of great Zeus” (4.27), that is, they look like kings, whom the poems call “nurtured by Zeus” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.27; see [[zeus-nurtured]]). Merry reads the doubt as a sign that the house was still in the bustle of the wedding feast, and reports a scholium that the treachery of Paris, once a guest in this house, had taught caution in receiving strangers (on 4.29; see [[paris]] and [[the-abduction-of-helen]]).

**The rebuke.** Menelaus answers “deeply troubled” (4.30): “You were no fool, Eteoneus, son of Boethous, / before now; but now you babble folly like a child” (4.31–32; see [[boethous]]). The reason is his own long wandering: “Surely the two of us ate much good cheer / of other men before we came here” (4.33–34), and the weight of the sentence falls on that hospitality, Merry says, which they must not now fail to give (on 4.34; see [[xenia]] and [[menelaus]]). “So he spoke, and the other hurried through the hall, and called the others, / the ready attendants, to follow along with him” (4.37–38): they unyoke the sweating horses, tie them at the mangers with spelt and white barley, lean the chariot against the gleaming walls, and lead the men into the house (4.39–43; see [[horses]] and [[chariot]]). The title κρείων, which the poem gives to lords, marks him, Merry thinks, as a man of gentle birth, and a scholium he reports describes him as the son of Alector, Menelaus' fellow father-in-law (on 4.22; see [[alector]]); the poem names his father Boethous (4.31). He is not seen again in the book. He comes back in Book 15 to roast and carve the meat for Telemachus' parting meal (15.95–98, 15.140).
