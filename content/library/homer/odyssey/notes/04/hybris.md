---
summary: Telemachus tells Menelaus that the suitors of his mother are “overweening in their arrogance” (4.321), and in Ithaca the suitors amuse themselves at their games “in their arrogance” (4.627).
---

**The suitors' arrogance.** Asked his errand, Telemachus describes his house: hostile men eat it up, slaughtering his sheep and cattle, “the suitors of my mother, overweening in their arrogance” (4.318–321; see [[the-suitors]]), the verse with which he addressed them in Book 1 (1.368). Menelaus answers with the simile of the doe that lays her fawns in a lion's lair: they wished to lie in the bed of a fierce-hearted man, “weaklings as they are” (4.333–340; see [[lion]] and [[alke]]). The narrator shows them on the leveled ground before Odysseus' hall, throwing the discus and the javelin, ὅθι περ πάρος, ὕβριν ἔχοντες, “where they used to, in their arrogance” (4.625–627, again 17.167–169).

**Overbearing words.** The book's other words for the same spirit are ὑπερφίαλος, “overbearing”, of Ajax's boast (4.503) and the suitors' talk (4.774, 4.790; see [[overbearing]]), and ὑπερηνορέων, “overweening”, in Penelope's prayer and the narrator's account (4.766, 4.769; see [[overweening]]). Penelope's rebuke sets their spirit and their shameful deeds against a king who did no man wrong (4.687–695; see [[atasthalie]] and [[no-thanks-for-good-deeds]]).
