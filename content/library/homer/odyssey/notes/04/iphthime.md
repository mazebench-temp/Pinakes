summary: Athena makes a phantom in the likeness of Penelope's sister Iphthime and sends it to her chamber; it tells her not to grieve, since Athena herself goes with her son, but will not say whether Odysseus is alive or dead (4.795–841). The speeches listed under her name (4.804–807, 4.825–829, 4.836–837) are the phantom's, in her form.

**The likeness.** Penelope has fallen asleep in her upper room, pondering whether her son will escape the suitors (4.787–794), when “the gray-eyed goddess Athena thought of another thing. / She made a phantom, and it was like a woman in form, / Iphthime, daughter of great-hearted Icarius, / whom Eumelus had married, who had his home in Pherae” (4.795–798; see [[athena]], [[eidolon]], [[icarius]], [[eumelus]] and [[pherae]]). The goddess does not come herself, as she came to Telemachus in the form of Mentes and Mentor, but sends a likeness, as Zeus sends the Dream to Agamemnon in the form of Nestor (*Iliad* 2.20–22; see [[dream]]). In Homer, Merry notes, the dream is no mere creation of the sleeper's mind but has a real existence apart from him (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.796): this one passes into the chamber “past the strap of the bolt”, “and it stood above her head and spoke to her” (4.802–803), the verse of the shade of Patroclus standing over Achilles (*Iliad* 23.68). It opens with a question, “Are you asleep, Penelope, grieving at heart?” (4.804), where the Iliad's dreams say “You are asleep” (*Iliad* 2.23, 23.69), and promises that her son “will yet come home” (4.806).

**The sisters.** Penelope, “slumbering very sweetly at the gates of dreams” (4.809), takes the phantom for her sister: “Why have you come here, sister? You have not been / a visitor before, since you live very far away” (4.810–811; see [[penelope]]). Pherae lies far to the north, in Thessaly (see [[pherae]]). She tells her griefs again in the verses of her lament to her women, her husband lost and now her son gone (4.814–816 = 4.724–726). The phantom answers that Athena herself goes with him as his escort and has sent it to tell her so (4.825–829), but when Penelope asks of Odysseus it refuses: “Of him I will not tell you from beginning to end, / whether he is alive or dead; and it is a bad thing to speak empty wind” (4.836–837). Then it slips away “past the bolt of the doorpost / into the blasts of the winds” (4.838–839), and Penelope wakes comforted, “so clear was the dream that had rushed upon her in the dead of night” (4.841).

**Who speaks.** The phantom's three speeches are listed under Iphthime's name, since they are spoken in her form; the poem calls the speaker εἴδωλον ἀμαυρόν, “the dim phantom” (4.824, 4.835). Iphthime herself is not seen, and she is not named again in Homer.
