summary: Menelaus tells his wanderings himself, twice. First as the price of his wealth: he brought it home in the eighth year, after wandering to Cyprus, Phoenicia and Egypt, the Ethiopians, the Sidonians, the Erembi and Libya, while his brother was murdered at home (4.78–99). Then in the tale of Egypt: the gods held him on Pharos for want of hecatombs, until Proteus sent him back to the river Aegyptus; there he sacrificed, heaped a mound for Agamemnon, and came home with a fair wind (4.351–586). The gifts of Egyptian Thebes and of the Sidonian king are the wealth of those years (4.125–132, 4.615–619).

**In his own words.** Telemachus whispers that the house is like the court of Olympian Zeus, and Menelaus, who has heard him, answers that no mortal could rival Zeus, but among men there may be one who will rival him in possessions, or there may be none (4.71–81; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.80, “few or none”; see [[possessions]]). Then he tells where the wealth came from:

{{quote:a4bf1b79-51cc-5c79-bda9-89592bfbf2f7}}

ἠγαγόμην, “I brought them home”, has the possessions for its object (Merry on 4.82). “In the eighth year” agrees with Nestor's story, in which Aegisthus ruled Mycenae for seven years after the murder, Orestes came back in the eighth, and Menelaus came home on the day of the funeral feast (3.304–312). ἐπαληθείς, “after wandering”, the scholia gloss as πλανηθείς, “having wandered”; some, they add, understood ἐπὶ τοὺς ἀληθεῖς Αἰγυπτίους, “to the truthful Egyptians”, since the Egyptians were skilled in prophecy (Merry's critical note on 4.83). The places run from Cyprus and Phoenicia to Egypt, and then to the Ethiopians, the Sidonians, the Erembi and Libya, a list that the ancient geographers argued over (see [[cyprus]], [[phoenicians]], [[egypt]], [[ethiopians]], [[sidonians]], [[erembi]] and [[libya]], and the general article for the debate).

**The price.** “While I, gathering much substance in those lands, / was wandering, meanwhile another man killed my brother / in secret, unawares, by the guile of his ruinous wife; / so it is with no joy that I am lord of these possessions” (4.90–93; see [[agamemnon]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). Merry set these verses beside Nestor's account of the same years, and would read ὡς, “while”, at 3.301, so that there too the wandering and the murder happen at once (on 3.301; see the Book 3 note). He would live with a third of his wealth, he says, if the men were safe who perished at Troy (4.97–99): Merry explains the house he lost as his own, left to be wasted when he went to Troy and more than replaced since, and the wish as a second reason for his joyless wealth, the lives lost for his sake (on 4.95; see [[troy]]).

**The wealth of the journey.** ἀγείρων, “gathering”, points to presents received, as Nitzsch observed and Merry agreed (on 3.301), and the gifts of this book show what the gathering meant. In Egyptian Thebes Polybus gave Menelaus two silver bathtubs, two tripods and ten talents of gold, and his wife Alcandre gave Helen a golden distaff and a silver basket on wheels (4.125–132; see [[polybus-of-thebes]], [[alcandre]] and [[thebes-in-egypt]]); Helen's drugs were the gift of Polydamna, wife of Thon (4.227–232; see [[polydamna]]); and the mixing bowl that Menelaus gives Telemachus is the gift of Phaedimus, king of the Sidonians, “when his house sheltered me / as I came there on my way home” (4.618–619; see [[phaedimus]] and [[mixing-bowl]]).

**Held in Egypt.** The tale that Menelaus tells Telemachus the next morning is the end of the wanderings: “In Egypt, eager as I was to come home, the gods / still held me back, since I had not offered them perfect hecatombs” (4.351–352; see [[hecatomb]]). τεληέσσας, “perfect”, Merry takes as “effective”, sacrifices that win fulfillment from the gods, rather than perfect victims (on 4.352). On the island of Pharos the gods kept him twenty days without a wind, until the provisions were almost spent (4.354–363; see [[pharos]]), and only the daughter of the Old Man of the Sea showed him how to learn his way home (see [[eidothea]] and [[proteus]]). The answer broke his heart, because the old man bade him go back again over the misty sea “to Egypt, a long and grievous way” (4.481–483), though, as Merry notes, it was a single day's sail (on 4.483, from 4.355–357).

**Home.** He went back to the river, offered perfect hecatombs, and ended the anger of the gods; he heaped up a tomb for Agamemnon, “so that his fame might be unquenchable”, and the immortals gave him a fair wind and quickly sent him home (4.581–586). Proteus had told him the end of his journeys too: not death in Argos but the Elysian plain (4.561–569; see [[elysium]]).

**Menelaus and Odysseus.** πολλὰ παθὼν καὶ πόλλʼ ἐπαληθείς, “after much suffering and much wandering” (4.81), is the phrase Helen will use of Odysseus himself in the fifteenth book, when she reads the omen of the eagle and the goose: so Odysseus, after many sufferings and much wandering, will come home and take vengeance (15.176–177, a plain rendering; see [[odysseus]]). Menelaus is the king who wandered abroad for years, held on an island with his men starving, until a goddess helped him home, while his brother was murdered at home; Odysseus, the old man tells him, is still held on an island (4.555–560; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).
