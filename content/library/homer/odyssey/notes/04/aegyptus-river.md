summary: Proteus tells Menelaus that he will not see his home until he has gone back to “the water of Aegyptus, the river fallen from Zeus”, and offered hecatombs to the gods; Menelaus sails back to the river, sacrifices, and heaps up a tomb for Agamemnon before the gods send him home (4.475–483, 4.581–586).

**The command.** Menelaus has asked which of the gods binds him on Pharos, and the old man answers that he should have sacrificed to Zeus and the other gods before he embarked (4.468–474; see [[proteus]] and [[pharos]]):

{{quote:bbc7be51-794e-5c72-b665-e72bcda7f2f2}}

The river is first named here, with the land's name, Αἰγύπτοιο, and the epithet the Iliad gives the Spercheius and the Scamander, διιπετέος ποταμοῖο, which Eustathius explained as “rain-fed”, of the Nile filled by the summer rains of Ethiopia (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.477; see the translation's note on 4.477, 4.581, and [[fallen-from-zeus]]). The command breaks Menelaus' heart, because it sends him back “over the misty sea / to Egypt, a long and grievous way” (4.482–483; see [[egypt]] and [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]).

**At the river.** At dawn the ships are launched, and the same verse names the river again: “And back to the water of Aegyptus, the river fallen from Zeus, / I brought my ships to a halt, and offered perfect hecatombs” (4.581–582; see [[hecatomb]]). With Αἰγύπτοιο Merry would supply ὕδωρ, “water”, from 4.477, or ῥοάς, “streams” (9.450), and compares the familiar εἰς Ἀίδαο, “to the house of Hades”; στῆσα, “I halted”, is the verb of Odysseus' false tale, στῆσα δʼ ἐν Αἰγύπτῳ ποταμῷ, “I halted my ships in the river Aegyptus” (14.258, a plain rendering; Merry on 4.581). When the gods' anger was ended, Menelaus heaped up a tomb for Agamemnon there, “so that his fame might be unquenchable”, and the immortals gave him a fair wind home (4.583–586; see [[agamemnon]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]).

**Where he landed.** Some, Merry reports, took Αἴγυπτος for the Canopic branch of the Nile and Naucratis for the place where Menelaus landed; he prefers to read the whole passage as a poet's use of a current story, not as topography (on 4.355).
