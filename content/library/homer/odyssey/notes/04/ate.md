grc: [ἀάσθη]
en: [blinded]
---

**Helen's folly.** At the end of her tale Helen says that her heart had already turned to going home, “and I groaned over the blind folly that Aphrodite / gave me, when she led me there away from my dear native land, / leaving behind my child and my bridal chamber and my husband” (4.260–263; see [[helen]] and [[aphrodite]]). In the Iliad she wished to have died before she followed Paris, “leaving my bridal chamber and my kinsmen, / and my darling daughter” (*Iliad* 3.173–175). Merry takes the passage to show how the poet joins good understanding to right conduct and ignorance to crime: wrong is a blinding of the mind rather than a perversion of the will, and the blindness may be a god's work, as Agamemnon pleads in the Iliad (*Iliad* 19.86–89, 19.137); here Aphrodite sends it, and from the blindness comes the sense of “damage”, “mischief” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.261; see [[who-is-to-blame]]).

**Ajax.** ἀάω, “blind, mislead”, is the verb of ἄτη. Poseidon saved Ajax from the sea on the Gyraean rock, and he would have escaped his doom, “had he not let fall an overbearing word and been greatly blinded” (μέγʼ ἀάσθη, 4.503): he said that he had escaped against the will of the gods. Poseidon split the rock, and the piece “on which Ajax had first sat when he was greatly blinded” carried him down into the sea (4.507–511; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[the-death-of-ajax-son-of-oileus]]).
