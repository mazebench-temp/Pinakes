---
summary: Menelaus names the Sidonians among the peoples of his wanderings (4.84), and the silver mixing bowl that he gives Telemachus was the gift of Phaedimus, “the king of the Sidonians”, whose house sheltered him on his way home (4.615–619).
---

**In the list.** “And I came to the Ethiopians and the Sidonians and the Erembi” (4.84; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]], [[ethiopians]] and [[erembi]]). The verse before has already named Phoenicia (4.83), and here, as in the history of Achilles' prize bowl in the Iliad, the Sidonians are kept apart from the Phoenicians (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.84; *Iliad* 23.740–745; see [[phoenicians]]). Strabo explains the double naming in two ways: the poet names the people and then their mother city, a familiar figure, as in “the Trojans and Hector”; and he means to show that Menelaus stayed longer among the Sidonians, as the praise of their craftsmanship shows, and the guest-friendship Helen had found there with Paris (*Geography* 1.2.33; see [[sidon]] and [[paris]]).

**The king's gift.** The finest of Menelaus' treasures comes from them. In place of the horses that Telemachus refuses he gives a mixing bowl of silver, rimmed with gold, “the work of Hephaestus. The hero Phaedimus gave it to me, / the king of the Sidonians, when his house sheltered me / as I came there on my way home” (4.615–619; see [[phaedimus]], [[mixing-bowl]] and [[guest-gifts]]). Strabo quotes the verses for the Sidonians' skill, and takes “the work of Hephaestus” for praise, as fine things are called the works of Athena, of the Graces or of the Muses (*Geography* 1.2.33; see [[hephaestus]]). The bowl and the verses return when Menelaus gives it at Telemachus' departure (15.113–119).
