---
summary: The finest of Menelaus' treasures, the mixing bowl he gives Telemachus in place of the horses, is “the work of Hephaestus”, a gift from Phaedimus, king of the Sidonians.
---

**The work of Hephaestus.** When Telemachus refuses the horses, since Ithaca is a land for goats, Menelaus changes the gift for the finest and most precious of the treasures in his house: “I will give you a well-wrought mixing bowl; of silver / it is all, and its rims are finished with gold, / the work of Hephaestus. The hero Phaedimus gave it to me, / the king of the Sidonians, when his house sheltered me / as I came there on my way home” (4.615–619; see [[mixing-bowl]], [[phaedimus]], [[sidonians]] and [[guest-gifts]]). ἔργον δʼ Ἡφαίστοιο, “the work of Hephaestus”, comes again when the gift is given at last in Book 15 (15.117, in the same five verses), and the golden jar in which the bones of Achilles and Patroclus lie, a gift of Dionysus, is ἔργον δὲ περικλυτοῦ Ἡφαίστοιο, the work of renowned Hephaestus (24.73–77). A god's work passes from hand to hand among men as a gift, as the scepter that Hephaestus made passed from Zeus through Hermes, Pelops, Atreus and Thyestes to Agamemnon (*Iliad* 2.101–108; see [[the-scepter-of-agamemnon]]). The Iliad's finest silver bowl was Sidonian too: the prize for the footrace at Patroclus' games, “a mixing bowl of silver, well wrought”, which “Sidonians, skilled in intricate work, had worked it finely”, and Phoenicians brought over the sea and gave to Thoas (*Iliad* 23.740–745; see [[sidon]]). Menelaus' bowl is the gift of a Sidonian king, and the work of a god.
