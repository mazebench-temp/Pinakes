Of the two Achaean leaders who perished on the way home Ajax is the first: wrecked among his ships, he was brought by Poseidon to the rocks of Gyrae and saved from the sea, but he boasted that he had escaped against the will of the gods, and Poseidon split the rock under him (4.495–511).

**The first of two.** Menelaus asks the old man whether all the Achaeans came home unharmed (4.486–490; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]), and is told that he will not be long without tears: “of the leaders of the bronze-armored Achaeans only two / perished in their homecoming; and at the battle you were there yourself” (4.496–497). μάχῃ, “at the battle”, stands against νόστῳ, “in their homecoming”: what happened at Troy Menelaus saw for himself. The two, Merry says, are the Locrian Ajax and Agamemnon, and the one still alive is Odysseus (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.497). Αἴας μέν, “Ajax”, is answered by σὸς δέ … ἀδελφεός, “But your brother” (4.512; Merry on 4.512; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]).

**The rocks.** “First Poseidon drove him onto the Gyrae, / the great rocks, and saved him from the sea” (4.500–501). Γυραί, the rocks, and Γυραίη πέτρη, “the Gyraean rock” (4.507), take their name from their rounded shape (Merry on 4.499, comparing Eurybates the herald, who is γυρὸς ἐν ὤμοισιν, “round-shouldered”, at 19.246, a plain rendering). Where they lay was disputed: near Myconos, or off Caphereus in Euboea (see the translation's note on 4.500 and [[gyrae]]). πρῶτα, “First”, prepares the reversal: Poseidon is the god who saves him, and the god who kills him (see [[poseidon]]).

**The boast.** “And he would have escaped his doom, hated though he was by Athena, / had he not let fall an overbearing word and been greatly blinded: / he said that against the will of the gods he had escaped the great gulf of the sea” (4.502–504; see [[athena]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). Merry takes the verse as the turn to “his second sin and final catastrophe”, answering πρῶτα, and ἀάσθη not as a physical injury but as the blindness or infatuation that heaven lets come upon the guilty (on 4.502; see [[ate]]); φῆ … φυγέειν is “said he had escaped”, since he thought himself safe on the rock (on 4.504). ὑπερφίαλον ἔπος, “an overbearing word” (see [[overbearing]]), is the language this book uses of the suitors in Ithaca: Antinous warns them to “avoid overbearing talk” (4.774), and Penelope lies awake wondering whether her son will “be brought down by the overbearing suitors” (4.790; see [[the-suitors]]). Poseidon “heard him speaking big words” (4.505): the scholia explain μεγάλʼ … αὐδήσαντος as speaking haughtily, and Merry hears a loud shout in the words as well, comparing the Cyclops' shouting to his neighbors (on 4.505; 9.399).

**The rock split.** He struck the rock with his trident, “and the one part stayed where it was, but the broken piece fell into the sea, / the piece on which Ajax had first sat when he was greatly blinded” (4.508–509). μέγʼ ἀάσθη, “greatly blinded”, is said of him twice, of the word and of the seat on which he spoke it (4.503, 4.509).

**The last verse.** “So he perished there, when he had drunk the salt water” (4.511). The scholia say that the verse was in no edition and call it very cheap, wondering that Aristarchus did not mark it as spurious (Merry's critical note). Nitzsch rejects it, as Eustathius did, for its poverty and flippancy, and Ameis hears a parody in it; Merry defends it, finding in it “a grim humour”, the bitter irony of a boaster's end, one moment sitting secure on the rocks and the next dead of a mouthful of salt water (on 4.510–511; see the translation's note on 4.511).

**Why it is told.** Ajax is the first answer to Menelaus' question, and the first lesson in it. Menelaus has just learned from the old man that he himself is held in Egypt because he did not sacrifice to the gods before he sailed (4.472–480), as he told Telemachus at the start of his tale, “and the gods always wished their commands to be remembered” (4.351–353); and he has said to Eidothea that “a god is hard for a mortal man to overpower” (4.397; see [[a-god-is-hard-for-a-mortal-to-overpower]]). Ajax is the man who said the opposite. The Iliad knows him already as the goddess's victim in a smaller way: in the footrace at Patroclus' funeral games Athena makes him slip in the dung of the oxen so that Odysseus wins, and he complains that she always stands by Odysseus “like a mother” (*Iliad* 23.768–783; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]).
