---
summary: Noemon needs the ship he lent Telemachus to cross over to Elis “with its wide dancing floors”, where he keeps twelve mares with unbroken mules at their teats (4.634–637).
---

Noemon's question gives the suitors their first news of the voyage: “He went off with a ship of mine; and I have need of it / to cross over to Elis with its wide dancing floors, where I have horses, / twelve mares, and at their teats hard-working mules, / unbroken; one of these I would drive off and break in” (4.634–637; see [[noemon-son-of-phronius]] and [[the-suitors]]). Elis is the mainland across the sea from the islands, as in the Catalogue, where Dulichium and the Echinae lie “across the sea, opposite Elis” (*Iliad* 2.626; see [[dulichium]]). Merry explains the paddock from what Telemachus has just said of Ithaca, that it is no place for horses: Noemon keeps his breeding mares on the mainland, though mules, the beasts of hill country, suited Ithaca well (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.635; see [[ithaca]], [[horses]] and [[mules]]). Ἤλιδʼ ἐς εὐρύχορον, “to Elis with its wide dancing floors”, gives the land an epithet of towns and countries, which the Iliad has for Mycalessus, Hellas and Sicyon (*Iliad* 2.498, 9.478, 23.299) and the later books of this poem for Lacedaemon (13.414, 15.1; see [[with-wide-dancing-floors]]).
