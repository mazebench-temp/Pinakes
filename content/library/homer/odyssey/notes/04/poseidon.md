summary: Poseidon appears in Proteus' tale. The Old Man of the Sea is his servant, and he is the god who saved Ajax, son of Oileus, from the sea on the rocks of Gyrae, and then, when Ajax boasted that he had escaped against the will of the gods, split the rock with his trident and drowned him.
# Unnamed: "and saved him from the sea" (4.501); he took his trident, struck the Gyraean rock and split it (4.506-508).

**The master of the Old Man.** Eidothea describes her father as “immortal Proteus of Egypt, who knows the depths / of the whole sea, the servant of Poseidon” (4.385–386; see [[proteus]]). ὑποδμώς, “servant”, is found only here in Homer; Eustathius took the ὑπό to mark service in general, not a servant of lower rank (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.389). The old god who knows all the depths of the sea is the subject of the lord of the sea.

**Ajax at the Gyraean rock.** In Proteus' account of the returns Poseidon is both the god who saves Ajax and the god who kills him: “First Poseidon drove him onto the Gyrae, / the great rocks, and saved him from the sea” (4.500–501), and he would have escaped his doom, hated though he was by Athena, had he not boasted that he had escaped the great gulf of the sea against the will of the gods. “And Poseidon heard him speaking big words; / at once he took his trident in his strong hands / and struck the Gyraean rock, and split it apart” (4.505–507), and the broken piece carried Ajax down into the sea (4.508–511; see [[the-death-of-ajax-son-of-oileus]], [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[gyrae]]). The trident, τρίαινα, is in Homer only in Poseidon's hands: with it he leads the waves against the Achaean wall after the war (*Iliad* 12.27–28), and with it he will gather the clouds and stir up the sea against Odysseus' raft (5.291–292). Here it splits a rock, and the boast it answers is against the gods, not against Poseidon alone. Proteus says nothing in this book of Poseidon's anger with Odysseus, which keeps him from home in the first books of the poem (1.20–21, 1.68–75); the god whom Menelaus must appease is not named, and the sacrifice he owes is to “Zeus and the other gods” (4.472; see [[zeus]]).
