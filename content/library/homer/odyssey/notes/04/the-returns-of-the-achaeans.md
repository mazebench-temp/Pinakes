summary: What Nestor did not know, Proteus tells. Menelaus asks whether all the Achaeans came home unharmed, and the old man answers that many were brought down and many survived, but of the leaders only two perished on the way home, Ajax and Agamemnon, and one is still alive and held back on the wide sea, Odysseus with Calypso (4.486–560). Menelaus' own homecoming, by way of the river of Egypt, frames the account (4.351–586).

**The question.** When Proteus has told him how he will get home himself, Menelaus asks about the others:

{{quote:d92dc4e1-09d2-59fc-99d9-92552a767a69}}

“Whom Nestor and I left behind when we went from Troy” are the Achaeans who stayed with Agamemnon when the fleet divided, and those who turned back with Odysseus from Tenedos (3.153–164; see [[the-departure-of-the-greeks]]). Nestor knew nothing of their fate, “which of the Achaeans were saved and which perished” (3.184–185), and Menelaus' question is the same. ἀδευκέι, “bitter”, is the old rendering, from δεῦκος, said to be Aetolian for “sweet”; Apollonius explains it as “unseemly” and Hesychius as “unexpected” (see the translation's note on 4.489). The last verse, “or in the arms of his friends, when he had wound out the war” (4.490), is the verse of Telemachus' wish that his father had died at Troy among his companions and had a tomb (1.237–239), and Eumaeus says it again (14.368).

**The answer.** The old man warns him that he will not be long without tears, and gives the sum before the stories: “For many of them were brought down, and many were left; / but of the leaders of the bronze-armored Achaeans only two / perished in their homecoming; and at the battle you were there yourself. / And one, still alive somewhere, is held back on the wide sea” (4.495–498; see [[bronze-armored]]). λίποντο, “were left”, is “survived” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.495), and δάμεν, “were brought down”, is the reading of Aristarchus' copies, where the common ones had θάνον, “died” (Merry's critical note). μάχῃ, “at the battle”, is set against νόστῳ, “in their homecoming”: of what happened at Troy Menelaus needs no telling (Merry on 4.497). The scholium on 4.498 is corrupt where it speaks of Zenodotus: Düntzer and Dindorf mend it to say that he struck the verse out, and La Roche disagrees; the scholium answers that the verse is needed, since Menelaus later asks him to name the third man (4.551; Merry's critical note).

**The three.** The two who perished are Ajax, son of Oileus, drowned by Poseidon for his boast (4.499–511; see [[the-death-of-ajax-son-of-oileus]]), and Agamemnon, killed at Aegisthus' feast (4.512–537; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). The third is Odysseus. Menelaus asks to hear of him, “who is still alive and held back on the wide sea, / or dead” (4.552–553), and the old man answers that he saw him on an island, shedding big tears in the halls of the nymph Calypso, who holds him by force, with no ships and no companions to take him home (4.555–560; see [[odysseus]] and [[calypso]]). The verse “or dead; I want to hear, grieved though I am” (4.553) was marked as spurious in every edition, the scholia say, since it is absurd to ask after a third who perished when the god has said that only two did; Eustathius excuses it as the doubt of a man confused by grief, and Merry agrees with Lowe, who rejects the verse and finds the cause of the interpolation in Menelaus' own words about Odysseus, “whether he is alive or dead” (4.109–110; Merry on 4.553 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 4.553).

**Menelaus' own.** The account of the others is set inside the story of the last man to come home. He was held in Egypt for want of hecatombs (4.351–353), learned from Proteus that he must go back to the river and sacrifice (4.472–480), and when he had done so, and heaped a mound for his brother, the gods gave him a fair wind home (4.581–586; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). The old man ends with Menelaus' own end: he will not die in Argos but be sent to the Elysian plain, because he has Helen as his wife (4.561–569; see [[elysium]] and [[helen]]). For Telemachus, who came to Sparta to hear of his father (4.316–331; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]), the whole story of the returns leads to one sentence, the news of Odysseus on Calypso's island.
