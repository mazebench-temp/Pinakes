---
summary: Penelope, slumbering “at the gates of dreams”, is visited by the phantom that Athena sends in her sister's likeness, and wakes with her heart warmed, “so clear was the dream that had rushed upon her in the dead of night”. Unlike Agamemnon's dream, hers tells the truth, and will not tell what it does not know.
# ὀνειρείῃσι, "of dreams" (4.809), is claimed by onar's Book 4 note; ὄνειρον (4.841) by the entry.
passages:
  - 4.838-841 | So clear was the dream

**At the gates of dreams.** Penelope answers the phantom “slumbering very sweetly at the gates of dreams” (4.809; see [[penelope]] and [[eidolon]]). κνώσσω, “slumber”, and ὀνείρειος, “of dreams”, are found only here in Homer (see the translation's key-word row). Merry compares the two gates of dreams of which Penelope herself speaks in Book 19, one of horn, through which true dreams come, and one of ivory, through which the deceiving ones come (on 4.809; 19.562–567; see [[onar]]). The dream of this book comes as a person, the likeness of her sister Iphthime, which stands above her head and speaks (4.795–803; see [[dream-scene]] and [[iphthime]]). In Homer, Merry notes, the dream is not merely the creation of the sleeper's mind, but has a real existence apart from him (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.796).

**So clear was the dream.** When the phantom has gone into the blasts of the winds, Penelope starts up out of sleep:

{{quote:155eebb9-ef3a-5ba3-9395-1e96d717f9e4}}

ἐναργής, “clear”, is the word for a god who appears “in plain sight”, as Athena came to Nestor (3.420) and as gods are hard to face when they so appear (*Iliad* 20.131; see [[seeing-the-gods]]). Merry finds nothing in it against ἀμαυρόν, “dim”, the phantom's epithet (4.824, 4.835): the dream, dim to the bodily sense, had a clear meaning to the mind (on 4.841). ἐπέσσυτο, “had rushed”, shows the speed with which the phantom hastened to her (Merry on 4.841, comparing 6.20), as Athena, coming to Nausicaa in a friend's likeness, ἀνέμου ὡς πνοιὴ ἐπέσσυτο, rushed like a breath of wind to the girl's bed (6.20–22). νυκτὸς ἀμολγῷ, “in the dead of night”, is the Iliad's phrase for the hour of the lion that scatters the cattle and of the stars (*Iliad* 11.173, 22.28, 22.317); its etymology, Merry says, is more than doubtful (on 4.841).

**A true dream.** Zeus' dream in the Iliad lies to Agamemnon about Troy (see the note on *Iliad* 2 and [[agamemnons-dream]]). The dream that Athena sends Penelope tells the truth: Telemachus “will yet come home” (4.806), with Pallas Athena as his escort (4.826–828; see [[athena]]). When Penelope asks it for more, whether Odysseus is alive or dead, it will not answer: “it is a bad thing to speak empty wind” (4.836–837; see [[it-is-bad-to-speak-empty-wind]]). Her heart is warmed, and in the same night the suitors sail out to lie in wait for her son at Asteris (4.842–847; see [[the-suitors]]).
