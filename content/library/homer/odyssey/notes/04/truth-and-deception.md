summary: A book of disguises and of truth asked for. Helen tells how Odysseus entered Troy as a beggar and she alone knew him (4.244–256), and Menelaus how she tried the men in the Horse with their wives' voices (4.274–289). Menelaus promises to tell Telemachus all that “the unerring Old Man of the Sea” told him, “nor deceive you” (4.347–350), and the old man is caught by a trick of sealskins (4.435–453). In Ithaca Noemon saw “Mentor, or a god” (4.653–656), and the phantom will not speak “empty wind” (4.837).

**Shall I be wrong?** Helen begins with a question to herself: ψεύσομαι ἦ ἔτυμον ἐρέω; “Shall I be wrong, or speak the truth? But my heart bids me speak” (4.140). Merry argues from the same verse in the Iliad, where Nestor hears hoofbeats in the night (*Iliad* 10.534), that the choice is between speaking the truth and saying nothing, “Shall I withhold the truth?”, since ψεύδεσθαι is to say what one knows to be false (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.140; see the translation's note on 4.140 and [[pseudos]]).

**Disguises at Troy.** Helen's tale is of Odysseus' craft. Marring himself with blows and throwing rags about his shoulders, “hiding himself he made himself like another man, / a beggar, he who was nothing of the kind by the ships of the Achaeans”; the Trojans suspected nothing, but she alone knew him, and when she questioned him, “by craft he evaded me” (4.244–251; see [[odysseus-enters-troy-as-a-beggar]] and [[kerdos]]). Only when she had bathed him and sworn not to betray him did he tell her “the whole plan of the Achaeans” (4.252–256), which Merry takes to be the plan of the Horse (on 4.256). Menelaus answers with her own deception: at the Horse she called the best of the Danaans by name in the voices of their wives, and Odysseus held back the men who would have answered (4.274–289; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]], [[eidos]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]).

**Tell me truly.** Telemachus asks Menelaus to “tell me truly” (4.331), and Menelaus will not swerve aside or deceive him, but tell all that the unerring Old Man of the Sea told him (4.347–350). Eustathius saw no difference between κρύψω, “conceal”, and ἐπικεύσω, “hide”; Merry suggests that κρύψω may carry the force of falsehood, as in Achilles' hatred of the man who hides one thing in his heart and says another (*Iliad* 9.313; Merry on 4.350). The old man himself is νημερτής, “unerring” (4.349, 4.384, 4.401, 4.542), Eidothea will tell Menelaus “very exactly” (4.383, 4.399), and Menelaus asks him for “an exact account” (4.486; see [[give-me-an-exact-account]]). But the truth is won by a trick: hidden under sealskins, the men are counted among the seals, and the old man “had no thought / in his heart that there was a trick” (4.452–453; see [[dolos]] and [[the-gods-know-all-things]]); he in turn asks what Menelaus needs, “trying to mislead me”, as Menelaus says (4.465; see the translation's note on 4.465).

**Mentor, or a god.** In Ithaca Antinous asks Noemon to tell him truly when and with whom Telemachus sailed (4.642–647). Noemon saw “Mentor, or a god, who was like him in every way” go on board as their leader, and marvels, since he saw Mentor in Ithaca the morning after (4.653–656; see [[mentor]] and [[in-the-likeness-of-mentor]]). Antinous bids the suitors avoid overbearing talk, lest someone report it inside, and carry out their plan in silence (4.774–777; see [[silence]]); Eurycleia, who kept Telemachus' voyage secret under oath, will now “not hide my word” from Penelope (4.744). The phantom, asked whether Odysseus lives, keeps silent rather than guess: “it is a bad thing to speak empty wind” (4.837; see [[it-is-bad-to-speak-empty-wind]]).
