---
summary: In Proteus' account Agamemnon, whom Hera had saved from the storm at sea, was just about to reach the steep mountain of Malea when a storm wind caught him up and carried him to the far edge of the land, where Thyestes had lived and Aegisthus now lived (4.512–518).
---

“But when he was just about to reach the steep mountain of Malea, / then a storm wind caught him up / and carried him over the fish-filled sea, groaning heavily, / to the far edge of the land, where Thyestes had his dwelling / before, but at that time Aegisthus, son of Thyestes, dwelt there” (4.514–518; see [[agamemnon]], [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]] and [[aegisthus]]). Μαλειάων ὄρος αἰπύ ends the verse as it did in Nestor's account of Menelaus' own storm at the same cape (3.287; see [[steep]]), and the two brothers' voyages turn there: Menelaus was carried south to Crete and Egypt, Agamemnon to the edge of his own land and to his death.

**Why Malea?** Merry asks what brought Agamemnon near Malea at all, on a voyage from the coast of Asia Minor that would not naturally pass it. He weighs the scholiast's view (on 3.272) that Thyestes lived on Cythera; Ernst Curtius' suggestion that Greek sailors on the Phoenician route always made their landfall at Malea; the possibility that Agamemnon took the long course by the islands; Nitzsch's view that 4.514–516 are a rhapsode's interpolation, since a storm off Malea could hardly drive a ship to the borders of Thyestes' land; and Bothe's transposition of 4.519–520 after 4.516, which Bekker follows. He prefers not to press the geography: the storm drives Agamemnon south, he works back to Malea to sail up the coast of Argolis, a second storm drifts him to the end of the Argolic promontory, and there the wind changes and he makes his own port near Mycenae; but the difficulty disappears altogether if, with the geographer Andron, Thyestes and Aegisthus after him had their home on Cythera (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.514; see the translation's note on 4.514–520, and [[cythera]]).
