summary: Peisistratus says that “a son whose father is gone has many sorrows / in his halls” (4.164–165); Eidothea asks Menelaus whether he takes delight “in suffering sorrows” (4.372), and Penelope cries that “the Olympian has given me sorrows beyond / all the women” (4.722–723). Telemachus answers the tales of his father's endurance in Troy, “it is all the harder” (4.292).
grc: [ἄλγιον]
en: [harder]
---

ἄλγεα are the sorrows that fall on a man. Peisistratus gives them to the fatherless son, with no others to help him among the people (4.164–167; see [[fathers-and-sons]]); Eidothea asks whether Menelaus has given up of his own will “and take delight in suffering sorrows” on Pharos (4.372); Penelope says that the Olympian has given her sorrows beyond all the women of her age, a husband lost and now a son gone (4.722–728; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]] and [[mothers-and-sons]]). When Helen and Menelaus have told of Odysseus' craft and endurance at Troy, Telemachus answers ἄλγιον, “it is all the harder; for none of this kept grim destruction from him, / not even if the heart within him had been of iron” (4.292–293; see [[kradie]]). Merry renders “all the harder” and compares the same comparative in the Iliad (*Iliad* 18.306; *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.292).
