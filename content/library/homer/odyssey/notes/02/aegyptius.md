summary: Aegyptius is the first to speak in the assembly. Still grieving for his son Antiphus, he asks who has called the first assembly since Odysseus sailed, and why, and wishes the man well; Telemachus takes the wish for an omen and answers his questions one by one.

**The old man.** The narrator brings him on with his age and his sons:

{{quote:ff12b18a-b20f-51e0-b498-fa055879123d}}

The words that bring in the son, καὶ γάρ (2.17), may explain the father's great age, since twenty years before he had a grown son; Merry finds it more probable that they give the reason why he is the first to speak, his yearning for the son he has lost (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 2.17; see the translation's note on 2.17). The father's fields are ἔργα, “farms” (Merry on 2.22), and τοῦ is “for him”, as ὀδύρεσθαί τινος, “mourn for someone” (Merry on 2.24, comparing 4.104). The narrator tells how the son died, in the Cyclops' cave (2.19–20; see [[antiphus-son-of-aegyptius]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]); the son who sits with the suitors is Eurynomus (see [[eurynomus]]). The Phaeacians have such an elder too: “the old hero Echeneus, who was the eldest of the Phaeacian men”, speaks first in their council (7.155–158, 11.342–343, a plain rendering).

**The question.** He begins with the time: “Never has there been an assembly of ours or a session / since brilliant Odysseus went away in the hollow ships” (2.26–27; see [[the-assembly-of-the-ithacans]] and [[assembly]]). θόωκος, “session”, is the council of the elders, βουλὴ γερόντων (Merry on 2.26; see the translation's note on 2.7, 2.26). Then he asks who has called it, a young man or an older one, and on what need (2.28–29), whether he has heard news of the army's coming, or whether it is “some other public matter” (2.30–32). The army may be an enemy's or the one that sailed for Ilios: the scholia give both, and Ameis takes the second, since a father longing for his son would think of the army's return; Merry finds it more reasonable that news of an invasion or a raid of pirates should be the cause of a sudden assembly (on 2.30). The old man's optative, εἴποι, “he might tell” (2.31), leaves it uncertain both whether the man has news and whether he would share it; when Telemachus repeats the verse with the subjunctive εἴπω, he means that he would tell it if he had it (Merry on 2.31).

**The wish.** “He seems to me a good man, may he prosper!” (2.33). ὀνήμενος is a participle of the aorist ὠνήμην; Merry rejects the older explanation, which supplied εἴη, and follows Classen, for whom the word describes one on whom the good wish ὄναιο, “may you have joy of it”, has been fulfilled, “blessed”, and a blessing to others (on 2.33). The old man prays that Zeus may bring to pass some good for the man who has called them, “whatever he has in his mind” (2.34; see [[zeus]] and the translation's note on 2.34), and Telemachus “rejoiced at the word of omen” (φήμη, 2.35; see [[divination]]). The force of such a word, Merry explains, lies in its being a chance utterance whose full bearing the speaker does not know: Aegyptius does not know that he is blessing Telemachus, who names himself as the man who called the assembly only in his answer (on 2.35, comparing κλεηδών, “a word of omen”, at 18.117 and 20.120).

**The answer.** Telemachus rises with the scepter and speaks to him first, “approaching the old man with words” (2.39; see the translation's table of key words). καθαπτόμενος implies earnestness, not necessarily roughness, and γέροντα goes with both the participle and the verb, “pointing his speech to the chief he addressed him” (Merry on 2.39). He answers the questions in order. “Old man, that man is not far off”: it was he who gathered the people (2.40–41). οὗτος ἀνήρ is not a way of saying “I”, Merry explains, but “this man (about whom you ask) is not far off”, as the old man will know when Telemachus tells him that he called the assembly himself (on 2.40). The other two questions come back in the same verses, turned into the first person: he has heard no news of the army, nor is it some other public matter, “but a business of my own” (2.42–45, as 2.30–32; see [[geron]] and [[telemachus]]).
