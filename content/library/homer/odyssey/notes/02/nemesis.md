---
summary: Telemachus bids the Ithacans “Be indignant yourselves, / and feel shame before others” (2.64–65), and Mentor is indignant “with the rest of the people” for sitting silent (2.239). Penelope began her web “lest one of the Achaean women in the land be indignant with me” (2.101), and Telemachus will not send his mother away, since “blame will come upon me from men” (2.136).

**What the people should feel.** Telemachus' appeal rises from men to gods: indignation of their own (νεμεσσήθητε, 2.64), shame before the neighbors (αἰδέσθητε, 2.65; see [[aidos]]), and fear of the gods' wrath, “lest, indignant at your evil deeds, they turn upon you” (2.66–67; see [[menis]]). It is the pair Poseidon bids each of the Achaeans set in his mind, αἰδώς and νέμεσις (*Iliad* 13.121–122). The appeal fails. When he has flung down the scepter, “all the others were silent” but Antinous (2.80–84; see [[silence]]).

**Blame as a reason.** Two speakers give the fear of others' blame as the reason for what they do. Penelope's pretext for the web was the women's: Laertes must not “lie without a winding-sheet, who won great possessions” (2.101–102; see [[laertes]] and [[penelope]]). The same verses return when she tells the tale herself to the stranger (19.146–147) and when the shade of Amphimedon tells it among the dead (24.136–137). Telemachus will not drive his mother out of the house for three reasons, the evil he would suffer from her father, the Erinyes she would call down, and the blame of men (2.132–137; see [[erinyes]], [[icarius]] and [[mothers-and-sons]]).

**Turned on the suitors.** “And if your own hearts are indignant at this, / leave my halls” (2.138–139): Telemachus gives the feeling to the suitors and repeats the warning of the evening before (2.139–145, as 1.374–380; see [[poine]]).

**Mentor's indignation.** At the end of the debate the word falls on the people. Mentor does not begrudge the suitors their violence, since they stake their own heads; “But it is with the rest of the people that I am indignant, that you all / sit silent, and do not approach them with words / and stop the suitors, few as they are, though you are many” (2.239–241; see [[mentor]], [[demos]] and [[laos]]). No one of the people answers; a suitor does, and dissolves the assembly (2.242–257; see [[assembly]]). At the end of the poem Odysseus tells the suitors that they feared neither the gods nor any νέμεσις of men to come (οὔτε θεοὺς δείσαντες … οὔτε τινʼ ἀνθρώπων νέμεσιν κατόπισθεν ἔσεσθαι, 22.39–40).
