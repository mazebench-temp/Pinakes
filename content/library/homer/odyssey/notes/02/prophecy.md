summary: The book sets prophecies against each other and fills them with the words of fulfillment. Halitherses reads the eagles as Odysseus' return and recalls the twentieth year he foretold; Eurymachus calls his oracle “never to be fulfilled” and prophesies against him; Leiocritus foretells that Telemachus will never make his journey; and Athena, as Mentor, tells Telemachus that the suitors will all perish in a single day.

**The words of fulfillment.** τελέω, “fulfill, accomplish”, and its kin run through the book. The people ponder “what was to be fulfilled” (τελέεσθαι, 2.156), in a verse built like the one that leaves Agamemnon, deceived by the dream, “pondering in his heart things that were not to be fulfilled” (*Iliad* 2.36; see [[agamemnons-dream]]). Halitherses' old prophecy “has been fulfilled” (τελευτηθῆναι, 2.171), “and now it is all being fulfilled” (τελεῖται, 2.176; see [[halitherses]]). Eurymachus answers that his own word “will surely come to pass” (τετελεσμένον ἔσται, 2.187; see [[it-will-be-fulfilled]]) and that the seer's oracle is ἀκράαντον, “never to be fulfilled” (2.202), from κραιαίνω, “accomplish”, the verb of a prayer's request in the Iliad (see [[fulfill-this-wish]]). Leiocritus says that Telemachus will “never accomplish this journey” (τελέει, 2.256). Athena, as Mentor, promises that the journey will not be “in vain or unfulfilled” (ἀτέλεστος, 2.273) if he is his father's son, and that there is hope that he will accomplish these things (τελευτῆσαι, 2.280; see [[athena]]); Antinous says that the Achaeans “will surely bring about” the ship and the oarsmen for him (τελευτήσουσιν, 2.306). Whose words will be fulfilled is the question of the book.

**A verse of remembered prophecy.** τὰ δὲ δὴ νῦν πάντα τελεῖται, “and now it is all being fulfilled” (2.176), closes a prophecy remembered at the time of its fulfillment wherever it comes in Homer: Calchas' reading of the portent at Aulis, as Odysseus recalls it (*Iliad* 2.330; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]), Hector's threat to burn the ships, as Agamemnon recalls it (*Iliad* 14.48), Calypso's warning that Odysseus would fill up his sorrows on the sea (5.300–302), Nausithous' prophecy that Poseidon would strike a Phaeacian ship (13.172–178), and Odysseus' parting words to Penelope when he sailed (18.259–271; plain renderings). Here alone the man who speaks the verse made the prophecy himself (see [[the-prophecy-of-halitherses]]).

**The contest.** Halitherses speaks from a sign of Zeus and with the authority of an old prophecy come true (2.161–176). Eurymachus, who claims to be “far better than you at prophesying” (2.180), sets a forecast of his own against it: the old man's fine, and a young man who will achieve nothing (2.188–193; see [[eurymachus]] and [[divination]]). Leiocritus forecasts that Telemachus will sit in Ithaca hearing news and never make the journey (2.255–256; see [[leiocritus-son-of-euenor]]), and he is proved wrong that night, when the ship sails (2.413–434; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). Athena's prophecy, in Mentor's shape, is the seer's in other words: the suitors know nothing “of death and black doom, / which is near them now, that they should all perish in a single day” (2.283–284; see [[black-doom]] and [[the-suitors]]). The poem fulfills it in Book 22.
