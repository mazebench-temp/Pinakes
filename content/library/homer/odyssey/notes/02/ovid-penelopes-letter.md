---
summary: Ovid gives Penelope the words and the cares of this book. Telemachus' cry to the assembly, that he would ward off the suitors if he had the strength, becomes hers; the father who should give her in marriage chides her delays; the old man of the shroud is useless in arms; and the journey that Telemachus prepares in secret from his mother becomes, in her letter, one on which she sent him.
refs: [2.60-62, 2.372-377]
---

**If I had the strength.** In the assembly Telemachus says that there is no man in the house such as Odysseus was, to ward off disaster from it: “And we are in no way such as to ward it off”, and “Truly I would ward it off myself, if I had the strength” (2.58–62; see the entry above). Ovid's Penelope says it of herself, “Nor have I the strength to drive the enemies from the house” (*Heroides* 1.109), and counts the household as one without a man to fight: “a wife without strength, old Laertes and Telemachus, a boy” (*Heroides* 1.97–98; Merry on 2.60 sets the couplet beside Telemachus' “we”). In this book Telemachus is a boy no longer: “But now that I am grown, and hearing the words of others / I learn, and the spirit within me grows” (2.314–315; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]).

**Icarius.** The father who chides her delays (*Heroides* 1.81–82) is the father of this book's speeches. The suitors “shudder at going to the house of her father” to let him give her to the man he wished (2.52–54); Antinous tells Telemachus to order her to marry “whichever man her father bids” (2.113–114), and Eurymachus to “bid his mother go back to her father's house” (2.195). Telemachus answers that he would have to pay back much to Icarius, and suffer evil from her father, if he sent her away of his own will (2.130–134; see [[icarius]] and [[the-icarius-problem]]). Ovid's Penelope adds what the book does not say, that her father is softened by her devotion and her chaste prayers (*Heroides* 1.85–86).

**Laertes.** The old man whom the letter calls useless in arms (*Heroides* 1.105–106) is the old man of the shroud, which Penelope said she must finish “against the time when / the deadly fate of death that lays men low takes him down” (2.99–100; see [[laertes]] and [[the-web-of-penelope]]).

**The journey.** In this book the journey is a secret kept from Penelope. Telemachus makes Eurycleia swear “to tell none of this to my dear mother / until the eleventh or the twelfth day has come, / or she herself misses me and hears that I have gone, / so that she may not mar her fair skin with weeping” (2.373–376), and tells the crew, “My mother has learned nothing of it” (2.411; see [[eurycleia]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). The suitors mock it: Leiocritus expects him to sit long in Ithaca hearing news “and never accomplish this journey” (2.256), and one of the young men wonders whether he too may perish far from his friends, “wandering like Odysseus” (2.333). Ovid's departure “against the will of all” (*Heroides* 1.100) is close to the journey of this book; his “we sent to Pylos” (*Heroides* 1.63–64) is not.
