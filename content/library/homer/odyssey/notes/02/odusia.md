---
summary: Three of the surviving lines of the Latin Odyssey render lines of Book 2. The first, which Gellius quotes, makes the death that Penelope's shroud awaits the day foretold by Morta; the others are Telemachus' threat to the suitors, at Pylos or at home, and an order at the start of the voyage.
refs: [2.99-100, 2.317, 2.422]
---

The lines are given as The Latin Library prints them, with the lines of Book 2 that it sets beside each. Gellius names the source of the first; for the other two this note relies on that collection, which does not name their ancient sources.

- ***quando dies adveniet quem profata Morta est.*** “when the day comes that Morta has foretold” (2.99–100): Penelope's words in Antinous' story of the web, the shroud for Laertes “against the time when / the deadly fate of death that lays men low takes him down” (see [[the-web-of-penelope]] and [[laertes]]). For μοῖρα, “fate”, Livius has Morta, and Homer's “time when” becomes the day that she has foretold. Gellius found the line in the *Ancient Readings* of Caesellius Vindex, who held that the three Parcae were called Nona, Decuma and Morta, and cited “this verse of Livius, the most ancient poet, from the Odyssey”; Gellius answers that Caesellius took Morta for a name when he should have taken it as Moera (*Attic Nights* 3.16.11; see [[moira]]).
- ***[aut] in Pylum deveniens aut ibi ommentans*** “either coming to Pylos or staying there” (2.317): Telemachus' answer to Antinous, “I will try to let loose the evil fates upon you, / whether I go to Pylos or stay here in this land” (2.316–317; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]] and [[pylos]]). The Latin keeps the two alternatives and gives each a participle: *deveniens*, “coming”, for ἐλθών, and *ommentans*, “staying”, for the second, which in Homer has no verb.
- ***tumque remos iussit religare struppis.*** “and then he ordered them to tie the oars with straps” (2.422): the start of the voyage, “And Telemachus urged on his companions and bade them / lay hold of the tackle” (2.422–423; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]] and [[oars]]). *Iussit* is Homer's ἐκέλευσεν, but where Homer has the tackle, the Latin has oars and their straps, which this passage does not name. Elsewhere the Odyssey's crews fit their oars “in leathern straps” (ἐρετμὰ τροποῖς ἐν δερματίνοισι, 4.782, 8.53; plain renderings).
