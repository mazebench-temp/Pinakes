---
summary: “So she spoke, and our proud spirit was persuaded” (2.103): Antinous tells how Penelope's request deceived the suitors; and the suitors are “the proud suitors” to Mentor (2.235) and to the narrator (2.299).
---

The formula θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ, which in the Iliad drives a man to dare, is here the suitors' spirit, persuaded by a woman's trick: ὣς ἔφαθʼ, ἡμῖν δʼ αὖτʼ ἐπεπείθετο θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ (2.103; [[the-web-of-penelope]], [[thymos]]). The verse returns when Circe persuades Odysseus' companions (10.466, 12.28) and when the shade of Amphimedon tells the story of the web (24.138), and Penelope tells it with ὣς ἐφάμην, τοῖσιν δʼ (19.148). The suitors' own epithet comes twice more: Mentor does not begrudge “the proud suitors” their deeds of violence, since they stake their own heads (2.235–237), and Telemachus finds “the proud suitors in the halls” when he comes back from the shore (2.299).
