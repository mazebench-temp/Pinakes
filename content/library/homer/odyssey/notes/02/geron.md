summary: The old men of Ithaca lead its assembly: the elders make way for Telemachus in his father's seat (2.14), and the hero Aegyptius, “bent with age” and knowing “countless things”, speaks first (2.15–16). Telemachus answers him as “Old man” with respect (2.40); Eurymachus gives Halitherses the same address in contempt, and ends with “old sir” (2.178, 2.192, 2.201).
grc: [γεραιέ]
en: [old sir]
---

**The old who know.** The book's old men are the ones who know: Aegyptius, “who was bent with age and knew countless things” (2.16; see [[aegyptius]]), Halitherses, who “alone surpassed those of his age / in knowing birds” (2.158–159; see [[halitherses]]), and Mentor, to whom Odysseus entrusted his house, “that it should obey the old man” (2.225–227; see [[mentor]] and the translation's note on 2.227). Merry takes καὶ γάρ (2.17) more probably as the reason why Aegyptius speaks first, his longing for his lost son, than as an explanation of his age, that twenty years before he had a grown son (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 2.17). Aegyptius asks whether the need has come on “one of the young men, or of those who are older” (2.29), and Eurymachus' threat turns on the same division: Halitherses, “who know many things of old”, must not urge “a younger man” to anger (2.188–189).

**The address.** Telemachus, rising to speak, approaches “the old man” with words (2.39) and answers his question, “Old man, that man is not far off” (2.40). Eurymachus uses the vocative to send Halitherses home to prophesy to his own children (2.178) and to threaten him with a fine (2.192; see [[poine]]), and he ends with γεραιέ, “old sir” (2.201; see [[divination]]). As in the Iliad, the address takes its tone from the speaker.
