---
summary: Antinous calls Penelope's web “this further trick” (2.93): “So for three years she escaped notice by her trick and persuaded the Achaeans” (2.106). Eurycleia fears that the suitors will plot that Telemachus “may perish by guile” (2.368).
---

**Penelope's trick.** Antinous has just said that she gives hope to all and sends messages to each man; the web is another trick besides, δόλον τόνδʼ ἄλλον (2.91–93; see [[noos]]). It is a woven one: she set up the loom, asked the suitors to wait until the shroud for Laertes was finished, wove by day and unraveled by night (2.94–105; see [[the-web-of-penelope]], [[loom]] and [[laertes]]). It worked by persuasion. “So she spoke, and our proud spirit was persuaded” (2.103), and for three years she “persuaded the Achaeans” (2.106; see [[peitho]]), until one of her women told (2.108–109). Penelope gives the same name to her own devices when she tells the stranger the tale: the suitors hasten the marriage, ἐγὼ δὲ δόλους τολυπεύω, while she winds up her tricks (19.137), and the shade of Amphimedon tells it among the dead with Antinous' verses (24.128, 24.141).

**The suitors' guile.** Eurycleia's fear for Telemachus is a trick of the suitors: once he has gone, “these men … will devise evil for you hereafter, / so that you may perish by guile” (2.367–368; see [[eurycleia]]). They do: Antinous asks for a swift ship and twenty companions, as Telemachus asked the people (2.212), to lie in wait for him in the strait between Ithaca and Samos (4.669–672, 4.842–847). The day before, Athena had told Telemachus to kill the suitors “whether by guile or openly” (1.296).
