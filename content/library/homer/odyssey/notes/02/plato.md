---
summary: The Phaedo makes Penelope's unraveling of her web, told in this book by Antinous, the figure of the endless work that the philosopher's soul refuses; the Republic would have no poet tell of a god in a man's likeness, as Athena takes Mentor's here; the Alcibiades makes the quarrel of the suitors and Odysseus a quarrel over the just and the unjust; and a phrase of the Timaeus was likened by Longinus to “the sacred strength of Telemachus”.
---

- The soul that philosophy has set free will not bind itself again to pleasures and pains, and “do the endless work of a Penelope handling her web the opposite way” (*Phaedo* 84a, on 2.104–105): [[penelopes-web-in-later-writers]].
- Let no poet tell of gods who go about “in the likeness of strangers from abroad” (*Republic* 2.381d, quoting 17.485–486); Proclus, answering, names Athena likened to Mentor (2.268, 2.401): [[athena-as-mentor-in-later-writers]] and [[seeing-the-gods]].
- The Odyssey and the Iliad are poems about a difference over the just and the unjust, and the battles and deaths came about through that difference, for the Achaeans and the Trojans, and for the suitors of Penelope and for Odysseus (*Alcibiades I* 112a–b). In this book Athena, in Mentor's likeness, calls the suitors “in no way discerning or just” (2.282): [[the-suitors]].
- Plato's “the kind of your condition” (*Timaeus* 19e), which Longinus, as Proclus reports, judged no different from Homer's “the sacred strength of Telemachus” (2.409): [[longinus-on-the-sacred-strength-of-telemachus]].
