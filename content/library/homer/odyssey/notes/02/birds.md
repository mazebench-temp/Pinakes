summary: The two eagles of Zeus are “the birds” at which the assembly marvels (2.155); Halitherses alone of his age knew how to read birds (2.159), and Eurymachus answers him that “Many are the birds that go to and fro under the rays of the sun, / and not all of them are birds of fate” (2.181–182).
# ὄρνιθας (2.155, 2.159) and ὄρνιθες (2.181), first met in the Odyssey here; in the later books they are always birds.
grc: [ὄρνιθας, ὄρνιθες]
# Unnamed: "not all of them are birds of fate" (2.182), the birds of 2.181.

**The birds of the omen.** When the eagles have darted away, “they were amazed at the birds, when they saw them with their eyes” (2.155; see [[eagle]] and [[the-omen-of-the-eagles]]). The book's word for birds is ὄρνις, the common word; οἰωνός, the word of the first book for the birds of omen (1.202), does not occur in it. The eagles are named as eagles once, when Zeus sends them (2.146), and are “the birds” from then on.

**Knowing birds.** Halitherses “alone surpassed those of his age / in knowing birds and in speaking words of fate” (2.158–159; see [[halitherses]] and [[divination]]). ὄρνιθας γνῶναι is the art that Athena, as Mentes, disowned when she prophesied all the same, “though I am no seer and have no clear knowledge of birds” (οὔτʼ οἰωνῶν σάφα εἰδώς, 1.202): the first book gives the prophecy without the art, the second the art and the prophecy together. In the Iliad the masters of it are the bird-seers, οἰωνοπόλοι, Calchas and Helenus (*Iliad* 1.69, 6.76; see [[best-of-bird-seers]]).

**Not all birds.** Eurymachus' answer turns on the word of the seer's credential: Halitherses speaks ἐναίσιμα, “words of fate”, and not all birds are ἐναίσιμοι, “birds of fate” (2.159, 2.182; see [[eurymachus]] and the translation's key-word row). The birds that “go to and fro” (φοιτῶσι) are the many birds of everyday, and ὑπʼ αὐγὰς ἠελίοιο, “under the rays of the sun”, is the Odyssey's phrase for the world of the living: Achilles in the underworld cannot help his father “under the rays of the sun”, and Odysseus asks Eumaeus whether his master's parents still live under them (11.498, 15.349, plain renderings). The Iliad's scorner of birds would not obey “long-winged birds” whether they went to the right or the left (*Iliad* 12.237–240; see [[one-omen-is-best]]); the suitor allows that some birds are signs and denies it of these. The saying has its own entry (see [[many-birds-not-all-of-fate]]).
