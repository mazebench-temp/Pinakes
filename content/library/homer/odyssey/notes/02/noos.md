summary: Antinous says of Penelope that “her mind has other things in view” (2.92), and that none of the women of old “knew devices like Penelope's” (2.121). Mentor speaks of the suitors' “mischief of their minds” (2.236), Athena of “the plan and the thought of the suitors” (2.281); Eurycleia guards the storeroom “with the great knowledge of her mind” (2.346), and Athena twice “thought of another thing” (2.382, 2.393).
grc: [νόου, νόοιο, νοήματα, νόημα, ἐνόησε, νοήμονες, ἀνοήμων]
en: [minds, devices, purpose, thought, discerning, fool]
---

**Penelope's mind.** Antinous' charge against her is a charge about her νόος: she gives hope to all and sends them messages, “but her mind has other things in view” (2.91–92). In Book 13 Athena tells Odysseus the same in the same verses, adding that his wife is always mourning for his homecoming (13.379–381), and the half-line returns when Penelope draws gifts from the suitors while her husband looks on and rejoices (18.281–283): what the suitor calls deceit, the goddess calls faithfulness (see [[penelope]]). Her νοήματα, “devices”, are beyond those of Tyro, Alcmene and Mycene, “and yet this one, at least, she did not devise rightly” (2.120–122; see [[kerdos]] and [[dolos]]), and Antinous grants that the gods are behind it: she keeps “to this purpose, which now / the gods put in her breast” (2.124–125).

**The suitors' minds.** Mentor does not begrudge the suitors “their deeds of violence in the mischief of their minds” (κακορραφίῃσι νόοιο, 2.236), and Athena tells Telemachus to “let be the plan and the thought of the suitors, / senseless men, since they are in no way discerning or just” (2.281–282; see [[boule]]). The half-line οὔ τι νοήμονες οὐδὲ δίκαιοι is Nestor's verdict on the Argives, for whom Zeus devised a grim homecoming (3.132–133). Telemachus, Athena says, will be “no coward and no fool” (οὐδʼ ἀνοήμων, 2.270, 2.278; see [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**Thinking of a way.** Eurycleia watches over the storeroom “with the great knowledge of her mind” (νόου πολυϊδρείῃσιν, 2.346; see [[eurycleia]]), and asks the boy why “this thought” has come into his mind (2.363–364). Athena's plans move the end of the book: twice “the gray-eyed goddess Athena thought of another thing” (2.382, 2.393), and she gathers the crew, borrows the ship, and pours sleep on the suitors (2.383–398; see [[athena]] and [[hypnos]]).
