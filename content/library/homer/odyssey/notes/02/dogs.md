**Two swift dogs.** “he went to the assembly, and in his hand he held the bronze spear, / not alone: with him went two swift dogs” (2.10–11; see [[telemachus]] and [[swift-dogs]]). The verse comes back twice, with ἔγχος ἔχων, “holding his spear”, for οὐκ οἶος, when Telemachus goes out through the hall to the place of assembly (17.61–62, 20.144–146), and the first time the next two verses come back with it, the grace that Athena pours on him and the people's gaze (17.63–64 = 2.12–13; see [[athena]]). οὐκ οἶος, ἅμα τῷ γε, “not alone: with him”, is a formula of attendance: Achilles springs out of the door of his house like a lion, “not alone: with him went two attendants” (*Iliad* 24.572–573), and Penelope, in the first book, comes down from her room “not alone; two handmaids went along with her” (1.331).

**The reading and the epithet.** Merry illustrates the reading δύω κύνες, “two dogs”, from Virgil (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 2.11; see the translation's note on 2.11). Virgil's Evander has the morning of this book: he rises at the dawn and the birdsong, dresses, binds sandals on his feet, girds a sword to his side, “and two guardian dogs go before him from the high threshold and keep step with their master” (*Aeneid* 8.454–462; see [[virgil]] and [[dressing-scene]]). The variant that Merry reports, κύνες πόδας ἀργοί, is the verse-end of the herdsmen's dogs on the Shield of Achilles, “nine dogs, fleet of foot, followed them” (*Iliad* 18.578). The older interpreters, he reports, took ἀργός as “white”, as Eustathius explains it by λευκόποδες, “white-footed”; Merry takes it of swiftness, a word that joins brightness and quickness, and compares the name of the Harpy Ποδάργη, “storm-swift” (on 2.11; *Iliad* 16.150). The Iliad's ἀργίποδας κύνας are “the swift dogs” that Hector was fated at his birth to glut, as Hecuba says (*Iliad* 24.209–211).

**What dogs are for.** Merry counts the uses of dogs in Homer: they watch the herds and the farmstead, as at Eumaeus' farm (17.200) and with the herdsmen on the Shield (*Iliad* 18.578); they hunt (*Iliad* 11.325); and Achilles has “nine dogs that fed at his table” (*Iliad* 23.173; on 2.11; see [[dogs]] and [[cremation]]). The house of Odysseus has its old dog too, Argus, whom Odysseus raised himself before he sailed for Ilios, and who knows him after twenty years (17.291–327; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).
