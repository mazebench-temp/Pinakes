summary: The book has two kinds of sign and two readers. Telemachus takes the old man's chance wish as a word of omen (2.33–35); when Zeus sends two eagles, Halitherses, who alone of his age knew how to read birds and to speak words of fate, prophesies “not untried, but with sure knowledge”, and Eurymachus tells him to prophesy to his own children and says that the suitors do not heed his oracle (2.155–202).

**The word of omen.** Aegyptius ends his questions with a blessing on the man who has called the assembly, whoever he may be: “He seems to me a good man, may he prosper! For him himself / may Zeus bring to pass some good, whatever he has in his mind” (2.33–34; see [[aegyptius]]), and “the dear son of Odysseus rejoiced at the word of omen” (2.35). φήμη is a saying heard as a sign. Merry reports that its force lies in its being a chance utterance whose full bearing the speaker does not know: Aegyptius does not know that he is blessing Telemachus, who says that he is the man who gathered the people only at 2.40–41 (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 2.35). The word comes twice more in Homer, at dawn on the day of the killing of the suitors, when Odysseus asks Zeus for a word of omen from someone waking inside the house and a sign outside it; Zeus thunders from Olympus, and a woman grinding at the mill prays that this may be the suitors' last meal (20.98–119, plain renderings). The poem then calls her word κλεηδών (20.120), as it calls the suitors' chance wish for the beggar (18.117), and Merry finds doubtful Autenrieth's attempt to keep the two words apart (on 2.35). Father and son both “rejoiced” at the word, χαῖρε (2.35; 20.120). This book has both of the signs that Odysseus asks for in Book 20, a word of omen near its beginning and a sign from Zeus in the middle (2.146–154; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagles]]).

**The reader of birds.** Halitherses' art has two parts, “in knowing birds and in speaking words of fate” (2.159; see [[halitherses]] and [[birds]]): the knowledge that reads the sign, and the speech that gives its meaning. He claims both: “For I do not prophesy untried, but with sure knowledge” (2.170). μαντεύομαι, “prophesy”, is formed from μάντις, “seer”, though the narrator does not give him that name. The book's other prophet is a goddess, who in the first book disowned the seer's arts, “though I am no seer and have no clear knowledge of birds” (1.202), and in this one, in the likeness of Mentor, tells Telemachus that death and black doom are near the suitors, “that they should all perish in a single day” (2.283–284; see [[athena]] and [[prophecy]]).

**The scorner.** Eurymachus turns the seer's verb against him: “come now, prophesy to your own children” (μαντεύεο, 2.178), since in these things he is “far better than you at prophesying” himself (μαντεύεσθαι, 2.180). He accuses Halitherses of prophesying for gain, “waiting for a gift for your own house” (2.186), and says that the suitors do not heed his θεοπροπίη, “oracle” (2.201–202; see [[theopropion]] and [[eurymachus]]), in the words with which Telemachus told him the day before that he no longer heeded the oracles his mother asks of prophets (οὔτε θεοπροπίης ἐμπάζομαι, 1.415–416). Then he prophesies in his turn, “But I will speak out to you, and it will surely come to pass” (2.187; see [[it-will-be-fulfilled]]), and what he foretells is the old man's fine and the young man's failure (2.188–193). Agamemnon makes the same complaint of Calchas in the Iliad: “It is always dear to your heart to prophesy evil” (*Iliad* 1.107; see [[calchas]] and [[prophet-of-evils]]).

**Right and left.** The eagles dart away on the right (δεξιώ, 2.154; see [[eagle]]), the side of good omen, as on the right fly the eagle of Telemachus' leave-taking at Sparta and the hawk of his landing on Ithaca (15.160, 15.525), while the eagle that turns the suitors from their plot against him comes on the left (20.242), as the eagle of the Iliad's portent at the ditch came on the left of the Trojans (*Iliad* 12.201; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]]).
