summary: One of Penelope's women betrays the web to the suitors; Eurycleia, the old nurse, is the housekeeper who keeps the storeroom night and day; and Telemachus sails with his mother and the other slave women knowing nothing, “only one has heard the word” (2.108, 2.345–347, 2.361, 2.411–412).
# Forms first met in this book (they apply in the Odyssey only): δμωαί, printed without the iota subscript (2.412; also
# 6.307, 7.103), which "=δμῳ*" misses; τροφὸς, the nurse, always Eurycleia in the Odyssey (2.361 and later books).
# Unnamed: "one of her women told us" (2.108).
grc: [δμωαί, τροφὸς]
en: [one of her women]
refs: [2.108]

**The woman who told.** The web was betrayed from inside the house: “then indeed one of her women told us, who knew it clearly, / and we caught her unraveling the splendid web” (2.108–109; see [[the-web-of-penelope]]). Antinous does not name her, and in the nineteenth book Penelope herself lays the betrayal on her slave women, “careless dogs” (διὰ δμῳάς, κύνας οὐκ ἀλεγούσας, 19.154; a plain rendering).

**The housekeeper and the nurse.** “and a woman was in it as housekeeper night and day, / who guarded everything with the great knowledge of her mind, / Eurycleia” (2.345–347; see [[eurycleia]] and [[the-storeroom-of-odysseus]]). In the first book the ταμίη, the “housekeeper”, set bread before the guest (1.139), and later books give the title to Eurynome (17.495, 23.154); here it is Eurycleia's, the keeper of the stores. Telemachus calls her μαῖα, “Nurse” (2.349, 2.372), and the narrator φίλη τροφός, “the dear nurse” (2.361), her formula in the later books.

**The slave women.** At night Telemachus tells the crew, “My mother has learned nothing of it, / nor have the other slave women; only one has heard the word” (2.411–412). δμωαί here is printed without the iota subscript (see the translation's note on 2.412).
