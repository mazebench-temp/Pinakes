---
summary: Telemachus tells the assembly that the suitors are in his house every day, “sacrificing oxen and sheep and fat goats” (2.56), and that it would be better for him if the Ithacans themselves ate up his treasures and his herds, since then he could ask for them back (2.74–78).
---

**The suitors' sacrifice.** βοῦς ἱερεύοντες, “sacrificing oxen” (2.56), stands at the head of the verse that names the victims of the suitors' daily feast, oxen, sheep and fat goats (2.55–58; see [[the-suitors]], [[sheep]] and [[goats]]). In the first book Athena told the gods of the suitors “who are forever / slaughtering his thronging sheep and shambling cattle with curving horns” (1.91–92; see [[with-curving-horns]]); here the complaint is Telemachus' own, made to the people. Penelope repeats it to Eumaeus (17.534–538 = 2.55–59), and when the suitors make ready their meals in the later books they slaughter the same beasts with fat hogs added, “great sheep and fat goats, / and fat hogs and a cow of the herd” (17.180–181 = 20.250–251, a plain rendering; see [[boar]]).

**Treasures and herds.** Telemachus would rather the Ithacans themselves ate up “my treasures and my herds” (κειμήλιά τε πρόβασίν τε, 2.75): then there would be recompense, for he could go about the city asking for his goods until all was given back (2.74–78; see [[poine]] and [[possessions]]). πρόβασις, which occurs only here in Homer, belongs with πρόβατα, “flocks”, the property that walks (προβαίνω, “go forward”), as κειμήλια, “treasures”, are the property that lies stored (κεῖμαι, “lie”). The herds were the larger part of Odysseus' wealth: the swineherd counts twelve herds of his cattle on the mainland, and as many flocks of sheep, droves of swine and flocks of goats (14.100–101, a plain rendering; see the Book 1 note).
