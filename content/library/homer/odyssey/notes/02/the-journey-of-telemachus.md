**The ship asked for.** When pleading has failed, Telemachus asks the assembly for what Athena told him to get, a ship and twenty men (1.280):

{{quote:e382312c-1149-59ab-bbdd-1e13f6159f5a}}

The verses are Athena's plan and counsel of the first book turned into his own words (2.214 as 1.93, 2.215–217 as 1.281–283). What he will do depends on what he hears, as she said: endure another year, or come home and build his father's tomb (2.218–223, as 1.287–292; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). Leiocritus answers that Mentor and Halitherses may speed him on his way, but that he will sit in Ithaca hearing news and never make the journey (2.253–256; see [[leiocritus-son-of-euenor]]).

**Athena's promise.** He prays on the shore to the god who bade him go in a ship over the misty sea (2.262–266; see [[prayer]]), and Athena answers in the likeness of Mentor: “But the journey you have in mind will not be far off much longer” (2.285). She will fit out a swift ship and go with him herself; he is to make ready the provisions, “wine in two-handled jars, and barley meal, the marrow of men, / in thick skins”, while she gathers willing companions and picks out the best of the many ships of Ithaca (2.285–295; see [[mentor]] and [[athena]]).

**The suitors' offer.** Antinous mocks him with the offer the assembly refused, a ship and choice oarsmen for Pylos (2.306–308), and Telemachus answers that he will go, but as ἔμπορος, “a passenger”, since he has no ship of his own (2.318–320; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]). One of the young men jests that he may perish at sea, “wandering like Odysseus” (2.332–333).

**The provisions.** In his father's storeroom he has Eurycleia fill twelve jars with the sweetest wine after the one kept for Odysseus, and pour twenty measures of barley meal into well-sewn leather bags (2.349–355; see [[the-storeroom-of-odysseus]], [[provisions]] and [[two-handled-jar]]); he will take them in the evening, “For I will go to Sparta and to sandy Pylos” (2.359, as 2.214; see [[sparta]], [[pylos]] and [[sandy]]). The old nurse weeps: he is an only and beloved son, the suitors will plot his death as soon as he is gone, and he has no need to suffer evils on the barren sea (2.363–370; see [[eurycleia]] and [[barren-sea]]). He answers that the plan is not without a god (see [[not-without-a-god]]), and makes her swear to say nothing to his mother until the eleventh or twelfth day (2.372–378; see [[oath]]).

**The crew and the ship.** “Then the gray-eyed goddess Athena thought of another thing” (2.382). In the likeness of Telemachus she goes through the city, bids each man gather at the ship in the evening, and asks Noemon, the shining son of Phronius, for a swift ship (2.383–387; see [[noemon-son-of-phronius]]). At sunset she drags it down to the sea, puts in the tackle, moors it at the far edge of the harbor, and urges on the crew (2.388–392; see [[sunset-lines]], [[ships]] and [[harbor]]). Then, with the same verse, she thinks of another thing: she pours sleep on the suitors and calls Telemachus out, in the likeness of Mentor (2.393–404; see [[sleep]]).

**The departure.** He follows in her footprints to the shore, has the provisions brought and stowed, and goes on board; Athena sits in the stern and he beside her, the crew loose the stern-cables and sit at the benches (see [[rowing-benches]]), and the goddess sends a following wind, “a fresh West Wind, sounding over the wine-dark sea” (2.405–421; see [[zephyrus]] and [[wine-dark]]). They raise the mast in its mast-box and hoist the sails (see [[mast-box]]), the wave roars about the cutwater, and when the tackle is made fast they pour libations “to the immortal, everlasting gods, / and most of all to the gray-eyed daughter of Zeus” (2.422–433; see [[sailing-type-scene]], [[mast]] and [[libation]]). “All night long and through the dawn the ship cut her way” (2.434), and the third book opens with their landing at Pylos (3.1–5).
