**The bird-reader.** The eagles have darted away over the city, and the people are pondering in their hearts “what was to be fulfilled” (2.155–156; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagles]] and [[eagle]]), when an old man rises whom the poem has not named before:

{{quote:1351b37d-6205-5530-a6cf-e77c45fba003}}

He is old, as Aegyptius, who opened the assembly, is old (2.15–16; see [[aegyptius]]), and the narrator gives him a single credential, that no man of his age was his equal in reading birds and in speaking what is fated. ἐναίσιμα, “words of fate”, are things in accord with αἶσα, the share that is fated, and Eurymachus will answer with the same word, that not all birds are ἐναίσιμοι, “birds of fate” (2.182; see [[birds]], [[divination]] and the translation's key-word row for ἐναίσιμος). The verse that brings in his speech brings in Mentor's after him (2.228; see [[with-good-intent]] and [[mentor]]), and his first verse is the address of Aegyptius before him and of Mentor after him, “Hear me now, men of Ithaca, in what I say” (2.161, as 2.25 and 2.229; see [[ithacans]]).

**The reading.** He speaks to all the Ithacans, “and it is above all to the suitors that I declare these things” (2.162): Odysseus is not far off but already near, planting slaughter and doom for all of them, and there will be evil for many others of the Ithacans; let them consider how to stop the suitors, and let the suitors stop of themselves (2.163–169; see [[the-suitors]] and [[ker-doom]]). He does not say what the two eagles stand for, or why they tore at each other; he declares what the sign means, and vouches for his reading: “For I do not prophesy untried, but with sure knowledge” (2.170). He told Odysseus, when the Argives were embarking for Ilios, that he would come home in the twentieth year, after suffering many evils and losing all his companions, unknown to all, “and now it is all being fulfilled” (2.171–176; see [[the-prophecy-of-halitherses]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). ἀπείρητος, “untried”, is said of one who has not made the attempt, as the lion of the Iliad's simile “has no mind to be driven from the steading without a try” (*Iliad* 12.304). The poem bears him out: Odysseus comes home alone, his companions lost, in the twentieth year (12.417–419, 16.205–206), and the suitors die in his hall (Book 22).

**Eurymachus' answer.** The suitor answers him face to face (2.177; see [[eurymachus]]). Let the old man go home and prophesy to his own children, lest they suffer some evil hereafter; Eurymachus is “far better than you at prophesying” (2.178–180). Birds are many, and not all of them are birds of fate; Odysseus has perished far away, and Halitherses ought to have perished with him (2.181–184; see [[birds]]). Then the charges: he is setting Telemachus on in his anger, “waiting for a gift for your own house, in case he may give one” (2.185–186), and if he goads a younger man to anger, “on you, old man, we will lay a fine, which you will / chafe at heart to pay, and grievous will be your sorrow” (2.192–193). θωή, “fine”, occurs elsewhere in Homer only of the penalty that the Achaeans laid on a man who would not go to Troy (*Iliad* 13.669; see [[euchenor]]). Last, the suitors fear no one, “nor do we heed the oracle that you, old sir, / pronounce, never to be fulfilled” (2.201–202; see [[theopropion]]). ἀκράαντος, “unfulfilled”, comes twice more in Homer: of the task “for which we came here”, which lies unfinished (*Iliad* 2.138), and of the dreams that come through the gate of ivory, “bringing words unfulfilled” (19.565, a plain rendering). The seer who reads a sign against the powerful meets the same answer in the Iliad, where Agamemnon calls Calchas “Prophet of evils” (*Iliad* 1.106; see [[prophet-of-evils]] and [[calchas]]).

**With Mentor.** Halitherses does not answer. Telemachus asks for a ship, Mentor rebukes the silent people, and Leiocritus dissolves the assembly with a jeer: “and Mentor and Halitherses will speed this one on his way, / who are his father's companions from of old” (2.253–254); he thinks that Telemachus will sit in Ithaca a long time and “never accomplish this journey” (2.255–256; see [[leiocritus-son-of-euenor]]). The suitor names the two old men who have spoken for the house of Odysseus. In the event it is Athena, in the likeness of Mentor, who speeds the journey that same night (2.267–295, 2.399–434; see [[athena]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).
