summary: The swine of Book 2 are the fatted hogs of Odysseus' house, not wild boars: when Telemachus comes back from the shore, he finds the suitors “flaying goats and singeing fat hogs in the courtyard” (2.299–300).
# σιάλους (2.300), "fat hogs": σίαλος, the fatted pig, is a common word in the Odyssey (σιάλους 14.41, 14.81, 17.181,
# 20.163, 20.251; σιάλων 14.19; σιάλοισιν 10.390) and comes twice in the Iliad, in the genitive (9.208, 21.363).

**Fat hogs in the courtyard.** “and found the proud suitors in the halls, / flaying goats and singeing fat hogs in the courtyard” (2.299–300; see [[the-suitors]] and [[goats]]). σίαλος is the hog fattened for the table; it is called σῦς as well, σύας σιάλους (14.41, 14.81). The suitors singe the carcasses, εὕοντας, burning the bristles off over the fire, as the hogs at the feasts of the Iliad are “stretched to singe across the flame of Hephaestus” (εὑόμενοι, *Iliad* 9.467–468 = 23.32–33; see [[the-flight-of-phoenix]] and [[cremation]]).

**Where the hogs come from.** The pork is Odysseus' own. His swineherd Eumaeus keeps the sows in twelve sties within his courtyard and always sends the best of the fattened hogs to the suitors, so that the boars are far fewer than the sows (14.13–20, a plain rendering; see [[eumaeus]]); “the suitors eat the fat hogs”, he tells the beggar, while the slaves eat the young pigs (14.80–81). On the day of the killing he brings in three fat hogs, the best of all (20.162–163), and the suitors slaughter fat hogs with the sheep, the goats and a cow of the herd (20.250–251; see [[cattle]] and [[sheep]]).
