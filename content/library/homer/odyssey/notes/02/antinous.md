summary: Antinous alone dares to answer Telemachus in the assembly. The suitors are not to blame, he says, but Penelope, who has cheated them for three years with promises and with the web; let Telemachus send her back to her father to marry, for until she marries they will not leave. After the assembly he meets Telemachus with a laugh, takes his hand, and invites him to eat and drink with them as before.

**He alone answers.** When Telemachus has flung down the scepter, “all the others were silent, and no one dared / to answer Telemachus with harsh words; / but Antinous alone, answering him, said” (2.82–84; see [[answering-him]]). He begins with the words of his first answer, the night before, ὑψαγόρης, “lofty in speech” (2.85, as 1.385; see [[lofty-in-speech]]), and adds μένος ἄσχετε, “uncontrollable in your fury”: Telemachus would like “to fasten blame on us” (2.85–86; see the translation's table of key words).

**The blame on Penelope.** “But it is not the Achaean suitors who are to blame toward you: / it is your dear mother, who knows crafty ways beyond all others” (2.87–88). His proof is the three years of promises and the web, woven by day and unraveled by night, until one of her women betrayed her (2.89–110; see [[penelope]] and [[the-web-of-penelope]]).

**The suitors' answer.** “But to you the suitors give this answer” (2.111): let Telemachus send his mother away to marry the man her father bids and who pleases her (2.113–114; see [[icarius]]). If she goes on vexing them, pondering in her heart the gifts Athena gave her beyond the women of old, they will go on eating his substance; “Great fame she makes / for herself, but for you a longing for much substance” (2.115–126; see [[kleos]]). And they will not go home to their fields until she marries (2.127–128; see [[the-suitors]]). Telemachus answers him by name and refuses (2.130–145; see [[telemachus]] and [[mothers-and-sons]]).

**The invitation.** When Telemachus comes back from the shore, Antinous “with a laugh, came straight up to Telemachus, / and he clung to his hand”, greeted him again as “lofty in speech, uncontrollable in your fury”, and bade him “eat and drink with me, just as before”: the Achaeans would give him a ship and choice oarsmen for sacred Pylos (2.301–308). Telemachus refuses, “Antinous, there is no way for me among you overbearing men / to feast in silence and make merry at my ease” (2.310–311), and draws his hand from the hand of Antinous (2.321; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]).
