summary: Eurymachus answers Halitherses. Let the old man go home and prophesy to his children: birds are many, Odysseus is dead, and the seer will pay a fine if he stirs the young man up. Then he gives Telemachus, as his own advice, Athena's counsel of the day before, that his mother go back to her father's house; the suitors fear no one, heed no oracle, and woo her for her excellence.

**Against the seer.** He is brought on with the verse of his first answer, “Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, answered him face to face” (2.177 = 1.399; see [[polybus-father-of-eurymachus]]), and his first words are for the old man:

{{quote:0837c415-8ad6-5307-9b6e-557414c9055f}}

The birds are the eagles of Zeus that Halitherses has just read, and “Many are the birds” is a saying of its own (2.146–176, 2.181–182; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagles]], [[halitherses]], [[many-birds-not-all-of-fate]] and [[divination]]). The gift he imputes to the seer is one from Telemachus for his own house (2.186; see [[doron]]), and the fine he threatens, θωή (2.192), is the word of the Iliad's Euchenor, who sailed for Troy to avoid “the grievous fine of the Achaeans” (*Iliad* 13.669; see the translation's table of key words).

**Athena's counsel in his mouth.** “And to Telemachus I myself will give counsel before them all: / let him bid his mother go back to her father's house” (2.194–195): the next two verses, the wedding and the great bride-price, are those of Athena's counsel to Telemachus in the likeness of Mentes (2.196–197 = 1.277–278; see [[athena-visits-telemachus]], [[bride-price]] and [[icarius]]).

**We fear no one.** The suitors will not cease from their wooing, he says, “since in any case we fear no one, / not even Telemachus, for all his many words, / nor do we heed the oracle that you, old sir, / pronounce, never to be fulfilled” (2.199–202): the last are Telemachus' own words to him the night before, “nor do I heed any oracle” (1.415; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]). The house will be eaten up and there will never be amends while Penelope keeps them waiting, and they strive “for the sake of her excellence” (2.203–207; see [[penelope]] and [[arete]]). Telemachus answers him, “Eurymachus, and you others, all the noble suitors, / I will no longer beg you or speak to you about these things” (2.209–210; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).
