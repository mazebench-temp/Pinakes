---
summary: The storeroom of Odysseus is shut by closely fitted double doors that can be locked, and a housekeeper keeps it night and day (2.344–346).
# σανίδες, the leaves of a door (2.344; later 22.128, 23.42), and δικλίδες, "double" (2.345; later 17.268). σανίς is
# also a plank or a raised floor (σανίδος, 21.51), which this exact form does not catch.
grc: [σανίδες, δικλίδες]
en: [double doors]
---

“And closely fitted doors were set on it that could be locked, / double doors” (2.344–345; see [[the-storeroom-of-odysseus]] and [[closely-fitted]]). σανίδες are the leaves of the door, the boards of which it is made; κληιστός, “that could be locked”, is from κληΐς, the bolt or the key (see the entry); δικλίδες, “double”, are doors of two leaves, like the high double gates of the Achaean wall, which two crossing bars held from inside and a single bolt fastened (*Iliad* 12.453–456; see [[the-achaean-wall]]), and the doors of Odysseus' own courtyard, which he praises as “well fenced, double” when he comes home as a beggar (17.266–268; a plain rendering). W. W. Merry notes that the bars, ὀχῆες, belonged above all to folding doors, σανίδες (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.441). ἔπεσαν is from ἔπειμι, “were on it”, not from πίπτω (see the translation's note on 2.344). Behind these doors “a woman was in it as housekeeper night and day” (2.345; see [[eurycleia]]): in the twenty-first book Penelope opens the doors of a storeroom with a bronze key, and they roar like a bull (21.42–50).
