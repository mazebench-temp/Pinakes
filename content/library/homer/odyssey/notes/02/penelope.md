summary: Penelope does not appear in the second book, but the assembly is about her. Antinous lays the blame on her crafty ways and tells of her web, and praises the gifts Athena gave her beyond the famous women of old; the suitors would have her sent back to her father, her son will not thrust her out, and Eurymachus says they woo her for her excellence. At night her son sails without her knowledge.

**Spoken of.** She is upstairs, out of sight, through the whole book (2.357–358; see [[upper-room]]), and the men in the assembly speak of her. Her son begins with the suitors who “have fallen upon my mother against her will”, the sons of the best men, who shudder at going to her father's house, where he would settle the bride-price and give her to the man he wished (2.50–54; see [[the-suitors]] and [[icarius]]).

**Crafty ways.** Antinous answers that the suitors are not to blame, “it is your dear mother, who knows crafty ways beyond all others” (2.88; see [[antinous]]). κέρδεα, “crafty ways”, are what Odysseus knows when he rises to wrestle in the Iliad (*Iliad* 23.709; see [[kerdos]]). For three years she has given hope to all and promises to each, “but her mind has other things in view” (2.91–92; see [[noos]]), and the web was “this further trick” (2.93–110; see [[the-web-of-penelope]] and [[dolos]]).

**Beyond the women of old.** His blame turns into praise:

{{quote:4eeb830f-8d8a-5299-8d65-e23f7778429a}}

Her gifts are Athena's, skill in handiwork, good wits and crafty ways (see [[athena]] and [[weaving]]), and the women of old are heroines of the past, “the Achaean women with lovely tresses”, Tyro, Alcmene and Mycene (2.119–120; see [[achaean-women]], [[tyro]], [[alcmene]] and [[mycene]]). Her present purpose, the gods' gift too, makes “Great fame” for herself and for her son a longing for his lost substance (2.123–126; see [[kleos]]). Eurymachus says the same in other words: the suitors wait every day and “strive for the sake of her excellence” (2.205–207). ἀρετή, the Iliad's word for a warrior's prowess, is here a woman's excellence (see [[arete]] and the translation's table of key words).

**Mother and wife.** The suitors' demand is that she be sent back to marry the man her father chooses (2.113–114, 2.195–197; see [[marriage]] and [[bride-price]]). Her son refuses: he will not thrust out “the woman who bore me, who raised me”, for fear of her father, of a god, and of her Erinyes (2.130–137; see [[mothers-and-sons]]), and if his father is dead he will give her to a husband himself (2.223). Leiocritus grants her a wife's longing: if Odysseus came home, “his wife, much though she longs for him, would have no joy / of his coming” (2.249–250). Athena, as Mentor, names her with her husband: “if you are not his offspring and Penelope's” (2.274).

**Kept in the dark.** Telemachus will take the provisions in the evening, when his mother has gone up to her room, and makes Eurycleia swear not to tell her until the eleventh or twelfth day, “so that she may not mar her fair skin with weeping” (2.357–358, 2.373–376; see [[eurycleia]]). On the shore he tells the crew, “My mother has learned nothing of it” (2.411).
