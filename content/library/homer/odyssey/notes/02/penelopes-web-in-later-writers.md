summary: The figure comes from Antinous' speech to the assembly, where the web is the suitors' grievance, a trick that cheated them for three years and ended when a woman betrayed her and she finished it against her will. The later writers keep the endless undoing and leave out the end.
refs: [2.104-105, 2.109-110]

**The suitors' story.** Antinous tells of the web to answer Telemachus. It is not the suitors who are to blame, he says, “it is your dear mother, who knows crafty ways beyond all others” (2.88; see [[antinous]] and [[kerdos]]). It is the third year, and soon the fourth, since she began “cheating the heart in the breasts of the Achaeans”, giving hope to all and making promises to each, “but her mind has other things in view” (2.89–92). Then comes the web: “And she pondered in her mind this further trick” (2.93), the great loom, the shroud for Laertes that must be finished before she marries, the weaving by day and the unraveling by torchlight (2.94–105; see [[the-web-of-penelope]] and [[laertes]]). “So for three years she escaped notice by her trick and persuaded the Achaeans” (2.106), until in the fourth year one of her women told, “and we caught her unraveling the splendid web” (2.108–109).

**What the later writers keep.** For Antinous the web is a δόλος, a “trick” (2.93, 2.106), and its point is the delay it won. Plato, Cicero and Proclus keep only the undoing, and make of it a figure: the endless work that the philosopher's soul refuses, the art that destroys its own premises, the body forever rebuilt (see the entry above). Propertius alone keeps Antinous' word, *dolo*. None of them keeps the end of the story, which in this book is the suitors' victory: “So she finished it, against her will, under compulsion” (2.110). Plato's “endless work” is the web as Penelope meant it to be, not as it was.

**Athena's gift.** In the same speech Antinous says that it is Athena who has given her, beyond all others, “to be skilled in beautiful handiwork, and good wits, / and crafty ways” (2.116–118; see [[athena]]). Propertius' “false Minerva”, the goddess's art of weaving used to deceive, names the same gift from the other side.
