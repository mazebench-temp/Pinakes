summary: Penelope's trick is a work of weaving: she weaves a shroud for Laertes by day and unravels it by night for three years. Antinous grants that Athena gave her, beyond all others, the skill “to be skilled in beautiful handiwork, and good wits”, in the verse that the Odyssey gives to the weavers of Phaeacia (2.93–110, 2.116–118).

**Weaving and unweaving.** Penelope “began to weave” on the great loom (ὕφαινε, 2.94), and asked the suitors to wait until she had finished the mantle, “so that my threads may not be wasted and perish” (2.98; see [[loom]] and [[shroud]]): νήματα are the spun threads, from νέω, “spin”. Then “by day she would weave the great web, / but by night she would unravel it” (2.104–105), ἀλλύω, the shortened form of ἀναλύω, “undo”, until “we caught her unraveling the splendid web” (2.109; see [[the-web-of-penelope]] and [[torches]]).

**Athena's gift.** Antinous admits what Penelope has from the goddess, “to be skilled in beautiful handiwork, and good wits, / and crafty ways” (2.117–118; see [[athena]], [[kerdos]] and [[penelope]]). The first verse is said of the women of Phaeacia, as skilled at the loom as their men are at sailing, “for Athena has given them beyond all others / to be skilled in beautiful handiwork, and good wits” (7.110–111; a plain rendering; see [[phaeacians]]). ἔργα, “handiwork”, is the women's work of the house, as in Agamemnon's praise of Chryseis (*Iliad* 1.115), and Penelope's handiwork is also her trick: “she escaped notice by her trick” (2.106; see [[dolos]]).
