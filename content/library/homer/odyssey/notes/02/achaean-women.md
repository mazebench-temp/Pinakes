---
summary: In Antinous' account Penelope wove the shroud for Laertes “lest one of the Achaean women in the land be indignant with me” (2.101), and he sets her above the Achaean women of old, Tyro, Alcmene and Mycene, in craft (2.118–122).
---

**The blame of the women.** Antinous gives Penelope's reason for the web in her own words, as she spoke them to the suitors: the mantle she weaves is a shroud for the hero Laertes, “lest one of the Achaean women in the land be indignant with me, / if he were to lie without a winding-sheet, who won great possessions” (2.99–102; see [[the-web-of-penelope]] and [[laertes]]). The women κατὰ δῆμον, “in the land”, would hold it against her, νεμεσήσῃ, if a man who had won so much were to lie in death without a winding-sheet (see [[nemesis]]). The same verses return when Penelope tells the story to the stranger and when the shade of Amphimedon tells it in the underworld (19.146–147, 24.136–137).

The Achaean women here are the heroines of the past, and their epithet, ἐυπλοκαμῖδες, “with lovely tresses”, is the one that Penelope gives the women who gather round her in her dream (19.542; see [[with-lovely-tresses]]). Tyro and Alcmene are among the heroines whom Odysseus sees in the underworld (11.235, 11.266; see [[tyro]], [[alcmene]] and [[mycene]]).
