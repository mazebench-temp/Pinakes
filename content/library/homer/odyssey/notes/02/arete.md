---
summary: Eurymachus says that the suitors “strive for the sake of her excellence, and do not go after other women” (2.206): Penelope's ἀρετή, the first use of the word in the Odyssey.
grc: [ἀρετῆς]
en: [excellence]
---

The translation renders the word “excellence” for Penelope and keeps “prowess” for a fighter's (see the translation's table). Eurymachus makes it the prize of a contest. The suitors wait “every day” and ἐριδαίνομεν, “strive”, the verb of rivalry (2.205–206; see [[eris]] and [[the-suitors]]), and they will not leave until she marries (2.198–207).

The poem comes back to it. Penelope tells Eurymachus, and then the stranger, that the immortals destroyed her excellence, her looks and her figure when the Argives embarked for Ilios and her husband went with them (18.251–253, 19.124–126), with the words in which Halitherses remembers that day (ὅτε Ἴλιον εἰσανέβαινον / Ἀργεῖοι, 2.172–173). At the end the shade of Agamemnon, hearing how she kept faith, says that the fame of her excellence will never perish, and that the gods will make a song for prudent Penelope (24.196–198; see [[kleos]] and [[penelope]]).
