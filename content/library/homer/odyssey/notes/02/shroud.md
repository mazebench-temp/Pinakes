summary: The web that Penelope weaves by day and unravels by night is a shroud for Laertes, who is still alive, made against the day of his death; when the suitors catch her unraveling it, she finishes it against her will (2.96–110).
# Unnamed: the mantle that is to be the shroud (φᾶρος, 2.97), and "So she finished it" (2.110).
refs: [2.97, 2.110]
---

{{quote:46eb195a-bc35-5c3e-8a2b-079a03e4728b}}

The shroud is made for a living man. Laertes is old and far from the house (1.189–193; see [[laertes]]), and the cloth is to be ready “against the time when / the deadly fate of death that lays men low takes him down” (2.99–100; see [[that-lays-men-low]]). Penelope's reason is the honor of the dead: a man who “won great possessions” must not lie without a winding-sheet, or the Achaean women of the land would blame her (2.101–102; see [[achaean-women]] and [[nemesis]]). Antinous says that the suitors believed her (2.103), and the work went on for three years, until one of her women told them and they caught her unraveling it: “So she finished it, against her will, under compulsion” (2.110; see [[the-web-of-penelope]], [[loom]] and [[weaving]]). The cloth is called φᾶρος, “this mantle” (2.97; see [[cloak]]), ταφήιον, “a shroud” (2.99), and, as what the dead man would lack, σπεῖρον, “a winding-sheet” (2.102).
