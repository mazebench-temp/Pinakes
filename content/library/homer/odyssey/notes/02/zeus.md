summary: In the assembly Aegyptius wishes that Zeus may bring good to the man who called it, and Telemachus takes the wish as an omen; Telemachus begs the people by Olympian Zeus and Themis and leaves the requital to Zeus, and far-seeing Zeus at once sends two eagles. Telemachus hopes for a rumor from Zeus of his father, and the narrator twice names Athena the daughter of Zeus.
passages:
  - 2.143-147 | Telemachus leaves the requital to Zeus, and Zeus sends the eagles
---

**A word of omen.** Aegyptius opens the assembly without knowing who has called it, and ends with a blessing on him: “He seems to me a good man, may he prosper! For him himself / may Zeus bring to pass some good, whatever he has in his mind” (2.33–34; see [[aegyptius]]). “So he spoke, and the dear son of Odysseus rejoiced at the word of omen” (2.35). Merry explains that the force of a φήμη lies in its being a chance utterance whose full bearing the speaker does not know: Aegyptius does not know that he is blessing Telemachus, who owns himself the summoner of the assembly only at 2.40–41 (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 2.35; see [[divination]]). He compares κλεηδών, the word at which Odysseus rejoices when the suitors wish that Zeus and the other gods may give him what he most desires (18.112–117), and again when he has asked Zeus for a word of omen from within the house and a sign from without, and a woman grinding at the mill prays that this may be the suitors' last feast; the same word is φήμη there at 20.105 and κλεηδών at 20.120 (20.98–121; Merry on 2.35).

**By Olympian Zeus.** Telemachus begs the people “by Olympian Zeus and by Themis” (2.68; see [[themis-the-goddess]] and [[olympian]]), naming Zeus by the title of the halls in which the gods sat in council in Book 1 (Ζηνὸς ἐνὶ μεγάροισιν Ὀλυμπίου, 1.27).

**Requital and the eagles.** He ends his second speech with the verses he spoke to the suitors the day before: “then eat it up; but I will call upon the gods who live forever, / if ever Zeus will grant that deeds of requital be done; / then, unavenged, you would perish inside my house” (2.143–145 = 1.378–380; see [[poine]] and [[gods-who-live-forever]]). This time the answer comes at once: “So Telemachus spoke, and for him far-seeing Zeus sent two eagles / flying from on high, from the peak of a mountain” (2.146–147; see [[far-seeing]], [[eagle]] and [[the-omen-of-the-eagles]]). In the Iliad Zeus answers a prayer with an eagle, for Agamemnon when the Achaeans are hard pressed and for Priam as he sets out for Achilles' hut (*Iliad* 8.245–252, 24.314–321). Here no prayer has been made, only the appeal; Halitherses reads the eagles as the return of Odysseus and the suitors' doom, and Eurymachus mocks the reading (2.157–207; see [[halitherses]], [[the-prophecy-of-halitherses]] and [[divination]]).

**A rumor from Zeus.** Telemachus will go to Sparta and Pylos to learn of his father's homecoming, “in the hope that some mortal may tell me, or I may hear a rumor / from Zeus, which most of all brings report to men” (2.216–217), the words with which Athena sent him (1.282–283; see [[kleos]] and the Book 1 note).

**Daughter of Zeus.** The narrator names Athena “daughter of Zeus” as she leaves Telemachus on the shore (Ἀθηναίη κούρη Διός, 2.296), and the crew's libations go “most of all to the gray-eyed daughter of Zeus” (2.433; see [[athena]] and [[daughter-of-zeus]]). Telemachus and Eurycleia both call Odysseus “sprung from Zeus” (διογενής, 2.352, 2.366; see [[sprung-from-zeus]]).
