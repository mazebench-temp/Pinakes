**A mother's curse.** Antinous has told Telemachus to send his mother away and bid her marry the man her father chooses (2.113–114; see [[antinous]] and [[penelope]]). He answers that he cannot thrust out of the house “the woman who bore me, who raised me” (2.130–131; see [[mothers-and-sons]]), and gives his reasons:

{{quote:37534d58-9a36-50b0-8fd9-00e1b2aab129}}

He fears three things: evil from her father, Icarius, to whom he would have to pay back much (2.132–134; see [[icarius]]); other evils that a god will give (ἄλλα δὲ δαίμων / δώσει, 2.134–135; see [[daimon]]); and blame from men (2.136–137; see [[nemesis]]). The Erinyes are the reason for the second. ἐπεί joins them to the god's evils: the curse a mother lays on her son is carried out by them, as the gods fulfilled the curses of Phoenix's father, who “called on the hateful Erinyes” (*Iliad* 9.454–457; see [[the-flight-of-phoenix]] and [[curses]]). στυγεράς, “hateful”, is their epithet there too (see [[hateful]]). ἀρήσεται, “will call down”, is the future of ἀράομαι, the verb of prayer, here of a curse (see [[prayer]]). The curse is only feared: she would call them down “as she leaves the house” (2.136).

**The Erinyes of a mother.** A mother's Erinyes are known to both poems. Athena tells Ares, whom she has struck down, that he is paying for the Erinyes of his mother, who is angry that he deserted the Achaeans (*Iliad* 21.412–414); Meleager's mother curses him, and the Erinys hears her (*Iliad* 9.566–572; see [[the-death-of-meleager]]); and when Oedipus' mother has hanged herself, she leaves him the many sorrows that a mother's Erinyes bring to pass (11.277–280; see [[oedipus]]). Murray prints the word here without a capital, ἐρινῦς, as at 20.78; the translation gives the name.
