summary: The assembly is about the suitors and they answer for themselves. Telemachus tells the people how they devour his house; Antinous answers for them that the fault is Penelope's and that they will not leave until she marries; Halitherses and Athena foretell their doom, Eurymachus says they fear no one, Mentor blames the people for letting a few men waste the king's house, and Leiocritus, one of them, dissolves the assembly. Back in the house they jeer at Telemachus, and at night Athena pours sleep on them.

**As Telemachus tells it.** The second of his two evils is the suitors:

{{quote:f22bd48a-a8d3-5193-8936-4586db32789c}}

Their feasting is μαψιδίως, “wantonly”, “and much of it is wasted” (2.58; see the translation's table of key words and [[possessions]]). They are “the dear sons of the men who are the best here”, and their fathers sit in the assembly that hears him (2.51; see [[aegyptius]] and [[eurynomus]]).

**Their answer.** Antinous speaks for them: the fault is not theirs but Penelope's (2.87–110; see [[antinous]] and [[the-web-of-penelope]]), and “to you the suitors give this answer”: let her marry the man her father bids, for “we will not go to our fields or anywhere else / until she marries whichever of the Achaeans she wishes” (2.111–128; see [[penelope]]). Eurymachus adds that they fear no one, will not cease from their “grievous wooing” (μνηστύς, 2.199), and wait every day for her excellence, not going after other women whom each of them might fitly marry (2.198–207; see [[eurymachus]]).

**Their doom foretold.** Halitherses declares his reading “above all to the suitors”: “For a great bane is rolling upon them” (2.162–163; see [[halitherses]] and [[the-prophecy-of-halitherses]]). Athena, in the likeness of Mentor, calls them “senseless men, since they are in no way discerning or just; / nor do they know anything of death and black doom, / which is near them now, that they should all perish in a single day” (2.282–284; see [[black-doom]] and [[ker-doom]]). Telemachus has already told them that they would perish “unavenged” in his house (2.145; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]).

**Few and many.** Mentor does not begrudge the suitors their violence, “for they stake their own heads” (2.235–238); he blames the people who let them be, “few as they are, though you are many” (2.241; see [[mentor]]). Leiocritus, a suitor, answers that the people would have to fight “with men who are more numerous”, and dissolves the assembly (2.244–257; see [[leiocritus-son-of-euenor]]). The suitors then go to the house of divine Odysseus (2.259).

**Their epithets.** The book calls them μνηστῆρες ἀγαυοί, “the noble suitors” (2.209, 2.247; see [[noble]]), and μνηστῆρες ἀγήνορες, “the proud suitors” (2.235, 2.299; see [[proud]]); they are ὑπερηνορέοντες, “overweening” (2.266, 2.324, 2.331; see [[overweening]]), and ὑπερφίαλοι, “overbearing” (2.310; see [[overbearing]]).

**The feast and the sleep.** Telemachus finds them flaying goats and singeing hogs in the courtyard, and they jeer at his journey (2.299–336; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]). Eurycleia fears that once he is gone they will plot his death by guile and divide his goods (2.367–368; see [[eurycleia]]). At night Athena pours sweet sleep on them, “and confused them as they drank, and made the cups fall from their hands”, and they go off to sleep through the city (2.395–398; see [[athena]], [[sleep]] and [[hypnos]]).
