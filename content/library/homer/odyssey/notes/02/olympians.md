summary: No god speaks among the gods in Book 2. The gods as a body are what the Ithacans appeal to and swear by. Telemachus bids the people fear their wrath and calls upon “the gods who live forever”; the gods know what the suitors do; Eurycleia swears a great oath by the gods; and at sea the crew pour libations to “the immortal, everlasting gods”, and to Athena above all.

**In men's mouths.** After the council of Book 1 the gods do not meet again until Book 5 (5.3–4; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]), and in Book 2 they are what men speak of. Telemachus bids the people “fear the wrath of the gods, / lest, indignant at your evil deeds, they turn upon you” (2.66–67; see [[menis]] and [[nemesis]]). Antinous says that the gods have put her purpose into Penelope's breast (2.124–125; see [[penelope]]). Telemachus answers that he will call upon “the gods who live forever”, if Zeus will grant requital (2.143–144; see [[gods-who-live-forever]] and [[zeus]]), and later tells the suitors that “the gods know them already, and all the Achaeans” (2.211).

**Oath and libation.** Eurycleia “swore a great oath by the gods not to tell” (2.377; see [[oath]] and [[eurycleia]]). At sea the crew pour libations “to the immortal, everlasting gods, / and most of all to the gray-eyed daughter of Zeus” (2.432–433; see [[libation]] and [[immortals]]). θεοῖς αἰειγενέτῃσιν, “the everlasting gods”, ends the verse as when the Achaeans and the Trojans pour wine and pray at the oath of the truce (*Iliad* 3.295–296).
