---
summary: Horace twice makes Penelope's suitors an example, in the Epistles as idlers born to consume the fruits of the earth and in the Satires as men too mean to give gifts and keener on the kitchen than on love; both portraits fit the feasters whom Telemachus accuses before the assembly.
---

- “The suitors of Penelope, good-for-nothings”, born to consume the fruits of the earth (*Epistles* 1.2.27–31, on the feasting of which Telemachus complains to the assembly, 2.55–58): [[horace-on-the-suitors]] and [[the-suitors]].
- Tiresias explains Penelope's honesty by the suitors' meanness: the young men came sparing of great gifts, and keener on the kitchen than on love (*Satires* 2.5.75–83, on 2.52–53 and 2.195–197, the bride-price, the only form in which the suitors' gifts appear in this book): [[horace-on-the-suitors]] and [[bride-price]].
