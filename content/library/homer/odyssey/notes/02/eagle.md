---
summary: Zeus answers Telemachus' call for requital with two eagles from a mountain peak. They fly with the wind to the middle of the assembly, wheel, look down at the heads of all, tear each other's cheeks and necks with their talons, and dart away on the right across the city; Halitherses reads them as the sign that Odysseus is near (2.146–176).

# The two eagles in the duals and plurals of the flight (2.147-154), and as ὄρνιθας, "the birds" (2.155).

passages:
  - 2.146-156 | The two eagles
---

**A pair.** αἰετώ, “two eagles” (2.146), is a dual, and the duals run through the flight, τώ, πλησίω, τιταινομένω (2.148–149), ἱκέσθην, ἐπιδινηθέντε, τιναξάσθην, ἰδέτην, δρυψαμένω, δεξιώ (2.150–154), with three plural verbs among them, ἐπέτοντο, ὄσσοντο and ἤιξαν. Everywhere else in Homer the eagle of omen comes alone (*Iliad* 8.247, 12.201, 13.822, 24.315; 15.161, 19.538, 20.243), and most of the others carry prey in their talons, a fawn, a serpent, a goose, a dove (*Iliad* 8.248, 12.202; 15.161, 20.243). These two carry nothing, and turn their talons on each other (see [[the-omen-of-the-eagles]]).

**Sent by Zeus.** “far-seeing Zeus sent two eagles / flying from on high, from the peak of a mountain” (2.146–147; see [[zeus]] and [[far-seeing]]). προέηκε, “sent”, answers the ἧκε of the Iliad's verse, “At once he sent an eagle, surest in omen of winged birds” (*Iliad* 8.247, 24.315; see [[surest-of-birds]]), and the eagle's home is the mountain: Helen's eagle comes “from the mountain, where its birth and offspring are”, and the eagle of Penelope's dream comes from the mountain to kill her geese (15.175, 19.538, plain renderings). These come “for him”, τῷ (2.146), as soon as Telemachus has called on the gods for deeds of requital (2.143–145).

**The flight.** “For a while they flew along in the blasts of the wind, / close beside one another, with wings at full stretch” (2.148–149). μετὰ πνοιῇς ἀνέμοιο is the phrase of the racehorses' manes, which “streamed in the blasts of the wind” (*Iliad* 23.367), and the eagle of the portent at the ditch flew off screaming “on the blasts of the wind” (*Iliad* 12.207). Over the middle of the assembly they wheel and shake “their thick wings” (2.150–151). πτερὰ πυκνά are the wings of the birds that eat raw flesh, “beating their thick wings around” a dead man in Odysseus' taunt over Socus (*Iliad* 11.453–454), and of the dove that Meriones shot in the archery, whose “thick feathers drooped” (*Iliad* 23.879). πολύφημος, “of many voices”, said of the assembly, is the word that is also the Cyclops' name (2.150; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]).

**The gaze and the talons.** They “looked down at the heads of all, and foresaw destruction” (2.152), and then, “tearing each other's cheeks and necks all round with their talons” (2.153), they leave. ὀνύχεσσι, “with their talons”, is the eagle's grip in the omens of both poems (*Iliad* 8.248, 12.202; 15.161). Here the talons are turned on the birds themselves, and the cheeks and necks they tear are the places that mourners tear: Briseis, over the body of Patroclus, “tore / her breasts and her tender neck and her beautiful face” (*Iliad* 19.284–285; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagles]] and [[lament]]).

**On the right.** “they darted on the right across the houses and the city of the people” (2.154). The right is the side of good omen (see [[divination]]). Walter Leaf, defending the reading διὰ ἄστεος, “across the city”, for the eagle that answers Priam, which most manuscripts give as ὑπὲρ ἄστεος, sets this verse beside it (*Iliad* 24.320; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.320; see [[wings-as-wide-as-a-door]]).

**The birds.** As soon as they are gone the eagles are ὄρνιθας, “the birds” at which the people marvel (2.155), and the reading of birds is the art of Halitherses, who reads them (2.157–176; see [[birds]], [[halitherses]] and [[the-prophecy-of-halitherses]]).
