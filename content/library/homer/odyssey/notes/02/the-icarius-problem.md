summary: This book gives the problem its premises. Every speaker in the assembly takes Penelope's father to be alive and within reach, someone the suitors should go to, Telemachus must fear, and Penelope could be sent back to; and in the same assembly Telemachus asks for a ship to go to Sparta.
refs: [2.52-54, 2.113-114, 2.132-134, 2.195-197]
---

**A father within reach.** The suitors, Telemachus complains, “shudder at going to the house of her father, / Icarius, so that he himself might settle the bride-price for his daughter / and give her to whom he wished” (2.52–54). Antinous answers that Telemachus should send his mother away and order her to marry “whichever man her father bids” (2.113–114). Telemachus will not: “it would be hard for me to pay back much / to Icarius, if of my own will I send my mother away. / For I shall suffer evil from her father” (2.132–134). Eurymachus repeats the demand, “let him bid his mother go back to her father's house” (2.195; see [[icarius]] and [[the-assembly-of-the-ithacans]]). None of them says where the house is. These are the verses from which Strabo argued that Icarius and his sons lived not in Lacedaemon but in Acarnania (*Geography* 10.2.24, quoting 2.52–53; see the Book 1 note and [[strabo]]).

**The journey to Sparta.** In the same assembly Telemachus asks for a ship to go “to Sparta and to sandy Pylos” (2.212–214), and he says it again to Eurycleia (2.359; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]] and [[sparta]]). The critics whom Aristotle answers asked why, if Icarius was a Laconian, Telemachus never met him when he came to Lacedaemon (*Poetics* 25, 1461b4–9; see the entry above). This book sets out both halves of their question: a grandfather close enough to take his daughter back, and a grandson about to sail for Sparta.
