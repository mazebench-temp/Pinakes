Telemachus comes from the shore to find the suitors preparing their feast; Antinous laughs, takes his hand and invites him to eat and drink as before, with a ship promised for Pylos; Telemachus refuses, vows to let loose the fates upon them and draws his hand away, and the young men jeer at his journey.
# The scene in the house (2.298-336).
refs: [2.298-336]
passages:
  - 2.298-321 | Antinous' invitation and Telemachus' answer
  - 2.322-336 | The suitors' jeers

**The invitation.** Telemachus comes back from the shore “grieving at heart”, and finds the proud suitors “flaying goats and singeing fat hogs in the courtyard” (2.298–300; see [[the-suitors]]). “And Antinous, with a laugh, came straight up to Telemachus, / and he clung to his hand, and spoke to him, and called him by name” (2.301–302; see [[antinous]] and [[called-him-by-name]]). He greets him with the words he used in the assembly, “Telemachus, lofty in speech, uncontrollable in your fury” (2.303, as 2.85), bids him think of no evil “either deed or word, / but eat and drink with me, just as before”, and promises that the Achaeans will give him a ship and choice oarsmen to take him to sacred Pylos (2.303–308; see [[pylos]] and [[egatheos]]).

**The refusal.** On the first evening Telemachus had bidden the suitors feast in peace before the assembly (1.369–371); now he will not feast with them, “in silence” and “at my ease” (2.310–311). His answer measures the change Athena wrought in him (1.296–297; see [[nepios]] and [[athena-visits-telemachus]]):

He will go, he adds, ἔμπορος, “a passenger”, on another man's ship, since he has none of his own and no oarsmen, “that, I suppose, is what seemed to you the better way” (2.318–320; see the translation's table of key words and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). Then “He spoke, and drew his hand from the hand of Antinous / easily” (2.321–322).

**The jeers.** The suitors go on with their feast, and “they insulted him and taunted him with words” (2.322–323). The narrator gives two of their jests, each brought in with an iterative, “and this is what one of the overweening young men would say”, “And another of the overweening young men would say in turn” (2.324, 2.331; see [[overweening]]). The first: “Surely Telemachus is pondering slaughter for us”, and will bring helpers from sandy Pylos or from Sparta, or drugs that destroy life from Ephyra, “that rich plowland”, to put in the mixing bowl and destroy them all (2.325–330; see [[odysseus-seeks-poison]], [[ephyra]] and [[mixing-bowl]]). The second: he may perish far from his friends, wandering like Odysseus, and then the suitors would divide his possessions and give the house to his mother and whoever married her (2.332–336; see [[penelope]]). Their first jest names what the poem will bring about, slaughter for the suitors in the house (see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).
