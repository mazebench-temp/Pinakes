---
summary: Aegyptius, Halitherses and Mentor each begin “Hear me now, men of Ithaca, in what I say” in the first assembly held since Odysseus sailed (2.25, 2.161, 2.229). The people pity Telemachus but sit silent, and Mentor rebukes them for letting the few suitors have their way (2.81–83, 2.239–241).
---

**Men of Ithaca.** Telemachus has the heralds summon “the long-haired Achaeans to the assembly” (2.7; see [[achaeans]] and [[heralds]]). When they are gathered, the old Aegyptius opens it with the address that the speakers after him use when they turn to the whole people (see [[hear-me-now-men-of-ithaca]]):

{{quote:7db0b16b-77e6-5e1b-9655-da1eb8cd809d}}

Halitherses speaks the same verse before he reads the eagles (2.161), and Mentor before his rebuke (2.229); in the last book Halitherses speaks it once more to the Ithacans gathered after the killing of the suitors (24.454). Telemachus does not use it. He answers Aegyptius, “Old man” (2.40), and turns to the people only as his speech goes on: “Be indignant yourselves” (2.64; see [[nemesis]]). Leiocritus, who closes the assembly, calls them only “people”: “But come, people, scatter, each to his work” (2.252).

**The silent people.** When Telemachus flings down the scepter, “pity seized all the people”, and “all the others were silent, and no one dared / to answer Telemachus with harsh words” (2.81–83); only Antinous answers him. Mentor turns his anger on them, not on the suitors:

{{quote:d46b997b-cbb6-53bb-8d24-2a3c714296d2}}

Leiocritus answers that even Odysseus could not fight so many over a feast (2.244–251), and the people scatter, “each to his own house” (2.258; see [[laos]]). Their silence is remembered at the end: in the last assembly Halitherses tells them that the deaths came of their own baseness, since they would not listen to him or to Mentor (24.455–462).

**The crew.** The companions of the voyage are Ithacans as well. Athena, in the likeness of Mentor, promises to “go through the land and quickly / gather companions who will go willingly” (2.291–292), and in the likeness of Telemachus she goes everywhere through the city and speaks to each man (2.383–385). In Book 4 Noemon tells the suitors that the young men who went with Telemachus are the best in the land after themselves (4.652; see [[noemon-son-of-phronius]]).
