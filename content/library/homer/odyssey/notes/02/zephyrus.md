**The fair wind.** When the crew have loosed the stern-cables and sat down at the rowing benches (2.418–419), the goddess sends the wind:

{{quote:8721ef08-abba-5b83-9ee7-f76e7b8ec051}}

The first verse is the Iliad's for the wind that Apollo sends the ship coming home from Chryse, with gray-eyed Athena in place of far-working Apollo (*Iliad* 1.479; see [[athena]] and [[the-fair-wind]]), and it returns when she sends Telemachus home from Pylos, where the wind is not named (15.292). What follows is the Iliad's voyage too: the wind blows full into the sail, the wave roars at the cutwater, and the ship runs on, accomplishing her way (2.427–429, nearly *Iliad* 1.481–483; see [[sailing-type-scene]] and [[cutwater]]).

**Fresh and sounding.** ἀκραής, “fresh”, is said in Homer only of winds that carry a ship: here, and of the North Wind before which the ships of Odysseus' Cretan tale run (14.253, 14.299; see [[boreas]]). κελάδων, “sounding”, is the Iliad's word for a river (*Iliad* 18.576, 21.16; see the translation's key-word row). The West Wind of Homer is usually stormy (see the article above); here, sent by the goddess, it carries the ship through the night (2.434; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).
