summary: Dawn opens the poem's second day, the day of the assembly that Telemachus had called for “at dawn”, in the verse that gave the Iliad its first and its last dawns. At the end of the book the ship cuts her way all night long and through the dawn.
# Unnamed: ἠῶ "through the dawn" (2.434), the time, as the Iliad's notes count ἠώς.
refs: [2.434]

**The second day.** The first day of the poem ended at nightfall, with Telemachus lying awake all night, planning the journey Athena had shown him (1.423–444; see [[telemachus]]). He had told the suitors: “And at dawn let us all go and sit in the assembly” (1.372). Book 2 opens with that dawn: ἦμος δʼ ἠριγένεια φάνη ῥοδοδάκτυλος Ἠώς, “And when early-born Dawn appeared, the rosy-fingered” (2.1; see [[dawn-line]], [[early-born]] and [[rosy-fingered]]). It is the Odyssey's first dawn, in a verse found twenty times in the poem and twice in the Iliad, where it brings the first dawn, at the return of Chryseis, and the last, at the funeral of Hector (*Iliad* 1.477, 24.788). Merry sees in ῥοδοδάκτυλος, if it is more than part of the goddess' beauty, the spreading rays of rosy light before sunrise, like the fingers of an open hand, and quotes Eustathius, who took Dawn's fingers for the rays of the sun (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 2.1; see the translation's note on 2.1).

**Through the dawn.** The book ends at sea: “All night long and through the dawn the ship cut her way” (2.434). ἠῶ is the time over which she sails, or the dawn at which she arrives (see the translation's note on 2.434). The next book opens with the sun rising and the ship coming to Pylos (3.1–5; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).
