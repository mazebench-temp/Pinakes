summary: The first assembly of Ithaca since Odysseus sailed is told in the order of the pattern: the heralds summon the men (2.6–8), they gather (2.9), the speakers rise with the scepter and are brought in with the Iliad's verses of the assembly (2.15–241), and a suitor dissolves it with Achilles' verse (2.252–259).

**Summons and gathering.** Telemachus has the clear-voiced heralds summon the long-haired Achaeans in the three verses of Agamemnon's summons in *Iliad* 2 (2.6–8; *Iliad* 2.50–52; [[clear-voiced]], [[long-haired]]), and the gathering is told in the whole line of the Iliad, “And when they had gathered and were all assembled” (2.9; [[when-they-had-gathered]]). He comes with his spear and two dogs, with Athena's grace upon him, and sits in his father's seat while the elders make way (2.10–14; [[swift-dogs]], [[wondrous]]).

**Speakers.** The old hero Aegyptius speaks first (2.15–34; [[aegyptius]]). The man who called the assembly stands in the middle of it, and the herald puts the scepter in his hand (2.36–38; [[wise]]); at the end of his speech he flings it to the ground (2.80). The others are brought in with the frames of the Iliad's assembly: Antinous “answering him” (2.84; [[answering-him]]), Eurymachus and Leiocritus with their fathers' names (2.177, 2.242), Halitherses and Mentor with the verse of good intent (2.160, 2.228; [[with-good-intent]]), and Mentor with the verse of sitting down and standing up (2.224; [[sat-down-stood-up]]). Three speeches open with the Ithacan address, “Hear me now, men of Ithaca, in what I say” (2.25, 2.161, 2.229; [[hear-me-now-men-of-ithaca]]).

**Dissolution.** Leiocritus sends the people away, “But come, people, scatter, each to his work” (2.252), and the narrator closes the assembly with the verse of Achilles in *Iliad* 19, “So he spoke, and quickly dissolved the assembly” (2.257 = *Iliad* 19.276). The next verse is the Iliad's too, with the houses of Ithaca in place of the ships before Troy: “And they scattered, each to his own house” (2.258; *Iliad* 19.277). The man who dissolves it is a suitor, not the man who called it (2.252–259; [[leiocritus-son-of-euenor]], [[the-assembly-of-the-ithacans]]).
