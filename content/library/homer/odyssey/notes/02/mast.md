---
summary: As Telemachus' ship puts out, the crew raise the mast of fir within its box and make it fast with forestays (2.424–425); the verses return when he sails home from Pylos (15.289–291).
---

The book has ἱστός in both its senses. In Antinous' story it is Penelope's loom and the web she weaves on it, rendered “loom” where it is set up and “web” where it is woven and unraveled (2.94, 2.104, 2.109; see the translation's table of key words, [[loom]] and [[the-web-of-penelope]]). On the ship it is the mast:

{{quote:521b8a8b-28e4-5210-b41a-c33d2c9fd2d2}}

The Iliad's ship to Chryse sets up its mast and spreads its sails in a single verse, “They set up the mast and spread the white sails” (*Iliad* 1.480). The Odyssey gives the mast its wood, εἰλάτινος, “of fir”, the wood of the beams of Odysseus' hall (19.38), and its hollow box (see [[mast-box]]); the crew raise it, stand it up and stay it with the [[forestays]], and then haul up the sails (see [[sails]]). The next verses, the wind in the sail and the wave at the cutwater, are those of the Iliad's voyage home from Chryse (2.427–429, as *Iliad* 1.481–483; see [[cutwater]]). The three verses of the mast return word for word when Telemachus sails home from Pylos, after the crew lay hold of the tackle at his bidding, as here (15.287–291, as 2.422–426; see [[sailing-type-scene]]).
