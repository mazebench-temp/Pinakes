---
summary: When Telemachus rises in the assembly, the herald Peisenor puts the scepter into his hand (2.37–38). He is named only here.
---

Aegyptius has asked who called the assembly, and Telemachus, glad of the word of omen, “stood in the middle of the assembly; and the scepter was put in his hand / by the herald Peisenor, who knew wise counsels” (2.37–38; see [[aegyptius]], [[scepter]] and [[heralds]]). The speaker stands in the middle and takes the scepter from a herald, as Menelaus does at the funeral games of Patroclus, when “a herald / put the scepter in his hand, and bade the Argives / be silent” (*Iliad* 23.567–569). W. W. Merry calls the scepter the badge of public office, carried by kings, priests, prophets, heralds and judges, so that the herald who puts it into a speaker's hand invests him for the time with a public office (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 2.37). The heralds of the Iliad carry scepters of their own: Talthybius and Idaeus hold theirs out between Hector and Ajax (*Iliad* 7.277), and the elders of the trial on the Shield of Achilles hold “the scepters of the far-voiced heralds” when they rise to give judgment (*Iliad* 18.505–506). Telemachus speaks with the scepter in his hand, and at the end of his appeal he flings it to the ground (2.80).

The herald's verse is the one the Iliad gives Idaeus (*Iliad* 7.278), and the poem says nothing more of Peisenor: he has no father and no other part. The father of Ops, whose name ends the verse of Eurycleia's lineage later in the book, is another man (2.347; see [[peisenor-father-of-ops]]).
