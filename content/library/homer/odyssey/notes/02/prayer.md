After the assembly he washes his hands in the gray sea and calls on the god who came to his house the day before, and Athena comes to him at once in the likeness of Mentor. In the assembly he begs the people by Olympian Zeus and Themis and leaves the requital to the gods, and at sea the crew pour libations.

**The form.** Telemachus goes apart along the shore and washes his hands in the sea before he prays (see [[hand-washing]] and [[shore]]). The Iliad's first prayer is made in the same way: Chryses goes along the shore of the loud-roaring sea and, “when he had gone far off”, prays to Apollo (ἀπάνευθε κιών, *Iliad* 1.34–36); Achilles sits apart on the shore of the gray sea to pray to his mother (*Iliad* 1.349–351). εὔχομαι is here the verb of prayer, as it never is in Book 1 (2.261, 2.267; see [[euchomai]]). The invocation is the usual “Hear me” (κλῦθί μευ; see [[hear-me]]), but where Chryses names the god and the places where he is honored, Telemachus can say only what the god did: “you god who came yesterday to our house / and bade me go in a ship over the misty sea”. θεός is a predicate: he knew the stranger for a god (1.323, 1.420; see the translation's note on 2.262). There is no claim of past offerings, as in Chryses' prayer (*Iliad* 1.39–41; see the article above), and no request. The god's own command stands for the claim, and the complaint that “all this the Achaeans are delaying, / and most of all the suitors” is left for the god to answer (2.265–266; see [[the-suitors]] and [[overweening]]).

**The answer.** The verse that closes a prayer in the Iliad is “So he spoke in prayer, and Phoebus Apollo heard him” (*Iliad* 1.43, 1.457). Here it ends “and Athena came near to him” (2.267), and she comes in the likeness of Mentor (see [[athena]] and [[divine-epiphany]]). The same half-verse brings her to Odysseus when he laments on the shore of Ithaca and when he lies awake on the night before the slaughter (13.221, 20.30).

**Appeals in the assembly.** In the assembly he begs the people “by Olympian Zeus and by Themis” (2.68; see [[themis-the-goddess]] and [[supplication]]), and warns the suitors, as he had the day before, that he will call upon the gods who live forever, “if ever Zeus will grant that deeds of requital be done” (2.143–144; see the Book 1 note). Zeus answers with two eagles (2.146; see [[zeus]] and [[the-omen-of-the-eagles]]). Aegyptius' wish that Zeus may bring good to the man who called the assembly is a blessing, which Telemachus takes as a word of omen (2.33–35). The other verb of prayer, ἀράομαι, is used of the curse a mother would lay on her son (2.135; see [[erinyes]] and [[curses]]).

**Libations.** At sea, when the tackle is made fast, the crew set up mixing bowls and pour libations “to the immortal, everlasting gods, / and most of all to the gray-eyed daughter of Zeus” (2.430–433; see [[libation]]).
