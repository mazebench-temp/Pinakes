---
summary: “Never has there been an assembly of ours or a session / since brilliant Odysseus went away in the hollow ships” (2.26–27): Telemachus calls the first assembly of Ithaca since his father sailed, appeals to “Themis, / who dissolves the assemblies of men and seats them” (2.68–69), and hears it dissolved by a suitor (2.257).
except: [2.318]
---

**The first since Odysseus sailed.** Telemachus has the heralds summon the people at dawn, as Athena told him to (2.6–8; 1.272–273). Aegyptius' first words are the measure of the island's state: there has been no assembly “since brilliant Odysseus went away in the hollow ships” (2.26–27; see [[aegyptius]]). He names the business that would call one: news of an army coming, or “some other public matter” (2.30–32; see [[demos]]). Merry reports the two readings of the scholia, an enemy's army or the host that went to Ilios, which the scholia prefer and Ameis adopts, since Aegyptius longs for his son; he himself finds it far more reasonable that an assembly should be called in haste for tidings of some invasion, a raid or a descent of pirates (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 2.30). Telemachus has neither: his business is his own (2.40–45; see [[the-assembly-of-the-ithacans]]). See the translation's note on 2.7, 2.26 for the council (βουλή, θῶκος) beside the gathering of the people (see [[boule]] and [[elders]]).

**How it is held.** The people gather until “all assembled”, the first clause of 2.9 being, Merry explains, the gathering and the second the assembly fully formed (on 2.9). The king's son takes his father's seat and the elders make way (2.14); an old man speaks first (2.15–16); the speaker stands in the middle and the herald puts the scepter into his hand (2.37–38; see [[heralds]] and [[scepter]]); and each speaker addresses the people with the same verse, “Hear me now, men of Ithaca, in what I say” (2.25, 2.161, 2.229; see [[ithacans]]). Telemachus calls on the goddess of the institution, “Themis, / who dissolves the assemblies of men and seats them” (2.68–69; see [[themis-the-goddess]] and [[themis]]). The Cyclopes, one of whom ate Aegyptius' son, have no such thing: they have no assemblies that bring counsel, and no ordinances (οὔτʼ ἀγοραὶ βουληφόροι οὔτε θέμιστες, 9.112; see [[cyclopes]]).

**What the people do.** The people listen, pity and keep silent (2.81–82; see [[laos]], [[pity]] and [[silence]]), and Mentor blames them for it (2.239–241; see [[nemesis]]). Zeus' eagles wheel over “the middle of the assembly of many voices” (μέσσην ἀγορὴν πολύφημον, 2.150; see [[eagle]] and [[of-many-voices]]). Nothing is decided: Leiocritus, one of the suitors, orders the people to scatter and “quickly dissolved the assembly” (2.252–257; see [[leiocritus-son-of-euenor]]), with the verse in which Achilles dissolves the assembly of reconciliation (*Iliad* 19.276). In the Iliad those who dissolve assemblies are the leaders, Achilles and Agamemnon (*Iliad* 1.305) or Hector (*Iliad* 2.808); here it is one of the suitors (see [[kingship-and-authority]]).

**The last assembly.** The poem ends with another assembly of the Ithacans, in the same words: when they “were all assembled” (24.421 = 2.9), a father speaks first, shedding tears for his son (24.425, as 2.24), pity seizes them (24.438, as 2.81), and Halitherses rises, introduced as he is here (24.451–454, as 2.157–161), to remind them that they would not listen to him or to Mentor (24.454–462; see [[halitherses]] and [[mentor]]). ἀγορεύω, “speak”, counts where the speaking is done in the assembly; at 2.318 Telemachus speaks to the suitors in the house, and the line is excepted.
