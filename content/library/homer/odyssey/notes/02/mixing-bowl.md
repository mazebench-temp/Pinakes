**Poison in the bowl.** One of the young men says what Telemachus may be planning (see [[odysseus-seeks-poison]], [[ephyra]] and [[the-suitors]]):

{{quote:dfed349e-45b8-533d-8266-eb7052908486}}

The bowl is the one vessel from which all the feasters are served, so that a drug in it reaches every cup. So it is with the drug that Helen puts into the wine at Sparta: whoever drank it “when it was mixed in the bowl” would not shed a tear all that day, not even if his mother and father died (4.220–226, a plain rendering; see [[helen]]). The jest takes up the voyage of the first book, when Odysseus went to Ephyra for a man-slaying drug to smear on his arrows (1.259–264), and turns the drug from the arrows to the wine.

**Mixing bowls at sea.** “they set up mixing bowls brim-full of wine, / and poured libations to the immortal, everlasting gods” (2.431–432; see [[libation]] and [[brim-full]]). The verse-end κρητῆρας ἐπιστεφέας οἴνοιο comes once in the Iliad, when Agamemnon reminds the Achaeans of their empty boasts on Lemnos, “eating much meat of straight-horned cattle / and drinking mixing bowls brim-full of wine” (*Iliad* 8.231–232). The bowls of the first book were filled for the suitors' drinking in the hall (1.110, 1.148); these are set up on the deck of a running ship (see the Book 1 note).
