---
summary: Both of Horace's portraits fit the suitors of this book, who are accused before the assembly of eating what is not theirs. Telemachus complains of their daily feasting and asks for payment, not gifts; the suitors' gifts appear in the book only as the bride-price, which Penelope's father should settle.
refs: [2.55-58, 2.300, 2.305]
---

**The kitchen.** The charge against the suitors in the assembly is the one Horace makes. Telemachus tells the people that they “haunt our house every day, / sacrificing oxen and sheep and fat goats, / and hold banquets and drink the sparkling wine, / wantonly” (2.55–58; see [[the-assembly-of-the-ithacans]]). Antinous answers: “For so long will they eat up your substance and your possessions, / as long as she keeps to this purpose” (2.123–124). Eurymachus grants that his goods “will be wretchedly eaten up” (2.203), Mentor says that they “violently devour / the house of Odysseus” (2.237–238), and Leiocritus calls the quarrel a fight “over a feast” (2.245). When Telemachus comes back from the shore he finds them “flaying goats and singeing fat hogs in the courtyard”, and Antinous bids him “eat and drink with me, just as before” (2.300, 2.305; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]).

**No gifts.** Telemachus asks for payment, not for gifts. It would be better for him, he says, if the people themselves ate up his treasures and herds, for then there would be recompense (2.74–78); and if the suitors think it better that one man's substance “should be destroyed without payment”, he will call on the gods, and they may perish “unavenged” inside his house (2.141–145; see [[poine]]). The suitors' gifts appear in the book only as the bride-price. The suitors “shudder at going to the house of her father, / Icarius, so that he himself might settle the bride-price for his daughter” (2.52–53), and Eurymachus, bidding Telemachus send her back to her father, says that “they will arrange a wedding and make ready the bride-price, / a very great one” (2.195–197; see [[bride-price]] and [[icarius]]). The one other gift in the book is the one Eurymachus accuses Halitherses of hoping to get from Telemachus (2.186). Horace's Tiresias, who says that the young men came sparing of great gifts, describes the suitors of this book.

**Penelope.** Horace's Ulysses calls her so honest and so chaste that the suitors could not drive her from the straight course (*Satires* 2.5.77–78). In the assembly the suitors give their own reasons. For Antinous it is her craft, the “crafty ways” that Athena gave her, and the web (2.88, 2.116–122; see [[penelopes-web-in-later-writers]]); for Eurymachus it is her excellence, for whose sake they “strive” and wait (2.205–206; see [[arete]]). Tiresias' reason, the suitors' meanness, is Horace's joke.
