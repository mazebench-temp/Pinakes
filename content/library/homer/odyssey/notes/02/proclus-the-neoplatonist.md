---
summary: Proclus answers Plato's ban on gods in disguise with Athena likened to Mentor, the likeness she first takes in this book, compares the body's nourishment, which puts together again what it has undone, to Penelope's web, and reports Longinus' judgment that a phrase of Plato's is no different from “the sacred strength of Telemachus”.
---

- The myths give changes to gods who do not change in three ways, and the third, the god come down to its lowest orders, is shown when “they say that Athena is likened to Mentor, Hermes to the gull and Apollo to the hawk” (*On the Republic* I 112–114 Kroll, on 2.268, 2.401): [[athena-as-mentor-in-later-writers]].
- The nutritive power, which makes good what flows away with what flows in and puts together again what it has undone, “like Penelope's web” (*On the Timaeus* III 332 Diehl, on 2.104–105): [[penelopes-web-in-later-writers]].
- Longinus found Plato's “the kind of your condition” (*Timaeus* 19e) as strange as “the might of Heracles” and “the sacred strength of Telemachus”; Origen defended such periphrases in prose, and Proclus denies that Plato's phrase is one (*On the Timaeus* I 68 Diehl, on 2.409): [[longinus-on-the-sacred-strength-of-telemachus]].
