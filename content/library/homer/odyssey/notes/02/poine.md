---
summary: Telemachus would rather the people ate up his goods themselves, since then “there would soon perhaps be recompense” (τίσις, 2.76); he could not send his mother away without having “to pay back much / to Icarius” (2.132–133); and he repeats his warning that the suitors would perish “unavenged” (2.141–145). Eurymachus threatens Halitherses with a fine that he will “chafe at heart to pay” (2.192–193).
grc: [ἀποτινύμενοι, ἀποτίνειν, τίνων]
en: [recompense, taking vengeance]
---

**Who would pay.** Telemachus' complaint is an account of payment. He asks the people to hold back, unless his father did harm to the Achaeans and they are “taking vengeance on me for these”, urging the suitors on (ἀποτινύμενοι, 2.70–74). Better that they ate his treasures and herds themselves: “If you were the ones to eat them, there would soon perhaps be recompense”, since he could go through the city “asking back our goods, until all of it was given back” (2.74–78). From the suitors there is nothing to recover (see [[possessions]] and [[the-suitors]]).

**Paying Icarius.** Sending his mother back to her father against her will would cost him a payment, κακὸν δέ με πόλλʼ ἀποτίνειν / Ἰκαρίῳ, “it would be hard for me to pay back much / to Icarius” (2.132–133; see [[icarius]] and [[mothers-and-sons]]).

**Requital.** He repeats to the suitors the warning of the evening before (2.139–145, as 1.374–380), lines that Kirchhoff, as Merry reports, held to be an awkward imitation in Book 1 of this passage (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.374). If the suitors think it better “that one man's substance should be destroyed without payment”, he will call on the gods, “if ever Zeus will grant that deeds of requital be done; / then, unavenged, you would perish inside my house” (2.142–145; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]). Zeus answers at once with the eagles (2.146; see [[eagle]]).

**A fine.** Eurymachus answers prophecy with a penalty: “and on you, old man, we will lay a fine, which you will / chafe at heart to pay” (θωήν, τίνων, 2.192–193; see [[halitherses]]). θωή occurs only once more in Homer, for the fine of the Achaeans that Euchenor escaped by going to Troy (*Iliad* 13.669; see [[euchenor]]). And the suitors will pay nothing for what they eat: “there will never / be amends” while Penelope keeps them waiting (2.203–205).
