summary: The likeness begins in this book. Athena comes to Telemachus on the shore as Mentor, the man to whom Odysseus entrusted his house, promises him a ship and her company, and calls him to it in the same form at nightfall; between the two she goes through the city as Telemachus himself.
refs: [2.267-268, 2.383, 2.401]
---

**Two likenesses.** After the assembly Telemachus goes apart to the shore, washes his hands in the gray sea and prays to “you god who came yesterday to our house” (2.260–266; see [[prayer-type-scene]]). Athena comes near him “in the likeness of Mentor, both in form and in voice” (2.268), the man who has just spoken for him in the assembly and to whom Odysseus, when he went in the ships, “had entrusted all his house” (2.224–227; see [[mentor]]). This is the Minerva disguised as Mentor that Cicero wished for (see the entry above). She promises that she, “such a companion of your father's”, will fit out a swift ship and go with him herself (2.286–287). Then she takes another likeness: “In the likeness of Telemachus she went everywhere through the city”, bidding each man gather at the ship in the evening, and she asks Noemon for the ship (2.383–387; see [[noemon-son-of-phronius]]). At nightfall she calls Telemachus out of the hall as Mentor again, leads the way to the shore and sits beside him in the stern (2.399–418; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).

**Proclus' orders.** In Proclus' scheme both likenesses of this book belong to the lowest order, since each is the form of one particular man, Mentor or Telemachus, and not a general human form, as when Poseidon and Athena come to Achilles “in form like men” (*Iliad* 21.285); in the Iliad's first book the goddess appears to Achilles alone, with no form described at all (*Iliad* 1.197–198).

**The companion.** Fénelon's Mentor is the goddess as guide and teacher. In this book her help is practical, a ship, a crew, the provisions and a following wind (2.287–295, 2.382–392, 2.420–421), and her counsel is the hope that he is his father's son: “Few sons are like their father; / most are worse, and few are better than their father” (2.276–277; see [[few-sons-are-like-their-father]]).
