summary: Athena pours a wondrous grace on Telemachus as he goes to the assembly. When he prays to her on the shore, she comes in the likeness of Mentor and promises a ship and her company; then, in his own likeness, she gathers a crew through the city and borrows a ship from Noemon, launches it at nightfall, pours sleep on the suitors, leads Telemachus to the ship as Mentor, sits beside him in the stern, and sends a fresh West Wind. The crew pour libations most of all to her.

**Grace for the assembly.** Telemachus goes to the assembly with a bronze spear in his hand and two swift dogs at his heels, “And Athena poured a wondrous grace upon him, / and all the people gazed at him as he came” (2.10–13; see [[telemachus]], [[charis]] and [[assembly]]). The verses return when he goes out to the place of assembly in Book 17 (17.62–64), and in Book 8 she pours the same wondrous grace on the head and shoulders of Odysseus as the Phaeacians gather (8.17–19). In Book 1 she put might and daring into his heart (1.320–321); here the people see the gift.

**Her gift to Penelope.** Antinous names her as the giver of the skill behind the web (see [[the-web-of-penelope]]): Penelope ponders in her heart “what Athena has given her beyond all others, / to be skilled in beautiful handiwork, and good wits, / and crafty ways” (2.116–118; see [[penelope]], [[kerdos]] and [[weaving]]). The women of the Phaeacians, skilled at the loom, have the same gift in the same words (7.108–111), and she taught the daughters of Pandareus their famous handiwork (20.72).

**The prayer and the answer.** After the assembly Telemachus goes apart along the shore, washes his hands in the gray sea, and prays to Athena. He does not name her: he calls on “you god who came yesterday to our house / and bade me go in a ship over the misty sea” (2.260–263; see [[prayer]] and the translation's note on 2.262), the stranger he knew in his heart for the immortal goddess (1.420). The answer comes at once: “So he spoke in prayer, and Athena came near to him / in the likeness of Mentor, both in form and in voice” (2.267–268; see [[divine-epiphany]] and [[mentor]]). In Book 1 she was Mentes, a stranger from Taphos (1.105); now she takes the shape of a man of Ithaca, the companion of Odysseus to whom he entrusted his house (2.225–227), whom Leiocritus had just named in mockery as the man who would speed Telemachus on his way (2.253).

**As Mentor.** She speaks to him of his father: “Telemachus, hereafter you will be no coward and no fool, / if indeed your father's noble might has been instilled in you” (2.270–271; see [[fathers-and-sons]]). The suitors are senseless, and death and black doom are near them (2.281–284; see [[the-suitors]]). She will do herself what the people did not do for him (2.212): “such a companion of your father's am I to you / that I will fit out a swift ship for you and go with you myself” (2.286–287; see [[hetairos]]). He is to make ready the provisions, while she gathers willing companions and picks out the best of the many ships in Ithaca (2.288–295; see [[provisions]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). The narrator closes the scene with her name and her father's, “So spoke Athena, daughter of Zeus”, and Telemachus goes, “once he had heard the voice of the goddess” (2.296–297; see [[daughter-of-zeus]]).

**In the likeness of Telemachus.** While he fills the jars and the bags with Eurycleia (2.348–381; see [[eurycleia]] and [[the-storeroom-of-odysseus]]), the goddess turns to the ship:

{{quote:83112f7b-4fdb-51bb-b321-eaa8fec969ce}}

The verse of her new thought, which returns at 2.393, is hers again in later books (4.795, 6.112, 18.187, 23.344), and the verse of her rounds is the one with which, in the likeness of Alcinous' herald, she calls the Phaeacians to their assembly (2.384 = 8.10). Noemon later tells Antinous that he gave the ship to Telemachus willingly, and that the leader he saw go aboard was Mentor, or a god who was like him in every way (Μέντορα, ἠὲ θεόν), though he had seen Mentor himself in Ithaca the morning before (4.649–656; see [[noemon-son-of-phronius]]). At sunset she drags the ship down to the sea, puts in its tackle, moors it at the far edge of the harbor, and urges on each of the companions as they gather (2.388–392; see [[ships]] and [[harbor]]).

**Sleep for the suitors.** Then she goes to the house:

{{quote:6a1b89d7-5ed1-50a4-ae7f-537f1c377631}}

In Book 1 she cast sweet sleep on Penelope's eyelids (1.363–364); here the sleep sends the suitors home through the city before Telemachus leaves (2.397–398; see [[hypnos]] and [[sweet-sleep]]).

**To the ship and to sea.** She calls him out of the hall, again “in the likeness of Mentor, both in form and in voice”, and tells him that the companions sit at the oars (2.399–404). “So she spoke, and Pallas Athena led the way / swiftly; and he followed in the footprints of the goddess” (2.405–406; see [[pallas]]), verses that return when she leads him to Nestor (3.29–30). On board she goes first and sits in the stern, with Telemachus close beside her (2.416–418), as at Pylos she leads him off the ship (3.12); on the voyage home it is Telemachus who sits in the stern, with the seer Theoclymenus beside him (15.285–286). She sends a following wind, “a fresh West Wind” (2.420–421; see [[zephyrus]]), with the verse in which Apollo sends the wind for the ship from Chryse (*Iliad* 1.479). The crew pour libations to the immortal gods, “and most of all to the gray-eyed daughter of Zeus” (2.432–433; see [[libation]] and [[gray-eyed]]).
