# Rhetoric — translation conventions

The English is a new line-for-line translation by Claude (Anthropic), made from W. D. Ross's Oxford
text in `grc/`. Every English row carries the Bekker line of the Greek it renders, so quotations, the
index and the reader can address any passage in either language. Greek prose does not break where
English would, so English syntax often runs across a line break; when it does, the words stay within
the same one or two lines as the Greek, and names stay on the line where the Greek names them.
American spelling is used throughout. The library has all three books, as chapters 1 to 60 (Book I is
chapters 1–15, Book II 16–41, Book III 42–60, shown as 2.1, 3.1 and so on); the conventions below are
Book I's, and the sections on Books II and III at the end add what is new in each.

## The text and its numbering

The Greek is W. D. Ross, *Aristotelis Ars Rhetorica* (Oxford Classical Texts, 1959), as digitized by
the Perseus Digital Library (CC BY-SA 4.0). The Rhetoric is cited by the page, column and line of
Immanuel Bekker's Berlin edition of Aristotle (1831). Book I runs from 1354a1 to 1377b12, and Book II
begins at 1377b16. Ross prints every fifth Bekker line in his margin, and so does the digitized text.
The lines between are divided here in proportion to their length, with two further anchors: each
chapter begins on the line where editions place it (chapter 2 at 1355b25, chapter 11 at 1369b33), and
each column ends where the length of its text puts it (both columns of every page come out the same
length, which they should). The last lines of Book I are divided so that it ends at 1377b12. The
every-fifth lines are Ross's; a line between them may begin a word or two earlier or later than in
Bekker's own print.

Ross prints, as his lines 1377a7a–d, a sentence about the hardy and the timid under torture that
Bekker did not have. It is kept within line 1377a7. The chapter divisions are the traditional ones,
which Ross prints.

Four slips in the digitized text are corrected:

| Line | Digitized | Corrected |
|---|---|---|
| 1371a1 | ταύταισγίγνεται | ταύταις γίγνεται |
| 1371b24 | φιλόμαιμοι | φιλότιμοι (“fond of honor”; the word returns at 1371b29) |
| 1372b2 | τυρρανίς | τυραννίς |
| 1376a11 | ὁμολογεῖνπονηροὺς | ὁμολογεῖν πονηροὺς |

The digitized text keeps the punctuation of the surrounding sentence inside the verse lines it
quotes; where that put a closing parenthesis inside the quotation marks, it is moved outside them
(1363a16, 1364a28, 1370a11), and a comma that separated two of the edition's source references
(1370b29) is dropped. The dash at 1357b29, printed between two words without a space, is given one
after it, so that ὅμοιον and ὅταν are read as two words.

### Editorial signs

| Sign | Meaning | In the English |
|---|---|---|
| ⟨ ⟩ | words Ross adds to the manuscripts | translated without comment |
| [ ] | words Ross deletes as not Aristotle's | translated in square brackets where they carry content (the list of virtue's parts, 1360b23–24); left out where they do not (a repeated article or particle, a doublet) |
| † † | a corrupt passage Ross cannot mend | translated as literally as possible between daggers |
| … | words Aristotle leaves out of a quotation (Antigone, 1375b1–2) | marked … |

## Quotations

Aristotle quotes Homer from memory or from texts that differ from ours. Where a quotation agrees with
our Iliad, the English keeps the wording of the Iliad translation (“Surely Priam would rejoice”,
1362b35 = Il. 1.255; “leave to Priam a thing to boast of”, 1363a5–6 = Il. 2.160 and 2.176; “It is
shameful to stay so long”, 1363a6 = Il. 2.298). Where it differs, the English follows Aristotle: at
1365a13–15 he gives Meleager's wife “all the ills” (ὅσσα κάκʼ) where our Iliad has “the sorrows”
(κήδεʼ, 9.592), and “the people perish” (λαοὶ μὲν φθινύθουσι) where it has “they kill the men”
(9.593). Il. 18.109 and 23.108 come from books the library has not yet translated, and the Odyssey
is not in it; they are translated here. Aristotle's Odyssey 15.400–401 differs from ours as well:
“remembering, one who has suffered much and done much” (μνημένος … πολλὰ ἐόργῃ), where our texts
have “who has suffered much and wandered much”.

Proverbs and sayings that Aristotle quotes in their own words are in quotation marks; those he
paraphrases (Mysian plunder, 1372b33; the water jar at the door, 1363a7) are not. The index gives
each quotation its source and its context.

## Names

Names have their Latin forms, as in the Iliad (Pisistratus, Iphicrates, Themistocles). Paris is
“Alexander”, as Aristotle and the Iliad call him (1363a19). Ἰάσων ὁ Θετταλός is “Jason the
Thessalian”, the tyrant of Pherae, not the Argonaut. Aristotle's other works are named as he names
them: the Analytics, the Topics, the Methodics, the Politics, and “the books on poetics” (1372a1–2).

## Places where the Greek is disputed

- **1355a23.** διʼ αὑτῶν ἡττᾶσθαι: if judgments go wrong, the true and the just “must have been
  defeated through their own fault”. The phrase is compressed; the fault is that of those who should
  have spoken for them, as the next words (“and this deserves reproach”) show.
- **1358b36, 1359a13.** Ross deletes a negative that the sense does not allow; the English follows him.
- **1360b23–24.** Ross deletes the list of virtue's parts after “virtue” as an addition to the text;
  the English keeps it in brackets.
- **1366b1–2.** Ross's list of the parts of virtue has eight members. The Loeb text (Freese, 1926)
  adds πραότης, “gentleness”, as a ninth; Aristotle goes on to define the others, but not gentleness.
- **1373b17–18.** The words of Alcidamas, “God has left all men free; nature has made no one a slave”,
  are not in the manuscripts of the Rhetoric. Ross prints them from an ancient commentary on the
  passage; the Messenian Speech itself is lost.
- **1373b30.** The one wronged must be harmed, “and harmed against his will”. The digitized text
  reads ἑκουσίως, “voluntarily”; the sense needs ἀκουσίως, since the wrong is done to him by one
  acting voluntarily (1373b28), and Freese (Loeb, 1926) translates “against his will”. The English
  follows ἀκουσίως; the Greek is left as digitized, since Ross's printed text could not be checked.
- **1374b32.** †καὶ ἀδύνατον†: a wrong with no remedy is the greater, “for it is hard †and
  impossible†”. The daggered words are corrupt.
- **1376a10.** The Plato whose remark Eubulus used against Chares is probably the comic poet, not the
  philosopher (so Freese's note); Archibius is otherwise unknown.
- **1377a7a–d.** The extra sentence on torture: the hardy hold out, while “cowards and cautious men are
  bold only until they have seen the instruments of torture” (καταθαρροῦσιν, “are bold”). The Greek is
  compressed; the sense is that their boldness does not survive the sight of the instruments.

### Quotations

Where Aristotle quotes Books 1–13 of the Iliad and agrees with our text, the English keeps the
wording of the Iliad translation: 1378b33 (Il. 1.356, which Aristotle quotes without its last two
words), 1378b34 (9.648), 1379a5 (2.196), 1379a5–6 (1.82), 1387a33–35 (11.542–543, with the verse that
our manuscripts lack), 1395a14 (12.243). Il. 18.109–110 (1378b5–7), 24.54 (1380b30) and 18.309
(1395a15, “Enyalius is impartial”, the rendering of the index) and *Odyssey* 9.504 (1380b23) are
translated here.

Aristotle's lines of Euripides differ in a word from our texts, and the English follows Aristotle:
“from the citizens”, παρʼ ἀστῶν (1394a35; *Medea* 297 has πρὸς ἀστῶν); “no man”, οὐκ ἔστιν ἀνδρῶν
(1394b3; *Hecuba* 864 has θνητῶν, “of mortals”); “no one is a lover”, οὐδεὶς ἐραστής (1394b16;
*Trojan Women* 1051 has οὐκ ἔστʼ ἐραστής).

Ross's quotation marks are followed. In the priestess's dilemma (1399a23–26) the counter-dilemma
stands inside the same quotation, and so it does in the English. Sayings that Aristotle reports
without quoting have no marks in the Greek and none in the English: Chilon's maxim (1389b3), Bias'
precept (1389b24), the proverbs of the corpse and of shame in the eyes (1383b24, 1384a34), Caunian
love (1402b2). Words cited as words are in quotation marks in the English only (“perhaps” and
“maybe”, 1389b19; “Hermes in common”, 1401a22). Every quotation has an entry in the index with its
source.

### Names

ὁ Ἀμάσιος (1386a20) is “the son of Amasis”: in Herodotus the king who did not weep for his son but
wept for his begging friend is Psammenitus, son of Amasis (3.14). Ἡγησίπολις (1398b33) is
“Hegesipolis”, Aristotle's spelling of the Spartan king Agesipolis. αἱ σεμναὶ θεαί, the Erinyes, are “the
Dread Goddesses” (1398b27). ἡ Δωδωνίς is “the woman of Dodona” (1398b4). βασιλεύς without the
article is “the king” of Persia (1386a14, 1393a32). Ἀλέξανδρος is Paris, as in Book I, and ἐν τῷ
Ἀλεξάνδρῳ (1398a22, 1401b20) is “in the Alexander”, a lost defense of him.

### Places where the Greek is disputed

- **1385a17–18.** χάρις, καθʼ ἣν ὁ ἔχων λέγεται χάριν ἔχειν: whether the chapter describes the feeling
  of the one who does the favor or of the one who receives it is disputed. The English keeps the
  Greek's “the one who has it”.
- **1387a30–31.** ἂν οὖν ἀγαθὸς ὢν μὴ τοῦ ἁρμόττοντος τυγχάνῃ: taken as “if a good man gets what does
  not suit him”, which keeps the sentence about undeserved good fortune, the subject of the chapter;
  the words can also mean “does not get what suits him”.
- **1388a8.** “For kinship knows how to envy too”: the author of the verse is unknown.
- **1394b12–13.** “For a man health is best”: a verse in Doric, of unknown authorship.
- **1397a11.** “The Messenian Speech” is probably Alcidamas', the speech Book I names (1373b18).
- **1397b21–22.** [τὸν Ἑλλάδος], “[of Greece]”: Ross brackets the words in the verse on Oeneus.
- **1400b1.** εἰ [μὴ] οὕτως ἔχει: Ross deletes the negative; with his text the sense is that if it
  was possible to act better, the man did not do it.
- **1400b18.** σαφῶς σιδήρῳ καὶ φοροῦσα τοὔνομα: Ross prints σιδήρῳ, “to iron”. The pun is on the
  name of Tyro's stepmother Sidero, Σιδηρώ, and the English, “Clearly of iron”, keeps it.
- **1401b13.** Which Dionysius is the thief of the example is not said; the name may stand for anyone.

## Book III

### The text and its numbering

Book III, on style and arrangement, has nineteen chapters, in the files `42.txt` to `60.txt` (chapter
k is file 41 + k, shown as “3.k”). The Greek is Ross's, as digitized by the Perseus Digital Library. It
runs from 1403b6 to 1420a8, the last line of the treatise. As in Book I, Ross's every-fifth Bekker
lines are fixed (with his mark at 1409b28, where a verse quotation begins, and without the digitized
text's second mark for 1420a1), each chapter begins on the line where the editions place it, each
column ends where the length of its text puts it, with both columns of a page the same length, and the
lines between are divided in proportion to their length, at word boundaries and never inside a
quotation mark. The chapters begin at 1403b6, 1404b1, 1405b35, 1406b20, 1407a19, 1407b26, 1408a10,
1408b21, 1409a24, 1410b6, 1411b21, 1413b3, 1414a31, 1414b19, 1416a4, 1416b16, 1417b21, 1418b40 and
1419b10. Ross's line mark falls on the first words of chapters 3 and 18, so they begin at 1405b35 and

### Key words

Book III keeps Book I's renderings (proof, enthymeme, example, character, emotion, slander, the
adviser and advice) and adds the words of style and arrangement. Λέξις is “style”, not the Poetics'
“diction”: in the Rhetoric it covers the whole manner of expression, the choice of words, their
rhythm, the period and the styles of the kinds of speech.

### Quotations

Where Aristotle's Homer agrees with our Iliad, the English keeps the library's wording: Achilles'
refusal (9.385, 388–390, at 1413a32–34), Nireus (2.671–673, at 1414a2–3), “they could be won with
gifts and swayed by words” (9.526, at 1410a30–31), the arrow and the spears (13.587, 4.126, 11.574,
13.799, at 1411b35–1412a9), and “Sing, goddess, the wrath” (1.1, at 1415a16). Where he differs, the
English follows him: “he rushed on like a lion” (1406b21) is not in our text (the nearest is 20.164);
he shortens 13.587 to “the arrow flew”; in the Odyssey's shameless stone (11.598) he has “down to the
ground” for “then to the plain”. The Odyssey (1.1, 4.204, 6.327, 11.598, 19.361) and Iliad 15.542 are
translated here. Of the tragedians, Euripides' Greeks dart forth “on their feet” (ποσίν, 1411b30)
where our *Iphigenia at Aulis* has “with the spear” (80); Hecuba's lines are abridged (*Trojan Women*
969, 971); the line Aristotle gives to Sophocles, “This land is Calydon” (1409b10), is the opening of
Euripides' *Meleager*. Aristotle's own examples of style, many of them anonymous, are in quotation marks
like the quotations of other authors; the structure files say which are which.

### Names

There are two Theodori: the tragic actor (1404b22) and the rhetorician of Byzantium (1412a26, 1412a34,
1414b14); and two Lycophrons: the sophist (1405b36, 1406a7) and Lycophron of Pherae, Peitholaus'
brother (1410a18). The Callias of 1405a19 is Callias son of Hipponicus; the Dionysius of 1405a32 is
Dionysius “the Brazen”, the poet; the Cleophon of 1408a15 is the tragic poet; “the Dog” (1411a24) is
Diogenes. Sophocles is the poet throughout Book III; the commissioner who answers Pisander (1419a26) is
taken to be the poet. Hellas is “Greece” and Ἕλληνες “Greeks”; Ἀλέξανδρος ὁ Πριάμου is “Alexander son of
Priam” (1414b39). Names keep their Latin forms (Rhadamanthus, Pisander, Phaeacians).

### Places where the Greek is disputed

- **1404a35.** Ross deletes ἀφείκασιν; the sense is unchanged.
- **1405a34.** †ταῖς ἀσήμοις φωναῖς†: the metaphor is bad “†with sounds that mean nothing†”. Corrupt.
- **1406b21.** Ross deletes “of Achilles”; the English keeps it in brackets, since the next line names him.
- **1409a28.** Ross brackets the words of Herodotus as an addition to Aristotle's text; the English keeps
  them in brackets. They give Herodotus as “of Thurii”, not “of Halicarnassus”.
- **1409b8, 1409b15.** †καὶ τῇ διανοίᾳ† and †ὥσπερ καὶ ἡ περίοδος†: translated between daggers.
- **1409b9.** The line called Sophocles' is Euripides' (*Meleager*, fr. 515 Nauck); the English keeps
  Aristotle's attribution.
- **1411a29, 1411b29.** Ross deletes ἐκκλησίας, a gloss on “concourses” (translated in brackets), and
  ἐλεύθερον (left out).
- **1412a25.** The parenthesis “(for they are learning, and metaphor too)” follows Ross's punctuation,
  corrected in the digitized text.
- **1412a35–36.** The jest turns on the near-identity of θράξει σε, “it will upset you”, and Θρᾷξ εἶ σύ,
  “you are a Thracian”; the English follows Ross's text and gives both.
- **1412b28.** Ross deletes ἀλλʼ οὐκ ἀστεῖον as a doublet of the next line.
- **1413b26.** The comedy is Γεροντομανία, *Old Men's Madness*; the digitized text has “Old Men's Battle”.
- **1416b29.** Ross marks a gap after “for not many know them”; the English marks it ….
- **1417a12–16.** The sentence on telling events as past, with the examples of the tale to Alcinous,
  Phayllus and the Oeneus, is compressed; the examples are usually taken as long stories told briefly.
- **1417b16.** †τότε οὐ†: “†then he does not†”. Corrupt.
