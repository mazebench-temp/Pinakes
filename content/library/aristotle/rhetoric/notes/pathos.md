The handbooks teach “slander and pity and anger and such emotions of the soul”, which are “not about the subject but are aimed at the juror” (1354a16–18; see [[the-handbooks]]); a well-governed court forbids them, for “one ought not to warp the juror by leading him into anger or envy or pity” (1354a24–25; see [[areopagus]] and [[pity]]). Yet emotion is the second artistic proof: people are persuaded “through the hearers, when they are led into emotion by the speech; for we do not give the same judgments when we are pained and when we are glad, or when we love and when we hate” (1356a14–16; see [[three-kinds-of-proof]]). The emotions are treated in Book II; in Book I, temper is left to that discussion (1373b36–37).

**Book II.** “The emotions are the things through which people change and differ in their judgments, and which are attended by pain and pleasure, such as anger, pity, fear and all other such, and their opposites” (1378a20–22). Each must be divided into three: the state of those who feel it, those toward whom, and the things at which (1378a22–28). Chapters 2–11 follow: anger and calmness, friendship and enmity, fear and confidence, shame and shamelessness, favor, pity, indignation, envy, emulation and contempt (see [[orge]], [[praotes]], [[philos]], [[phobos]], [[aischyne]], [[charis]], [[pity]], [[nemesis]], [[phthonos]] and [[zelos]]).

**Book III.** The emotional style is part of the appropriate: outrage spoken of angrily, the impious with disgust, the pitiable humbly (1408a16–19; see [[prepon]]); poetic words suit a man carried away by emotion (1408b10–20). The narration should draw on what is emotional and its known signs (1417a36–b7; see [[diegesis]]), and enthymemes should be kept out of passages of emotion (1418a12–15). The epilogue's third part puts the hearer into emotions, “pity and indignation, anger and hatred, envy, emulation and strife” (1419b25–26; see [[epilogos]]).
