---
summary: Outrage lies in the purpose, to dishonor the other or to please oneself; outrages are among the wrongs that victims are ashamed to report.
heading: In the Rhetoric
grc: ["ὑβρ*"]
en: [outrage]
---

Defendants admit the act but deny its name: they struck first “but did not commit outrage” (1374a2–3). The name signifies the choice, “for if a man struck someone, he did not necessarily commit outrage, but only if he did it for some purpose, such as to dishonor the other or to please himself” (1374a13–15; see [[prohairesis]]). People wrong those who are ashamed to speak of it, “such as outrages against the women of their house, or against themselves or their sons” (1373a34–35), and Euctemon killed himself after suffering outrage (1374b36; see [[euctemon]]). The second book defines ὕβρις among the forms of slight that cause anger.

**Book II.** Outrage is the third kind of slight: “for outrage is doing and saying things at which the victim feels shame, not so that anything may come to oneself other than what has happened, but for the pleasure of it” (1378b23–25); the young and the rich are given to it, since they think themselves superior (1378b26–29; see [[oligoria]]). Those who think themselves supremely happy commit outrage instead of pitying (1385b21); the young commit their wrongs from outrage, the old from malice (1389b7–8, 1390a17–18); wit is “educated outrage” (1389b12); the rich are given to outrage (1390b33). Socrates called it outrage to be unable to repay benefits (1398a24–26; see [[archelaus]]); striking free men is outrage only when one starts the unjust blows (1402a1–3).
