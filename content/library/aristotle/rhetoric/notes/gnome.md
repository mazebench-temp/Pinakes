**Book II.** The common proofs are example and enthymeme, “for the maxim is a part of an enthymeme” (1393a24–26). “A maxim is a statement, not, however, about particulars … but about universals; nor about all things … but about the things that are matters of action, and ⟨what⟩ is to be chosen or avoided with a view to action” (1394a21–26); with the syllogism removed, the conclusions and premises of enthymemes are maxims, as Medea's lines show (1394a26–35; see [[children-taught-too-wise]]). There are four kinds: maxims with or without a supplement, the reason, and of those with it, some are part of an enthymeme and some enthymematic in themselves (1394b7–26; see [[no-man-is-free]], [[health-is-best]], [[no-lover-who-does-not-love-always]], [[immortal-anger]] and [[mortal-thoughts]]); Aristotle's own example puts the reason first: “For my part, since one should neither be envied nor be idle, I say one should not be educated” (1394b31–32). Speaking in maxims suits older men and matters one knows; in the young it is unbecoming, and rustics are the greatest coiners of maxims (1395a2–8). One may use hackneyed maxims (see [[one-omen-is-best]], [[a-fool-who-kills-the-father]] and [[an-attic-neighbor]]) and maxims that go against public sayings (see [[know-yourself]], [[nothing-too-much]] and [[love-as-though-you-will-hate]]), the last of Aristotle's examples being “Nor do I like ‘Nothing too much’; for the bad, at least, one should hate too much” (1395a32–33). Maxims please the vulgar hearer, who is glad to hear in general terms what he already believes in particular, and above all they make speeches show character (1395b1–17; see [[ethos]]).

**Book III.** “Maxims should be used both in narration and in proof, for they show character: ‘And I gave it, though I knew one should not trust’” (1418a17–19), or with emotion, “And I do not regret it, though I have been wronged; his is the profit, mine the justice” (1418a19–21; see [[ethos]] and [[pathos]]). And one should “sometimes change enthymemes and make maxims of them” (1418b33–34): “Men of sense should make peace when they are prosperous; for in this way they would gain the most” is the maxim, and the enthymeme says the same with its reason (1418b34–39; see [[enthymema]]).
