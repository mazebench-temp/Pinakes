# αὔξειν at 1411b12 and αὔξησις at 1419b22 are increase and growth.
---

“One should also use many means of amplification: for instance, if he did something alone, or first, or with a few, or more than anyone; for all these are noble” (1368a10–12), and if he did it often, if honors were created for him (see [[hippolochus-of-the-encomium]] and [[harmodius]]), if he is compared with famous men (1368a10–26). “In general, of the forms common to all speeches, amplification is best suited to epideictic” (1368a26–27; see [[three-kinds-of-rhetoric]]). In chapter 15 a speaker amplifies the contracts and tortures that favor him (1376a33–34, 1376b7, 1376b34; see [[non-artistic-proofs]]).

**Book II.** Amplification is common to all speeches (1391b30–32) and most appropriate to epideictic (1392a4–5; see [[koina]]); amplifications are drawn from the advantageous, the noble and the just (1393a13–16). Exaggeration that amplifies without showing is an apparent enthymeme (1401b3–9; see [[apparent-enthymemes]]). Amplifying and diminishing are not an element of enthymeme, but enthymemes for showing that something is great or small (1403a16–24).

**Book III.** Describing a thing by what it does not have, as Antimachus does, amplifies it without limit (1408a2–4; see [[antimachus-of-colophon]]); asyndeta amplify, making one thing seem many (1413b34–1414a7; see [[asyndeton]]); comparison is an amplification of one's own case and so a part of the proofs (1414b10–11); in epideictic, “the amplification should be that the deeds are noble and beneficial” (1417b31–32); and amplifying and depreciating is the epilogue's second part (1419b12, 1419b20–24; see [[epilogos]]).
