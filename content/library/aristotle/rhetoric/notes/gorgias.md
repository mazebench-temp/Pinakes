**Book III.** Because the poets seemed to win their reputation through their style, “the first style was poetic, like that of Gorgias” (1404a25–26). His compounds and metaphors are among the frigidities of chapter 3: “beggar-muse-flatterers forsworn against one well-sworn” (1405b37–1406a1), “affairs pale and bloodless”, and “You have sown these things shamefully and reaped them badly” (1406b9–10); while his cry to a swallow that let fall her droppings on him, “Shameful, Philomela!”, is “the best of the tragic manner” (1406b15–19; see [[philomela]] and [[psychron]]). Poetic words may be used with irony, “as Gorgias did” (1408b20).

His speeches supply proems. The *Olympic Speech* opens with praise of those who founded the festivals: “By many are you worthy to be admired, men of Greece” (1414b31–32); the *Encomium of the Eleans* begins without any warming up, “Elis, happy city” (1416a1–3; see [[elis]] and [[prooimion]]). He used to say “that he never ran short of things to say” (1418a35), which comes to the same thing as Isocrates' episodes of praise: “for if he speaks of Achilles he praises Peleus, then Aeacus, then the god” (1418a36–37). And he said, rightly, that “one should destroy the opponents' seriousness with laughter and their laughter with seriousness” (1419b4–5; see [[geloion]]).
