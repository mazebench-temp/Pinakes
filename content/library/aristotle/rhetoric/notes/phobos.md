The coward does wrong in dangers, “for cowards abandon their comrades in danger out of fear” (1368b18–19). A wrong is greater if it is one “that hearers fear more than they pity” (1375a7–8; see [[pity]]), and one who commits wrongs “that are feared and penalized” would commit the rest (1375a18–20). Fear is defined in the second book (2.5).

**Book II.** Chapter 5: “Let fear be a kind of pain or disturbance arising from the imagination of a future evil that is destructive or painful” (1382a21–22). People fear evils that are near, and those who can and will do them harm (1382a22–1382b27); they fear when they think they may suffer, from whom and when, and the speaker who would make them afraid must show that greater men have suffered (1382b27–1383a12). Confidence is the opposite (1383a14–1383b10; see [[tharsos]]). The terrible drives out pity (1386a22–24; see [[pity]]).
