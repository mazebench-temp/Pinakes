# Ἑλένης at 1414b27 is Isocrates' Helen, the speech.
except: [1414b27]
---

Helen is twice an example of the good. Theseus chose her, as the wise choose what is good (1363a18; see [[theseus]] and [[helen-and-theseus]]); and Hera's words, “leave to Priam a thing to boast of”, which Aristotle quotes for the topic that what has cost much toil is a good, are spoken of her (1363a5–6; see [[a-thing-to-boast-of-for-priam]]).

**Book II.** Isocrates wrote that she was good, if Theseus chose her (1399a1–2); Alexander took her justly, since her father gave her the choice, though only the first time (1401b35–1402a1); Hecuba answers her in the *Trojan Women* with the pun on Aphrodite (1400b23–24; see [[aphrodite-and-folly]]).

**Book III.** The proem of Isocrates' *Helen* is about the eristics, though “there is nothing in common between the eristics and Helen” (1414b27–28): the speaker of an epideictic proem says what he likes and then strikes the keynote (see [[isocrates]] and [[prooimion]]).
