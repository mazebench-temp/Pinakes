“Next we should run briefly over the so-called non-artistic proofs, which follow on what has been said; for these belong to judicial speeches. They are five in number: laws, witnesses, contracts, tortures, oaths” (1375a22–25).

**Laws.** If the written law is against one's case, appeal to the common law and to equity, to the jurors' oath, to Antigone's unwritten laws; if it favors one's case, argue that the oath does not license judging against the law and that trying to be wiser than the laws is what good laws forbid (1375a25–b25; see [[nomos]], [[epieikes]], [[to-the-best-of-my-judgment]] and [[not-of-today-or-yesterday]]).

**Witnesses.** Ancient witnesses are poets and famous men whose judgments are on record, interpreters of oracles and proverbs; recent witnesses are known men who have judged a question; and some share the risk of the trial (1375b26–1376a17; see [[solon-and-salamis]], [[tell-red-haired-critias]], [[the-wooden-wall]], [[never-do-good-to-an-old-man]] and [[a-fool-who-kills-the-father]]). A speaker without witnesses argues from probabilities, which cannot be bribed (1376a17–23; see [[eikos]]).

**Contracts.** A favorable contract is “a private and particular law” (1376b7), and the law itself is a kind of contract; an unfavorable one can be fought like a bad law, since contracts are made even by people deceived or forced (1376a33–b30).

**Tortures.** If favorable, they are the only true testimony; if not, “people under compulsion tell lies no less than the truth” (1377a2–3), and the hardy hold out while the timid break (1376b31–1377a7).

**Oaths.** A man may tender or accept an oath, or both, or neither, and each choice has its reasons; Xenophanes' saying fits the refusal to accept (1377a8–b12; see [[not-a-fair-challenge]] and [[oath]]).
