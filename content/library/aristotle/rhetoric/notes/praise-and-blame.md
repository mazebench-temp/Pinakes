“Epideictic is either praise or blame” (1358b12–13), and its end is the noble and the shameful (see [[three-kinds-of-rhetoric]] and [[kalon]]). “Praise is speech that sets forth greatness of virtue” (1367b28), while encomium is concerned with deeds, and “praise and advice have a common form; for what you would suggest in giving advice becomes encomium when the wording is changed” (1367b36–38). Praise must suit its audience (see [[praising-athenians-among-athenians]]), may call a quality by its better neighbor, and is best served by amplification (1368a26–27; see [[auxesis]]). “Blame comes from the opposites” (1368a37).

**Book III.** Epideictic proems are drawn from praise or blame (1414b30–35; see [[prooimion]]); the hearer must think himself praised with the subject (1415b28–32; see [[praising-athenians-among-athenians]]); epideictic narration goes through the deeds piecemeal (1416b16–29; see [[diegesis]]); its amplification shows deeds noble and beneficial (1417b31–32); and episodes of praise vary the speech, as Isocrates and Gorgias do (1418a33–37; see [[isocrates]] and [[gorgias]]). The epilogue praises and blames as well (1419b15).
