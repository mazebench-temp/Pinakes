---
summary: Against the written law, the oath means not to follow the letter absolutely; for it, the oath is no license to judge against the law; without witnesses, it means judging from probabilities.
heading: In the Rhetoric
grc: ["γνώμῃ τῇ ἀρίστῃ"]
en: [to the best of my judgment]
---

If the written law is against one's case, one should say “that the words ‘to the best of my judgment’ mean this: not to follow the written laws absolutely” (1375a29–31). If it favors one's case, “that ‘to the best of my judgment’ is not there so that the judge may decide against the law, but so that he may not perjure himself if he does not know what the law says” (1375b16–18). And a speaker without witnesses can say “that the judges must decide from probabilities, and that this is what ‘to the best of my judgment’ means” (1376a18–19; see [[non-artistic-proofs]] and [[eikos]]).

**Book II.** The judge must decide not only from what is necessary but also from what is probable, “for this is judging to the best of one's judgment” (1402b31–33; see [[enstasis]]).
