# παράδειγμα at 1414b27, 1417a13 and 1417a29 is an instance, not the rhetorical example.
---

“For the example is an induction, the enthymeme a syllogism” (1356b2–3), and “to show on the basis of many similar cases that something is so is there induction and here example” (1356b14–15). Speeches that proceed by examples “are no less persuasive” than those full of enthymemes (1356b22–23). The example stands “as part to part, like to like: when both fall under the same class, but one is better known than the other, there is an example” (1357b28–30), and Aristotle's instance is Dionysius' request for a guard, measured against Pisistratus' and Theagenes' (1357b30–36; see [[dionysius-of-syracuse]] and [[pisistratus]]). Examples suit deliberative speeches, “for we judge the future by divining it from the past” (1368a29–31; see [[three-kinds-of-rhetoric]]).

**Book II.** Chapter 20: example is like induction, and of two kinds, the telling of past events and making them up oneself; the made-up kind is comparison, the Socratic sort, or fable, Aesopic or Libyan (1393a26–31; see [[darius]], [[the-horse-and-the-stag]] and [[the-fox-and-the-hedgehog]]). Fables are easier to provide, past events more useful for deliberation; examples serve as demonstrations when one has no enthymemes, and as testimony after them, where even one is enough (1394a2–16). Examples are solved like probabilities (1403a5–10; see [[enstasis]]).

**Book III.** “Examples are better suited to speaking to the assembly, enthymemes to judicial speech” (1418a1–2), for deliberation concerns the future, and examples must be drawn from the past (1418a3–5; see [[enthymema]] and [[three-kinds-of-rhetoric]]).
