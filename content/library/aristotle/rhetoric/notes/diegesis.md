**Book III.** Narration belongs to judicial speech (1414a37–b1; see [[taxis]]); in epideictic it is piecemeal, “for one must go through the actions on which the speech is based” (1416b17–18), and well-known actions, like Achilles', need only be recalled, while Critias' must be told (1416b26–29; see [[achilles]] and [[critias]]). It should be in due measure, not quick (1416b30–36); narrate along the way what shows your own virtue and the other's vice, as in Herodotus' Egyptian deserters (1417a3–7; see [[herodotus]]); tell past events as past, unless they move pity or indignation, as in the tale to Alcinous (1417a12–16; see [[alcinous]] and [[phayllus]]). It should show character, through choice, as the Socratic dialogues do (1417a16–b1; see [[ethos]] and [[socratic-dialogues]]), and draw on the emotions and their signs, as Aeschines' Cratylus and Homer's old woman who “covered her face with her hands” (1417b1–6; see [[aeschines-of-sphettus]] and [[odyssey]]). In speeches to the assembly narration is rarest (1417b12–20; see [[carcinus]] and [[haemon-son-of-creon]]).
