The book opens with the claim that “Rhetoric is the counterpart of dialectic” (1354a1): both deal with things that are in a way the common business of all to know and belong to no one defined science. Everyone examines and upholds arguments, accuses and defends; some do it at random, others from habit, and what can be done both ways can be studied, which is the work of an art (1354a1–11; see [[dialektike]]). The art is concerned with proofs, and the enthymeme is their body (1354a13–15; see [[pistis]] and [[enthymema]]).

Rhetoric is useful “because by nature the true and the just are stronger than their opposites” (1355a21–22), because some hearers cannot be taught from exact knowledge, because one must be able to argue both sides in order to refute the unjust argument, and because it would be absurd if it were shameful to be unable to defend oneself with the body but not with speech (1355a21–b2). That it can be misused is true of every good except virtue (1355b2–7). Its function “is not to persuade but to see the means of persuasion available in each case” (1355b10–11), as medicine does not make everyone healthy but brings each as far toward health as is possible. Its proofs are three, lying in the speaker's character, in the hearer's frame of mind, and in the speech itself, so that rhetoric is “a kind of offshoot of dialectic and of the study of characters, which it is right to call politics” (1356a25–27; see [[three-kinds-of-proof]]). It has three kinds, as there are three kinds of hearer (1358a36–b8; see [[three-kinds-of-rhetoric]]).
