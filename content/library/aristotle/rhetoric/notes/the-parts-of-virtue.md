“Virtue is, it seems, a capacity for providing and preserving goods, and a capacity for conferring many great benefits, indeed all kinds of benefits in all things” (1366a36–b1).

{{quote:487ade96-074f-542e-b5d4-8d6d50fc5da1}}

Each is then defined: justice gives each his own as the law prescribes; courage does noble deeds in dangers as the law commands; self-control governs the pleasures of the body as the law commands; generosity does good with money; greatness of soul produces great benefits; magnificence greatness in expenditure; practical wisdom is “the virtue of the intellect by which people are able to deliberate well about the goods and evils mentioned in relation to happiness” (1366b20–22). In chapter 5 the list of the parts of happiness includes virtue, and the words that list its parts are bracketed by Ross (1360b23–24). Ross's list here has no gentleness, which other texts add (see the translation notes).
