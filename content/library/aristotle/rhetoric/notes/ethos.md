The first of the three artistic proofs lies “in the character of the speaker” (1356a2), when the speech is so spoken as to make him worthy of belief, “for we believe decent people more fully and more readily on all matters in general” (1356a6–7). It must be made by the speech, not by a prior opinion, and “character is, one might almost say, the most authoritative proof” (1356a13; see [[three-kinds-of-proof]]). The study of characters is politics, and rhetoric is its offshoot (1356a25–27). Each constitution has a character, “for characters are revealed by choice” (1366a14–15; see [[politeia]] and [[prohairesis]]), and chapter 9 serves the speaker's own character as well as praise (1366a25–28).

**Book II.** The speaker is credible through practical wisdom, virtue and goodwill (1378a6–19). The characters of the hearers by age and fortune are set out in chapters 12–17 (see [[the-characters-of-the-ages]] and [[the-gifts-of-fortune]]), for everyone accepts speeches spoken in his own character (1390a25–27); the characters of the constitutions were treated in Book I (1391b19–21). Maxims make speeches show character, since they declare the speaker's choice in general terms (1395b12–17; see [[gnome]]).

**Book III.** Style shows character when it suits the speaker's class and state (1408a25–32; see [[prepon]]), and the style of debate is either that which shows character or the emotional (1413b9–10; see [[styles-of-writing-and-debate]]). The narration should show character, which comes from showing choice (1417a16–b10; see [[diegesis]] and [[prohairesis]]); without demonstrations one should speak so as to show character, “and it suits a decent man better to appear good than to make his speech precise” (1418a38–b1; see [[epieikes]]); and to avoid envy one should put words about oneself in another's mouth, as Isocrates, Archilochus and Sophocles do (1418b23–33; see [[isocrates]], [[archilochus]] and [[haemon-son-of-creon]]).
