summary: Signs stand as particular to universal or universal to particular; the necessary sign is a tekmērion, and the others can be refuted.
heading: In the Rhetoric
grc: ["σημει*"]
en: [sign, signs]
---

“Of signs, one kind stands as a particular to the universal, the other as a universal to the particular” (1357a35–b2). That Socrates was wise and just is a sign that the wise are just, but it can be refuted; that a man breathes fast is a sign of fever, but one can breathe hard without one; that a woman has milk is a necessary sign that she has given birth, and that alone is a tekmērion (1357b10–21; see [[tekmerion]] and [[socrates]]). “Deeds are signs of a man's state, since we would praise even one who had not acted, if we believed him to be such” (1367b33–34; see [[kalon]]).

**Book II.** An argument from a sign is not a valid syllogism: “Lovers are useful to cities; for the love of Harmodius and Aristogeiton overthrew the tyrant Hipparchus” (1401b9–14; see [[apparent-enthymemes]]). Signs can be solved even when true (1403a2–5; see [[enstasis]]).
