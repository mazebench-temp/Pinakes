The Corinthians took offense at Simonides' line that Ilium does not blame them (1363a15–16; see [[ilium-does-not-blame-the-corinthians]]), and his epitaph for Archedice praises a tyrant's daughter who was not arrogant (1367b20–21; see [[she-whose-father-was-a-tyrant]]). Aristotle does not name the poet of the epigram on the Olympic victor (1365a25–27, 1367b19; see [[once-with-a-rough-yoke]]).

**Book II.** His answer to Hieron's wife, that it was better to be rich (1391a8–12; see [[rich-or-wise]]).

**Book III.** Epithets can be drawn from the worse or the better side: “Simonides, when the victor with the mule team offered him a small fee, would not write the poem, as if disgusted at writing about half-asses; but when he was given enough, he wrote, ‘Hail, daughters of storm-footed mares!’ And yet they were daughters of asses too” (1405b23–28; fragment 515 in Page's *Poetae Melici Graeci*; see [[epitheton]]). The paeonic line “After earth and waters, night made the ocean vanish” (1409a17) is unattributed in Aristotle; the digitized edition refers it to Simonides (see [[paean]]).
