**Book II.** The characters “according to the emotions, the states, the ages and the fortunes” come after the emotions (1388b31–1389a2). The young (1389a2–b12) are given to appetite and to sex, changeable, “passionate and quick-tempered”, fond of honor and more of victory, not fond of money, as Pittacus said (see [[pittacus]]), simple-natured, trusting and hopeful, for “on the first day one can remember nothing, but can hope for everything” (1389a23–24); prone to shame, great-souled, choosing the noble over the advantageous, fond of their friends, doing everything too much, “contrary to Chilon's maxim” (1389b3; see [[nothing-too-much]]); fond of laughter and of wit, which is “educated outrage” (1389b11–12). The old (1389b13–1390a27) have the opposite characters; they love and hate as Bias' precept advises (1389b23–25; see [[love-as-though-you-will-hate]]), are small-souled, ungenerous, cowardly, shameless rather than prone to shame, despondent, garrulous, and live by memory and calculation. Those in the prime (1390a28–b13) have the mean of both.
