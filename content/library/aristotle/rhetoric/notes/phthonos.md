**Book II.** In the chapter on indignation Aristotle distinguishes envy from it: “envy too is a disturbing pain, and at good fortune, but not of the undeserving but of one's equal and like” (1386b16–20). The definition: “if envy is a kind of pain at apparent good fortune in the goods mentioned, toward those like oneself, not in order that one may get something oneself, but because of them” (1387b22–25). The envious are those who have equals, those who lack little of everything, those honored for wisdom or happiness, those fond of honor and the small-souled (1387b25–35). People envy what brings reputation and honor, and those near them in time, place, age and reputation (1387b35–1388a8; see [[kinship-knows-how-to-envy]]); rivals, above all, as in “potter against potter” (1388a14–17; see [[potter-against-potter]]). The envious man is also the one who rejoices in others' misfortunes (1386b34–1387a3). Envy is bad and belongs to the bad, as emulation is decent and belongs to the decent (1388a35–37; see [[zelos]]). In the chapter on maxims Medea's lines on the envy that cleverness earns show a maxim with its reason (1394a29–35; see [[children-taught-too-wise]]).
