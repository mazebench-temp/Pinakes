**Book III.** A word is more proper to a thing when it sets the thing before the eyes (1405b11–12). Urbane sayings come from metaphor, antithesis and being set before the eyes, “for one must see things being done rather than things about to be done” (1410b34–35); chapter 10 collects metaphors that do this: Aesion's city poured out into Sicily, “so that Greece cried out”, Iphicrates' road of his words, Lycoleon's suppliant statue (1411a25–b10; see [[aesion]], [[iphicrates]] and [[lycoleon]]). Chapter 11 explains: “I say that those words set things before the eyes which signify things in activity” (1411b24–25). Calling a good man “four-square” is a metaphor without activity, while Isocrates' “of one whose prime is in flower” and Euripides' Greeks “darting forth” have it (1411b26–31; see [[isocrates]] and [[euripides]]); and Homer makes lifeless things alive (1411b31–1412a10; see [[homers-lifeless-things-alive]]). The funeral speech's Greece cutting her hair at the tomb is another example (1411a31–b1; see [[salamis]]).
