“For slander and pity and anger and such emotions of the soul are not about the subject but are aimed at the juror” (1354a16–18), and “one ought not to warp the juror by leading him into anger or envy or pity” (1354a24–25; see [[the-handbooks]] and [[pathos]]). A wrong is greater if it is one “that hearers fear more than they pity” (1375a7–8; see [[phobos]]). Pity is defined in the second book (2.8).

**Book II.** Chapter 8: “Let pity be a kind of pain at an apparent evil, destructive or painful, befalling one who does not deserve it, which one might expect oneself to suffer, or one of one's own, and this when it appears near” (1385b13–16). Those who think themselves utterly ruined or supremely happy do not pity, nor those in an emotion of courage, nor the panic-stricken (1385b19–1386a3). What is pitiable is the destructive and painful and the evils of fortune (1386a4–16; see [[diopeithes]]); people pity those they know if they are not too close, for the terrible drives out pity (1386a17–24; see [[psammenitus]]), and those like them, and what appears near, so that delivery that puts sufferings before the eyes is more pitiable (1386a24–1386b8). Indignation is opposed to pity (1386b8–15; see [[nemesis]] and [[aristotle-nemesis]]). The young pity from love of mankind, the old from weakness (1389b8–10, 1390a18–21).
