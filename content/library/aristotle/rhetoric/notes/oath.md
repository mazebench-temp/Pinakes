A wrong is greater if a man has broken “oaths, pledges, assurances, marriage rights” (1375a10; see [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]). The jurors' oath, “to the best of my judgment”, is an argument on both sides (see [[to-the-best-of-my-judgment]]). The chapter on oaths ends Book I: “Oaths can be divided in four ways: a man either both tenders and accepts an oath, or neither, or one but not the other” (1377a8–9), and for each case Aristotle gives the arguments, with Xenophanes' saying that an oath tendered by the impious to the pious is no fair challenge (1377a8–b12; see [[not-a-fair-challenge]] and [[non-artistic-proofs]]). Perjury lies in the mind, not in the mouth, for doing wrong is voluntary (1377b3–7).
