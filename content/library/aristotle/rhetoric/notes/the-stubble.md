**Book III.** Chapter 10 asks where urbane and well-liked sayings come from, and begins from learning: “Learning easily is naturally pleasant to all, and words signify something, so that the words that give us learning are the most pleasant. Rare words are unknown to us, and standard words we know already; it is metaphor that does this most. When the poet calls old age ‘stubble’, he gives us learning and knowledge through the genus, for both have lost their bloom” (1410b10–15; see [[asteion]], [[glotta]], [[kyrion]] and [[metaphora]]). The example is Homer's, though the poet is not named and the verse is not quoted: the disguised Odysseus' “looking at the stubble, I think you can / tell what it was” (*Odyssey* 14.214–215). Aristotle reads καλάμη as old age itself, and the likeness lies in what the two share, ἀπηνθηκότα, “past their bloom”. The simile, he goes on, does the same but pleases less, since it is longer and does not say that this is that (1410b15–20; see [[eikon]]).
