See [[not-of-today-or-yesterday]] (1373b9–13, 1375a33–b2), [[antigone]] and [[antigone-play]]. The Sophocles who pleaded for Euctemon is taken to be another man (see [[sophocles-the-orator]]).

**Book II.** “Clearly of iron, and bearing the name”, from the *Tyro* (1400b17–18; see [[plainly-of-iron]]).

**Book III.** Book III names Sophocles seven times. The line Aristotle calls one of “the iambics of Sophocles”, “This land is Calydon, of Pelops' country” (1409b9–10), is in fact the opening of Euripides' *Meleager* (see [[calydon]]). “My father was Polybus” (1415a21; *Oedipus the King* 774) shows how tragedy makes clear what the play is about (see [[oedipus-the-king]] and [[prooimion]]); the reason Antigone gives for caring more for her brother than for a husband or children supplies a cause for what is incredible (1417a29–33; see [[antigone-play]]); and Haemon both keeps promising (1417b20) and speaks for Antigone to his father as if others were saying it (1418b31–32; see [[haemon-son-of-creon]]). Two sayings of a Sophocles are probably the poet's: he said that he trembled “not, as his slanderer alleged, so as to seem old, but from necessity” (1416a15–16), since he was eighty; and, as one of the commissioners of 411 BCE, asked by Pisander whether he had approved the Four Hundred, he answered “for there was nothing better” (1419a25–30; see [[pisander-the-oligarch]] and [[erotesis]]). The *Teucer*, in which Odysseus and Teucer argue from Teucer's kinship with Priam (1416b1–3), is taken to be Sophocles' lost play (see [[hesione]] and [[teucer]]).
