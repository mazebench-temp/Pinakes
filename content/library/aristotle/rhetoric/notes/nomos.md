Well-framed laws should define as much as possible and leave as little as possible to the judges, for “the lawgiver's judgment is not about particulars but about what is future and universal” (1354b4–6). Doing wrong is defined against the law (1368b6–7), and in chapter 13 the law is divided:

{{quote:ee5aec08-1005-563f-b140-753dccbdd1b8}}

The witnesses to the common law are Sophocles' Antigone, Empedocles and Alcidamas (see [[not-of-today-or-yesterday]], [[the-law-of-all]] and [[god-has-left-all-men-free]]). Unwritten justice also covers what the written law leaves out, which is equity (see [[epieikes]]). In chapter 15 laws are the first of the non-artistic proofs, with the arguments for and against the written law (1375a25–b25; see [[non-artistic-proofs]] and [[to-the-best-of-my-judgment]]).
