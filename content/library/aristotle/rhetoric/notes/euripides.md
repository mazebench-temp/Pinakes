From the *Andromeda*, “It is sweet to remember toils when one is safe” (1370b4); from the *Orestes*, “Change in all things is sweet” (1371a28); and from the *Antiope*, “giving the greatest part of each day to where he happens to be at his best” (1371b33–34). See [[sweet-to-remember-toils]], [[change-in-all-things-is-sweet]] and [[where-he-is-at-his-best]].

**Book II.** Euripides is named twice: for his answer to the Syracusans (1384b15–16; see [[syracusans]]) and for Hecuba's pun on Aphrodite in the *Trojan Women* (1400b23–24; see [[aphrodite-and-folly]]). His lines are quoted unnamed for maxims: Medea on educating children (1394a29–35; see [[children-taught-too-wise]]), the opening of the *Stheneboea* (1394b1–2; see [[no-man-is-happy-in-all-things]]), Hecuba's “There is no man who is free” (1394b3–6; see [[no-man-is-free]]) and “No one is a lover who does not love always” (1394b16; see [[no-lover-who-does-not-love-always]]); for the uneducated who speak more musically before a crowd (1395b28–30; see [[the-uneducated-before-the-crowd]]); and for unbelievable truths (1397a17–19; see [[unbelievable-truths]]).

**Book III.** Euripides “was the first to show the way” in composing from the words of ordinary conversation (1404b24–26; see [[lexis]]). Telephus' “ruling over the oar, and landing in Mysia” (1405a28–29; *Telephus*, fragment 705 Nauck) is too grand for the subject (see [[prepon]]); the opening of the *Meleager*, “This land is Calydon, of Pelops' country”, is cited as Sophocles' (1409b9–10; see [[calydon]]); “Then the Greeks, darting forth on their feet” (1411b30) is an example of activity (see [[before-the-eyes]]); in our text of *Iphigenia at Aulis* (80) they dart forth “with the spear”, δορί, not ποσίν. Euripides makes clear what the play is about in the prologue (1415a19–20). Accused of impiety by Hygiaenon for the line “My tongue has sworn, but my mind is unsworn” (*Hippolytus* 612), he answered that the judgments of the Dionysiac contest should not be brought into the courts (1416a28–35; see [[hygiaenon]] and [[diabole]]). And Hecuba, in the *Trojan Women*, begins her answer to Helen with its silliest point, “First I will be an ally of the goddesses” (1418b20; *Trojan Women* 969), an example of refutation (see [[pistis]]).
