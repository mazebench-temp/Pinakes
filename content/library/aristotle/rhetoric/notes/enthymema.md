The handbooks say nothing “about enthymemes, which are the body of proof” (1354a14–15). The rhetorical demonstration is the enthymeme, “and the enthymeme is a kind of syllogism” (1355a6–8), so whoever understands syllogisms will be most skilled in enthymemes, once he learns what they deal with and how they differ from logical syllogisms (1355a8–14). “I call an enthymeme a rhetorical syllogism, and an example a rhetorical induction” (1356b4–5; see [[syllogismos]] and [[paradeigma]]). Speeches full of enthymemes “are cheered more loudly” (1356b23–24).

The enthymeme deals with what can be otherwise and is addressed to hearers who cannot take in many steps, so it uses few premises and leaves out what the hearer will supply: there is no need to say that the Olympic prize is a crown (1357a1–21; see [[dorieus]]). Its premises are probabilities and signs (1357a30–33; see [[eikos]], [[semeion]] and [[tekmerion]]). Its premises come from the special topics of each subject more than from the common topics (1358a1–35; see [[topos]]). Enthymemes suit judicial speeches best, “for what has happened most admits of a cause and demonstration” (1368a31–33).

**Book II.** How to find enthymemes: not from far back nor taking in every step, which is why the uneducated persuade crowds better than the educated (1395b22–31; see [[the-uneducated-before-the-crowd]]); from the facts that belong to the subject, as praise of Achilles must take what belongs to him alone (1396a4–1396b19; see [[cycnus]]). Enthymemes are demonstrative or refutative (1396b22–27); their general topics are listed in chapter 23 (see [[topics-of-enthymemes]]), the apparent ones in chapter 24 (see [[apparent-enthymemes]]), and their solution in chapter 25 (see [[enstasis]]). The maxim is part of an enthymeme (1394a26–35; see [[gnome]]).

**Book III.** Enthymemes are urbane when they give quick learning (1410b20–21, 1410b28; see [[asteion]]). In chapter 17 they suit judicial speech, as examples suit the assembly (1418a1–5); they should not be stated in a row but mixed in, and limited to “all that a wise man would” (1418a6–9; *Odyssey* 4.204); they are not for passages of emotion or character, “for demonstration has neither character nor choice” (1418a12–17; see [[ethos]]). The refutative are better liked than the demonstrative (1418b2–4), and an enthymeme can be turned into a maxim (1418b33–39; see [[gnome]]).
