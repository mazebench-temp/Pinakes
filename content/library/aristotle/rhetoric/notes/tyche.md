“Good fortune is the coming and the presence of the goods of which fortune is the cause” (1361b39–1362a1), and its gifts defy reckoning: the one handsome brother, the treasure found, the arrow that hit the man beside one (1362a7–12). Among the causes of action, “things that happen by chance are those whose cause is indefinite and that happen for no purpose” (1369a32–33; see [[seven-causes-of-action]]). One should pride oneself “not on what came to him by fortune but on what he owed to himself” (1368a2–8), and misfortunes, unlike errors and wrongs, “are acts that are unexpected and do not come from depravity” (1374b6–7; see [[epieikes]]).

**Book II.** Among the pitiable evils are those of which fortune is the cause (1386a6–12; see [[pity]]); good fortune makes men more arrogant and thoughtless, but also god-loving (1391a30–1391b4; see [[the-gifts-of-fortune]]); in a tragedy the deity gives good fortune so that disasters may be more conspicuous (1399b22–25; see [[the-deity-gives-great-fortune]]).
