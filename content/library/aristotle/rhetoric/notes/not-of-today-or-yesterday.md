---
summary: Antigone speaks for the common law of nature, which never changes; a speaker whose case is against the written law may appeal to it.
heading: In the Rhetoric
grc: ["οὐ γάρ τι νῦν γε κἀχθές"]
en: [For this is not of today or yesterday]
---

In chapter 13 Antigone is the first witness to the common law: “as Sophocles' Antigone clearly says that it is just, though forbidden, to bury Polynices, as being just by nature” (1373b9–11; see [[nomos]]). In chapter 15 the same lines become an argument: if the written law is against one's case, one should say “that equity always remains and never changes, nor does the common law (for it is according to nature), but the written laws often do; hence the words in Sophocles' Antigone” (1375a31–34; see [[epieikes]] and [[non-artistic-proofs]]).
