**Book III.** The epilogue is not a necessary part of a speech, but at most a speech has four parts, of which it is the last (1414b1–12; see [[taxis]]); the accuser slanders in the epilogue (1415a31–34; see [[diabole]]). Chapter 19 gives its four parts (1419b10–13), the topics of which have been given before (1419b18–27; see [[auxesis]] and [[pathos]]): “what remains is to recall what has been said” (1419b27–28), here and not in the proems (1419b28–29; see [[prooimion]]). The comparison may be direct, “He said this about it, and I said that, and for these reasons” (1419b36–1420a1), ironical, “For he said this, and I that” (1420a1–2; see [[geloion]]), or by questions, “What, then, has been proved?” (1420a3; see [[erotesis]]); and the end is asyndetic (1420a6–8; see [[asyndeton]]).
