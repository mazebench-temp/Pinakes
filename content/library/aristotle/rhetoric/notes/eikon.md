**Book III.** Chapter 4 defines the simile as a metaphor (1406b20–26) and gives examples from orators: Androtion's Idrieus like a puppy let off the chain, Theodamas' Archidamus, the similes of Plato's *Republic*, Pericles' Samians and Boeotians, Demosthenes' seasick passengers, Democrates' nurses and Antisthenes' frankincense (1406b27–1407a11; see [[androtion]], [[theodamas]], [[plato]], [[pericles]], [[demosthenes]], [[democrates]] and [[antisthenes]]). All of them “can be spoken either as similes or as metaphors, so that it is clear that those which are well liked when spoken as metaphors will also be similes, and that similes are metaphors that need a word more” (1407a12–15). In chapter 10 the simile is less pleasant than the metaphor, since it is longer (1410b17–20), and in chapter 11 the well-liked similes are those made like metaphors by analogy: the shield as the cup of Ares, the bow as a stringless lyre, Niceratus as a Philoctetes bitten by Pratys, and the comic poets' “Like Philammon sparring with the punching bag” (1412b34–1413a16; see [[the-cup-of-ares]], [[niceratus]] and [[philammon]]).
