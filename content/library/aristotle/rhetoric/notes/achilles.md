---
summary: Praise counts it to Achilles' credit that he came to the aid of Patroclus knowing that he must die; Homer chose him; his words on anger and his grief for Patroclus are quoted.
heading: In the Rhetoric
---

The ends of the three kinds of speech differ, and praise looks to the noble even against the advantageous: “so they praise Achilles because he came to the aid of his companion Patroclus knowing that he must die, when he could have lived. For him such a death was nobler, but to live was advantageous” (1359a3–5; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]] and [[three-kinds-of-rhetoric]]). Among the choices of the wise, “Homer chose Achilles” (1363a19). In chapter 11 his words on anger, “far sweeter than dripping honey”, and the longing for lament he roused in the Myrmidons show what is pleasant (1370b11–12, 1370b28–29; see [[sweeter-than-dripping-honey]] and [[the-longing-for-lament]]).

**Book II.** His anger at being dishonored (1378b32–35; see [[aristotle-anger]]) and Apollo's words on his outrage to Hector's body (1380b28–30; see [[the-senseless-earth]]). Praise must take what belongs to him alone, killing Hector and Cycnus and going to Troy youngest and unsworn, not what he shares with others (1396a25–1396b18; see [[cycnus]] and [[hemitheos]]). If Hector did no wrong in killing Patroclus, neither did Alexander in killing Achilles (1397b24–25); his wrath at Tenedos for not being invited is a fallacy of the accidental (1401b17–19; see [[tenedos]]).

**Book III.** Homer calls Achilles a lion: “he rushed on like a lion” is a simile, “a lion, he rushed on” a metaphor (1406b20–24; see [[eikon]]). His angry refusal, “not if he gave me as much as the sand and the dust”, is a hyperbole (1413a31–34; see [[the-sand-and-the-dust]]). An epideictic speech that praises him needs no narration, “for everyone knows his deeds” (1416b27–28; see [[diegesis]]); and Gorgias, praising him, praised Peleus, then Aeacus, then the god (1418a36–37; see [[gorgias]]).
