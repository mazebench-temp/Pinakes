# In Book I ἀντικειμένη and ἀντικειμένως are logical opposites.
except: [1364b3, 1368a8]
---

**Book III.** The style in clauses is divided or opposed (1409b33–1410a1), and the examples of the opposed are from Isocrates' *Panegyricus* (1410a1–17; see [[isocrates]]) and a speech against Peitholaus and Lycophron: “These men sold you when they were at home, and now that they have come to you they have bought you” (1410a17–19; see [[peitholaus]]). “This, then, is antithesis. Parisōsis is when the clauses are equal; paromoiōsis is when each clause has its extremities alike” (1410a24–26), as in “they could be won with gifts and swayed by words” (1410a30–31, from the Iliad; see [[homer]]). Epicharmus' antitheses are false (1410b4–5; see [[epicharmus]]). Antithesis makes sayings urbane (1410b27–31; see [[asteion]]), and the funeral speech's “freedom with their virtue” “has a kind of antithesis” (1411b1; see [[salamis]]).
