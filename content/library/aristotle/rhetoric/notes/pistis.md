The compilers of handbooks have provided no part of the art, “for proofs are the only thing within the art, and the rest is accessory” (1354a13–14). The artistic method is concerned with proofs, “and proof is a kind of demonstration (for we are most convinced when we suppose a thing to have been demonstrated)” (1355a4–6).

{{quote:f5949f27-8a88-5dce-be9a-b1fef511d7ae}}

The three artistic proofs are the subject of [[three-kinds-of-proof]]; the non-artistic ones, laws, witnesses, contracts, tortures and oaths, of chapter 15 (see [[non-artistic-proofs]]).

**Book II.** Since rhetoric exists for the sake of judgment, the speaker must not only make the argument demonstrative and credible but present himself as a certain kind of person and prepare the judge (1377b20–24; see [[ethos]] and [[pathos]]). The proofs common to all speeches are example and enthymeme (1393a23–26; see [[paradeigma]] and [[enthymema]]).

**Book III.** Proof is the first of Book III's three subjects, already treated (1403b6–13), and with the statement it is one of the two necessary parts of a speech: “Of these, the one is the statement and the other the proof” (1414a35–36; see [[taxis]]). Chapter 17 treats proof in the speech: “The proofs should be demonstrative” (1417b21), aimed at the point in dispute; examples suit the assembly and enthymemes the courts (1418a1–5; see [[paradeigma]] and [[enthymema]]); the reply to the opponent is part of the proofs (1418b5–6); the first speaker gives his proofs first, the second refutes first, as Hecuba begins with the goddesses (1418b7–22; see [[callistratus]] and [[euripides]]).
