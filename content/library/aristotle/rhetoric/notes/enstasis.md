**Book II.** “One can solve an argument either by a counter-syllogism or by bringing an objection” (1402a31). Counter-syllogisms come from the same topics, since many reputable opinions are contrary to one another; objections are brought, as in the *Topics*, in four ways: from the thing itself, from the like, from the opposite, and from previous judgments (1402a31–1402b12; see [[caunian-love]] and [[pittacus]]). Since enthymemes come from probabilities, examples, tekmēria and signs, and probabilities hold only for the most part, they can always be met by an objection, though the solution is apparent and not always true; the judge must decide from probabilities too, “for this is judging to the best of one's judgment” (1402b13–1403a2; see [[eikos]] and [[to-the-best-of-my-judgment]]). Signs can be solved even when true, examples like probabilities, but tekmēria only by showing that what is stated is not so (1403a2–15; see [[semeion]] and [[tekmerion]]). Enthymemes that solve are not a separate kind, and an objection is not an enthymeme (1403a24–33).
