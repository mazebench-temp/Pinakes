---
summary: Iphicrates praised himself by the distance he had come, “from what beginnings”, and his “from what to what” is Aristotle's example of praise for success beyond expectation.
heading: In the Rhetoric
grc: [Ἰφικράτ*]
---

Among the things that make a deed great are the occasion, the age and the powers of the doer: “Iphicrates praised himself by saying from what beginnings he had come to this” (1365a27–28; see [[megethos]]). In chapter 9 the same boast, in its proverbial form, shows that praise looks to what is fitting or better than expected: “Such is Iphicrates' saying, ‘From what to what’” (1367b17–18; see [[from-what-to-what]]).

**Book II.** His speech against Harmodius (1397b30–32, 1398a17–22; see [[iphicrates-against-harmodius]]); his question to Aristophon (1398a4–8; see [[iphicrates-and-aristophon]]); his answer when his tall son was compelled to perform a public service: if they consider tall boys men, they will vote that short men are boys (1399a35–39). In chapter 21 he is the example of a particular, “what sort of man Iphicrates is” (1394a22–23).

**Book III.** Iphicrates is the author of several of Book III's metaphors. He “called Callias a begging priest of the Mother and not a torchbearer” (1405a19–20; see [[callias-son-of-hipponicus]]); when the Athenians made a truce with Epidaurus and its coast, he said “that they had robbed themselves of their provisions for the war” (1411a11–13); and he said, “The road of my words runs through the midst of Chares' deeds” (1411b2–3), where “through the midst” sets things before the eyes (see [[metaphora]] and [[before-the-eyes]]). In answering slander, he admitted to Nausicrates “that he had done what the other alleged and had done harm, but had not done wrong” (1416a10–12; see [[nausicrates]] and [[diabole]]).
