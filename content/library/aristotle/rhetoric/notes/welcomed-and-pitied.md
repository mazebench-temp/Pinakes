**Book III.** Some proems are aimed at the hearer, to make him well disposed or angry, attentive or the opposite (1415a34–38; see [[prooimion]]). Aristotle closes his account of them with goodwill: “How one should make hearers well disposed has been said, and each of the other such things. And since it is well said, ‘Grant that I come to the Phaeacians as one welcomed and pitied,’ these are the two things to aim at” (1415b25–28). The two words of the prayer, φίλον and ἐλεεινόν, name two of the emotions of Book II, friendliness (2.4; see [[philos]]) and pity (2.8; see [[pity]]), and the verse makes them the aim of the opening: the speaker is to come before his hearers as Odysseus hoped to come before the Phaeacians, a stranger to be liked and pitied. The verse is Odysseus' prayer to Athena in her grove at the end of the sixth book of the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 6.327); Priam prays to Zeus in the same words to come to Achilles (*Iliad* 24.309; see [[odysseus]] and [[phaeacians]]).
