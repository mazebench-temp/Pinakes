**Book III.** The epideictic proem draws on praise or blame, as Gorgias' *Olympic Speech* and Isocrates' blame of the founders of the festivals (1414b30–35; see [[gorgias]] and [[isocrates]]), on advice, as to honor Aristides or Alexander son of Priam (1414b36–1415a1; see [[aristides]] and [[paris]]), or on appeals to the hearer, as Choerilus (1415a2–4; see [[choerilus-of-samos]]). The dithyramb's proem is like the epideictic, “For your sake, and your gifts or spoils” (1415a10–11); epics and plays give a sample of the subject, as the openings of the Iliad and the Odyssey, “Lead me to another tale”, and Sophocles' “My father was Polybus” (1415a12–21; see [[proem]], [[asia]] and [[oedipus-the-king]]). Calls for attention belong anywhere but the beginning: “And attend to me, for this is no more my business than yours” (1415b12–13), or Prodicus' bit of the fifty-drachma lecture (1415b15–17; see [[prodicus]]). Proems may slander or dispel fears, as the guard's “My lord, I will not say that I come in haste” and Thoas' “Why this preamble?” (1415b19–21; see [[antigone-play]] and [[iphigenia-among-the-taurians]]). The speaker should aim to be thought a friend and pitiable, as Odysseus prays among the Phaeacians (1415b26–28; see [[phaeacians]]); in epideictic, to make the hearer share the praise (1415b28–32; see [[praising-athenians-among-athenians]]). Speeches to the assembly need a proem least (1415b33–1416a3). The epilogue, not the proem, is the place for recapitulation (1419b28–29; see [[epilogos]]). Book I had already named the proem, there rendered “introduction”, among the parts on which the handbooks spend themselves, “what the introduction or the narration should contain” (1354b18; see [[the-handbooks]]).
