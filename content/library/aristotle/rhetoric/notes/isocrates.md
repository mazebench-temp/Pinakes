“And if you have not much to say about the man himself, compare him with others, as Isocrates used to do because of his unfamiliarity with pleading in court” (1368a19–21; see [[auxesis]]). Isocrates wrote speeches to be read and taught rather than pleading in court himself; his praises of Helen and Evagoras proceed by such comparisons.

**Book II.** His remark about Euthynus (1392b12–13; see [[euthynus]]); his *Helen*, on Theseus' choice and the goddesses' (1399a1–3; see [[helen]]); his *Evagoras*, on Conon's (1399a4–6; see [[conon-came-to-evagoras]]); and a defense in which the jurors judge not Isocrates but a pursuit (1399b10–11; see [[not-about-isocrates]]).

**Book III.** Isocrates is Book III's chief source of examples, above all from the *Panegyricus* (380 BCE). At its end he uses poetic words when he has the hearers in his hands, “fame and memory” and “who endured” (1408b15–17). Its opening, “I have often wondered at those who convened the festivals and established the athletic contests” (1409b34–36; *Panegyricus* 1), is the divided style, and nine of its sentences are Aristotle's examples of antithesis and parisōsis (1410a1–17; see [[antithesis]]); others give metaphors: those who “run together” at the festivals (1411a29–30), “practicing in every way to think small” (1411b11), and “For we do not end our wars, we postpone them” (1411b13–14; *Panegyricus* 172; see [[metaphora]]). From the *Philippus* (346 BCE) come an antithesis (1410b29–31) and two examples of activity, “of one whose prime is in flower” and “you, like a sacred beast roaming at will” (1411b28–29; see [[before-the-eyes]]).

He said that the empire was the beginning of the city's troubles (1412b6–7), a play on ἀρχή (see [[asteion]]). His *Helen* has a proem that has nothing to do with Helen (1414b26–28), and he blames the founders of the festivals for honoring the body and not good sense (1414b33–35; see [[prooimion]]). Even when giving advice he accuses, “the Lacedaemonians in the Panegyricus and Chares in the Symmachicus” (1418a31–32); he varies epideictic speeches with episodes of praise, “for he is always bringing someone in” (1418a34); and in the *Philippus* and the *Antidosis* he makes someone else speak, to avoid envy (1418b26–27; see [[ethos]]).
