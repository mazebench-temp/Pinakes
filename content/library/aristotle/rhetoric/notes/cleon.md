**Book II.** Anger is always directed at some individual, “for instance with Cleon but not with man in general” (1378a34–35); hatred, by contrast, can be felt toward a whole class, such as thieves (1382a4–7; see [[aristotle-anger]]). Aristotle takes the best-known name of the previous century as his example of a particular man.

**Book III.** Cleon's name comes twice: in Aristotle's example of connectives held too far apart, “(for Cleon came begging and demanding)” (1407a27), where it may be any Cleon; and as the answer that children shout before the herald has finished, “Whom does the freed slave choose as his patron?” “Cleon!” (1408b24–26), an example of the expectation that meter creates (see [[rhythmos]]).
