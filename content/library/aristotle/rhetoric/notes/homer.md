summary: Homer is the Rhetoric's poet: named as the judge who chose Achilles and as an ancient witness about Salamis, and quoted, mostly without his name, for topics of the good, the greater good and pleasure.
heading: In the Rhetoric
grc: ["Ὅμηρ*"]
en: [Homer]
---

Aristotle names Homer three times in Book I. Among the goods that the wise have preferred, “Homer chose Achilles” (1363a19; see [[achilles]]); Homer wrote of temper “which is far sweeter than dripping honey” (1370b11–12; see [[sweeter-than-dripping-honey]]); and the Athenians “used Homer as a witness about Salamis” (1375b29–30; see [[solon-and-salamis]]). Elsewhere he is “the poet” (1365a12, 1365a30) or not named at all, for the verses were known to every hearer.

The quotations serve the topics of deliberative speech and of pleasure. Nestor's “Surely Priam would rejoice” shows that what the enemy wants is bad for us (1362b35); Hera's “a thing to boast of” and Odysseus' “It is shameful to stay so long” that what has cost much toil is good (1363a5–6); the plea that moved Meleager that division makes a thing seem greater (1365a12–15); Phemius' “I am self-taught” that the natural is greater than the acquired (1365a30). In chapter 11 Eumaeus' words on remembered sorrows, Achilles on anger, the longing for lament, and “Like always to like” show what is pleasant (1370b5–6, 1370b11–12, 1370b28–29, 1371b16; see [[surely-priam-would-rejoice]], [[a-thing-to-boast-of-for-priam]], [[shameful-to-stay-long]], [[aristotle-cleopatras-plea]], [[i-am-self-taught]], [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]], [[the-longing-for-lament]] and [[like-to-like]]). Several of his quotations differ from our text (see [[aristotle-cleopatras-plea]] and [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]]).

**Book II.** Homer is quoted without his name for the sweetness of anger (1378b5–7), Achilles' dishonor (1378b32–34), the spirit of kings (1379a5–6), Odysseus naming himself to the Cyclops (1380b22–24), Apollo on Achilles' outrage to Hector's body (1380b28–30), Zeus's indignation at Hector (1387a33–35), and Hector's two maxims (1395a13–15); he is named in Alcidamas' list: the Chians honored him though he was not a citizen (1398b12–13; see [[all-men-honor-the-wise]]).

**Book III.** Book III quotes Homer more than any other poet, mostly for style. When the poet says of Achilles “he rushed on like a lion” it is a simile, when “a lion, he rushed on”, a metaphor (1406b20–24; see [[eikon]]); “they could be won with gifts and swayed by words” (1410a30–31; *Iliad* 9.526) is an example of paromoiōsis (see [[antithesis]]). Homer makes lifeless things alive through metaphor, and so sets them before the eyes (1411b31–1412a10; see [[homers-lifeless-things-alive]]); Achilles' refusal is a hyperbole (1413a31–34; see [[the-sand-and-the-dust]]); the triple name of Nireus amplifies a man named only once (1414a2–7; see [[aristotle-nireus]]). The openings of both poems show what a proem does (1415a16–17; see [[proem]]); Odysseus' prayer to come to the Phaeacians names the aims of a proem (1415b26–28; see [[prooimion]]); the old woman covering her face is an emotional detail for narration (1417b3–6; see [[diegesis]]); and “as much as a sensible man would” limits the number of enthymemes (1418a8; see [[enthymema]]).
