**Book III.** “Since these matters have been settled, we must say where urbane and well-liked sayings come from” (1410b6–7). The gift belongs to the talented or the trained, but the method can show what they are (1410b7–9): they give quick learning, through metaphor (1410b10–20), antithesis (1410b27–31; see [[antithesis]]) and activity (1410b33–36; see [[before-the-eyes]]).

Chapter 11 adds the surprise. The soul seems to say, “How true! And I was wrong” (1412a21–22); so with Stesichorus' cicadas, with riddles and with Theodorus' “novel things” (1412a22–27; see [[stesichorus]] and [[theodorus-of-byzantium]]); with verses that end unexpectedly, as when one expected “sandals” and heard “chilblains” (1412a29–32); with jokes from changing a letter, like the jest at Nicon (1412a33–b2; see [[nicon]]); and with words used in two senses: the empire that was not the beginning of troubles (1412b5–11; see [[isocrates]]), “Anaschetus is not to be endured” (1412b12–14), the stranger who should not always be a stranger (1412b14–17), and Anaxandrides' “It is noble to die before doing anything worthy of death” (1412b17–21; see [[anaxandrides]]). The more briefly and antithetically a thing is said, the better it is liked (1412b21–25), and the more qualities a saying has, the more urbane it seems (1412b31–33).
