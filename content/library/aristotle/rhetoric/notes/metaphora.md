**Book III.** “Clarity, pleasantness and the unfamiliar belong to metaphor above all, and it cannot be borrowed from anyone else” (1405a8–10). It must fit: to adorn, take it from the better things of the genus, to blame, from the worse (1405a10–19; see [[iphicrates]] and [[callias-son-of-hipponicus]]); it must not be too grand, as Telephus' “ruling over the oar” (1405a28–30), nor ugly in sound, as Dionysius' “scream of Calliope” (1405a31–34; see [[dionysius-the-brazen]]); it should come from things akin and not obvious (1405a35–b5; see [[the-riddle-of-the-cupping-glass]]) and from beautiful things (1405b5–19). Metaphors that are laughable, tragic or far-fetched are frigid (1406b5–19; see [[psychron]]); the simile is a metaphor with a word more (1406b20–1407a18; see [[eikon]] and [[the-cup-of-ares]]). In chapters 10 and 11 metaphor is the main source of urbane sayings, for it gives quick learning (1410b10–20; see [[asteion]]): of its four kinds, those by analogy are the best liked (1411a1–b20), and the best of all set things before the eyes (1411b21–1412a10; see [[before-the-eyes]]). Proverbs and hyperboles are metaphors too (1413a14–b2; see [[carpathus]] and [[hyperbole]]). The kinds of metaphor have been given “in the books on poetics” (1405a3–6; see [[poetics]]).
