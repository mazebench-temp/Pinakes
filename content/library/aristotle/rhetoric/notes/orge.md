**Book II.** “Let anger be a longing, accompanied by pain, for revenge [that is apparent], because of an apparent slight against oneself or ⟨something⟩ of one's own, when the slight is not warranted” (1378a30–33). Since it is a response to a person, the angry man is angry with an individual, “for instance with Cleon but not with man in general” (1378a33–35; see [[cleon]]), and every anger is followed by the pleasure of the hope of revenge (1378b1–10; see [[sweeter-than-dripping-honey]]). The slight is of three kinds, contempt, spite and outrage (1378b14–15; see [[oligoria]] and [[hybris]]); Achilles' complaints show how the dishonored speak (1378b30–35; see [[aristotle-anger]]). The chapter goes on to the states in which people are irascible, when they are pained and their desire is thwarted (1379a10–30), the people with whom they are angry (1379a30–1379b38), and the things at which they are angry, and it ends with the speaker's task: to bring the hearers into the state of the angry and to show the opponents guilty of what angers them (1380a2–5).

The opposite emotion, calming, is treated in the next chapter (see [[praotes]]); hatred differs from anger in being toward classes as well as individuals, curable by time in the one case and not in the other, and in wishing the other's harm rather than his pain (1382a1–15; see [[philos]]). Anger makes men confident (1383b5–7), and the young are “passionate and quick-tempered and apt to follow their anger” (1389a9).
