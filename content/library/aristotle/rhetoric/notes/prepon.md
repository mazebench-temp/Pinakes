**Book III.** The excellence of style is to be clear and appropriate (1404b1–5). The appropriate governs every choice of word: the poetic style “is not appropriate to prose” (1404b5); metaphors must fit, or the inappropriateness shows (1405a10–14), as in Telephus' “ruling over the oar”, which “is inappropriate, because ‘ruling’ is grander than the subject deserves” (1405a28–30; see [[euripides]]); poetic epithets are inappropriate in prose (1406a12–13), and “those who speak poetically, by being inappropriate, produce the laughable and the frigid” (1406a32–33; see [[psychron]]).

Chapter 7 divides the appropriate into the emotional, what shows character, and the proportionate (1408a10–11). Weighty matters are not to be spoken of offhandedly, nor cheap ones with dignity, or it looks like comedy, as with Cleophon (1408a12–16; see [[cleophon]]). The emotional style speaks of outrage like an angry man, and of the pitiable humbly (1408a16–19; see [[pathos]]); the style that shows character suits the speaker's age, sex, people and state of life (1408a25–32; see [[ethos]] and [[thessalians]]). The speechwriters' “everyone knows” shames the hearer into agreeing (1408a32–36). Poetic words suit a man carried away by emotion (1408b10–20). At the end of the treatment of style, “the persuasive that comes from the appropriate” is part of what makes it pleasant (1414a28).
