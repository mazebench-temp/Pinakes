**Book III.** “Style must be either strung-on and made one by the connective, like the preludes in dithyrambs, or compact and like the antistrophes of the ancient poets” (1409a24–26). The strung-on style is the ancient one, as in Herodotus' opening (1409a27–29; see [[herodotus]]), and it is unpleasant because it is unlimited. “By a period I mean a stretch of speech that has a beginning and an end in itself and a magnitude easily taken in at a glance” (1409a35–37). The period must be complete in thought, not broken off like “This land is Calydon” (1409b8–12; see [[calydon]]); its clauses must be neither too short, which makes the hearer stumble, nor too long, which leaves him behind, as Democritus of Chios mocked Melanippides' long preludes (1409b13–32; see [[democritus-of-chios]]). The beginnings of periods are listed in the *Theodectea* (1410b2–3; see [[theodectes]]).
