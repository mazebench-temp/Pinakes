Enthymemes are drawn from probabilities and from signs, “for a probability is what happens for the most part, not simply, as some define it, but what, among things that can be otherwise, stands to that to which it is probable as the universal to the particular” (1357a34–b1; see [[enthymema]] and [[semeion]]). All three kinds of speech need premises from tekmēria, probabilities and signs (1359a7–8). In chapter 15 probabilities are set against witnesses: a speaker who has none can say “that probabilities cannot be bribed to deceive, and that probabilities are not convicted of false witness” (1376a19–21; see [[non-artistic-proofs]]).

**Book II.** What is probable only in a respect makes apparent enthymemes: Agathon's paradox and the art of Corax (1402a8–28; see [[the-probable-improbable]] and [[the-art-of-corax]]). Arguments from probability can always be met with an objection, though the solution is only apparent; so the defendant has the advantage, and the judge must decide from probabilities too (1402b13–1403a2; see [[enstasis]]).
