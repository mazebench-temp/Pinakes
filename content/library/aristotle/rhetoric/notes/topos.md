“I say that dialectical and rhetorical syllogisms are those about which we speak of topics; and these are the ones common to questions of justice and of nature and of politics and of many things that differ in species, such as the topic of more and less” (1358a10–14). The special premises belong to each subject, and the better one selects them, “the more he will unawares produce a science other than dialectic and rhetoric” (1358a23–25). Most enthymemes are drawn from the special premises, so the book goes through them for each kind of speech: the goods for deliberation, virtue and the noble for praise, wrongdoing and pleasure for the courts. The common premises, the possible and the impossible, past and future fact, and greatness, serve all three (1359a7–29; see [[megethos]] and [[adynaton]]). The common topics themselves are listed in Book II.

**Book II.** “By element I mean the same thing as a topic of enthymeme” (1396b20–21; again 1403a17–18). The topics of enthymemes are listed in chapter 23 (see [[topics-of-enthymemes]]) and the common topics in chapters 18–19 (see [[koina]]); amplifying and minimizing are not a topic (1403a16–24; see [[auxesis]]).

**Book III.** The topics of enthymemes have been treated (1403b13–14). Chapter 15 gives topics for meeting slander: “one topic is the means by which one may dispel an unpleasant suspicion” (1416a4–5; see [[diabole]]); and the epilogue draws on topics already given, for showing men good and bad, for amplifying and depreciating, and for the emotions (1419b17–27; see [[epilogos]]).
