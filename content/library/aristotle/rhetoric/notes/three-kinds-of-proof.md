Character persuades only if it is made by the speech, “and not through a prior opinion of what sort of man the speaker is”; against the handbook writers, “character is, one might almost say, the most authoritative proof” (1356a9–13). The emotions are the one thing “the present writers on the art try to devote themselves” to (1356a16–17; see [[the-handbooks]]). So the orator must be able to reason, and to study characters, virtues and the emotions, and “rhetoric is a kind of offshoot of dialectic and of the study of characters, which it is right to call politics” (1356a20–27; see [[rhetorike]]). The account of virtue in chapter 9 serves the second proof as well as praise, for “by the same means we shall be able to make both ourselves and another worthy of belief in respect of virtue” (1366a26–28), and the characters of the constitutions persuade their citizens (1366a8–16; see [[politeia]]).

**Book II.** The second book begins with the proofs from character and emotion (1377b20–1378a28; see [[ethos]] and [[pathos]]).
