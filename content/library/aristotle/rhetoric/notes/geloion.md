“Likewise, since play and all relaxation are among pleasant things, and laughter too, laughable things must be pleasant too, whether people, words or deeds. The laughable has been treated separately in the books on poetics” (1371b35–1372a2; see [[poetics]] and [[the-lost-book-on-comedy]]).

**Book III.** Metaphors can be inappropriate because they are laughable, “for the comic poets use metaphors too” (1406b6–7), and poetic diction in prose produces “the laughable and the frigid” (1406a32–33; see [[psychron]]); the twisted words of laughable sayings deceive like jokes from a changed letter (1412a28–30; see [[asteion]]). The laughable has its use in debates: Gorgias said “one should destroy the opponents' seriousness with laughter and their laughter with seriousness” (1419b4–5; see [[gorgias]]), and “it has been said in the books on poetics how many kinds of the laughable there are” (1419b6; see [[the-lost-book-on-comedy]]). Of these, some suit a free man: “Irony is more befitting a free man than buffoonery; for the ironical man produces the laughable for his own sake, the buffoon for another's” (1419b8–9). Irony also serves the comparison in the epilogue (1420a1–2; see [[epilogos]]), and poetic words may be used with irony, as Gorgias did (1408b20).
