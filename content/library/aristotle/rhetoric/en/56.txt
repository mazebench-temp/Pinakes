1416a4	As for slander, one topic is the means by which one may dispel
1416a5	an unpleasant suspicion (for it makes no difference whether anyone has voiced it
1416a6	or not, so that this topic is universal). Another topic is
1416a7	to meet the points in dispute: either that the thing is not so, or that it is not
1416a8	harmful, or not to this man, or not so great, or not unjust,
1416a9	or not much so, or not shameful, or of no importance; for these are
1416a10	the matters in dispute, as in Iphicrates' reply to Nausicrates:
1416a11	he said that he had done what the other alleged and had done harm, but had not
1416a12	done wrong. Or one may offset the wrong: if it was harmful,
1416a13	still it was noble; if painful, still beneficial; or something else
1416a14	of the kind. Another topic: that it was an error, or a misfortune, or
1416a15	necessary; as Sophocles said that he trembled, not, as his slanderer
1416a16	alleged, so as to seem old, but from necessity; for it was not by his own will
1416a17	that he was eighty years old. And one may substitute the motive,
1416a18	saying that one did not want to do harm but this other thing, and did not mean to do what
1416a19	one is slandered with, but harm happened to result: “It would be just
1416a20	to hate me if I had acted so that this would happen.” Another, if
1416a21	the slanderer has been implicated, now or before, himself or one of
1416a22	those close to him. Another, if others are implicated who
1416a23	all agree are not liable to the slander: for example, if because he is neat
1416a24	so-and-so is an adulterer, then so is so-and-so. Another, if he has slandered
1416a25	others, or someone else has, or if others, without slander, were suspected
1416a26	as he is now, who have been shown to be innocent. Another, from
1416a27	slandering the slanderer in return; for it would be absurd if a man
1416a28	who is himself untrustworthy were to have his words trusted. Another,
1416a29	if there has already been a judgment, as Euripides replied to Hygiaenon in
1416a30	the exchange suit, when Hygiaenon accused him of impiety for having
1416a31	written a line urging perjury, “My tongue has sworn,
1416a32	but my mind is unsworn.” Euripides said that Hygiaenon was doing wrong
1416a33	in bringing the judgments of the Dionysiac contest into the
1416a34	courts; for he had given an account of them there,
1416a35	or would give one, if Hygiaenon wished to accuse him. Another, from accusing slander
1416a36	itself: how great an evil it is, both because it creates new issues for judgment and because it does not
1416a37	trust the facts. A topic common to both sides is to cite tokens,
1416b1	as in the Teucer Odysseus says that Teucer is a kinsman of
1416b2	Priam, for Hesione was Priam's sister; and Teucer replies that his father,
1416b3	Telamon, was Priam's enemy, and that he did not
1416b4	denounce the spies. Another, for the slanderer:
1416b5	to praise something small at length and blame something great briefly; or,
1416b6	after setting out many good points, to blame the one that bears on the matter.
1416b7	Such speakers are the most artful and the most unjust; for
1416b8	they try to do harm with good things, mixing them with the bad.
1416b9	Common to the slanderer and to the man rebutting slander: since
1416b10	the same thing can be done for more than one reason, the
1416b11	slanderer must put a malicious construction on it, taking it in the worse sense, and the man
1416b12	rebutting in the better: for example, when Diomedes chose Odysseus,
1416b13	the one says that it was because he thought
1416b14	Odysseus the best, the other that it was not, but because he alone would not rival him,
1416b15	being worthless. So much for slander.
