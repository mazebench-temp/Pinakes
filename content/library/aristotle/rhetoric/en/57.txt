1416b16	In epideictic speeches the narration is not
1416b17	continuous but piecemeal; for one must go through the actions
1416b18	on which the speech is based; the speech is composed
1416b19	of a part that is non-artistic (for the speaker is in no way the cause
1416b20	of the actions) and a part that comes from the art; and this is either to show that
1416b21	the thing is, if it is incredible, or that it is of a certain quality or quantity,
1416b22	or all of these. That is why one should sometimes not narrate
1416b23	everything continuously, because what is shown in this way is hard to remember: from
1416b24	these actions he is brave, from those wise or just. And
1416b25	a speech of this kind is simpler, while the other is intricate and not
1416b26	plain. Well-known actions one need only recall; that is why
1416b27	most epideictic speeches need no narration at all, if you wish, for example, to praise Achilles
1416b28	(for everyone knows his deeds); one need only use
1416b29	them. But if you praise Critias, you need it; for not many know them …
1416b30	As things are, people say laughably that the narration should be quick.
1416b31	Yet, as someone said to the baker who asked whether he should knead the dough hard
1416b32	or soft, “What? Is it impossible to knead it well?”, so here
1416b33	too: one should not narrate at length, just as one should not make a long proem
1416b34	or state the proofs at length. For here too
1416b35	good does not lie in quickness or conciseness
1416b36	but in due measure; that is,
1416b37	in saying as much as will make the matter clear,
1417a1	or as much as will make the hearer believe that it happened, or that he did harm, or
1417a2	did wrong, or that it was as great as you wish, and for the opponent the opposite.
1417a3	Narrate along the way whatever bears on your own virtue (for example,
1417a4	“I kept admonishing him, always telling him what was just, not to abandon
1417a5	his children”), or on the other's vice: “He answered me
1417a6	that wherever he was, there would be other children”, the answer that
1417a7	Herodotus says the Egyptian deserters gave; or whatever pleases
1417a8	the jurors. For the defendant the narration is shorter; for the
1417a9	points at issue are that the thing did not happen, or was not harmful, or
1417a10	not unjust, or not so great, so that one should not spend time
1417a11	on what is agreed, unless something bears on that point, for example if
1417a12	it was done, but was not unjust. Again, events should be told as past, except
1417a13	those that bring pity or indignation when told as happening; an example
1417a14	is the tale to Alcinous, which is retold to Penelope in sixty
1417a15	lines, and the way Phayllus treated the Cycle, and the prologue in the
1417a16	Oeneus. The narration should show character; it will do
1417a17	this if we know what produces character. One way is to make the choice
1417a18	plain: the character is of a certain kind by the choice's being of a certain kind, and the choice
1417a19	by its end. That is why mathematical
1417a20	discourses have no characters, because they have no choice either (for they do not have
1417a21	a purpose), but the Socratic dialogues do; for they speak
1417a22	of such things. Other things that show character are what goes with each character, as that
1417a23	a man walked off while still speaking; for this shows boldness and boorishness
1417a24	of character. And do not speak as if from thought, as people do now,
1417a25	but as if from choice: “But I wished it; and I chose
1417a26	it; and if I gained nothing by it, it was still the better course.” The one
1417a27	is the mark of a wise man, the other of a good man: the wise man's lies in pursuing
1417a28	the beneficial, the good man's in pursuing the noble. If it is incredible, then
1417a29	add the reason, as Sophocles does; an example
1417a30	is the one from the Antigone, that she cared more for her brother
1417a31	than for a husband or children; for these could come again
1417a32	if they were lost, “but with my mother and father gone to Hades,
1417a33	there is no brother who could ever be born.” If you
1417a34	have no reason, still say that you are not unaware that what you say is incredible,
1417a35	but that you are by nature such a man; for people do not believe that anyone does anything
1417a36	willingly except what is to his advantage. Again, in narrating, speak from
1417a37	what is emotional, telling both what goes with it, which the hearers know, and what is peculiar
1417a38	to yourself or to the opponent: “He went off, scowling at me.”
1417b1	And as Aeschines said of Cratylus, that he was hissing
1417b2	and shaking his fists; for these things are persuasive, because what the hearers know
1417b3	becomes a token of what they do not know.
1417b4	Very many such things can be taken from Homer:
1417b5	“So she spoke, and the old woman covered her face with her hands”; for
1417b6	those who are beginning to weep put their hands over their eyes. And
1417b7	bring yourself on at once as a man of a certain kind, so that the hearers see you
1417b8	as such, and your opponent too; but do it without being noticed. That this
1417b9	is easy you can see from messengers: about those of whom we know nothing
1417b10	we nevertheless form some impression. Narration should come in
1417b11	many places, and sometimes not at the beginning. In speaking to the assembly
1417b12	there is least narration, because no one narrates
1417b13	the future; but if there is narration, let it be of past events,
1417b14	so that, reminded of them, the hearers may deliberate better
1417b15	about what is to come, whether the speaker slanders or praises; but
1417b16	†then he does not† do the work of an adviser. If something is incredible,
1417b17	one must promise to give the reason at once, and to arrange things
1417b18	as the hearers wish, as Jocasta in Carcinus'
1417b19	Oedipus keeps promising when the man who is searching for her
1417b20	son questions her; and Sophocles' Haemon.
