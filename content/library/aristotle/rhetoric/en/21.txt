# Aristotle, Rhetoric, Book II, Chapter 6 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.

1383b11	What people feel shame at and are shameless about, and before whom,
1383b12	and in what state, will be clear from the following. Let shame be a kind of pain
1383b13	or disturbance concerning those evils that appear to bring disrepute,
1383b14	whether present, past or future, and shamelessness
1383b15	a kind of slight and unconcern about these same things. If
1383b16	shame is what has been defined, people must feel shame at
1383b17	those evils that seem shameful either to themselves or to those
1383b18	they care about; such are the deeds that come from vice, for instance
1383b19	throwing away one's shield or running away; for that comes from cowardice. And
1383b20	withholding a deposit [or doing wrong]; for that comes from injustice.
1383b21	And having sex with those one should not, or where one should not, or when one should not;
1383b22	for that comes from licentiousness. And making a profit from petty or shameful things,
1383b23	or from the helpless, for instance the poor or the dead, whence the
1383b24	proverb about carrying off even from a corpse; for this comes from sordid greed and
1383b25	lack of generosity. And not helping with money when one can,
1383b26	or helping less. And being helped by those less well off,
1383b27	and borrowing when it will seem like begging, and begging when it will seem like asking for repayment,
1383b28	and asking for repayment when it will seem like begging, and praising what it will seem one is asking for, and
1383b29	asking none the less after failing; for all these are signs
1383b30	of lack of generosity; and praising people to their face is a sign of flattery,
1383b31	and overpraising their good points
1383b32	and glossing over their faults, and grieving too much
1383b33	with one in grief when one is present, and everything else of
1383b34	the kind; for these are signs of flattery. And
1383b35	not enduring toils that older men endure,
1384a1	or the pampered, or those in positions of greater power, or in general
1384a2	the weaker; for all these are signs of softness. And receiving benefits from another,
1384a3	and often, and reproaching him with the good one has done;
1384a4	for all these are signs of smallness of soul and abjectness. And
1384a5	talking all about oneself and making grand promises, and claiming
1384a6	what belongs to others as one's own; for this is boastfulness. And likewise from each of the other
1384a7	vices of character, their deeds, their signs, and things
1384a8	like them; for these are shameful and shameless. And besides these, not
1384a9	having a share in the noble things in which all have a share, or all one's equals, or most of them
1384a10	—by equals I mean those of the same people, fellow citizens, those of the same age,
1384a11	kinsmen, in general one's peers—for then it is already shameful not
1384a12	to share, for instance, in education to the same degree, and in other things likewise.
1384a13	All these are more shameful if they appear to be one's own fault; for then
1384a14	they come rather from vice, if one is oneself the cause of what has been,
1384a15	is, or will be. And people feel shame when they suffer, have suffered, or
1384a16	will suffer such things as bring dishonor
1384a17	and reproach; these are the services rendered with one's body or by
1384a18	shameful deeds, among which is being outraged. And what serves
1384a19	licentiousness is shameful whether willing or unwilling, what is done under force when unwilling; for
1384a20	submitting and not defending oneself come from unmanliness or cowardice.
1384a21	The things at which people feel shame, then, are these and the like. Since
1384a22	shame is an imagination concerning disrepute, and of disrepute
1384a23	for its own sake and not for its consequences, and no one cares about opinion
1384a24	except because of those who hold it, people must
1384a25	feel shame before those they take into account; and they take into account those who admire them,
1384a26	and those they admire, and those by whom they wish to be admired, and those
1384a27	with whom they compete for honor, and those whose opinion they do not despise.
1384a28	People wish to be admired by, and they admire, those
1384a29	who have some good thing that is held in honor, or from whom they happen to be
1384a30	in great need of something those people control, as lovers are;
1384a31	they compete for honor with their equals; and they care about the wise, as
1384a32	speaking the truth, and such are older men and
1384a33	the educated. And people feel more shame at what is before their eyes and in the open
1384a34	(whence the proverb that shame is in the eyes); that is
1384a35	why they feel more shame before those who will always be with them
1384a36	and those who pay attention to them,
1384b1	since in both cases they are before their eyes. And before those not liable to the same
1384b2	charges; for clearly these hold the opposite opinions. And before those
1384b3	who are not forgiving to those who appear to err; for what
1384b4	a man does himself, he is said not to be indignant at in his neighbors,
1384b5	so that what he does not do, he clearly is indignant at. And before those who tell
1384b6	many people; for not being thought to have done it is no different from not being reported;
1384b7	those given to telling are those who have been wronged, because they keep watch, and
1384b8	slanderers; for if they slander even those who do no wrong, still more
1384b9	those who do. And before those who spend their time on their neighbors'
1384b10	errors, for instance mockers and comic poets; for these are in a way
1384b11	slanderers and tellers. And before those with whom they have never failed;
1384b12	for they are disposed toward them as though admired; that is why people feel shame
1384b13	before those who ask something of them for the first time, as never yet having lost their good name in
1384b14	their eyes; such are those who have only lately wished to be friends (for they
1384b15	have seen only the best; that is why Euripides' answer to
1384b16	the Syracusans was a good one), and those of long acquaintance who
1384b17	know nothing against them. People feel shame not only at the shameful things
1384b18	mentioned, but also at their signs: not only at having sex,
1384b19	for instance, but also at its signs; and not only at doing
1384b20	shameful things, but also at speaking of them. Likewise they feel shame not only before those mentioned
1384b21	but also before those who will report to them, such as
1384b22	their servants and friends. In general people feel no shame before those
1384b23	whose opinion about the truth they greatly despise (no one
1384b24	feels shame before children or animals), nor at the same things before acquaintances
1384b25	and strangers, but before acquaintances at what seems wrong
1384b26	in truth, before those far off at what offends convention. They themselves
1384b27	would feel shame in this state: first, if there are
1384b28	people disposed toward them in the way we said those are
1384b29	before whom they feel shame. These were those ⟨who are⟩ admired, or who
1384b30	admire them, or by whom they wish to be admired, or from whom they need
1384b31	some service they will not obtain if they lose their good name; and these
1384b32	either looking on (as Cydias said in his speech to the assembly about the Samian cleruchy:
1384b33	he asked the Athenians to imagine that, standing
1384b34	around them in a circle, were the Greeks, seeing and not only going to hear of
1384b35	whatever they would vote), or if such people are near, or are going to notice;
1384b36	that is why people do not wish to be seen in misfortune by those who once emulated them;
1385a1	for those who emulate admire. And when they have deeds and circumstances that bring shame,
1385a2	either their own or their ancestors' or those of others
1385a3	to whom they are closely related. And in general
1385a4	those on whose behalf they feel shame; these are the people mentioned
1385a5	and those connected with them, or those whose teachers or
1385a6	advisers they have been, or if there are others, their equals, with whom
1385a7	they compete for honor; for out of shame before such people they do
1385a8	many things and leave many undone. And when they are going to be seen
1385a9	and to go about in public among those who know, they are more
1385a10	prone to shame; whence Antiphon the poet, when he was about
1385a11	to be put to death on the plank by Dionysius, seeing those who were to die with him
1385a12	veiling their faces as they went through the gates, said, “Why
1385a13	do you veil your faces? Is it so that none of these may see you tomorrow?”
1385a14	So much, then, for shame; as for shamelessness, it is clear
1385a15	that we shall have plenty to say from the opposites.
