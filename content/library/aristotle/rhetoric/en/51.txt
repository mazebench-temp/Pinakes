1410b6	Since these matters have been settled, we must say where urbane
1410b7	and well-liked sayings come from. To make them belongs
1410b8	to the gifted or the trained man; to show what they are belongs to this
1410b9	method. Let us, then, state them and count them up; and let this
1410b10	be our starting point. Learning easily is naturally pleasant to all,
1410b11	and words signify something, so that the words
1410b12	that give us learning are the most pleasant. Rare words are unknown to us,
1410b13	and standard words we know already; it is metaphor that does this most.
1410b14	When the poet calls old age “stubble”, he gives us learning and
1410b15	knowledge through the genus, for both have lost their bloom. The
1410b16	similes of the poets do the same; and so, if they are good,
1410b17	they seem urbane. For the simile, as has been said
1410b18	before, is a metaphor differing by an added word; that is why it is less pleasant: it is
1410b19	longer, and it does not say that this is that, so the soul
1410b20	does not even look for it. In style and enthymemes, then, those must be
1410b21	urbane that give us quick learning. That is why
1410b22	superficial enthymemes are not well liked (by superficial
1410b23	we mean those obvious to everyone, which need no inquiry),
1410b24	nor those that we do not understand when they are spoken, but those which are understood
1410b25	as soon as they are spoken, even if the knowledge was not there before, or where the understanding
1410b26	lags only a little behind; for then a kind of learning takes place, but in
1410b27	neither of the other cases. In the thought of what is said, then, such
1410b28	enthymemes are well liked; in style,
1410b29	by their form, if they are spoken antithetically, as “and holding that the peace
1410b30	common to the others was war against their own
1410b31	private interests”: war is opposed to peace. In words, if
1410b32	they have metaphor, and that neither strange, for that is hard
1410b33	to take in, nor superficial, for that has no effect. Again, if
1410b34	they set things before the eyes; for one must see things being done rather than
1410b35	things about to be done. So one should aim at these
1410b36	three: metaphor, antithesis, activity.
1410b37	Of the four kinds of metaphor,
1411a1	those by analogy are the best liked, as
1411a2	Pericles said that the youth who perished in
1411a3	the war had vanished from the city as if
1411a4	someone had taken the spring out of the year. And
1411a5	Leptines, about the Lacedaemonians: he would not look on while Greece
1411a6	was made one-eyed. And Cephisodotus, when
1411a7	Chares was eager to submit to his audit for the Olynthian war,
1411a8	was indignant, saying that he was trying to submit to his audit while he had the people
1411a9	by the throat. And once, urging the Athenians
1411a10	to Euboea, he said they must take provisions and march out, a
1411a11	decree of Miltiades. And Iphicrates, when the Athenians had made a truce
1411a12	with Epidaurus and its coast, was indignant, saying
1411a13	that they had robbed themselves of their provisions for the war. And Peitholaus
1411a14	called the Paralus “the people's club”, and Sestus “the meal-tray of
1411a15	the Piraeus”. And Pericles urged them to remove Aegina,
1411a16	“the eyesore of the Piraeus”. And Moerocles said he was no more wicked
1411a17	than a certain decent man whom he named; for that man
1411a18	was wicked at thirty-three percent interest, and he himself at ten.
1411a19	And Anaxandrides' iambic line about daughters slow
1411a20	to marry: “My maidens are overdue for their weddings.”
1411a21	And what Polyeuctus said of a man with a stroke, Speusippus:
1411a22	that he could not keep still, though fortune had bound him
1411a23	in a five-holed disease. And Cephisodotus called the triremes
1411a24	“painted mills”, and the Dog called the Attic taverns
1411a25	“Spartan messes”; and Aesion said that they had poured the city out into Sicily;
1411a26	for this is a metaphor and sets things before the eyes. And “so that
1411a27	Greece cried out”: this too is in a way a metaphor and sets things before
1411a28	the eyes. And as Cephisodotus urged them to beware of
1411a29	making their “concourses” many [assemblies]. And Isocrates,
1411a30	of those who “run together” at the festivals. And, for example,
1411a31	in the funeral speech, that it was fitting that at the tomb of those
1411a32	who died at Salamis Greece should cut her hair, since
1411a33	her freedom was being buried with their virtue. If
1411a34	he had said that it was fitting to weep, since virtue was being buried with them,
1411a35	it would be a metaphor and set things before the eyes;
1411b1	but “freedom with their virtue” has a kind of antithesis. And
1411b2	as Iphicrates said, “The road of my words runs through the midst
1411b3	of Chares' deeds”: a metaphor by analogy, and
1411b4	“through the midst” sets things before the eyes. And saying that one calls on
1411b5	“dangers to come to the help of dangers” sets things before the eyes
1411b6	and is a metaphor. And Lycoleon, speaking for Chabrias: “not even ashamed before
1411b7	his suppliant, the bronze statue”;
1411b8	for it is a metaphor for the moment, not always, but it sets things before
1411b9	the eyes: while he is in danger the statue is a suppliant, the
1411b10	“lifeless thing alive”, the memorial of the city's deeds.
1411b11	And “practicing in every way to think small”; for
1411b12	to practice is to increase something. And that “God kindled intelligence
1411b13	as a light in the soul”; for both make something plain. “For we do not
1411b14	end our wars, we postpone them”; for both
1411b15	lie in the future, postponement and a peace of this kind.
1411b16	And saying that a treaty is a trophy far nobler
1411b17	than those set up in war; for those are for small gains
1411b18	and a single stroke of fortune, but a treaty is for the whole war;
1411b19	for both are signs of victory. And that cities, through the blame of
1411b20	men, undergo a great audit; for an audit is a kind of just penalty.
