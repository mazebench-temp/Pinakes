1369b33	Let us assume that pleasure is a certain movement of the soul,
1369b34	a sudden and perceptible settling down into its natural
1369b35	state, and pain the opposite. If pleasure is something of this kind,
1370a1	it is clear that what produces the condition described
1370a2	is pleasant, and what destroys it, or produces the opposite settling,
1370a3	is painful. So it must be pleasant, for the most part, to move into a state
1370a4	according to nature, and most of all when the processes that accord with it have recovered
1370a5	their own nature; and habits are pleasant
1370a6	(for what has become habitual becomes, as it were, natural; for habit
1370a7	is something like nature: “often” is close to
1370a8	“always,” and nature belongs to “always,” habit to
1370a9	“often”); and what is not forced (for force is contrary to nature, which is why
1370a10	the necessary is painful, and it was rightly said, “For every
1370a11	necessary thing is by nature grievous”); while attentions,
1370a12	efforts and exertions are painful, for they are necessary
1370a13	and forced, unless people are used to them; then habit
1370a14	makes them pleasant. Their opposites are pleasant; that is why ease,
1370a15	freedom from toil, carelessness, amusements, rest
1370a16	and sleep are among pleasant things, for none of these is done under necessity.
1370a17	And everything for which there is appetite is pleasant; for appetite
1370a18	is longing for the pleasant. Of appetites some are irrational,
1370a19	others rational. By irrational I mean those that do not come from
1370a20	forming a belief (such are those said to be
1370a21	natural, like those that come through the body, such as
1370a22	thirst and hunger for nourishment, and for each kind of nourishment
1370a23	a kind of appetite; and those concerned with taste and sex
1370a24	and touch in general, and with smell [of fragrance] and hearing and
1370a25	sight); rational, those that come from being persuaded;
1370a26	for people long to see and acquire many things because they have heard of them
1370a27	and been persuaded. Since being pleased lies in
1370a28	perceiving a certain feeling, and imagination is a kind of weak
1370a29	perception, there will always be in one who remembers and one who hopes
1370a30	some image of what he remembers or hopes for; and if so,
1370a31	it is clear that there are pleasures too for those who remember and hope, since
1370a32	there is perception; so all pleasant things must lie either in
1370a33	perceiving them present, or remembering them past, or
1370a34	hoping for them future; for people perceive the present,
1370a35	remember the past, and hope for the future.
1370b1	Things remembered are pleasant, then, not only those that were pleasant
1370b2	at the time, when they were present, but some that were not, if
1370b3	what came after was noble and good; hence
1370b4	this saying: “It is sweet to remember toils when one is safe,”
1370b5	and “For a man takes delight even in his sorrows afterward, remembering,
1370b6	one who has suffered much and done much.” The reason
1370b7	is that not having an evil is also pleasant. Things hoped for are pleasant when
1370b8	their presence seems likely to delight or benefit greatly, and
1370b9	to benefit without pain. In general, things that delight when present delight
1370b10	us for the most part when we hope for them and remember them. That is why even
1370b11	being angry is pleasant, as Homer wrote of temper,
1370b12	“which is far sweeter than dripping honey” (for no one
1370b13	is angry with someone on whom it seems impossible to get revenge,
1370b14	and with those far above them in power people are either not angry or
1370b15	less so); and with most appetites some pleasure
1370b16	goes along; for remembering how they got something, or hoping that they will get it,
1370b17	they feel a certain pleasure: those in fevers who are gripped
1370b18	by thirst enjoy remembering how they drank and hoping they will drink,
1370b19	and lovers are always glad to talk or write
1370b20	or do something about the beloved; for in
1370b21	all such things, remembering, they seem to perceive
1370b22	the beloved. And this is the beginning of love
1370b23	in everyone: when they not only delight in the beloved present but also, when he is absent,
1370b24	remembering, feel pain that he is not there.
1370b25	And likewise in mourning and lamentation some pleasure
1370b26	arises; for the pain is at his not being there, the pleasure
1370b27	in remembering him and in a way seeing him, what he did
1370b28	and what he was like; that is why this too was fittingly said: “So
1370b29	he spoke, and in all of them he stirred the longing for lament.”
1370b30	And revenge is pleasant; for where failing to get a thing is painful,
1370b31	getting it is pleasant; and the angry are pained beyond measure if they do not
1370b32	get revenge, and rejoice when they hope to. And winning is pleasant,
1370b33	not only to lovers of victory but to all; for there arises an image of superiority,
1370b34	which all have an appetite for, either mildly or strongly. And since
1370b35	winning is pleasant, games of combat and of contest must be pleasant
1371a1	(for in these there is often winning),
1371a2	and knucklebones and ball games and dice and
1371a3	draughts. And with serious games
1371a4	it is the same: some become pleasant once a person is
1371a5	used to them, others are pleasant at once, such as hunting with dogs and all
1371a6	hunting; for where there is competition there is victory too. That is why
1371a7	pleading in court and disputation are pleasant to those used to them and
1371a8	good at them. And honor and good reputation are among the most pleasant things, because
1371a9	each man imagines that he is such as a man of worth is,
1371a10	and the more so when those say it whom he thinks truthful. Such
1371a11	are those near rather than those far off, acquaintances and
1371a12	fellow citizens rather than strangers, contemporaries rather than posterity,
1371a13	the wise rather than the foolish, many rather than few; for those named are more likely
1371a14	to tell the truth than their opposites; since for those whom one greatly
1371a15	despises, such as children or animals, one cares nothing for their
1371a16	honor or regard, at least not for the regard itself, but
1371a17	if at all, for some other reason. A friend too is among pleasant things; for
1371a18	loving is pleasant (no one is fond of wine who does not enjoy wine), and
1371a19	being loved is pleasant; for here too there is an image of one's own
1371a20	being good, which all who have perception desire;
1371a21	and being loved is being cherished for one's own sake.
1371a22	Being admired is pleasant for the same reason as being honored. Being
1371a23	flattered and the flatterer are pleasant; for the flatterer is an apparent admirer
1371a24	and an apparent friend. And doing the same things
1371a25	often is pleasant, for the familiar was pleasant. And change
1371a26	is pleasant; for change brings a return to nature,
1371a27	since sameness always produces an excess of the settled state;
1371a28	hence it was said, “Change in all things is sweet.” For this reason
1371a29	too what comes at intervals is pleasant, both people and
1371a30	things; for it is a change from the present, and at the same time
1371a31	what comes at intervals is rare. Learning and wondering are pleasant
1371a32	for the most part; for in wondering there is the desire to learn,
1371a33	so that the wonderful is desired, and in learning
1371a34	there is a settling into the natural state. Doing good and
1371a35	receiving good are among pleasant things; for
1371a36	receiving good is getting what one desires,
1371b1	and doing good is having, and having more, both of which
1371b2	people desire. Because doing good is pleasant,
1371b3	setting one's neighbors on their feet again is pleasant to people,
1371b4	and completing what is incomplete. And since learning
1371b5	and wondering are pleasant, things of the following kind must be
1371b6	pleasant too: imitations, such as painting and
1371b7	sculpture and poetry, and everything that is well imitated,
1371b8	even if the thing imitated is not itself pleasant; for one does not
1371b9	delight in that, but there is a syllogism, that this is that, so that
1371b10	one comes to learn something. So too reversals and narrow
1371b11	escapes from danger; for all these are
1371b12	wonderful. And since what is natural is pleasant, and kindred things are
1371b13	natural to one another, all kindred and similar things are pleasant
1371b14	for the most part, as man to man, horse to horse,
1371b15	young to young; hence the proverbs, “Age
1371b16	delights in age,” and “Like always to like,” and “Beast
1371b17	knows beast,” “Jackdaw beside jackdaw,” and all the others like them.
1371b18	And since everything similar and kindred to oneself is pleasant,
1371b19	and each man stands in this relation most of all to himself,
1371b20	all must be lovers of self, more or less; for all
1371b21	such relations hold most of all toward oneself. And since all are
1371b22	lovers of self, their own things must be pleasant to all, such as
1371b23	their works and words; that is why people are for the most part fond of flatterers,
1371b24	fond of their lovers, fond of honor, and fond of their children; for
1371b25	children are their own work. And completing what is incomplete is pleasant, for it
1371b26	then becomes one's own work. And since ruling is most pleasant,
1371b27	seeming wise is pleasant too; for wisdom fits one to rule, and
1371b28	wisdom is knowledge of many wonderful things. Further, since
1371b29	people are for the most part fond of honor, it must be pleasant to rebuke one's neighbors
1371b30	and to rule; and to spend one's time on that in which one seems
1371b31	to be at one's best,
1371b32	as the poet says, and to this he
1371b33	presses on, “giving the greatest part of each day
1371b34	to where he happens to be at his best.”
1371b35	Likewise, since play and all relaxation are among pleasant things, and laughter too,
1371b36	laughable things must be pleasant too, whether people, words or deeds.
1372a1	The laughable has been treated separately in the books on
1372a2	poetics. Let this be what we say about pleasant things;
1372a3	painful things are clear from their opposites.
