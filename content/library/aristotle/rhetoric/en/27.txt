1388b31	Next let us go through what the characters are like according to the emotions, the states,
1388b32	the ages and the fortunes. By emotions
1388b33	I mean anger, appetite and the like, of which we have spoken
1388b34	[before]; by states, virtues and vices, which have also been discussed
1388b35	before, as well as what each kind of person
1388b36	chooses and what they are apt to do; the ages
1388b37	are youth, the prime and old age.
1389a1	By fortune I mean good birth, wealth, powers and
1389a2	their opposites, and in general good fortune and bad fortune. The
1389a3	young, then, are given to appetite in character, and apt to do whatever
1389a4	they desire. Of the bodily appetites they are most
1389a5	apt to follow that of sex, and have no self-control in it;
1389a6	they are changeable and quickly sated in their appetites, and
1389a7	desire violently but soon stop (for their wishes are keen
1389a8	but not great, like the thirsts and hungers of the sick);
1389a9	and they are passionate and quick-tempered and apt to follow their anger.
1389a10	And they are mastered by their temper; for through love of honor they cannot bear
1389a11	being slighted, but are vexed if they think they are wronged.
1389a12	And they are fond of honor, but even more fond of victory (for youth
1389a13	longs for superiority, and victory is a kind of superiority), and both of these
1389a14	more than fond of money (and least fond of money because they have not yet
1389a15	experienced want, as Pittacus' saying about
1389a16	Amphiaraus has it); and they are not ill-natured but simple-natured, because they have not yet
1389a17	seen much wickedness, and trusting, because they have not yet often
1389a18	been deceived, and hopeful; for as those who are drunk are, so
1389a19	the young are heated by nature; and at the same time because
1389a20	they have not often failed. And they live mostly in hope;
1389a21	for hope is of the future and memory of the
1389a22	past, and for the young the future is long and the past
1389a23	short; for on the first day one can remember nothing,
1389a24	but can hope for everything. They are easily deceived, for the reason stated
1389a25	(for they hope easily), and more courageous (for they are hot-tempered and
1389a26	hopeful, of which the one makes them unafraid and the other confident; for
1389a27	no one is afraid while he is angry, and hoping for something good
1389a28	inspires confidence), and prone to shame (for they do not yet suppose other
1389a29	things noble, but have been educated by the law alone), and
1389a30	great-souled (for they have not yet been humbled by life, but
1389a31	are inexperienced in its necessities, and to think oneself worthy of great things
1389a32	is greatness of soul; and this belongs to one who is hopeful). And they choose
1389a33	to do noble things rather than advantageous ones; for they live by character more
1389a34	than by calculation, and calculation is concerned with the advantageous,
1389a35	virtue with the noble. And they are fond of their friends and
1389a36	of their companions more than the other ages,
1389b1	because they enjoy living together and do not yet judge anything
1389b2	by advantage, and so not their friends either. And they err in everything
1389b3	on the side of excess and vehemence, contrary to Chilon's maxim
1389b4	(for they do everything too much: they love too much and
1389b5	hate too much and everything else likewise); and they think they know everything,
1389b6	and insist on it (this indeed is the cause of their
1389b7	doing everything too much); and the wrongs they commit are from outrage, not from
1389b8	malice. And they are prone to pity because they suppose all men good and better than they are
1389b9	(for they measure their neighbors by their own innocence,
1389b10	so that they suppose them to suffer what they do not deserve),
1389b11	and fond of laughter, and therefore fond of wit;
1389b12	for wit is educated outrage.
