1411b21	It has been said, then, that urbane sayings come from metaphor
1411b22	by analogy and from setting things before the eyes;
1411b23	we must now say what we mean by “before
1411b24	the eyes”, and what makes it happen. I say
1411b25	that those words set things before the eyes which signify things in activity:
1411b26	for example, to call a good man “four-square”
1411b27	is a metaphor (for both are complete), but it does not signify activity;
1411b28	but “of one whose prime is in flower” is activity, and
1411b29	“you, like a sacred beast roaming at will” is activity, and
1411b30	“Then the Greeks, darting forth on their feet”: “darting”
1411b31	is activity and metaphor, for it means “swiftly”. And as
1411b32	Homer often does, making lifeless things alive through
1411b33	metaphor. In all of them it is by making activity that he is well liked, as
1411b34	in these: “Back down to the ground rolled the shameless
1411b35	stone,” and “the arrow flew,” and “eager to fly,”
1412a1	and “stood fixed in the ground, longing to glut themselves on flesh,” and
1412a2	“the spear point sped through his chest, raging.” In all
1412a3	these the things seem active because they are alive: being
1412a4	shameless and raging and the rest are activity.
1412a5	He attached these through metaphor by analogy:
1412a6	as the stone is to Sisyphus, so the shameless man is
1412a7	to the one he treats shamelessly. In his
1412a8	well-liked similes too he does the same with lifeless things: “arched
1412a9	and white-crested, some in front, and others behind;”
1412a10	for he makes everything moving and alive, and activity
1412a11	is movement. Metaphors should be taken, as has been said before, from
1412a12	things that are related but not obvious, as in philosophy too it is the mark of a
1412a13	sharp mind to see the likeness even in things far apart; so Archytas
1412a14	said that an arbitrator and an altar are the same, for the wronged man
1412a15	takes refuge with both. Or if one were to say that an anchor and
1412a16	a pot-hook are the same; for both are in a way the same, but they differ
1412a17	in being from above and from below. And “the cities have been made level”
1412a18	is the same in things far apart: equality in surface and in
1412a19	powers. Most urbane sayings, too, come through metaphor and
1412a20	from deceiving the hearer besides; for it becomes clearer that he has learned something
1412a21	from its being contrary to what he expected, and the soul seems to say, “How
1412a22	true! And I was wrong.” Urbane apophthegms too
1412a23	come from not meaning what is said, as Stesichorus' saying, that
1412a24	the cicadas will sing to themselves from the ground. And well-made riddles
1412a25	are pleasant for the same reason (for they are learning, and metaphor too),
1412a26	and so is what Theodorus calls “saying novel things”. This happens
1412a27	when a thing is surprising and, as he says, not in keeping with
1412a28	the opinion one held before, but like the twisted words
1412a29	in laughable sayings (which jokes made by changing a letter can also do,
1412a30	for they deceive), and in verse too; for it does not go as the
1412a31	hearer expected: “On he strode, and under his feet were his chilblains”; but
1412a32	he thought he would say “sandals”. This must be clear as soon as
1412a33	it is said. Jokes from changing a letter make one mean not what one says
1412a34	but what the twisted word means, as in Theodorus' jest at Nicon
1412a35	the citharode, “thraxei se”, “it will upset you”; for he makes as if to say
1412a36	“Thrax ei sy”, “you are a Thracian”, and deceives, for what he says is something else.
1412b1	So it is pleasant to one who understands; but if he does not take Nicon to be a Thracian,
1412b2	it will not seem urbane. And “boulei
1412b3	auton persai”, “you want to sack him”. Both meanings must be
1412b4	fitting. So too with urbane sayings like saying
1412b5	that for the Athenians the empire (archē) of the sea was not the beginning (archē) of
1412b6	their troubles, for they profited by it. Or as Isocrates said that the empire was for the
1412b7	city the beginning of its troubles. In both, what one would not have expected
1412b8	to hear said has been said, and is recognized as true; for
1412b9	to say that the empire is a beginning is nothing clever, but
1412b10	he does not mean it in that way but another, and he denies the archē he named not in the sense in which he named it
1412b11	but in another. In all these, it is good when the
1412b12	word is brought in fittingly, by homonymy or by metaphor. For example,
1412b13	“Anaschetus is not to be endured”: he denied the name by homonymy, but fittingly,
1412b14	if the man is unpleasant. And “You could not be more of a stranger than you should”:
1412b15	for “not more of a stranger than you should” is the same as “The
1412b16	stranger should not always be a stranger”; for here too the word is used in
1412b17	another sense. The same with Anaxandrides' much-praised line,
1412b18	“It is noble to die before doing anything worthy of death”;
1412b19	for this is the same as saying “It is worthy to die when one is not
1412b20	worthy to die”, or “It is worthy to die when one is not worthy of
1412b21	death”, or “when one is not doing what is worthy of death”. The form
1412b22	of style in these is the same; but the more briefly and
1412b23	antithetically a thing is said, the better it is liked. The reason
1412b24	is that learning comes more through opposition and
1412b25	faster through brevity. There must always be, besides, the saying's being correctly
1412b26	applied to the person it is said of, if what is said is to be true and not
1412b27	superficial; for these can be had separately, as in
1412b28	“One should die having done no wrong”, or “The
1412b29	worthy man should marry the worthy woman”; but these are not urbane, unless
1412b30	it has both at once: “It is worthy to die when one is not worthy
1412b31	to die.” The more of these qualities it has, the more urbane
1412b32	it seems: for example, if the words were a metaphor, and a metaphor
1412b33	of a particular kind, and antithesis and parisōsis, and it had activity.
1412b34	Similes too, as has been said above,
1412b35	are in a way metaphors when they are well liked; for they are always made from two terms, like the metaphor by analogy:
1412b36	we say, for example, “The shield is the cup of Ares”, and “The bow is a stringless lyre”.
1413a1	Said in this way the metaphor is not simple, but
1413a2	to call the bow a lyre or the shield a cup
1413a3	is simple. People also make similes in this way: a piper
1413a4	to an ape, a short-sighted man to a lamp in the drizzle;
1413a5	for both contract. It is good when there is a metaphor;
1413a6	for one can liken the shield to the cup of Ares, and a ruin
1413a7	to the rag of a house, and say that Niceratus is a Philoctetes
1413a8	bitten by Pratys, as Thrasymachus likened him
1413a9	on seeing Niceratus, beaten by Pratys in reciting epic,
1413a10	with his hair still long and unwashed. It is in these above all
1413a11	that poets fail if they do them badly,
1413a12	and win approval if they do them well; I mean when
1413a13	they make them answer: “He carries his legs as curly
1413a14	as parsley. Like Philammon sparring with the punching bag.”
1413a15	All such things are similes. That similes
1413a16	are metaphors has been said often. Proverbs
1413a17	too are metaphors from species to species: if someone
1413a18	brings something in himself, expecting good from it, and then is harmed,
1413a19	“Like the Carpathian with the hare,” people say; for both suffered
1413a20	what was described. Where urbane sayings come from, then, and
1413a21	why, the cause has been more or less stated. Well-liked
1413a22	hyperboles are also metaphors, as of a man with a black eye: “You would have thought
1413a23	him a basket of mulberries”; for the bruise is something red,
1413a24	but this exaggerates greatly. “Like this and that” is a hyperbole
1413a25	differing only in style. “Like Philammon
1413a26	sparring with the punching bag”: “You would have thought him
1413a27	Philammon fighting the punching bag.” “Carrying
1413a28	his legs as curly as parsley”: “You would have thought
1413a29	he had not legs but parsley, so curly were they.”
1413a30	Hyperboles are adolescent, for they show
1413a31	vehemence. That is why angry people use them most:
1413a32	“not if he gave me as much as the sand and the dust.
1413a33	And I will not marry the daughter of Agamemnon, son of Atreus, not if
1413a34	she rivaled golden Aphrodite in beauty, and Athena in handiwork.”
1413b1	So it is inappropriate for an older man to use them;
1413b2	the Attic orators use this most of all.
