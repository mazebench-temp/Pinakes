1418b40	As for interrogation, it is most opportune to use it
1419a1	when the opponent has stated one of two points, so that when one more question is asked
1419a2	the absurdity follows; as Pericles questioned Lampon about
1419a3	the initiation into the rites of the Savior goddess, and when Lampon said that
1419a4	no one uninitiated could hear of them, asked whether he knew them himself, and when he said
1419a5	he did, “And how, when you are uninitiated?” Second, when one point
1419a6	is evident, and it is clear that the opponent will grant the other if asked; for once one has
1419a7	learned the one premise by asking, one should not ask the evident one as well,
1419a8	but state the conclusion; as Socrates, when Meletus
1419a9	denied that he believed in gods, but had said that he spoke of some
1419a10	daimonion, asked whether daimones were not either children of gods or
1419a11	something divine, and when Meletus said they were, said, “Is there anyone, then, who thinks
1419a12	that there are children of gods, but not gods?” Again, when one is going to show
1419a13	the opponent saying contradictory or paradoxical things. Fourth, when he cannot
1419a14	answer the question except by a sophistical answer; for if he answers in this way,
1419a15	that it is and it is not, or partly yes and partly no, or in one sense
1419a16	yes and in another no, the hearers shout him down as being at a loss. Otherwise do not
1419a17	attempt it. For if he raises an objection, you will seem to have been beaten; for it is not
1419a18	possible to ask many questions, because of the weakness of the hearer;
1419a19	that is why enthymemes too should be made as compact as possible.
1419a20	In answering, one should meet ambiguous questions by drawing distinctions
1419a21	in full, and not curtly; and meet apparent contradictions by bringing the
1419a22	solution at once in the answer, before the opponent asks the
1419a23	next question or draws his conclusion; for it is not hard to foresee where the
1419a24	argument is going. This, and the solutions, are plain to us from the Topics.
1419a25	And when the opponent is drawing his conclusion, if he puts the conclusion
1419a26	as a question, one should state one's reason; as Sophocles, asked
1419a27	by Pisander whether he had decided, like the other
1419a28	commissioners, to set up the Four Hundred, said he had. “What? Did
1419a29	this not seem wicked to you?” He said it did. “Then did you not
1419a30	do these wicked things?” “Yes,” he said, “for there was nothing better.”
1419a31	And as the Laconian at the audit of his ephorate, when asked whether
1419a32	he thought his colleagues had been justly put to death, said he did; and the other: “Then did you not
1419a33	vote the same as they did?” And he said he did. “Then would you not”,
1419a34	said the other, “justly be put to death too?” “Certainly not,” he said, “for they
1419a35	did it for money, and I did not, but from conviction.”
1419a36	That is why one should not ask a further question after the conclusion,
1419b1	nor put the conclusion as a question, unless the truth
1419b2	is overwhelmingly on one's side. As for the laughable, since
1419b3	it seems to have some use in debates, and
1419b4	Gorgias said one should destroy the opponents' seriousness
1419b5	with laughter and their laughter with seriousness, and said it rightly,
1419b6	it has been said in the books on poetics how many kinds of the laughable there are, of which some
1419b7	suit a free man and others do not, so that he may take what suits him.
1419b8	Irony is more befitting a free man than buffoonery; for the
1419b9	ironical man produces the laughable for his own sake, the buffoon for another's.
