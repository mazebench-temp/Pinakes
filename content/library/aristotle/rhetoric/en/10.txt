1368b1	As for accusation and defense, from how many and what kinds of premises
1368b2	syllogisms should be made would be next to discuss. We must
1368b3	grasp three things: first, for what and for how many reasons people do wrong;
1368b4	second, in what state they themselves are; third, what kind of people they wrong
1368b5	and in what condition these are. Let us define doing wrong and then speak
1368b6	in order. Let doing wrong be causing harm voluntarily against the
1368b7	law. Law is either particular or common: by
1368b8	particular I mean the written law by which each people governs itself; by common,
1368b9	the unwritten principles that seem to be agreed on among all. People act voluntarily
1368b10	when they know what they are doing and are not compelled. Not everything
1368b11	done knowingly is done by choice, but everything done by choice
1368b12	is done knowingly; for no one is ignorant of what he chooses. The causes for which
1368b13	people choose to harm others and do bad things against the law
1368b14	are vice and lack of self-control; for if people have some depravity,
1368b15	one or more, it is in the matter in which they are depraved
1368b16	that they are also unjust: the mean man in money,
1368b17	the licentious in the pleasures of the body, the soft
1368b18	in matters of ease, the coward in dangers (for cowards
1368b19	abandon their comrades in danger out of fear),
1368b20	the ambitious for honor, the quick-tempered from anger,
1368b21	the contentious for victory, the bitter for revenge,
1368b22	the foolish because they are deceived about the just and
1368b23	unjust, the shameless from contempt for reputation; and likewise
1368b24	each of the others in each of the matters that concern him.
1368b25	But this is clear, partly from what has been said about the virtues
1368b26	and partly from what will be said about the emotions. It remains
1368b27	to say for what reason and in what state people do wrong, and to whom.
1368b28	First, then, let us distinguish what people are longing for and what they are avoiding
1368b29	when they set about doing wrong; for clearly the accuser
1368b30	must consider how many and which of these are present in his opponent,
1368b31	the things for desire of which all people wrong their neighbors, and the defendant
1368b32	which and how many of them are not present. All people do all
1368b33	they do either not through themselves or through themselves. Of
1368b34	what they do not through themselves, some they do by chance, some
1368b35	of necessity, and of what they do of necessity, some by force and some by nature; so
1368b36	all that they do not through themselves is done by chance, or
1368b37	by nature, or by force. What they do through themselves, and of which they are the cause,
1369a1	is done either through habit or through longing, and some through rational
1369a2	longing, some through irrational. Wish is
1369a3	longing for the good (for no one wishes for anything unless he thinks
1369a4	it good); irrational longings are anger and appetite.
1369a5	So all that people do they must do from seven
1369a6	causes: chance, nature, force, habit, reasoning,
1369a7	temper, appetite. To divide actions further by
1369a8	age or states or anything else is superfluous; for if
1369a9	the young happen to be irascible or full of appetite,
1369a10	it is not because of youth that they do such things but because of anger
1369a11	and appetite. Nor do they act because of wealth or poverty; it
1369a12	happens that the poor, through their need, desire
1369a13	money, and the rich, through their means, desire
1369a14	unnecessary pleasures; but these too will act not because of
1369a15	wealth or poverty but because of appetite. Likewise
1369a16	the just and the unjust, and the others who are said
1369a17	to act according to their states, will act from these causes; for either
1369a18	by reasoning or by emotion; but some from good characters and
1369a19	emotions, others from the opposite. It does happen, though, that
1369a20	such and such acts follow from such and such states, and from others
1369a21	others; for perhaps in the self-controlled man, because he is self-controlled,
1369a22	good opinions and appetites follow at once about
1369a23	pleasant things, and in the licentious the opposite ones about the same
1369a24	things. So such divisions must be set aside, and we must consider
1369a25	what usually follows what. If a man is fair or dark,
1369a26	tall or short, nothing of this kind is fixed to follow;
1369a27	but if he is young or old, just or unjust, it does
1369a28	make a difference; and in general, whatever circumstances make men's
1369a29	characters differ: thinking oneself rich or poor, for instance,
1369a30	will make some difference, and being fortunate or unfortunate. These things
1369a31	we shall discuss later; let us first speak now about the rest.
1369a32	Things that happen by chance are those whose
1369a33	cause is indefinite and that happen for no purpose and neither always
1369a34	nor for the most part nor in any order (this is clear from the
1369a35	definition of chance); by nature, those whose cause is in themselves and ordered,
1369b1	for they turn out the same way always or for the most part.
1369b2	As for things contrary to nature, there is no need to inquire precisely whether
1369b3	they happen according to nature or by some other cause;
1369b4	it would seem that chance too is a cause of such things.
1369b5	By force, whatever happens against the appetite or the reasoning
1369b6	of the agents themselves. By habit, what people do because they have
1369b7	done it often. By reasoning, what seems
1369b8	advantageous among the goods mentioned, either as an end or as
1369b9	a means to the end, when it is done because it is advantageous; for even
1369b10	the licentious do some advantageous things, but not because they are
1369b11	advantageous but for pleasure. By temper and anger, acts of
1369b12	revenge. Revenge differs from punishment: for
1369b13	punishment is for the sake of the one who suffers it, revenge for the sake of the one
1369b14	who inflicts it, that he may have his fill. What anger is will be made clear
1369b15	in the discussion of the emotions. By appetite, whatever
1369b16	seems pleasant is done. The familiar and the habitual are also among
1369b17	pleasant things; for people do with pleasure many things that are not pleasant by nature,
1369b18	once they are used to them. So, to sum up, all that
1369b19	people do through themselves is either good or apparently
1369b20	good, or pleasant or apparently pleasant. And since what they do through themselves
1369b21	they do voluntarily, and what not through themselves involuntarily,
1369b22	everything they do voluntarily would be either good or
1369b23	apparently good, or pleasant or apparently pleasant. For I count
1369b24	riddance of evils or apparent evils, or the exchange of a
1369b25	greater for a lesser, among goods (for they are in a way
1369b26	desirable); and riddance of the painful or apparently painful, or
1369b27	the exchange of greater pains for lesser, I count among
1369b28	pleasant things likewise. We must grasp, then, the advantageous and the pleasant:
1369b29	how many and what kinds. The advantageous was discussed earlier
1369b30	under deliberative speeches; the pleasant
1369b31	let us discuss now. Definitions should be thought adequate
1369b32	if on each point they are neither obscure nor overprecise.
