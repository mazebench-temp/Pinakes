1386b8	Opposed to pity above all is what people call being indignant; for to
1386b9	being pained at undeserved misfortunes is opposed,
1386b10	in a way, and comes from the same character, being pained
1386b11	at undeserved good fortune. And both emotions belong to a good character;
1386b12	for at those who fare badly undeservedly one should
1386b13	share their grief and pity them, and at those who fare well so, be indignant; for what comes contrary to
1386b14	desert is unjust; that is why we attribute
1386b15	indignation to the gods as well. Envy too might seem to be opposed
1386b16	to pity in the same way, as being close to
1386b17	and the same as indignation, but it is different; for envy
1386b18	too is a disturbing pain, and at
1386b19	good fortune, but not of the undeserving but of one's equal and
1386b20	like. That it is not because something different will happen to oneself, but
1386b21	because of the neighbor himself, must be true of all alike;
1386b22	for it will no longer be envy in the one case and indignation in the other, but
1386b23	fear, if the pain and the disturbance arise because
1386b24	something bad will come to oneself from the other's success.
1386b25	It is clear that the opposite emotions will also follow
1386b26	these; for the one who is pained at those who fare badly undeservedly
1386b27	will be pleased, or free from pain, at those who fare badly in the opposite way:
1386b28	for instance, when parricides and murderers
1386b29	meet with punishment, no good man would be pained;
1386b30	for one should rejoice at such things, and likewise
1386b31	at those who fare well deservedly; for both are just,
1386b32	and make the decent man rejoice; for he must hope
1386b33	that what came to his like may come to himself too. And all these belong
1386b34	to the same character, and their opposites to the
1386b35	opposite; for the same man rejoices in others' misfortunes and is envious;
1387a1	for whatever one is pained to see coming about and existing, one must
1387a2	rejoice at its loss and destruction;
1387a3	so all these hinder pity, but they differ
1387a4	for the reasons stated, so that for making things not pitiable
1387a5	all are alike useful. First, then, let us speak about
1387a6	indignation: with whom people are indignant, and at what, and
1387a7	in what state they themselves are; and then after this about the others.
1387a8	It is clear from what has been said; for if
1387a9	being indignant is being pained at someone who appears to fare well undeservedly,
1387a10	it is clear, first, that one cannot be indignant at all good things;
1387a11	for if a man is just or brave, or will acquire virtue,
1387a12	one will not be indignant with him (for neither is there pity at the opposites of these),
1387a13	but at wealth and power and the like, all those things of which,
1387a14	simply speaking, the good are worthy, and those who have natural
1387a15	goods, such as good birth and beauty and the like. Since
1387a16	what is old seems something close to the natural, people must be
1387a17	more indignant with those who have the same good if they happen to have it newly and fare well
1387a18	because of it; for the newly rich give more pain
1387a19	than those rich of old and by birth; likewise those who rule
1387a20	and have power, many friends, good children and anything
1387a21	of the kind. And if through these some other good comes to them,
1387a22	it is the same; for here too the newly rich give more pain
1387a23	when they rule through their wealth than those rich of old. And likewise
1387a24	with the rest. The cause is that the former seem to have what is their own,
1387a25	the others not; for what has always appeared so seems genuine,
1387a26	so that the others seem not to have what is their own. And since each good
1387a27	is not deserved by just anyone, but there is a certain proportion and
1387a28	fitness, as beauty of arms suits not the just man but the brave,
1387a29	and distinguished marriages not the newly rich but the
1387a30	well-born: if, then, a good man gets what does not
1387a31	suit him, it is a cause for indignation. So is it for the inferior
1387a32	to contend with the superior, above all those
1387a33	in the same field, whence this too has been said: “but he avoided battle with Ajax,
1387a34	son of Telamon; for Zeus
1387a35	was indignant with him whenever he fought a better man”;
1387b1	and if not, then if the inferior contends with the superior in any way at all,
1387b2	for instance the musician with the just man; for justice
1387b3	is better than music. With whom, then,
1387b4	people are indignant, and why, is clear from this; for these
1387b5	and the like are the cases. People themselves are prone to indignation if
1387b6	they happen to deserve the greatest goods and to have
1387b7	acquired them; for that those who are not their like should be thought worthy of like things is not
1387b8	just. Second, if they happen to be good and
1387b9	of worth; for they judge well, and they hate what is unjust. And if they are
1387b10	fond of honor and long for certain things, and above all ⟨if⟩
1387b11	they are fond of honor in the things that others get undeservedly.
1387b12	And in general those who think themselves worthy of what they do not think others worthy of
1387b13	are prone to indignation at these people and for these things; that is why the slavish
1387b14	and the bad and those indifferent to honor are not prone to indignation; for there is nothing
1387b15	they think themselves worthy of. It is clear from this
1387b16	at what sort of people's misfortune, bad fortune or failure one should
1387b17	rejoice or be free from pain; for from what has been said
1387b18	the opposites are clear, so that if the speech puts the judges
1387b19	in such a state, and shows that those who claim
1387b20	pity, and the things for which they claim it, do not deserve
1387b21	to get it and deserve not to get it, pity is impossible.
