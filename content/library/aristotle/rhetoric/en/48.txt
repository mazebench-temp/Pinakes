1408a10	The style will be appropriate if it is emotional, shows character,
1408a11	and is proportionate to the subject matter. It is
1408a12	proportionate if weighty matters are not spoken of offhandedly
1408a13	nor cheap ones with dignity, and if ornament is not added to a cheap
1408a14	word; otherwise it looks like comedy, as in what
1408a15	Cleophon does; for some things he said were as if one were to say “queenly
1408a16	fig tree”. The style is emotional if, when there is outrage, it is the style of an angry man;
1408a17	if impious or shameful things, of a man who is disgusted and reluctant
1408a18	even to speak of them; if praiseworthy things, admiringly; if pitiable things,
1408a19	humbly; and so on in the other cases. The proper style also
1408a20	makes the matter persuasive; for the soul draws the false inference that the speaker
1408a21	is telling the truth, because people in such circumstances feel this way,
1408a22	so that they think, even if things are not as the speaker says, that
1408a23	the facts are so; and the hearer always shares the feelings of one
1408a24	who speaks with emotion, even if he says nothing. That is why many overwhelm
1408a25	their hearers by making a din. And this display from
1408a26	signs also shows character, when the style that suits each class
1408a27	and state goes with it. By class I mean by age, as boy
1408a28	or man or old man, and woman or man, and Laconian or Thessalian;
1408a29	and by states, those that make a man of a certain kind in his life; for it is not by every
1408a30	state that lives are of a certain kind. So if one also uses words proper
1408a31	to the state, one will convey the character; for a boor and an educated man
1408a32	would not say the same things, or say them in the same way. Hearers are also
1408a33	affected by what the speechwriters use to excess,
1408a34	“Who does not know?”, “Everyone knows”; for the hearer agrees
1408a35	out of shame, so that he may share in what all the
1408a36	others share. Using these at the right moment or not
1408b1	is common to all the kinds. The remedy for
1408b2	every excess is the well-worn one: one should
1408b3	rebuke oneself in advance; for then it seems to be true,
1408b4	since what he is doing does not escape the speaker. Again,
1408b5	do not use all the corresponding means at once (for in this way the hearer
1408b6	is deceived). I mean, for example, if the words are harsh,
1408b7	do not use a voice and a face to suit them as well;
1408b8	otherwise each is seen for what it is. But if one is used
1408b9	and the other not, one does the same thing without being noticed. If, then, gentle things
1408b10	are said harshly and harsh things gently, it becomes persuasive.
1408b11	Double words, more numerous epithets and strange words
1408b12	suit above all a man speaking with emotion; for an angry man is forgiven
1408b13	for calling an evil “sky-high” or saying “monstrous”; and so is a speaker when
1408b14	he already has the hearers in his hands and has stirred them to enthusiasm by praise
1408b15	or blame or anger or friendship, as Isocrates does at
1408b16	the end of the Panegyricus, “fame and memory” and “who
1408b17	endured”; for people utter such things when they are enthusiastic, so that
1408b18	the hearers too, clearly, accept them when they feel the same. That is why it suits
1408b19	poetry too, for poetry is inspired. So one should use them either thus or
1408b20	with irony, as Gorgias did, and as in the Phaedrus.
