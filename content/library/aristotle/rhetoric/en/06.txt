1362a15	What one should aim at when exhorting, as things that will be or
1362a16	already are, and what when dissuading, is clear; for the latter are the opposites
1362a17	of the former. Since the target set before the adviser
1362a18	is the advantageous (for people deliberate not about the end but
1362a19	about the means to the end, and these are what is advantageous in
1362a20	actions, and the advantageous is a good), we should grasp the
1362a21	elements concerning the good and the advantageous in general. Let
1362a22	good be that which is chosen for its own sake, and that for whose sake we choose something
1362a23	else, and what all things aim at, or all things that have perception
1362a24	or intelligence, or would if they acquired intelligence; and whatever intelligence would
1362a25	assign to each, and whatever intelligence about each case assigns to each,
1362a26	for that is the good for each; and that whose presence puts one in a good condition
1362a27	and makes one self-sufficient; and the self-sufficient; and what produces
1362a28	or preserves such things, and what such things follow upon,
1362a29	and what prevents or destroys their opposites. One thing follows another
1362a30	in two ways (either at the same time or later: knowing follows
1362a31	learning later, living follows being healthy at the same time); and things
1362a32	produce in three ways: some as being healthy produces health, some
1362a33	as food produces health, and some as exercise does, in that for
1362a34	the most part it produces health. With this laid down, it follows that
1362a35	acquiring goods is good, and so is getting rid of
1362a36	evils; for not having the evil follows the latter at once, and
1362a37	having the good follows the former later. So is acquiring a greater
1362a38	good instead of a lesser, and a lesser evil instead of a greater;
1362b1	for by the amount by which the greater exceeds the lesser, there is
1362b2	a gain of the one and a riddance of the other. The virtues too must
1362b3	be a good (for by them their possessors are in a good condition,
1362b4	and they produce good things and practice them;
1362b5	what each is, and of what sort, must be stated separately), and
1362b6	pleasure must be a good; for all living things by nature
1362b7	desire it; so both pleasant things and noble things must
1362b8	be goods: the former produce pleasure, and of
1362b9	noble things some are pleasant and others are chosen for themselves.
1362b10	To speak of them one by one, the following must be goods. Happiness:
1362b11	for it is chosen for itself and is self-sufficient, and for its sake
1362b12	we choose everything else. Justice, courage, self-control,
1362b13	greatness of soul, magnificence, and the other such states:
1362b14	for they are virtues of the soul. Health, beauty and the like:
1362b15	for they are excellences of the body and produce much; health, for instance,
1362b16	produces both pleasure and life, which is why it seems to be best, since
1362b17	it is the cause of two of the things the many prize most, pleasure
1362b18	and life. Wealth: for it is the excellence of possession and produces
1362b19	much. A friend and friendship: for a friend is chosen for his own sake
1362b20	and produces much. Honor, reputation: for they are pleasant
1362b21	and produce much, and they are usually accompanied by
1362b22	possession of the things for which people are honored. Ability to speak,
1362b23	to act: for all such things produce goods. Also
1362b24	natural gifts, memory, ease of learning, quick wit, and all such things:
1362b25	for these capacities produce goods. Likewise
1362b26	all the sciences and the arts. And life:
1362b27	for even if no other good were to follow from it, it is chosen for itself.
1362b28	And justice: for it is something advantageous to the community.
1362b29	These, then, are more or less the goods that are agreed on; in
1362b30	disputed cases the syllogisms come from the following. A thing whose opposite
1362b31	is bad is good. So is that whose opposite is advantageous to the enemy;
1362b32	for instance, if it is most advantageous to the enemy for us to be
1362b33	cowards, it is clear that courage is most beneficial to the citizens.
1362b34	And in general, the opposite of what the enemy wants, or of what they rejoice at,
1362b35	seems beneficial; hence it was said, “Surely Priam would rejoice.”
1362b36	This is not always so, but for the most part; for nothing
1362b37	prevents the same thing from sometimes being advantageous to opponents; hence it is said that
1362b38	misfortunes bring people together, when the same thing is harmful to both.
1363a1	And that of which there is no excess is good, while what
1363a2	is greater than it should be is bad. And that for whose sake much
1363a3	toil or expense has been spent; for this already seems
1363a4	a good, and such a thing is taken to be an end,
1363a5	the end of many efforts, and the end is a good. Hence these words: “And leave
1363a6	to Priam a thing to boast of,” and “It is shameful to stay so long.”
1363a7	And the proverb about the water jar at the door. And what many
1363a8	desire, and what is evidently fought over; for what all
1363a9	desire was the good, and the many seem as good as all.
1363a10	And what is praised; for no one praises what is not good. And what
1363a11	enemies and bad men praise; for it is as if everyone already
1363a12	agrees, if even those who have suffered from it do; they would agree because
1363a13	it is obvious; just as those whom their friends blame are bad,
1363a14	and those whom their enemies do not blame [good] (that is why
1363a15	the Corinthians thought themselves insulted by Simonides when he wrote, “The Corinthians
1363a16	Ilium does not blame”). And what one of the wise
1363a17	or of the good, men or women, has preferred,
1363a18	as Athena chose Odysseus, Theseus Helen, and Alexander
1363a19	was chosen by the goddesses, and Homer chose Achilles. And in general, what is chosen;
1363a20	and people choose to do the things mentioned, and what is
1363a21	bad for their enemies and good for their friends, and what is possible;
1363a22	and this is of two kinds, what could happen and what happens
1363a23	easily; easy things are those done without pain or in a short time;
1363a24	for the difficult is defined either by pain or by length of time. And
1363a25	whatever goes as they wish; and they wish either for no evil at all or for less evil
1363a26	than good (this will be so if either it goes unnoticed or the penalty
1363a27	is small). And what is one's own, what no one else has, and what is extraordinary; for so
1363a28	the honor is greater. And what suits them; such are
1363a29	the things fitting their birth and power, and those they think they lack,
1363a30	even if they are small; for they choose to do these
1363a31	none the less. And what is easily done; for that is possible and
1363a32	easy; things easily done are those in which all, or most, or one's equals
1363a33	or inferiors have succeeded. And what will please one's friends
1363a34	or annoy one's enemies. And whatever those whom people admire
1363a35	choose to do. And what they have a gift and experience for;
1363a36	for they think they will succeed more easily. And what no bad man does; for such things are more
1363a37	praiseworthy. And what they happen to desire; for that seems not only pleasant but
1363a38	also better. And above all, each kind of person chooses what he is fond of:
1363b1	lovers of victory, if there will be victory; lovers of honor,
1363b2	if there will be honor; lovers of money, if there will be money; and
1363b3	the rest likewise. About the good, then, and the
1363b4	advantageous, proofs are to be drawn from these things.
