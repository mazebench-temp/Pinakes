1407b26	The following contribute to the bulk of style: using a description
1407b27	instead of a name, for example not “circle” but “a plane figure
1407b28	equidistant from the middle”; and, for conciseness, the opposite, the name instead of
1407b29	the description. And if something is shameful or inappropriate: if the shame lies in the
1407b30	description, use the name; if it lies in
1407b31	the name, the description. And making things plain by metaphor and by
1407b32	epithets, while guarding against the poetic. And making
1407b33	one into many, as the poets do: though there is one
1407b34	harbor, they still say “to Achaean harbors”,
1407b35	and “Here are the many-leaved folds of the tablet.” And not joining two words to one article,
1407b36	but giving each its own, “the wife, the one of ours”; but if
1407b37	for conciseness, the opposite, “our wife”. And speaking with a connective;
1407b38	but for conciseness, without a connective, though not disconnected,
1408a1	as “having gone and having conversed”, “having gone, I conversed”.
1408a2	And Antimachus' practice is useful, describing a thing by what it does not have, as he
1408a3	does with Teumessus: “There is a small windy
1408a4	hill”; for in this way the description is amplified without limit. This can be done with
1408a5	good things and bad, saying what a thing is not, whichever is useful;
1408a6	and it is from this that the poets get their words, the “stringless”
1408a7	and the “lyreless” song; for they apply epithets from privations.
1408a8	This is well liked in metaphors by
1408a9	analogy, as saying that the trumpet sends forth a lyreless song.
