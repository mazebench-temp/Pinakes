1407a19	Speech, then, is composed of these. The foundation
1407a20	of style is speaking good Greek, and this lies in five things. First,
1407a21	in the connectives, if one places them as they naturally come before
1407a22	and after one another, as some of them require:
1407a23	as men and egō men require de and ho de. One should
1407a24	make them answer each other while the hearer still remembers, and neither
1407a25	hold them far apart nor put another connective before the
1407a26	one required, for that is seldom fitting: “I on my part, when he told me
1407a27	(for Cleon came begging and demanding), set out,
1407a28	taking them with me.” Here many connectives have been thrown in
1407a29	before the one that is to answer; and if there is a great deal
1407a30	in between before “set out”, it is unclear. One point, then, is good use
1407a31	of connectives; the second is to speak in specific words
1407a32	and not with general ones. The third is to avoid ambiguous words, that is,
1407a33	unless one chooses the opposite on purpose, as people do when they have nothing
1407a34	to say but pretend to be saying something; such people
1407a35	say these things in poetry, as Empedocles does; for a long
1407a36	roundabout way of talking cheats the hearers, and they feel what the
1407a37	many feel at the seers': when the seers speak ambiguously,
1407a38	they nod along: “Croesus, crossing the Halys, will destroy a great empire.”
1407b1	And because error is less likely on the whole, the seers speak in the general terms
1407b2	of the matter; for in the game of odds and evens one would more often
1407b3	be right by saying “even” or “odd” than by saying how many
1407b4	the other holds, and by saying that a thing will be than when; that is why oracle-mongers
1407b5	do not add the when. All these are alike, so unless
1407b6	it is for some such purpose, they are to be avoided. The fourth: as Protagoras
1407b7	divided the genders of nouns, male, female and
1407b8	things; for these too must be given correctly: “She, having come
1407b9	and conversed, departed.” The fifth lies in naming many, few
1407b10	and one correctly: “They came and beat me.”
1407b11	In general, what is written should be easy to read and
1407b12	easy to speak, which is the same thing. Many connectives do not
1407b13	allow this, nor do writings that are not easy to punctuate, like those of Heraclitus.
1407b14	To punctuate Heraclitus is a task, because it is unclear
1407b15	which word goes with which, the later or the earlier, as at
1407b16	the very beginning of his book; for he says, “Though this account
1407b17	holds always men prove uncomprehending”; it is unclear with which word
1407b18	“always” should be punctuated. Again, this makes for solecism:
1407b19	failing to make the words correspond, if you do not join to two words one that suits both;
1407b20	for example, with “sound” and “color”, “seeing” is not common to both, but
1407b21	“perceiving” is. And it is unclear if you do not put first what you mean, when you are going
1407b22	to insert much in between, as “I was going, after talking
1407b23	with him about this and that and in this way, to set out”, rather than “I was going,
1407b24	after talking with him, to set out; then this and that and in this way
1407b25	happened.”
