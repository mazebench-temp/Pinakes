# Aristotle, Rhetoric, Book I, Chapter 1 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line for line from Ross's Greek; each row carries the Bekker line it renders.

1354a1	Rhetoric is the counterpart of dialectic. For both
1354a2	are concerned with such things as it is in a way the common business of all
1354a3	to know, and as belong to no one defined science; and so
1354a4	everyone in a way has a share in both, for all, up to
1354a5	a point, try both to examine and to uphold an argument, and to defend themselves
1354a6	and to accuse. Now among the many, some do these things at random,
1354a7	others by practice that comes from habit; and since
1354a8	both are possible, it is clear that they could also be done by a method.
1354a9	For the cause by which both those who succeed by practice and those who succeed
1354a10	spontaneously succeed can be studied, and such a study
1354a11	everyone would at once agree is the work of an art. As things are,
1354a12	those who compile the Arts of speeches have provided, so to speak, no
1354a13	part of it (for proofs are the only thing within the art, and the
1354a14	rest is accessory); they say nothing about enthymemes,
1354a15	which are the body of proof, but busy themselves for the most part with matters outside
1354a16	the subject. For slander and pity and
1354a17	anger and such emotions of the soul are not about the subject
1354a18	but are aimed at the juror. So if in all
1354a19	trials things were as they now are in some cities at least,
1354a20	and above all in the well-governed ones, they would have nothing
1354a21	to say. For everyone either thinks that the laws ought to
1354a22	prescribe this, or actually applies it and forbids speaking outside
1354a23	the subject, as on the Areopagus too, and in this they are
1354a24	right; for one ought not to warp the juror by leading him into anger
1354a25	or envy or pity: that is as if someone were to make the rule he is about
1354a26	to use crooked. Further, it is clear
1354a27	that the litigant has nothing to do beyond showing that the
1354a28	thing is or is not, has happened or has not happened. Whether
1354a29	it is great or small, just or unjust, in whatever the lawgiver
1354a30	has not defined, the juror must surely decide for himself, and not
1354a31	learn it from the litigants. It is most fitting, then,
1354a32	that rightly framed laws should, as far as possible, define everything
1354a33	themselves, and leave as little as possible to those who judge:
1354a34	first, because it is easier to find one or a few than many
1354b1	who are sensible and able to make laws and to judge; next,
1354b2	laws are made by people who have considered them over a long time,
1354b3	while judgments are given on the spur of the moment, so that it is hard for those who judge
1354b4	to award what is just and advantageous well. But the greatest reason of all
1354b5	is that the lawgiver's judgment is not about
1354b6	particulars but about what is future and universal, while the
1354b7	member of the assembly and the juror judge things present and
1354b8	defined; and with them love
1354b9	and hatred and private advantage are often bound up,
1354b10	so that they can no longer see the truth adequately, but
1354b11	their own pleasure or pain darkens their judgment. On the other
1354b12	matters, then, as we say, one should make the judge master of
1354b13	as few as possible; but whether something has happened or not,
1354b14	or will be or will not be, or is or is not, must be left
1354b15	to the judges, for the lawgiver cannot
1354b16	foresee these things. If this is so, it is clear that matters outside
1354b17	the subject are what all those reduce to an art who define the other things, such as
1354b18	what the introduction or the narration should contain, and each of the other
1354b19	parts; for in these they concern themselves with nothing
1354b20	except how to put the judge in a certain frame of mind, and about the
1354b21	artistic proofs they show nothing, though that is how
1354b22	one would become skilled in enthymemes. This is why, although the
1354b23	method is the same for speaking in the assembly and in the courts, and
1354b24	the business of the assembly is nobler and more political
1354b25	than that of contracts, they say nothing about the former
1354b26	but all try to make an art of litigation:
1354b27	because it is less to the purpose to speak of matters outside the subject
1354b28	in speeches to the assembly, and speaking to the assembly is less mischievous
1354b29	than pleading in court, since it is of more common concern. For there the judge
1354b30	judges his own affairs, so that nothing more is needed than to show that
1354b31	things are as the adviser says; but in judicial speeches this is not enough,
1354b32	and it is to the purpose to win over the hearer; for the judgment is
1354b33	about other people's affairs, so that, looking to their own interest and
1354b34	listening to please themselves, they give in to the litigants and do not judge.
1355a1	That is why in many places, as I said before, the law forbids
1355a2	speaking outside the subject; in the assembly the judges themselves
1355a3	guard against this well enough. Since it is clear
1355a4	that the artistic method is concerned with proofs, and
1355a5	proof is a kind of demonstration (for we are most convinced when
1355a6	we suppose a thing to have been demonstrated), and the rhetorical demonstration is the enthymeme,
1355a7	and this is, generally speaking, the most authoritative of proofs,
1355a8	and the enthymeme is a kind of syllogism, and every syllogism
1355a9	alike is for dialectic to consider, either dialectic as a whole or
1355a10	some part of it, it is clear that whoever is best able to see
1355a11	from what and how a syllogism comes about will also
1355a12	be most skilled in enthymemes, once he has learned besides what the enthymeme
1355a13	deals with and how it differs from logical
1355a14	syllogisms. For the true and what is like the true
1355a15	are seen by the same capacity; and at the same time human beings
1355a16	are by nature adequately disposed toward the truth, and for the most part attain
1355a17	the truth; so one who is good at aiming at reputable opinions
1355a18	is also one who is good at aiming at the truth. That
1355a19	the others, then, make an art of matters outside the subject,
1355a20	and why they have leaned rather toward pleading in court, is clear.
1355a21	Rhetoric is useful, first, because by nature the true and the just
1355a22	are stronger than their opposites, so that if judgments are not given as they should be,
1355a23	they must have been defeated through their own fault, and this
1355a24	deserves reproach. Next, before some audiences, even if we
1355a25	had the most exact knowledge, it would not be easy to persuade them
1355a26	by speaking from it; for discourse based on knowledge belongs to teaching,
1355a27	and teaching here is impossible: we must build our proofs and arguments
1355a28	on common notions, as we said in the Topics too
1355a29	about conversing with the many. Further, one must be able to persuade
1355a30	of opposite things, just as in syllogisms,
1355a31	not so that we may do both (for one must not persuade people of what is bad),
1355a32	but so that we may not fail to see how things stand, and so that, if another uses
1355a33	arguments unjustly, we ourselves may be able to refute them. Now
1355a34	none of the other arts draws opposite conclusions; dialectic
1355a35	and rhetoric alone do this, for both are equally
1355a36	concerned with opposites. The facts that underlie them, however, are not
1355a37	equally balanced: what is true and better is by nature always easier to reason from
1355a38	and more persuasive, generally speaking. Besides,
1355a39	it would be absurd if it were shameful to be unable to defend oneself with the body
1355b1	but not shameful to be unable with speech, which is more proper to a human being
1355b2	than the use of the body. And if it is objected that great harm
1355b3	could be done by one who uses such a power of speech unjustly,
1355b4	that is common to all good things except
1355b5	virtue, and above all to the most useful, such as strength,
1355b6	health, wealth, generalship: by these one could do
1355b7	the greatest good by using them justly, and the greatest harm by using them unjustly. That
1355b8	rhetoric, then, does not belong to any one defined class of things,
1355b9	but is like dialectic, and that it is useful, is clear;
1355b10	and also that its function is not to persuade but to see the
1355b11	means of persuasion available in each case, as in all the other
1355b12	arts (for neither is it the function of medicine to make a person healthy, but
1355b13	to bring him as far toward health as is possible; for it is possible
1355b14	to treat well even those who can never regain their health);
1355b15	and further, that it belongs to the same art to see both the persuasive and
1355b16	the apparently persuasive, just as in dialectic it sees
1355b17	both the syllogism and the apparent syllogism; for what makes sophistry
1355b18	is not the capacity but the choice. Except that here
1355b19	one man will be an orator by his knowledge and another by his choice,
1355b20	while there one is a sophist by choice, and a dialectician
1355b21	not by choice but by capacity. Let us now
1355b22	try to speak about the method itself: how and from
1355b23	what we shall be able to achieve our aims. Let us, then,
1355b24	as if starting again, define what rhetoric is, and then speak of the rest.
