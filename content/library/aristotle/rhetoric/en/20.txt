1382a20	What people fear, and whom, and in what state, will be clear
1382a21	as follows. Let fear be a kind of pain or disturbance arising from the imagination
1382a22	of a future evil that is destructive or painful; for people do not fear all
1382a23	evils, for instance that they will be unjust or slow, but those that
1382a24	can bring great pains or destruction, and these if they appear not far off
1382a25	but near, so as to be imminent. What is very far off
1382a26	people do not fear; all know that they will die, but because
1382a27	it is not near, they give it no thought. If this is what fear is,
1382a28	those things must be fearful that appear to have great power
1382a29	to destroy or to do harm that tends toward great
1382a30	pain; that is why even the signs of such things
1382a31	are fearful; for the fearful thing appears near; for this
1382a32	is danger, the approach of something fearful. Such are
1382a33	the enmity and anger of those able to do something (for it is clear
1382a34	that they both wish and are able, so that they are near
1382a35	to doing it), and injustice that has power; for the unjust man is unjust by his choice.
1382b1	And virtue outraged, when it has power (for it is clear that it chooses to retaliate
1382b2	whenever it is outraged, always, and now it is able), and fear
1382b3	on the part of those able to do something; for such a man too must be ready
1382b4	to act. And since most people are rather bad and
1382b5	slaves to gain and cowardly in dangers, it is for the most part fearful
1382b6	to be at another's mercy, so that those who know that one has done
1382b7	something terrible are fearful, as likely to denounce or abandon one. And those
1382b8	able to do wrong are fearful to those who can be wronged; for for the most part
1382b9	people do wrong when they can. And those who have been wronged or
1382b10	think they are wronged; for they are always watching for an opportunity. And those
1382b11	who have done wrong, if they have power, are fearful, being afraid of retaliation;
1382b12	for such a thing was assumed to be fearful. And rivals for the same
1382b13	things, when both cannot have them at once; for people are always
1382b14	at war with such rivals. And those who are fearful to their superiors
1382b15	are fearful to us; for they could harm us all the more, if they can harm even
1382b16	our superiors. And those whom our superiors fear, for the same reason.
1382b17	And those who have destroyed men superior to them, and those who
1382b18	attack men inferior to them; for they are either fearful already or will be when they have grown. And
1382b19	among those who have been wronged, and enemies or rivals, not the quick-tempered and
1382b20	outspoken, but the calm, the ironic and the unscrupulous;
1382b21	for it is unclear whether they are near, so that it is never clear that they are far off. All
1382b22	fearful things are more fearful when, if people make a mistake, it cannot
1382b23	be put right, but is either altogether impossible, or not in their own power but in the power
1382b24	of their opponents. And those for which there is no help, or no easy help. To
1382b25	put it simply, things are fearful that are pitiable when they happen
1382b26	or are about to happen to others. The fearful things, then, and what
1382b27	people fear are, so to speak, more or less these, the greatest of them;
1382b28	and in what state people themselves fear, let us now say. If
1382b29	fear is accompanied by an expectation of suffering
1382b30	something destructive, it is clear that no one fears who thinks
1382b31	he would suffer nothing, nor fears the things he does not think he would suffer,
1382b32	nor the people by whom he does not think, nor at a time when he does not think.
1382b33	Those, then, must fear who think they might suffer something,
1382b34	and from these people, and these things, and at this time. Those do not think
1382b35	they could suffer who are in great prosperity, or seem to be
1383a1	(that is why they are given to outrage, slight and rashness, and what makes
1383a2	people so is wealth, strength, many friends, power),
1383a3	nor those who think they have already suffered every
1383a4	terrible thing and have grown cold toward the future, like
1383a5	those already being put to death on the plank; there must be some hope underlying
1383a6	of being saved, for them to be anxious. A sign of this: fear
1383a7	makes people deliberate, and yet no one deliberates about what is hopeless.
1383a8	So, when it is better that the hearers should be afraid, one must put them
1383a9	in that state, by saying that they are the kind who might suffer (for
1383a10	others greater than they have suffered), and by showing people like them
1383a11	suffering or having suffered, and at the hands of those from whom they did not
1383a12	expect it, and things ⟨they did not expect⟩, and at times when they did not expect it. Since
1383a13	it is clear what fear is, and what fearful things are, and
1383a14	in what state each kind of people are afraid, it is clear from this too what
1383a15	confidence is, and about what things people are confident, and in what
1383a16	state they are confident; for confidence is the opposite of
1383a17	⟨fear, and what inspires confidence of⟩ what is fearful, so that it is hope, with an image,
1383a18	of what brings safety as near, and of fearful things ⟨as⟩ either
1383a19	nonexistent or far off. Things inspire confidence when terrible things are far
1383a20	off and safe things near, and if there are means of putting things right and
1383a21	sources of help, many or great or both, and if people have neither been wronged nor
1383a22	done wrong, and if they have either no rivals at all, or none that
1383a23	have power, or rivals who have power are friends or have done them
1383a24	good or received good from them, or if those who have the same interests are more numerous
1383a25	or stronger, or both. And people themselves are confident in this state:
1383a26	if they think they have often succeeded and have not suffered,
1383a27	or if they have often met with terrible things and escaped them;
1383a28	for people become unmoved in two ways, either by
1383a29	lack of trial or by having means of help, as in dangers at
1383a30	sea those inexperienced in storms are confident about what is
1383a31	to come, and so are those who have means of help through their experience. And
1383a32	when something is not fearful to their equals, nor to their inferiors and those
1383a33	they think themselves superior to; and they think so of those they have mastered, either themselves
1383a34	or their superiors or their equals. And if they think they possess
1383a35	more and greater advantages, superiority in which makes men fearful;
1383b1	these are abundance of money, strength of bodies, of friends,
1383b2	of land, and of the equipment for war, either all of it or
1383b3	the most important. And if they have wronged no one, or not
1383b4	many, or not those from whom they fear something; and in general if their relations with
1383b5	the gods are good, both in other respects and in what comes from signs
1383b6	and oracles; for anger inspires confidence, and not doing wrong but
1383b7	being wronged produces anger, and the divine is supposed to help
1383b8	those who are wronged. And when, as they set about something, they think they will either suffer nothing
1383b9	[nor will suffer] or will succeed. About
1383b10	fearful things and things that inspire confidence, then, enough has been said.
