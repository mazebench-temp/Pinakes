1397a7	One topic of demonstrative enthymemes is from opposites;
1397a8	for one must see whether the opposite belongs to the opposite,
1397a9	refuting if it does not belong, establishing if it does:
1397a10	for instance, that self-control is good; for
1397a11	licentiousness is harmful. Or as in the Messenian Speech: “For if
1397a12	war is the cause of our present evils, it is with
1397a13	peace that we must set them right.” “For if
1397a14	it is not just to fall into anger even with those who have done ill
1397a15	unwillingly, then neither, if someone under compulsion
1397a16	does good to another, is it fitting that gratitude be owed
1397a17	to him.” “But if among mortals it is possible
1397a18	to tell persuasive lies, you must believe the opposite too,
1397a19	that many unbelievable truths befall mortals.”
1397a20	Another is from like inflections; for they must alike belong or
1397a21	not belong: for instance, that the just is not altogether good; for then
1397a22	what is done justly would be good too, but as it is, dying justly is not desirable.
1397a23	Another is from things relative to each other; for if acting nobly
1397a24	or justly belongs to the one, being so treated belongs to the other; and if giving the order,
1397a25	so does having carried it out: as the tax-farmer Diomedon
1397a26	said about the taxes, “For if selling is not shameful for you, neither
1397a27	is buying for us.” And if being nobly or justly treated belongs to the one who suffered,
1397a28	so does acting so to the one who did it. But here one can reason falsely;
1397a29	for if a man suffered something justly, it was perhaps not
1397a30	at your hands; that is why one must
1397a31	examine separately whether
1397a32	the sufferer deserved to suffer
1397b1	and whether the doer deserved to do it, and then use whichever argument fits; for sometimes
1397b2	the two disagree, and nothing prevents it, as in the
1397b3	Alcmaeon of Theodectes: “But did no mortal hate
1397b4	your mother?” And he answers, “One must make a distinction and look into it”;
1397b5	and when Alphesiboea asks how, he takes her up and says, “They
1397b6	judged that she should die, but not that I should kill her.” And the trial about
1397b7	Demosthenes and those who killed Nicanor: since
1397b8	they were judged to have killed him justly, it seemed that he had died justly.
1397b9	And in the case of the man who died at Thebes, about whom the defendant asks
1397b10	that it be judged whether he deserved to die, since it was not unjust
1397b11	to kill one who died justly. Another is from the more
1397b12	and the less: for instance, “If not even the gods know everything,
1397b13	hardly can men”; for this amounts to: “If something does not belong where it would be more likely
1397b14	to belong, clearly it does not belong where it is less likely.” And the argument that
1397b15	a man who beats even his father beats his neighbors comes from: “If the
1397b16	less ⟨likely⟩ belongs, the more likely belongs too”; for people
1397b17	beat their fathers less than their neighbors. Either, then, it is so, or if
1397b18	it does not belong where it is more likely, or if it belongs where it is less likely, ⟨it belongs⟩,
1397b19	whichever one must show, whether that it belongs or that it does not. Further, if
1397b20	it is neither more nor less; whence it has been said, “Your father
1397b21	is pitiable, for he lost his children; and is not Oeneus, then, who lost
1397b22	[the] glorious son [of Greece]?” And that if not even
1397b23	Theseus did wrong, neither did Alexander; and if not the
1397b24	sons of Tyndareus, neither did Alexander; and if Hector did not in killing Patroclus,
1397b25	neither did Alexander in killing Achilles. And if no other craftsmen
1397b26	are worthless, neither are philosophers. And if
1397b27	generals are not worthless because they are often put to death, neither are
1397b28	sophists. And that “If a private citizen must care for your
1397b29	good opinion, you must care for that of the Greeks.”
1397b30	Another is from considering the time, as Iphicrates said in the speech against Harmodius: “If before I acted I had asked
1397b31	to receive the statue if I did it, you would have given it; will you not give it now that I have done it? Do not, then, promise
1397b32	when you are expecting something, and take away when you have received it.” And again, about the Thebans letting Philip pass through into Attica,
1398a1	that if he had asked before he helped them against the Phocians, they would have promised;
1398a2	it would be absurd, then, if because he let it pass and trusted them, they will not let him through.
1398a3	Another is from what has been said turned against the one who said it,
1398a4	as in the Teucer. Different is the method that
1398a5	Iphicrates used against Aristophon: he asked him whether he would betray the
1398a6	ships for money; and when he said no, he said, “You, being
1398a7	Aristophon, would not betray them; and would I, being Iphicrates?” But
1398a8	the opponent must be the one who would seem more likely to do wrong; if not,
1398a9	it would look absurd, ⟨as⟩ if, when Aristides was the accuser,
1398a10	someone else were to say this to cast doubt on the accuser;
1398a11	for in general the accuser claims to be better than the
1398a12	defendant; and this, then, must be refuted. In general a man is absurd
1398a13	when he rebukes others for what he does or would do himself,
1398a14	or urges them to do what he does not do and would not do himself.
1398a15	Another is from definition: for instance, what the divine sign is: “Is it a god
1398a16	or a god's work? Yet whoever thinks it a god's work must
1398a17	think that gods exist too.” And as Iphicrates argued
1398a18	that the best man is the most nobly born; for Harmodius and
1398a19	Aristogeiton had nothing of nobility before they did a deed of
1398a20	nobility. And that he was himself more akin to them: “At any rate my deeds
1398a21	are more akin to those of Harmodius and Aristogeiton
1398a22	than yours are.” And as in the Alexander, that all would agree
1398a23	that the disorderly are not content with the enjoyment of one body.
1398a24	And why Socrates said he would not go to Archelaus: for he said
1398a25	it was outrage to be unable to repay benefits alike
1398a26	as well as injuries. For all these
1398a27	define and grasp what a thing is, and
1398a28	reason about what they are speaking of. Another is from
1398a29	the number of senses, as in the Topics about “rightly.”
1398a30	Another is from division: for instance, if all men do wrong for three reasons
1398a31	(for this reason or this or this), and for
1398a32	two of them it is impossible, and for the third not even the accusers claim it.
1398a33	Another is from induction: for instance, from the Peparethian woman, that about
1398a34	their children women everywhere determine the truth;
1398b1	for at Athens, when Mantias the orator
1398b2	disputed with his son, the mother declared the truth,
1398b3	and at Thebes, when Ismenias and Stilbon
1398b4	disputed, the woman of Dodona showed that he was Ismenias'
1398b5	son, and for this reason they held Thettaliscus to be Ismenias' son.
1398b6	And again from the Law of Theodectes: “If people do not hand over their own horses
1398b7	to those who have taken bad care of other people's,
1398b8	nor ⟨their own ships⟩ to those who have capsized other people's ships,
1398b9	then, if it is the same in all cases, one should not use those who have guarded
1398b10	other people's safety badly for one's own.”
1398b11	And as Alcidamas argued that all men honor the wise: “The Parians
1398b12	at any rate have honored Archilochus, though he was a slanderer, and the Chians
1398b13	Homer, though he was not a citizen, and the Mytilenaeans Sappho, though she
1398b14	was a woman, and the Lacedaemonians made Chilon one of their elders,
1398b15	though they are the least fond of letters, and the Italiots honored Pythagoras,
1398b16	and the Lampsacenes buried Anaxagoras, though he was a foreigner, and honor him
1398b17	still to this day, and the Athenians prospered when they used the laws of Solon,
1398b18	and the Lacedaemonians when they used those of Lycurgus, and at Thebes,
1398b19	as soon as the leaders became philosophers, the city
1398b20	prospered.” Another is from a judgment on the same or a like
1398b21	or an opposite question, above all if everyone always judged so; if
1398b22	not, at least most people, or the wise, either all or
1398b23	most of them, or the good, or if the judges themselves did,
1398b24	or those whom the judges accept, or those against whom one cannot
1398b25	judge, for instance those in authority, or those against whom it is not noble
1398b26	to judge, for instance gods or a father or teachers: as
1398b27	Autocles said against Mixidemides, that it was right for the Dread Goddesses
1398b28	to submit to justice on the Areopagus, but not for Mixidemides.
1398b29	Or as Sappho said, that dying is an evil; for the gods
1398b30	have so judged, or they would die. Or as Aristippus
1398b31	said to Plato, when he spoke, as he thought, rather too boastfully: “Well,
1398b32	our friend at any rate,” he said, “said nothing of the sort,” meaning
1398b33	Socrates. And Hegesipolis at Delphi, after first consulting the oracle
1398b34	at Olympia, asked the god whether he thought the same as his father,
1399a1	since it would be shameful to say the opposite. And about Helen,
1399a2	as Isocrates wrote that she was good, if Theseus chose her;
1399a3	and about Alexander, that the goddesses preferred him; and
1399a4	about Evagoras, that he was good, as Isocrates says:
1399a5	“Conon at any rate, in his misfortune, passing over all the others,
1399a6	came to Evagoras.” Another is from the parts,
1399a7	as in the Topics, what kind of motion the soul is; for it is this
1399a8	or that. An example from the Socrates of
1399a9	Theodectes: “Against what sanctuary has he committed impiety? Which of the gods
1399a10	that the city recognizes has he not honored?” Another, since in
1399a11	most cases it happens that something good and something bad follow
1399a12	the same thing, is to exhort or
1399a13	dissuade, accuse or defend, praise or
1399a14	blame from the consequence: for instance, “Envy follows education,
1399a15	and that is ⟨an⟩ evil, but being wise is a good; so one should not
1399a16	be educated, for one should not be envied; rather, one should be educated,
1399a17	for one should be wise.” This topic is the art of Callippus,
1399a18	with the addition of the possible and the other things mentioned.
1399a19	Another, when one must exhort or dissuade about two
1399a20	opposed things, is to use the method stated before for
1399a21	both. The difference is that there any things at all
1399a22	are set against each other, here opposites: for instance, a priestess would not let her son
1399a23	speak to the assembly: “For if you say what is just,” she said, “men
1399a24	will hate you, and if what is unjust, the gods; rather, one should
1399a25	speak to the assembly; for if you say what is just, the gods will
1399a26	love you, and if what is unjust, men.” This
1399a27	is the same as the saying about buying the marsh and the salt;
1399a28	and this is blaisōsis, when good and bad follow each of two
1399a29	opposites, each opposite to the other.
1399a30	Another, since people do not praise the same things openly and
1399a31	in secret, but in public they praise most the just and the noble,
1399a32	while in private they rather wish for what is
1399a33	advantageous, is to try to infer one of the two from these;
1399a34	for among paradoxes this topic is the most effective.
1399a35	Another is from the fact that things correspond by analogy: for instance,
1399a36	Iphicrates, when they tried to compel his son, who was under age,
1399a37	to perform a public service because he was tall, said
1399a38	that if they consider tall boys men,
1399a39	they will vote that short men are boys;
1399b1	and Theodectes in the Law: “You make citizens
1399b2	of mercenaries such as Strabax and Charidemus
1399b3	because of their decency; will you not make exiles of those
1399b4	among the mercenaries who have done irreparable wrongs?”
1399b5	Another is that if the consequence is the same, the things
1399b6	from which it follows are the same: for instance, Xenophanes said that those who say
1399b7	the gods are born are as impious as those who say they
1399b8	die; for both ways it follows that at some time the gods do
1399b9	not exist. And in general to take the consequence of each as
1399b10	always the same: “You are going to judge not about
1399b11	Isocrates but about a pursuit, whether one should practice philosophy.”
1399b12	And that giving earth and water is slavery,
1399b13	and that sharing in the common peace is doing what is
1399b14	commanded. One must take whichever of the two is useful.
1399b15	Another is from the fact that people do not always choose the same things later and earlier,
1399b16	but the reverse: for instance, this enthymeme: “When we were in exile
1399b17	we fought to return; now that we have returned, shall we go into exile
1399b18	so as not to fight?” For at one time they chose to stay at home at the cost of fighting,
1399b19	at another not to fight at the cost of not staying.
1399b20	Another is to say that the purpose for which something might be or come about
1399b21	is the purpose for which it is or has come about: for instance, if someone
1399b22	were to give a man something in order to cause him pain by taking it away; whence
1399b23	this has been said: “To many the deity gives
1399b24	great good fortune, not out of goodwill, but
1399b25	so that they may suffer more conspicuous disasters.” And
1399b26	the lines from the Meleager of Antiphon: “Not
1399b27	to kill the beast, but to be witnesses to the valor
1399b28	of Meleager before Greece.” And the argument from the
1399b29	Ajax of Theodectes, that Diomedes chose
1399b30	Odysseus not to honor him but so that his follower might be inferior;
1399b31	for it is possible that he did it for this reason. Another, common
1399b32	to litigants and advisers alike,
1399b33	is to consider what exhorts and dissuades, and
1399b34	the reasons why people act and avoid acting; for these
1399b35	are the things which, if they are present, one should act, and if they are not,
1399b36	not act: for instance, if it is possible and easy and beneficial,
1399b37	either to oneself or to friends, or harmful to enemies, or, if it is costly,
1399b38	if the cost is less than the gain; and people are exhorted
1399b39	from these, and dissuaded from the opposites.
1400a1	From these same things people both accuse and
1400a2	defend: they defend from what dissuades,
1400a3	and accuse from what exhorts. This topic
1400a4	is the whole art both of Pamphilus and of Callippus.
1400a5	Another is from things that are thought to happen but are incredible: that people would not
1400a6	have thought so if it were not so, or nearly so. And that it is all the more likely; for people
1400a7	suppose either what is or what is probable; if, then, it is incredible and not
1400a8	probable, it would be true; for it is not because it is probable and persuasive
1400a9	that it seems so: as Androcles of Pitthus said when he was accusing
1400a10	the law, and they shouted him down when he said, “The laws
1400a11	need a law to correct them; so too fish
1400a12	need salt, though it is neither probable nor persuasive that creatures raised in brine
1400a13	should need salt, and olive-cakes need oil, though
1400a14	it is incredible that what oil comes from should need oil.”
1400a15	Another, refutative, is to look at what is inconsistent, if anything
1400a16	is inconsistent in places, times, actions or words,
1400a17	separately in the case of the opponent, for instance, “He says
1400a18	he loves you, yet he conspired with the Thirty”; separately
1400a19	in one's own case, “He says I am litigious, yet he cannot
1400a20	show that I have ever brought a single suit”; and separately in the case of
1400a21	oneself and the opponent, “This man
1400a22	has never lent anything, while I have even ransomed many of you.”
1400a23	Another, for men and things that have been slandered
1400a24	beforehand, or seem to have been, is to state the cause of the paradox;
1400a25	for there is something because of which it appears so: for instance, a woman had passed off
1400a26	her own son as another's, and because she embraced him she seemed to be living with the
1400a27	youth; but when the cause was stated the slander was dissolved;
1400a28	and as in the Ajax of Theodectes, Odysseus tells
1400a29	Ajax why, though he is braver than Ajax, he does not seem so.
1400a30	Another is from the cause: if it is present, that the thing is, and if it is not
1400a31	present, that it is not; for the cause and that of which it is the cause go together, and
1400a32	without a cause nothing exists: as Leodamas said in his defense,
1400a33	when Thrasybulus accused him of having had his name on a stele
1400a34	on the Acropolis, but of having had it cut out under the Thirty: he said
1400a35	it was impossible; for the Thirty would have trusted him more if his enmity
1400a36	toward the people had been inscribed. Another is to see whether it was possible to do better otherwise, or
1400a37	is possible, in the matters on which a man advises or acts or has acted; for it is clear that
1400b1	if [not] so, he has not done it; for no one willingly
1400b2	and knowingly chooses what is bad. But this
1400b3	is false; for often it becomes clear later
1400b4	how one could have acted better, though it was unclear before.
1400b5	Another, when something is about to be done that is opposite to what has been done,
1400b6	is to look at both together: as Xenophanes advised the Eleates, when they asked whether they should sacrifice
1400b7	to Leucothea and lament her or not, that if they
1400b8	suppose her a goddess they should not lament her, and if a woman, they should not sacrifice to her. Another
1400b9	topic is to accuse or defend from the mistakes made:
1400b10	as in the Medea of Carcinus some accuse her of having
1400b11	killed her children, since at any rate they are not to be seen (for
1400b12	Medea made a mistake in sending the children away), and she
1400b13	defends herself by saying that she would have killed not the children but Jason;
1400b14	for it would have been a mistake not to do this, if she
1400b15	had really done the other. This topic of
1400b16	enthymeme, and this kind, is the whole of Theodorus' former
1400b17	art. Another is from the name: as Sophocles,
1400b18	“Clearly of iron, and bearing the name”; and as people
1400b19	usually speak in the praises of the gods; and as
1400b20	Conon called Thrasybulus the bold in counsel, and Herodicus said to Thrasymachus,
1400b21	“You are always bold in fight,” and to Polus, “You are always a colt,” and
1400b22	of Draco the lawgiver, that his laws were not a man's
1400b23	but a dragon's (for they were harsh); and as Euripides' Hecuba says of
1400b24	Aphrodite, “And rightly does the goddess's name begin with folly,”
1400b25	and as Chaeremon says, “Pentheus, named for the grief to come.”
1400b26	Of enthymemes, the refutative are more admired than the
1400b27	demonstrative, because the refutative enthymeme is a bringing together
1400b28	of opposites in a small space, and things side by side are clearer
1400b29	to the hearer. Of all syllogisms, refutative and
1400b30	demonstrative, those are most applauded
1400b31	whose conclusion people foresee from the start, provided they are not superficial (for at the same time
1400b32	they are pleased with themselves for anticipating it), and those
1400b33	in which they are so far behind that they understand as soon as they are stated.
