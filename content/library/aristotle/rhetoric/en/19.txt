1380b35	Whom people love and hate, and why, let us say after defining friendship and
1380b36	loving. Let loving be
1380b37	wishing for someone what one thinks good, for his sake and not for one's own,
1381a1	and being disposed, so far as one can, to bring it about. A friend is
1381a2	one who loves and is loved in return; and people think they are friends when they
1381a3	think they are so disposed toward each other. On these assumptions,
1381a4	a friend must be one who shares one's pleasure in good things and one's pain in
1381a5	painful ones, not for any other reason but for the friend's sake; for when what they wish
1381a6	comes about, everyone rejoices, and at the opposite they are pained,
1381a7	so that pains and pleasures are a sign of one's wish. And those
1381a8	for whom the same things are good and bad, and those who are friends to the same people and
1381a9	enemies to the same people; for they must wish the same things, so that
1381a10	one who wishes for another what he wishes for himself appears to be his friend.
1381a11	And people love those who have done good to them or to those they care for, either
1381a12	if the benefits were great, or given eagerly, or at such critical moments, and
1381a13	for their sake; or those whom they think willing to do them good. And
1381a14	the friends of their friends, and those who love whom they themselves love. And those
1381a15	who are loved by those whom they love. And those who are enemies
1381a16	to the same people and hate whom they themselves hate, and those
1381a17	hated by those whom they themselves hate; for to all of these the
1381a18	same things appear good as to themselves, so that they wish what is
1381a19	good for them, which was the mark of a friend. Further, those who are beneficent with
1381a20	money and in matters of safety; that is why people honor the generous and the brave
1381a21	and the just; and they take to be such those
1381a22	who do not live off others; such are those who live by their work,
1381a23	and among these the farmers, and among the rest above all those who work with their own hands.
1381a24	And the self-controlled, because they are not unjust. And those who mind their own business,
1381a25	for the same reason. And those whose friends we wish to be, if they appear
1381a26	to wish it too; such are those who are good in virtue and
1381a27	those of good repute, either among all or among the best or among those
1381a28	admired by us or among those who admire us. Further,
1381a29	those who are pleasant to live and spend the day with; such are
1381a30	the good-tempered, those who are not given to censuring our errors, and those who are not
1381a31	contentious or quarrelsome (for all such people are combative,
1381a32	and those who fight appear to wish the opposite of what we wish), and those who are
1381a33	deft both at teasing and at taking a tease; for in both ways
1381a34	they aim at the same thing as their neighbor, being able to take a joke
1381a35	and joking in good taste. And those who praise the good qualities we have,
1381a36	and especially those we are afraid we do not have.
1381b1	And those who are clean in their appearance, in their dress, in
1381b2	their whole way of life. And those who do not reproach us either with our
1381b3	errors or with their benefits; for both are censorious.
1381b4	And those who do not bear grudges or nurse
1381b5	their complaints, but are easily reconciled; for as they suppose them to be
1381b6	toward others, so they think they will be toward themselves. And
1381b7	those who are not slanderers and know neither their neighbors' faults
1381b8	nor ours, but only the good things; for the good man does this. And
1381b9	those who do not resist us when we are angry or in earnest;
1381b10	for such people are combative. And those who are
1381b11	seriously disposed toward us in some way, for instance who admire us and think us
1381b12	good and enjoy our company, and who feel this
1381b13	most about the things in which we ourselves most wish either
1381b14	to be admired or to seem good or pleasant. And those who are
1381b15	like us and have the same pursuits, if they do not get in our way and our
1381b16	living does not come from the same source; for then it becomes “potter
1381b17	against potter.” And those who desire the same things, if it is possible
1381b18	for both to share in them at once; if not, the same thing happens here too.
1381b19	And those toward whom people are so disposed as not to feel shame at what offends
1381b20	opinion, provided they do not despise them. And those before whom they feel shame at what offends
1381b21	truth. And those with whom they compete for honor, or by whom
1381b22	they wish to be emulated and not envied: these they either love
1381b23	or wish to be their friends. And those whom they help to get good things,
1381b24	if they themselves are not going to suffer greater evils from it. And those who love
1381b25	the absent and the present alike; that is why everyone loves those
1381b26	who are such toward the dead. And in general
1381b27	those who are very fond of their friends and do not abandon them; for among the good
1381b28	people love most those who are good at loving. And those who do not
1381b29	pretend with them; such are those who tell even their own
1381b30	faults; for it has been said that before friends we do not feel shame at what offends
1381b31	opinion; if, then, one who feels shame does not love,
1381b32	one who does not feel shame is like one who loves. And those who are not fearful,
1381b33	and in whom we have confidence; for no one loves the man he fears. The kinds
1381b34	of friendship are comradeship, intimacy, kinship and all such.
1381b35	What produces friendship is a favor, and doing it unasked, and not making it known
1381b36	when one has done it; for then it appears to be done for the friend's own sake and not for some other reason.
1382a1	As for enmity and hating, it is clear that they can be studied
1382a2	from the opposites. What produces enmity is anger, spite,
1382a3	slander. Anger comes from things done to oneself, but enmity arises even
1382a4	without anything done to oneself; for if we suppose a man to be of a certain sort,
1382a5	we hate him. And anger is always about particulars, for instance
1382a6	against Callias or Socrates, but hatred is also against classes; for
1382a7	everyone hates the thief and the informer. And the one is curable by time,
1382a8	the other incurable. And the one aims at causing pain, the other at causing harm;
1382a9	for the angry man wishes the other to perceive it, but to the other it makes no difference.
1382a10	All painful things are perceptible, but the greatest evils are
1382a11	least perceptible: injustice and folly; for the presence of vice
1382a12	causes no pain. And the one is accompanied by pain, the other is not
1382a13	accompanied by pain; for the angry man is pained, but the one who hates is not. And
1382a14	the one might feel pity if much befell his enemy, the other never; for the one
1382a15	wishes the man he is angry with to suffer in return, the other wishes him not to exist.
1382a16	It is clear from this, then, that it is possible to show that people are enemies and
1382a17	friends when they are, and to make them so when they are not,
1382a18	and to break down their claims when they say they are, and, when they dispute through anger or enmity,
1382a19	to bring them over to whichever side one chooses.
