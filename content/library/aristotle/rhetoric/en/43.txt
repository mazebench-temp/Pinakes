1404b1	Let those matters, then, be taken as studied, and let the excellence of style be defined as being clear
1404b2	(for speech is a kind of sign, and if it does not make its meaning plain it will not
1404b3	do its own work), and neither mean nor above the
1404b4	dignity of the subject, but appropriate; for the poetic style is perhaps not mean,
1404b5	but it is not appropriate to prose. Of nouns and verbs,
1404b6	the standard ones make the style clear, and it is made not mean but ornamented
1404b7	by the other words that have been discussed in the books on poetics;
1404b8	for departing from the ordinary makes it seem more dignified. For as
1404b9	people feel toward strangers and toward fellow citizens,
1404b10	so they feel toward style too. That is why one should make one's language
1404b11	foreign; for people are admirers of what is far off, and the marvelous
1404b12	is pleasant. In verse many things are done in this way,
1404b13	and it suits there (for the things and the persons the speech is about
1404b14	are further removed from the ordinary), but in bare prose far less,
1404b15	for the subject is humbler; even in poetry, if a slave
1404b16	or a very young man used fine language it would be rather inappropriate, or if it were about very small matters;
1404b17	but even in these cases what is appropriate contracts and expands.
1404b18	That is why speakers must do it without being noticed, and not seem
1404b19	to speak artificially but naturally (for the natural is persuasive,
1404b20	the artificial the opposite; people grow suspicious of it, as of a man
1404b21	plotting against them, as they do of mixed wines), and the style should be
1404b22	like the voice of Theodorus compared with those of the other actors:
1404b23	his seems to be the voice of the speaker himself, theirs the voices of others.
1404b24	The art is well concealed if one composes by picking words from ordinary
1404b25	conversation; this is what Euripides does, and he was the first
1404b26	to show the way. Since speech is composed of nouns and verbs,
1404b27	and nouns have as many kinds as
1404b28	have been studied in the books on poetry, of these, rare words,
1404b29	double words and coined words should be used seldom and in few places
1404b30	(where, we shall say later; why has been said:
1404b31	they depart from the appropriate in the direction of excess); the standard
1404b32	and the proper word and metaphor are the only ones useful for the style of
1404b33	bare prose. A sign of this is that these are the only ones everyone
1404b34	uses; for everyone converses with metaphors and with proper
1404b35	and standard words. So it is clear that if one composes well,
1404b36	there will be something unfamiliar, the art can escape notice, and the meaning will be clear;
1404b37	and this, we said, is the excellence of rhetorical speech. Of
1404b38	words, homonyms are useful to the sophist (for it is with
1404b39	these that he does his mischief), and synonyms to the poet.
1405a1	By words that are standard and synonymous I mean, for example, “proceed” and
1405a2	“walk”; for both of these are standard words and synonymous
1405a3	with each other. What each of these is, and how many kinds
1405a4	of metaphor there are, and that it counts for most both in poetry and
1405a5	in prose, has been said, as we were saying, in
1405a6	the books on poetics; but in prose one must take all the more
1405a7	pains over metaphors, as prose has fewer resources
1405a8	than verse. Clarity, pleasantness and the unfamiliar
1405a9	belong to metaphor above all, and it cannot be borrowed from
1405a10	anyone else. Epithets and metaphors must be fitting
1405a11	when one uses them. This will come from analogy; otherwise
1405a12	they will seem inappropriate, because opposites are most apparent
1405a13	side by side. One must consider: as a scarlet cloak suits a young man, so what suits an old one?
1405a14	(For the same clothing is not appropriate to both.) And if you want to adorn,
1405a15	you should take the metaphor from the best things in the same genus,
1405a16	and if you want to blame, from the worse. I mean, for example,
1405a17	since opposites are in the same genus, to say that the man
1405a18	who begs prays, and that the man who prays begs, since both
1405a19	are requests, is to do what has been said; as Iphicrates called Callias
1405a20	a begging priest of the Mother and not a torchbearer, and Callias said he must be uninitiated,
1405a21	or he would not have called him a begging priest but a torchbearer;
1405a22	both have to do with a god, but the one is honored and the other is not.
1405a23	And someone calls actors “flatterers of Dionysus”, but they call themselves “artists”
1405a24	(both of these are metaphors, the one of people who would sully, the other
1405a25	the opposite); and pirates now call themselves
1405a26	“purveyors”. (So one may say that the man who did wrong
1405a27	erred, or that the man who erred did wrong, and that the thief
1405a28	both “took” and “procured”.) But to say, as Telephus
1405a29	does in Euripides, “ruling over the oar, and landing in Mysia,”
1405a30	is inappropriate, because “ruling” is grander than the subject deserves; so the art is not
1405a31	concealed. There is also error in the syllables, if they are not signs of a pleasant
1405a32	sound: so Dionysius the Brazen calls
1405a33	poetry “the scream of Calliope” in his elegies, because both are
1405a34	sounds; but the metaphor is bad †with sounds that mean nothing†. Again,
1405a35	one should not take metaphors from far off, but from things akin
1405a36	and of the same species, transferring a name to things that have none
1405a37	in such a way that, once it is said, it is clear that it is akin
1405b1	(as in the well-liked riddle, “I saw a man who with fire had welded bronze
1405b2	upon a man”; for the process has no name, but both are a kind of
1405b3	application, and so he called the applying of the cupping glass a welding); and in general
1405b4	good metaphors can be taken from well-made riddles; for metaphors
1405b5	are riddles, so it is clear that the transfer is well made. And
1405b6	they should come from beautiful things. The beauty of a word lies, as Licymnius
1405b7	says, in its sounds or in its meaning, and its ugliness
1405b8	likewise. There is also a third point, which refutes the sophistic argument: it is not
1405b9	true, as Bryson said, that no one uses foul language, if it means the same
1405b10	to say this instead of that; this is false;
1405b11	for one word is more standard than another, and more like the thing,
1405b12	and more proper to it, by setting the thing before the eyes. Again,
1405b13	this word and that do not signify a thing in the same light, so that in this way too one
1405b14	must be counted more beautiful or more ugly than another; both signify the beautiful
1405b15	or the ugly, but not in so far as it is beautiful, or not in so far as it is
1405b16	ugly; or if they do, then more and less so. Metaphors
1405b17	should be taken from here: from things beautiful in sound or in
1405b18	force or to sight or to some other sense. It makes a difference
1405b19	to say, for example, “rosy-fingered Dawn” rather than “crimson-fingered”,
1405b20	or, worse still, “red-fingered”. In epithets too
1405b21	one can make the application from what is bad or shameful,
1405b22	as “the matricide”, or from what is better, as “the
1405b23	avenger of his father”. And Simonides, when the victor
1405b24	with the mule team offered him a small fee, would not write the poem, as if
1405b25	disgusted at writing about half-asses; but when
1405b26	he was given enough, he wrote, “Hail, daughters of storm-footed
1405b27	mares!” And yet they were daughters of asses
1405b28	too. Again, there is the use of diminutives. The
1405b29	diminutive is what makes both the bad
1405b30	and the good less, as Aristophanes jokes
1405b31	in the Babylonians, “goldlet” for “gold”,
1405b32	“cloaklet” for “cloak”, “abuselet” for
1405b33	“abuse”, and “diseaselet” for “disease”.
1405b34	But in both one must be careful and observe due measure.
