1355b25	Let rhetoric, then, be a capacity to see, in each case, the
1355b26	possible means of persuasion. For this is the function of no other
1355b27	art: each of the others teaches and persuades about
1355b28	its own subject matter, as medicine does about
1355b29	what is healthy and what causes disease, geometry about the properties
1355b30	that belong to magnitudes, arithmetic about numbers,
1355b31	and likewise the rest of the arts and sciences;
1355b32	but rhetoric seems able to see what is persuasive about,
1355b33	so to speak, whatever is given. That is why we say that its
1355b34	art is not concerned with any special, defined class of things.
1355b35	Of proofs, some are non-artistic and some artistic. By non-artistic
1355b36	I mean those that are not provided by us but were there beforehand,
1355b37	such as witnesses, tortures, contracts and the like; by artistic,
1355b38	those that can be built by the method and by ourselves;
1355b39	so that of these the first have only to be used, the second have to be found.
1356a1	Of the proofs furnished by the speech there are three kinds.
1356a2	Some lie in the character of the speaker, some
1356a3	in putting the hearer into a certain frame of mind, and some in the speech
1356a4	itself, by showing or seeming to show something. Through
1356a5	character, when the speech is so spoken as to make the speaker
1356a6	worthy of belief; for we believe decent people
1356a7	more fully and more readily on all matters in general, and where there is
1356a8	no certainty but room for doubt, completely. But this too
1356a9	must come about through the speech, and not through a prior
1356a10	opinion of what sort of man the speaker is. For we do not, as
1356a11	some of the writers on the art do, leave out of the art
1356a12	the decency of the speaker, as if it contributed nothing to persuasiveness;
1356a13	on the contrary, character is, one might almost say, the most authoritative proof.
1356a14	Through the hearers, when they are led into emotion by the speech;
1356a15	for we do not give the same judgments when we are pained and
1356a16	when we are glad, or when we love and when we hate. It is to this alone,
1356a17	we say, that the present writers on the art try to devote themselves. These
1356a18	matters will be made clear one by one when we speak about the
1356a19	emotions. Through the speech people are persuaded when we show the truth
1356a20	or what seems true from what is persuasive in each case. Since
1356a21	proofs come about through these, it is clear that they are to be grasped
1356a22	by one who can reason syllogistically and can study characters
1356a23	and virtues, and, third, the emotions: what
1356a24	each of the emotions is and of what sort, and from what it arises
1356a25	and how. So it turns out that rhetoric is a kind of offshoot
1356a26	of dialectic and of the study of characters, which
1356a27	it is right to call politics. That is why rhetoric slips into
1356a28	the guise of politics, and so do those who lay claim to
1356a29	it, partly through lack of education, partly through pretension,
1356a30	and partly for other human reasons. For it is a part
1356a31	of dialectic and a likeness of it, as we said at the
1356a32	beginning: neither of them is a science of how any
1356a33	defined thing stands, but they are capacities for furnishing
1356a34	arguments. About their capacity, then, and how they are related
1356a35	to each other, enough has almost been said.
1356a36	Of the proofs that work by showing or seeming
1356a37	to show, just as in dialectic
1356b1	there is induction, and syllogism, and apparent
1356b2	syllogism, so it is here too: for the
1356b3	example is an induction, the enthymeme a syllogism, and the
1356b4	apparent enthymeme an apparent syllogism. I call an enthymeme
1356b5	a rhetorical syllogism, and an example a rhetorical
1356b6	induction. All who produce proofs by showing do so
1356b7	by speaking either examples or enthymemes, and by nothing
1356b8	besides; so if in general anything at all must be shown
1356b9	either by syllogism or by induction (and this is clear to us
1356b10	from the Analytics), each of the two must be the same as each
1356b11	of these. What the difference is
1356b12	between example and enthymeme is clear from the Topics
1356b13	(for syllogism and induction were discussed there
1356b14	earlier): to show on the basis of many similar cases
1356b15	that something is so is there induction and here
1356b16	example; to show that, when certain things are so, something else follows
1356b17	beyond them because they are so, either universally or
1356b18	for the most part, is there called a syllogism and here an enthymeme.
1356b19	It is clear too that each kind of rhetorical speaking
1356b20	has its merits; for as was said in the Methodics, so
1356b21	it is with these: some speeches are full of examples,
1356b22	others of enthymemes, and likewise some orators
1356b23	favor examples and others enthymemes. Speeches that
1356b24	proceed by examples are no less persuasive, but those full of enthymemes
1356b25	are cheered more loudly. The cause [of these], and
1356b26	how each should be used, we shall state later; for now let us
1356b27	define these themselves more clearly. Since
1356b28	the persuasive is persuasive to someone, and some of it is at once
1356b29	persuasive and credible in itself, and some because it seems to be shown
1356b30	by what is so; and since no art studies the particular, as
1356b31	medicine studies not what is healthy for Socrates or for Callias but what is healthy for
1356b32	this sort of person or these sorts (for that belongs to the art, while the particular
1356b33	is infinite and cannot be known), neither will rhetoric study what is reputable
1356b34	to the individual, Socrates or Hippias, but what is reputable to such and such people,
1356b35	just as dialectic does. For dialectic too draws its conclusions not from whatever
1356b36	comes to hand (some things seem so even to those who talk nonsense), but from
1356b37	what needs argument, and rhetoric from what people are already used to deliberating about.
1357a1	Its function concerns such things as
1357a2	we deliberate about but have no arts for, and among
1357a3	hearers of the sort who cannot take in a view through many steps
1357a4	or reason from far back. We deliberate about things
1357a5	that seem capable of being either way; about things
1357a6	that cannot have happened, or be going to happen, or be, otherwise, no one deliberates
1357a7	who believes them so, for there is nothing to gain. One can
1357a8	reason and draw conclusions either from premises concluded before
1357a9	or from premises not concluded but needing a syllogism
1357a10	because they are not reputable; and of these, the first must
1357a11	be hard to follow because of their length (for the judge is assumed
1357a12	to be a simple person), and the second unpersuasive, because they are not drawn from
1357a13	what is agreed or reputable. So the enthymeme
1357a14	and the example must be concerned with things that can
1357a15	for the most part be otherwise, the example being an induction and the
1357a16	enthymeme a syllogism; and they must be drawn from few premises, often
1357a17	fewer than those of a primary syllogism. For if one of these is
1357a18	well known, it need not even be stated, since the hearer
1357a19	supplies it himself: for instance, that Dorieus has won a contest whose prize is a crown;
1357a20	it is enough to say that he has won at Olympia; that the Olympic prize
1357a21	is a crown need not even be added, for everyone knows it.
1357a22	Since few of the premises from which rhetorical syllogisms are drawn
1357a23	are necessary (for most of the things about which judgments and
1357a24	inquiries are made can also be otherwise: people deliberate and inquire about
1357a25	what they do, and all actions are of this
1357a26	kind, and none of them, so to speak, is necessary;
1357a27	and what happens for the most part and is possible must be concluded
1357a28	from other premises of the same kind, as the necessary from
1357a29	the necessary; this too is clear to us from the Analytics),
1357a30	it is clear that of the premises from which enthymemes are spoken, some
1357a31	will be necessary, but most will hold for the most part; and
1357a32	enthymemes are drawn from probabilities and from signs, so that
1357a33	each of these must be the same as each of those. For
1357a34	a probability is what happens for the most part, not
1357a35	simply, as some define it, but what, among things that
1357a36	can be otherwise, stands to that to which it is probable
1357b1	as the universal to the particular. Of signs, one
1357b2	kind stands as a particular to the universal,
1357b3	the other as a universal to the particular. Of these, the
1357b4	necessary sign is a tekmērion, while the one that is not necessary has no name
1357b5	for the difference. I call necessary the signs from which
1357b6	a syllogism comes about; and that is why such a sign
1357b7	is a tekmērion: for when people think that what has been said cannot be
1357b8	refuted, then they think they are bringing a tekmērion, as something shown and
1357b9	concluded; for tekmar and limit mean the same in
1357b10	the ancient tongue. Of signs, the one that stands as the
1357b11	particular to the universal is like this: if someone were to say
1357b12	that it is a sign that the wise are just that Socrates was wise
1357b13	and just. This is a sign, but it can be refuted even if
1357b14	what is said is true, for it is not a syllogism. But if
1357b15	someone were to say that it is a sign that a man is ill that he has a fever, or that a woman has given birth
1357b16	that she has milk, that sign is necessary. Of signs this alone is a tekmērion,
1357b17	for it alone, if it is true, cannot be refuted. The kind that stands as the
1357b18	universal to the particular is like this: if someone were to say that it is
1357b19	a sign that a man has a fever that he breathes fast. This too can be refuted
1357b20	even if it is true, for one can breathe hard
1357b21	without a fever. What a probability is, then, and what a sign and a tekmērion are,
1357b22	and how they differ, has been said here too; but they are defined more
1357b23	clearly, along with the reason why some
1357b24	are not syllogisms and others are, in the Analytics,
1357b25	where they are discussed. That the example is
1357b26	an induction, and about what kinds of things, has been said. It stands neither as
1357b27	part to whole, nor as whole to part, nor as whole
1357b28	to whole, but as part to part, like to
1357b29	like: when both fall under the same class, but one is better known
1357b30	than the other, there is an example. For instance, that in asking for the guard
1357b31	Dionysius is plotting a tyranny; for
1357b32	Pisistratus before him asked for a guard when he was plotting, and when he got it
1357b33	made himself tyrant, and so did Theagenes in Megara; and all the others they know of
1357b34	become examples for Dionysius, of whom they do not
1357b35	yet know whether he asks for this reason. All these fall under the same
1357b36	universal: that one who plots a tyranny asks for a guard.
1358a1	From what, then, the proofs that seem to be
1358a2	demonstrative are drawn, has been said. Among enthymemes the greatest difference, and
1358a3	the one that has escaped nearly everyone, is the very one that holds for
1358a4	syllogisms in the dialectical method too: some of them
1358a5	belong to rhetoric, as some syllogisms belong to the dialectical
1358a6	method, and others to other arts and
1358a7	capacities, some that exist and some not yet discovered. That is why
1358a8	they escape the hearers' notice, and those who handle them
1358a9	properly pass out of these arts. What I mean will be clearer
1358a10	if it is said at greater length. I say that dialectical
1358a11	and rhetorical syllogisms are those about which we speak of
1358a12	topics; and these are the ones common to questions of justice
1358a13	and of nature and of politics and of many things that differ
1358a14	in species, such as the topic of more and less; for it will be no
1358a15	easier to draw a syllogism or state an enthymeme from it
1358a16	about justice than about nature or anything else, though these
1358a17	differ in species. Special topics are those drawn from the premises proper to each species and
1358a18	genus; for instance, there are premises about nature from
1358a19	which there is neither enthymeme nor syllogism about ethics,
1358a20	and others about ethics from which there will be none about nature;
1358a21	and so it is with all. The common topics will not make anyone
1358a22	understand any genus, for they are about no underlying subject;
1358a23	but the better one selects the special ones [the premises],
1358a24	the more he will unawares produce a science other than dialectic and rhetoric;
1358a25	for if he hits upon first principles, it will no longer be dialectic or rhetoric
1358a26	but the science whose principles he has. Most
1358a27	enthymemes are drawn from these species,
1358a28	the particular and special ones, and fewer from the common.
1358a29	As in the Topics, then, so here we must distinguish
1358a30	the species of enthymemes and the topics from which they are to be taken.
1358a31	By species I mean the premises special to each genus,
1358a32	and by topics those common to all alike. Let us speak first, then,
1358a33	about the species; but first let us take the kinds of rhetoric,
1358a34	so that, having determined how many there are, we may take for each separately
1358a35	its elements and premises.
