1419b10	The epilogue is composed of four things: disposing the hearer well
1419b11	toward oneself and badly toward the opponent;
1419b12	amplifying and depreciating; putting
1419b13	the hearer into emotions; and recapitulation.
1419b14	For it is natural, after showing oneself truthful and the
1419b15	opponent a liar, then to praise and blame and hammer the point home.
1419b16	One should aim at one of two things: that one is good to these hearers
1419b17	or good absolutely, and that the opponent is bad to them or bad absolutely. The
1419b18	topics from which one should establish this have been given, the sources from which
1419b19	men are represented as good and as bad. The next thing,
1419b20	once the facts have been shown, is naturally to amplify or depreciate;
1419b21	for the facts must be agreed if one is going to speak of their size,
1419b22	just as the growth of bodies comes from what is there before.
1419b23	The topics for amplifying and depreciating have been set out
1419b24	before. After this, when the nature and the size of the facts are clear,
1419b25	one should bring the hearer into emotions. These are pity and
1419b26	indignation, anger and hatred, envy, emulation and strife.
1419b27	Their topics too have been given before, so that what remains
1419b28	is to recall what has been said. It is fitting to do this here, and not, as
1419b29	some wrongly say, in the proems; to make the matter
1419b30	easy to learn, they tell you to say it many times. There one should
1419b31	state the matter, so that what the judgment is about is not overlooked; here
1419b32	one should state in summary the means by which it has been shown. Begin by saying that you have
1419b33	done what you promised, so that you must say what you showed and why. This is
1419b34	done by comparison with the opponent's case. One may compare what
1419b35	both have said on the same point, or set them face to face
1419b36	(“He said this about it, and I said that,
1420a1	and for these reasons”), or use irony (as “For he
1420a2	said this, and I that”, and “What would he have done, if he had proved this
1420a3	and not that?”), or interrogation (“What, then, has been proved?”
1420a4	or “What has he proved?”). Either in this way, by comparison,
1420a5	or in the natural order, as the points were made, one's own, and then again,
1420a6	if you wish, separately, those of the opponent's speech. For the end
1420a7	the asyndetic style is fitting, so that it may be an epilogue
1420a8	and not a speech: “I have spoken; you have heard; you have the facts; judge.”
