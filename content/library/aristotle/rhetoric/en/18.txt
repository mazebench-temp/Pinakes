1380a6	Since growing angry is the opposite of growing calm, and anger
1380a7	of calmness, we must take up in what state people are calm, toward whom
1380a8	they are calm, and by what means they are calmed. Let
1380a9	calming be a settling and quieting of anger. If, then, people are angry
1380a10	with those who slight them, and a slight is voluntary, it is clear that toward those
1380a11	who do none of these things, or do them involuntarily, or appear
1380a12	to be such, they are calm. And toward those who wished the opposite of what they did.
1380a13	And toward all who are like that to themselves as well; for no one seems
1380a14	to slight himself. And toward those who admit their fault and are sorry;
1380a15	for, as though they had justice in the offender's being pained at what he has done, they cease
1380a16	from their anger. A sign of this is seen in the punishment of servants: those
1380a17	who contradict us and deny it we punish more, but toward
1380a18	those who admit that they deserve punishment we cease to be enraged. The cause
1380a19	is that denying what is evident is shamelessness, and shamelessness
1380a20	is slight and contempt; at any rate, before those we greatly despise
1380a21	we feel no shame. And toward those who humble themselves before
1380a22	them and do not contradict; for they appear to admit
1380a23	that they are inferior, and inferiors are afraid, and no one who is afraid
1380a24	slights. That anger ceases toward those who humble themselves
1380a25	even dogs show, by not biting those who
1380a26	sit down. And toward those who are serious with them when they are serious;
1380a27	for they think they are taken seriously and not despised. And toward those
1380a28	who have done them greater favors. And toward those who beg and plead with them;
1380a29	for they are humbler. And toward those who are not given to outrage or mockery
1380a30	or slight toward anyone, or not toward good men, or toward
1380a31	people such as they are themselves. In general one should look at what calms
1380a32	from the opposites. And they are not angry with those whom they fear or before whom they feel shame, as long as they are
1380a33	in that state; for it is impossible to fear and
1380a34	be angry at the same time. And with those who acted in anger they are either not angry
1380a35	or less angry; for they do not appear to have acted
1380a36	out of slight; for no one who is angry
1380a37	slights, since a slight is painless,
1380b1	but anger is accompanied by pain. And toward those who feel shame before them.
1380b2	It is clear too that those in a state opposite to anger are calm,
1380b3	as at play, in laughter, at a festival, in prosperity,
1380b4	in success, in fulfillment, in general in freedom from pain and in pleasure that is not
1380b5	outrageous, and in reasonable hope. Again, those whose anger has had time and is not
1380b6	fresh; for time stops anger. And
1380b7	a revenge taken earlier on someone else also stops a greater anger against another;
1380b8	that is why Philocrates answered well when someone, the people being
1380b9	angry, said, “Why don't you defend yourself?” “Not yet,” he said. “But when?”
1380b10	“When I see someone else slandered.” For people become calm when
1380b11	they have spent their anger on another, as happened in the case of Ergophilus:
1380b12	though they were more exasperated with him than with Callisthenes, they acquitted him because they had
1380b13	condemned Callisthenes to death the day before. And if they have convicted the man.
1380b14	And if the offenders have suffered a greater evil than the angry
1380b15	would have done to them; for they think they have as good as taken revenge. And
1380b16	if people think that they themselves are in the wrong and are suffering justly; for anger
1380b17	does not arise against what is just; for they no longer think they are suffering
1380b18	contrary to what is fitting, and that was what anger was. That is why one should first punish
1380b19	in words; for even slaves are less vexed when they are
1380b20	punished. And if people think that the other will not perceive that it is because
1380b21	of them and in return for what they suffered; for anger is against the
1380b22	individual, as is clear from the definition. That is why the poet has rightly
1380b23	written “say that Odysseus, sacker of cities,” as though he would not have
1380b24	been avenged unless the other perceived both by whom and for
1380b25	what. So people are not angry either with any others who do not perceive,
1380b26	or with the dead any longer, since they have suffered the
1380b27	utmost and will feel no pain or perception, which is what the
1380b28	angry aim at. That is why the poet spoke well about Hector,
1380b29	when he wished to stop Achilles' anger against the dead man:
1380b30	“for it is the senseless earth that he maltreats in his fury.” It is clear, then,
1380b31	that those who wish to calm others must speak from these
1380b32	topics, putting the hearers in that state, and making those with whom
1380b33	they are angry seem either fearful or people before whom one feels shame, or
1380b34	to have done them favors, or to have acted unwillingly, or to be deeply pained at what they have done.
