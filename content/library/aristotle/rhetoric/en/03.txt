1358a36	Rhetoric has three kinds in number; for there are just as many
1358a37	kinds of hearers of speeches.
1358a38	A speech is made up of three things: the speaker,
1358b1	what he speaks about, and the one to whom he speaks; and the end is directed at this last,
1358b2	I mean the hearer. The hearer must be either a spectator
1358b3	or a judge, and a judge either of what has happened or of what is to come.
1358b4	The one who judges about the future is the member of the assembly,
1358b5	the one who judges about the past is the juror, and the one who judges
1358b6	the speaker's ability is the spectator; so there must be three
1358b7	kinds of rhetorical speeches: deliberative, judicial,
1358b8	epideictic. Advice is either exhortation or
1358b9	dissuasion; for both those who advise in private and those who
1358b10	speak to the people in public always do one of these. A lawsuit is
1358b11	either accusation or defense; for the litigants must
1358b12	do one or the other. Epideictic is either
1358b13	praise or blame. Each of these has its time: for the
1358b14	adviser it is the future (for he advises about what is to be,
1358b15	exhorting or dissuading); for the litigant, the past
1358b16	(for it is always about what has been done that one accuses
1358b17	and the other defends); for the epideictic speaker, above all
1358b18	the present (for all praise or blame according to
1358b19	what is the case), though they often make use of the past too,
1358b20	recalling it, and of the future, forecasting it. Each of these
1358b21	has a different end, and as there are three, there are three ends:
1358b22	for the adviser, the advantageous and the harmful; for he who
1358b23	exhorts advises a thing as better, and he who dissuades dissuades from it as
1358b24	worse, and he brings in the other ends in support of this,
1358b25	the just or unjust, the noble or shameful. For litigants
1358b26	it is the just and the unjust, and they too bring in the others
1358b27	in support of these. For those who praise and
1358b28	blame, it is the noble and the shameful, and they too
1358b29	refer the others to these. A sign that the end stated
1358b30	is each one's end: about the other points they sometimes would not
1358b31	dispute; the litigant, for instance, may not deny that a thing happened or that
1358b32	he did harm, but that he did wrong he would never admit, for then there would be no
1358b33	need of a trial. Likewise advisers often
1358b34	give up the other points, but that they advise
1358b35	what is not advantageous or dissuade from what is useful they would never admit; but that it is
1358b36	unjust to enslave their neighbors, even those who
1358b37	have done no wrong, they often do not care at all. Likewise
1358b38	those who praise and those who blame do not consider
1359a1	whether a man did what was advantageous or harmful, but in praise
1359a2	they often count it to his credit that, heedless of his own profit,
1359a3	he did something noble: so they praise Achilles because he came to the aid of
1359a4	his companion Patroclus knowing that he must die, when he could have lived.
1359a5	For him such a death was nobler, but to live
1359a6	was advantageous. It is clear from what has been said that one must
1359a7	first have premises about these; for tekmēria,
1359a8	probabilities and signs are rhetorical premises: in general
1359a9	a syllogism is made of premises, and the enthymeme
1359a10	is a syllogism made of the premises we have described.
1359a11	And since what is impossible cannot be done or have been done,
1359a12	but only what is possible, and what did not happen or will not happen
1359a13	cannot have been done or be going to be done,
1359a14	the adviser, the litigant and
1359a15	the epideictic speaker must all have premises about the possible and the impossible,
1359a16	about whether a thing has happened or not, and whether it will be or not. Further, since
1359a17	all, whether they praise or blame, exhort or
1359a18	dissuade, accuse or defend, try to show not
1359a19	only the points mentioned but also that something is great or
1359a20	small, the good or the bad, the noble or the shameful, or
1359a21	the just or the unjust, whether speaking of things by themselves or setting them
1359a22	against one another, it is clear that one would also need premises about greatness
1359a23	and smallness and the greater and the lesser,
1359a24	both universally and about each thing: for instance, what is the greater
1359a25	or lesser good, or wrong, or just act; and likewise
1359a26	with the rest. The subjects, then, about which one must necessarily obtain
1359a27	premises have been stated; after this we must distinguish separately
1359a28	the subjects of each kind: those of advice, those
1359a29	of epideictic speeches, and third those of lawsuits.
