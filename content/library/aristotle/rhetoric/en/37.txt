1395b20	As for enthymemes, let us speak first in general about the way
1395b21	one should look for them, and after that about the topics;
1395b22	for each of these is a different kind of thing. That
1395b23	the enthymeme is a syllogism has been said
1395b24	before, and how it is a syllogism, and how it differs from the
1395b25	dialectical ones; for one should not draw the conclusion from far back, nor
1395b26	by taking in every step: the first is unclear because of the length, the second is idle talk
1395b27	because it states the obvious. This is also why the uneducated are more
1395b28	persuasive than the educated before crowds, as
1395b29	the poets say that the uneducated speak more
1395b30	musically before a crowd; for the educated state what is common and universal,
1395b31	the uneducated speak from what they know, and of what is near at hand; so one must speak not from
1395b32	all opinions but from those that are defined,
1395b33	for instance those of the judges, or of those whom the judges accept,
1396a1	and this because it is evident that this seems so to all or
1396a2	most; and one should draw conclusions not only from what is necessary,
1396a3	but also from what holds for the most part. First, then,
1396a4	one must grasp that about whatever one must speak and reason,
1396a5	whether with a political syllogism or any other, one must
1396a6	also have the facts that belong to it, either all or some; for if
1396a7	you had none you would have nothing to draw a conclusion from. I mean, for instance, how could
1396a8	we advise the Athenians whether to go to war or
1396a9	not, without knowing what their power is, whether naval
1396a10	or infantry or both, and how great, and what their revenues are, or
1396a11	their friends and enemies, and then what wars they have waged and how,
1396a12	and all the rest of the kind? Or how could we praise them, if we did not have the
1396a13	sea battle at Salamis, or the battle at Marathon, or what was done for
1396a14	the Heraclidae, or something else of the kind? For it is from the
1396a15	noble things that belong, or seem to belong, to them that all people praise.
1396a16	Likewise they blame from the opposites, looking to see what
1396a17	such thing belongs or seems to belong to them: for instance, that they
1396a18	enslaved the Greeks, and that they reduced to slavery those who had
1396a19	fought with them against the barbarian and distinguished themselves, the Aeginetans
1396a20	and Potidaeans, and all other such things, if any other
1396a21	such error belongs to them. In the same way
1396a22	accusers and defenders, looking at the facts,
1396a23	accuse and defend. It makes no difference whether it is about
1396a24	Athenians or Lacedaemonians, or a man or a god, that one does this same thing;
1396a25	for in advising Achilles, and in praising and
1396a26	blaming him, and accusing and defending him,
1396a27	one must take the facts that belong, or seem to belong, to him, so that we may speak from these,
1396a28	praising or blaming if anything noble or shameful belongs to him,
1396a29	accusing or defending if anything just or unjust,
1396a30	advising if anything advantageous or harmful. And likewise with these
1396a31	about any subject whatever, for instance about justice, whether it is good or not
1396a32	good, from what belongs to justice and to the good. So, since
1396a33	all evidently demonstrate in this way, whether they reason more precisely
1396b1	or more loosely (for they do not take premises from everything
1396b2	but from what belongs to each subject), and since through argument it is clear that
1396b3	it is impossible to demonstrate otherwise, it is evident that one must, as in the
1396b4	Topics, first have for each subject a selection of premises about what is possible
1396b5	and most opportune, and about what arises on the spur of the moment
1396b6	look in the same way, looking not to what is indefinite
1396b7	but to what belongs to the subject of the speech, and marking out
1396b8	as many facts as possible, and as close as possible to the subject; for the more
1396b9	facts one has, the easier it is to demonstrate, and the
1396b10	closer they are, the more appropriate they are and the less common. By
1396b11	common I mean praising Achilles because he is a man and one of
1396b12	the demigods and because he went on the expedition against Ilium; for these
1396b13	belong to many others too, so that such a speaker praises
1396b14	Achilles no more than Diomedes; by particular, what has happened to no one else
1396b15	but Achilles: for instance, killing Hector,
1396b16	the best of the Trojans, and Cycnus, who prevented all
1396b17	from landing, being invulnerable; and that he went on the expedition when youngest and not
1396b18	bound by the oath; and all other such things. One
1396b19	way of selection, then, and the first, is this, by topics;
1396b20	now let us speak of the elements of enthymemes; by element I mean
1396b21	the same thing as a topic of enthymeme. Let us speak first of what
1396b22	must be spoken of first. There are two kinds of enthymemes:
1396b23	some are demonstrative, that something is or is not, others
1396b24	refutative; and they differ as refutation and syllogism differ in
1396b25	dialectic. The demonstrative enthymeme
1396b26	draws its conclusion from what is agreed, the refutative
1396b27	brings together what is inconsistent. We
1396b28	have, then, pretty much the topics about each of the kinds that are useful
1396b29	and necessary; for the premises about each have been
1396b30	selected, so that the topics from which one must draw
1396b31	enthymemes about good or bad, noble or shameful,
1396b32	just or unjust, and likewise about characters, emotions and
1396b33	states, have already been grasped by us.
1397a1	Further, let us take another way, a general one about all things, and
1397a2	speak, marking off the refutative and
1397a3	demonstrative topics, and those of apparent enthymemes, which are not
1397a4	enthymemes, since they are not syllogisms either. When these
1397a5	have been made clear, let us determine about solutions and objections,
1397a6	from what sources one should bring them against enthymemes.
