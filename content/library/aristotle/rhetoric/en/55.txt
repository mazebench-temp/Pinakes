1414b19	The proem is the beginning of a speech, as the prologue is in
1414b20	poetry and the prelude in piping; for all
1414b21	these are beginnings, and as it were pave the way for what follows. The
1414b22	prelude is like the proem of epideictic speeches; for
1414b23	pipers play first whatever they can play well
1414b24	and attach it to the keynote; and in epideictic
1414b25	speeches one should write in the same way: say at once whatever one likes,
1414b26	then strike the keynote and join on, as everyone does.
1414b27	An example is the proem of Isocrates' Helen; for there is nothing
1414b28	in common between the eristics and Helen. At the same time,
1414b29	even if one strays far afield, it is fitting, and the whole speech should not be
1414b30	all of one kind. The proems of epideictic speeches are drawn from
1414b31	praise or blame (as Gorgias in the Olympic Speech: “By
1414b32	many are you worthy to be admired, men of Greece”; for he praises
1414b33	those who convened the festivals; while Isocrates blames them
1414b34	because they honored the excellences of the body with gifts, but for
1414b35	men of good sense established no prize), and from
1414b36	advice (for example, that one should honor the good, which is why
1414b37	the speaker himself praises Aristides; or should honor those
1414b38	who are neither of good repute nor worthless, but who,
1414b39	though good, are obscure, like Alexander son of Priam;
1415a1	for this is giving advice); and again from the proems of judicial speeches,
1415a2	that is, from appeals to the hearer, if the speech is about something paradoxical
1415a3	or difficult or much talked of by many, so as
1415a4	to win pardon, as Choerilus: “But now, when all has been parceled out.”
1415a5	The proems of epideictic speeches, then, come from these:
1415a6	from praise, from blame, from exhortation, from dissuasion, from
1415a7	appeals to the hearer; and the keynote must be either foreign or
1415a8	akin to the speech. As for the proems of judicial speech, one must
1415a9	grasp that they have the same effect as the prologues of plays
1415a10	and the proems of epics; for those of dithyrambs
1415a11	are like the epideictic: “For your sake, and your gifts or spoils.”
1415a12	But in prologues and epics there is a sample of the subject, so that
1415a13	the hearers may know in advance what the story is about and their mind not be left in suspense;
1415a14	for what is undefined leads it astray. So whoever puts the beginning, as it were,
1415a15	into the hearer's hand makes him hold on and follow the story.
1415a16	That is why: “Sing, goddess, the wrath.” “Tell me, Muse, of the man.”
1415a17	“Lead me to another tale, how from the land of Asia
1415a18	a great war came to Europe.” And the
1415a19	tragic poets make clear what the play is about, if not at once,
1415a20	as Euripides does in the prologue, then somewhere at least, as
1415a21	Sophocles does: “My father was Polybus.” And comedy
1415a22	does the same. This, then, is the most necessary function of the proem,
1415a23	and its own: to make clear what the end is for the sake
1415a24	of which the speech is made (and so, if the matter is clear and small,
1415a25	no proem should be used); the other kinds that people use
1415a26	are remedies, and common. They are drawn from the speaker,
1415a27	the hearer, the subject and the opponent.
1415a28	Those about oneself and the opponent are whatever serves to dispel slander
1415a29	or to create it (though not in the same way: for the defendant
1415a30	what concerns slander comes first, for the accuser in the
1415a31	epilogue; and why is not unclear: the defendant,
1415a32	when he is about to bring himself forward, must remove what stands in his way,
1415a33	so he must dispel the slander first; but the slanderer
1415a34	should slander in the epilogue, so that the hearers remember it better). Those
1415a35	aimed at the hearer come from making him well disposed, or
1415a36	angry, and sometimes attentive, or the opposite;
1415a37	for it is not always advantageous to make him attentive, which is why many try to lead him
1415a38	into laughter. Everything will lead him to learn easily,
1415a39	if one wishes, including seeming decent;
1415b1	for people attend more to such men. They attend to great things,
1415b2	to their own concerns, to marvels, to pleasant things; so one should give the impression
1415b3	that the speech is about such things. If one wants them not to attend, that the matter is small,
1415b4	that it is nothing to them, that it is painful. It must not escape us
1415b5	that all such things are outside the argument; for they are aimed at a poor
1415b6	hearer, one who listens to what is outside the subject; for
1415b7	if he is not of this kind, there is no need of a proem, except so much as to
1415b8	state the matter in summary, so that, like a body, it has
1415b9	a head. Again, making the hearers attentive is common to all the parts,
1415b10	if it is needed; for they slacken anywhere rather than at the beginning.
1415b11	So it is laughable to put it at the beginning, when everyone
1415b12	is listening most attentively; so wherever the moment comes, one should say “And
1415b13	attend to me, for this is no more my business than yours”,
1415b14	and “I will tell you something more terrible than you have ever heard, or
1415b15	more marvelous”. This is, as Prodicus said,
1415b16	when the hearers are nodding, to throw in for them a bit
1415b17	of the fifty-drachma lecture. That this is aimed at the hearer
1415b18	not in his capacity as hearer is clear; for in their proems everyone
1415b19	either slanders or dispels fears:
1415b20	“My lord, I will not say that I come in haste,” “Why
1415b21	this preamble?” So too those who have a bad case, or
1415b22	seem to; for it is better to linger anywhere rather than
1415b23	on the matter itself; that is why slaves do not answer what they are asked
1415b24	but talk around it, and make proems.
1415b25	How one should make hearers well disposed has been said, and each of the other
1415b26	such things. And since it is well said, “Grant that I
1415b27	come to the Phaeacians as a friend and one to be pitied,” these are the
1415b28	two things to aim at. In epideictic speeches one must make the hearer
1415b29	think that he is praised along with the subject, either himself
1415b30	or his family or his way of life, or in some other way; for what
1415b31	Socrates says in the funeral speech is true, that it is not hard
1415b32	to praise Athenians among Athenians, but among Lacedaemonians.
1415b33	The proems of speeches to the assembly come from those of judicial speech,
1415b34	but by its nature it has least need of them; for the hearers know what it is about,
1415b35	and the matter needs no proem, except because of
1415b36	the speaker himself or his opponents, or if the hearers do not take the matter to be as important as you
1415b37	want, but greater or less; so one must either slander
1415b38	or dispel slander, and either amplify or minimize.
1415b39	For these purposes a proem is needed, or for ornament,
1416a1	since a speech seems offhand if it has none. Such is
1416a2	Gorgias' encomium of the Eleans: without any preliminary sparring
1416a3	or warming up, he begins at once, “Elis, happy city.”
