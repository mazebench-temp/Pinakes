1377b16	The materials, then, from which one should exhort and dissuade,
1377b17	praise and blame, accuse and defend, and
1377b18	what opinions and premises are useful for the proofs of these,
1377b19	are these; for the enthymemes are about these and drawn from these,
1377b20	so to speak, for each kind of speech separately. But since
1377b21	rhetoric exists for the sake of judgment (for people judge advice,
1377b22	and a lawsuit is a judgment), one must look not only to the argument,
1377b23	that it may be demonstrative and credible, but must also present oneself
1377b24	as a certain kind of person and prepare the judge; for it makes a great difference
1377b25	to proof, above all in advice, but also in
1377b26	lawsuits, that the speaker should appear to be a certain kind of person and
1377b27	that the hearers should suppose him to be disposed in a certain way toward them, and besides
1377b28	this, if they themselves happen to be disposed in a certain way. That
1377b29	the speaker should appear to be of a certain kind is more useful for
1377b30	advice, that the hearer should be disposed in a certain way
1377b31	in lawsuits; for things do not appear the same to those who love
1377b32	and those who hate, nor to the angry and the calm,
1378a1	but either altogether different or different in magnitude: to the one who loves,
1378a2	the man he is judging seems either to do no wrong or to do little wrong; to
1378a3	the one who hates, the opposite; and to one who desires something and is hopeful,
1378a4	if what is to come is pleasant, it appears both that it will come and that it will be good; to
1378a5	one who is unmoved ⟨or⟩ annoyed, the opposite. That
1378a6	the speakers themselves are credible has three
1378a7	causes; for there are just so many things through which we believe, apart from demonstrations.
1378a8	These are practical wisdom, virtue and goodwill;
1378a9	for people speak falsely in what they say or advise either through
1378a10	all of these or through one of them: either through folly they do not form
1378a11	correct opinions, or, forming correct opinions, through depravity they do not say what they think,
1378a12	or they are practically wise and decent but not
1378a13	well disposed, and so may not advise what is best even though they know it;
1378a14	and there is nothing besides these. So one who seems to have
1378a15	all of these must be credible to his hearers. How, then,
1378a16	people may appear practically wise and good must be taken from the
1378a17	distinctions made about the virtues; for from the same materials one could
1378a18	present either another or oneself as such. About
1378a19	goodwill and friendship we must speak in the discussion of the emotions.
1378a20	The emotions are the things through which people change and differ in their judgments,
1378a21	and which are attended by pain and pleasure, such as anger, pity, fear and all other
1378a22	such, and their opposites. About each one must divide the subject into
1378a23	three: I mean, for instance, about anger, in what state people are irascible, and
1378a24	with whom they are usually angry, and at what sort of things; for if we had one or
1378a25	two of these, but not all, it would be impossible
1378a26	to produce anger; and likewise with the
1378a27	others. So, just as for the subjects discussed before
1378a28	we drew up the premises, so let us do for these too,
1378a29	and divide them in the way described.
