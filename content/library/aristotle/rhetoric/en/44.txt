1405b35	Frigidities in style arise in four ways. First, in double
1405b36	words, as in Lycophron's “the many-faced sky of the great-peaked earth”
1405b37	and “narrow-passaged shore”, and as Gorgias spoke of “beggar-muse-flatterers
1406a1	forsworn against one well-sworn”; and as Alcidamas: “filled with fury
1406a2	in his soul, and his face becoming fire-hued”;
1406a3	and he thought their zeal would be “end-fulfilling”, and
1406a4	he made the persuasion of his words “end-fulfilling”, and “azure-hued”
1406a5	the floor of the sea; for all these seem poetic because
1406a6	they are doubled. This is one cause; another is
1406a7	using rare words, as Lycophron called Xerxes “a monstrous man”
1406a8	and Sciron “a ravager of a man”, and Alcidamas said “a plaything
1406a9	for poetry”, “the reckless folly of nature”, and “whetted
1406a10	with the unmixed anger of his thought”. A third is in epithets:
1406a11	using them long, or untimely, or crowded. In
1406a12	poetry it is appropriate to say “white milk”, but in prose some are
1406a13	rather inappropriate, and others, if overdone, give the game away and make it plain
1406a14	that it is poetry. One should use them, though (for they vary
1406a15	the usual and make the style unfamiliar), but one must
1406a16	aim at due measure, since overdoing them does more harm than speaking
1406a17	carelessly: careless speech lacks the good, but excess has the bad. That is why
1406a18	the writings of Alcidamas seem frigid: he uses epithets not as a seasoning
1406a19	but as the main dish, so crowded are they,
1406a20	so long and so obvious: not “sweat” but “the
1406a21	moist sweat”; not “to the Isthmia” but “to the festival of the Isthmia”;
1406a22	not “laws” but “the laws, the kings
1406a23	of cities”; not “at a run” but “with the running impulse of his
1406a24	soul”; not “a shrine of the Muses” but “having received nature's
1406a25	shrine of the Muses”; and “the scowling care of his soul”; and not
1406a26	“craftsman of favor” but “craftsman of the people's universal favor”; and “steward
1406a27	of the hearers' pleasure”; and he hid not “with branches” but “with the branches
1406a28	of the wood”; and he covered not “his body”
1406a29	but “the shame of his body”; and “the counter-imitating desire
1406a30	of his soul” (this is at once a double word and an epithet,
1406a31	so that it becomes a poem); and “so outlandish the excess
1406a32	of his depravity”. So those who speak poetically, by being inappropriate,
1406a33	produce the laughable and the frigid, and obscurity through
1406a34	verbosity; for when one heaps words on someone who already understands, one destroys clarity
1406a35	by darkening it. People use double words
1406a36	when a thing has no name and the compound is easily formed, as
1406a37	“to time-waste”; but if this is overdone, it is wholly poetic.
1406b1	So double words are most useful to the writers of dithyrambs
1406b2	(for they are noisy), rare words to
1406b3	epic poets (for they are dignified and self-assured), and metaphor
1406b4	to iambic verse (for iambics are what poets now use, as
1406b5	has been said). And the fourth kind of frigidity arises in metaphors.
1406b6	For there are inappropriate metaphors too, some because
1406b7	they are laughable (for the comic poets use metaphors too),
1406b8	others because they are too dignified and tragic; and they are obscure if
1406b9	far-fetched, as Gorgias' “affairs pale and bloodless”, and “You
1406b10	have sown these things shamefully and reaped them badly”;
1406b11	for these are too poetic. And as Alcidamas called philosophy
1406b12	“a fortress raised against the law”, and the Odyssey “a beautiful mirror of human
1406b13	life”, and “bringing no such plaything to poetry”;
1406b14	all of these are unpersuasive, for the reasons given.
1406b15	But what Gorgias said to the swallow, when she flew over him
1406b16	and let fall her droppings, is the best of
1406b17	the tragic manner: he said, “Shameful, Philomela!” For a bird
1406b18	it was not shameful to have done it, but for a maiden it would be.
1406b19	So he rebuked her well by calling her what she was, not what she is.
