1408b21	The form of the style should be neither metrical nor
1408b22	without rhythm. The metrical is unpersuasive (for it seems artificial),
1408b23	and it also distracts the hearer, for it makes him attend to the recurrence, to when
1408b24	it will come again; just as children anticipate the heralds'
1408b25	“Whom does the freed slave choose as his patron?”
1408b26	with “Cleon!” What is without rhythm is unlimited; it should be limited,
1408b27	but not by meter, for the unlimited is unpleasant and unknowable.
1408b28	All things are limited by number; and the number of the form of
1408b29	style is rhythm, of which meters too are segments.
1408b30	So prose must have rhythm, but not meter, or it will be a
1408b31	poem. And the rhythm should not be exact; this will be so if
1408b32	it goes only to a certain point. Of the rhythms, the heroic needs a melody that is dignified,
1408b33	not that of speech; the iambus is the very
1408b34	speech of the many (that is why, of all meters, people most often
1408b35	utter iambics when they talk); but there must be dignity and
1408b36	the power to transport. The trochee is too much like the cordax; the tetrameters show it,
1409a1	for tetrameters are a tripping rhythm. There remains
1409a2	the paean, which speakers have used since Thrasymachus began it, though they could not
1409a3	say what it was. The paean is a third rhythm,
1409a4	close to those mentioned: for it is three to two,
1409a5	while of those, one is one to one and the other two to one; and between
1409a6	these ratios is the one and a half, which is the paean.
1409a7	The others, then, are to be given up, for the reasons given and
1409a8	because they are metrical; but the paean should be taken up, for it is the only one of
1409a9	the rhythms mentioned from which no meter is made, so that it best escapes notice.
1409a10	Now people use one kind of paean both at the beginning and
1409a11	at the end, but the end ought to differ from the beginning. There are
1409a12	two kinds of paean, opposite to each other, of which one suits
1409a13	the beginning, as indeed people use it: it is the one in which a long syllable
1409a14	begins and three short ones end: “Delos-born, or Lycia,”
1409a15	and “Golden-haired Far-Shooter, son of Zeus”; the other is the opposite,
1409a16	in which three short syllables begin and the long one comes last:
1409a17	“After earth and waters, night made the ocean vanish.” This
1409a18	makes an ending; for the short syllable, being incomplete,
1409a19	makes it seem docked. It should be cut off with the long syllable, and
1409a20	the end should be plain not through the scribe, nor through
1409a21	the marginal stroke, but through the rhythm. That
1409a22	the style should be rhythmical and not without rhythm, and what
1409a23	rhythms make it rhythmical, and how they are arranged, has been said.
