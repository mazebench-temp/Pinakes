# Aristotle, Rhetoric, Book I, Chapter 13 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.
# Translated line for line from Ross's Greek; each row carries the Bekker line it renders.
# English syntax runs across line breaks, as prose must; names stay on their own lines. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1373b1	Let us classify all wrongs and just acts,
1373b2	beginning first from this. Just and
1373b3	unjust acts have been defined in relation to two laws and to persons in two ways. By
1373b4	law I mean on the one hand particular, on the other common: particular, the
1373b5	law that each people has defined for itself, and this partly
1373b6	unwritten and partly written; common, the law according to nature. For there is
1373b7	something that all divine, a justice and injustice common by nature,
1373b8	even where there is no association or
1373b9	covenant between people, as Sophocles' Antigone clearly says
1373b10	that it is just, though forbidden, to bury Polynices,
1373b11	as being just by nature: “For this is not
1373b12	of today or yesterday; it lives forever,
1373b13	and no one knows from when it appeared”; and as Empedocles
1373b14	says about not killing what has life;
1373b15	for this is not just for some and unjust for others, “but the
1373b16	law of all stretches without a break through the wide-ruling air and through
1373b17	the boundless light”; and as Alcidamas says in his Messenian Speech,
1373b18	“God has left all men free; nature has made no one a slave.”
1373b19	In relation to persons there is a twofold distinction; for what one must do and not do is defined
1373b20	in relation either to the community or to one of its members;
1373b21	so with wrongs and just acts there are two ways
1373b22	of doing wrong and doing right: either toward one defined person
1373b23	or toward the community; for the adulterer and the man who strikes another wrong
1373b24	some defined person, while the man who does not serve in the army wrongs the community.
1373b25	All wrongs being thus divided, some
1373b26	against the community and others against another or others,
1373b27	let us recall what being wronged is and speak of it. Being
1373b28	wronged is suffering unjust things at the hands of one acting voluntarily; for doing wrong
1373b29	was defined earlier as voluntary. And since the one
1373b30	wronged must be harmed, and harmed against his will, the
1373b31	harms are clear from what was said before; for goods and
1373b32	evils in themselves were discussed earlier, and voluntary acts too,
1373b33	that they are those done knowingly; so all charges must be
1373b34	either public or private, and against one who acted either in ignorance
1373b35	and involuntarily or voluntarily and knowingly, and of the latter some
1373b36	by choice and some through emotion. Temper will be discussed
1373b37	under the emotions; what people choose and in what state
1373b38	has been said before. And since people often admit having done something
1374a1	but do not admit the charge's name or what the name covers,
1374a2	saying, for instance, that they took but did not steal, struck first
1374a3	but did not commit outrage, had intercourse but did not commit adultery,
1374a4	or stole but did not rob a temple (for the thing was not a god's),
1374a5	or encroached on land but not on public land, or talked
1374a6	with the enemy but did not betray them, for this reason we should
1374a7	define these too: what theft is, what outrage, what
1374a8	adultery, so that whether we wish to show that it applies or that it does not,
1374a9	we may be able to make the just plain. All
1374a10	such disputes are about whether a man is unjust and bad or not unjust;
1374a11	for depravity and wrongdoing lie in the choice,
1374a12	and such names as these also signify the
1374a13	choice, as outrage and theft do; for if a man struck someone, he did not necessarily
1374a14	commit outrage, but only if he did it for some purpose, such as to dishonor the other or
1374a15	to please himself. Nor, if he took something secretly, did he necessarily steal,
1374a16	but only if he took it to harm the one he took it from and to appropriate it
1374a17	for himself. And it is the same with the rest as
1374a18	with these. Since there were two kinds of just and unjust
1374a19	acts (some written, some unwritten),
1374a20	and those that the laws pronounce on have been discussed, there are two kinds
1374a21	of unwritten ones: some are a matter of excess of virtue
1374a22	and vice, for which there are reproaches and praises, dishonors and
1374a23	honors and rewards (being grateful to a benefactor, for instance, and
1374a24	doing good in return to the one who did good, and being ready to help one's
1374a25	friends, and the like); others make up what the particular
1374a26	written law leaves out. For equity seems to be just,
1374a27	and equity is justice that goes beyond the written
1374a28	law. This comes about partly with the lawgivers' consent, partly
1374a29	against it: against it when something escapes them; with it
1374a30	when they cannot define precisely, but must speak universally
1374a31	where the matter is not universal but holds for the most part; and in cases that are not easy
1374a32	to define because they are endless, such as wounding with an iron of what size
1374a33	and of what kind; for a lifetime would run out in enumerating them. If,
1374a34	then, the matter is indefinite, but a law must be made, one must speak in general terms,
1374a35	so that if a man wearing a ring lifts his hand or strikes,
1374a36	by the written law he is liable and does wrong,
1374b1	but in truth he does no wrong, and this is equity.
1374b2	If equity is what has been described, it is clear what sorts of acts are
1374b3	equitable and not equitable, and what sort of people are not equitable;
1374b4	for acts one should pardon are equitable, and it is equitable
1374b5	not to count errors and wrongs as equal, nor
1374b6	errors and misfortunes. For misfortunes
1374b7	are acts that are unexpected and do not come from depravity; errors,
1374b8	those that are not unexpected and do not come from wickedness; wrongs,
1374b9	those that are not unexpected and do come from wickedness; for acts done from
1374b10	appetite come from wickedness. And to pardon human
1374b11	failings is equitable. And to look not to the law but
1374b12	to the lawgiver, and not to the words but
1374b13	to the intention of the lawgiver; and not to
1374b14	the action but to the choice; and not to
1374b15	the part but to the whole; nor to what sort of man someone is now, but
1374b16	what sort he has always or for the most part been. And to remember
1374b17	the good one has received rather than the bad, and the good one has received
1374b18	rather than the good one has done. And to bear being wronged.
1374b19	And to be willing to settle matters by words rather than by deeds. And to
1374b20	prefer going to arbitration rather than to court; for the
1374b21	arbitrator looks to equity, the juror to the law; and
1374b22	the arbitrator was invented for this purpose, that equity might prevail.
1374b23	Let this be our account of what is equitable.
