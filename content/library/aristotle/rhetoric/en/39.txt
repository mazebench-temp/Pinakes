1400b34	Since it is possible for one syllogism to be real and another not
1400b35	to be one but to appear so, there must also be some enthymemes
1400b36	that are real and others that are not enthymemes but appear
1400b37	to be, since the enthymeme is a kind of syllogism.
1401a1	Of the topics of apparent enthymemes, one is
1401a2	from diction, and one part of this, as in
1401a3	dialectic, is to state the last point as a conclusion without having
1401a4	reasoned to it: “Therefore not this and that; necessarily,
1401a5	therefore, this and that.” In enthymemes, to speak concisely and
1401a6	in antitheses appears to be an enthymeme (for such
1401a7	diction is the proper ground of the enthymeme); and such a thing seems to
1401a8	come from the form of the diction. It is useful, for speaking
1401a9	syllogistically in diction, to state the heads of many syllogisms:
1401a10	that he saved some, avenged others,
1401a11	and freed the Greeks; for each of these was demonstrated from
1401a12	other premises, but when they are put together it seems that something follows from them
1401a13	too. One is from homonymy: saying that the mouse is
1401a14	excellent, since from it comes the most honored of all rites;
1401a15	for the mysteries are the most honored rite of all.
1401a16	Or if someone praising the dog were to bring in the dog in the heavens
1401a17	as well, or Pan, because
1401a18	Pindar said, “O blessed one, whom, as the Great Goddess's
1401a19	dog of every shape, the Olympians name,”
1401a20	or that to be no dog at all is the most dishonorable thing, so that the dog
1401a21	is clearly honored. And saying that Hermes is the most sharing
1401a22	of the gods; for he alone is called “Hermes in common.” And saying that
1401a23	speech is most excellent, because good men are worthy
1401a24	not of money but of speech; for “worthy of speech” is not
1401a25	said in the simple sense. Another is to speak of what is divided by combining it, or of what is
1401a26	combined by dividing it; for since what is not the same often seems
1401a27	the same, one should do whichever is more useful.
1401a28	This is the argument of Euthydemus: for instance, knowing that
1401a29	there is a trireme in the Piraeus, since he knows each of the two. And that one who knows the
1401a30	letters knows the line; for the line is the same
1401a31	thing. And since twice so much is unhealthy, saying that one portion is not
1401a32	healthy either; for it is absurd if two goods are one evil.
1401a33	So put, it is refutative; put thus, demonstrative: for one good is not
1401a34	two evils. The whole topic is fallacious. Again,
1401a35	what Polycrates said of Thrasybulus, that he overthrew thirty tyrants;
1401a36	for he combines them. Or the argument in the Orestes of Theodectes; for
1401a37	it is from division: “It is just, when a woman kills her husband,”
1401a38	that she should die, and that the son should avenge the father;
1401b1	so these things too have been done; but put together, perhaps they are no longer
1401b2	just. It could also come from omission; for it leaves out by
1401b3	whom. Another topic is to establish or demolish by exaggeration;
1401b4	this is when, without having shown that he did it ⟨or that
1401b5	he did not⟩, one amplifies the deed; for it makes it seem either
1401b6	that he has not done it, when the accused amplifies, or
1401b7	that he has, when the accuser amplifies. It is not, then, an enthymeme;
1401b8	for the hearer reasons falsely that he did it or did not,
1401b9	when it has not been shown. Another is from a sign; for this too
1401b10	is not a valid syllogism: for instance, if one were to say, “Lovers are useful
1401b11	to cities; for the love of Harmodius and Aristogeiton overthrew
1401b12	the tyrant Hipparchus”; or if one were to say that
1401b13	Dionysius is a thief, for he is wicked; for this too is invalid; for not
1401b14	every wicked man is a thief, but every thief is wicked. Another
1401b15	is through the accidental: for instance, what Polycrates says of the mice,
1401b16	that they came to the rescue by gnawing through the bowstrings; or if one were to say that being
1401b17	invited to dinner is the greatest honor; for it was because he was not invited
1401b18	that Achilles conceived his wrath against the Achaeans at Tenedos; but he
1401b19	conceived his wrath as one dishonored, and this happened to come about through his not being invited.
1401b20	Another is from the consequent: for instance, in the Alexander, that
1401b21	he was great-souled; for he despised the company of the many and spent his time on Ida
1401b22	by himself; since the great-souled are of this kind,
1401b23	he too would seem great-souled. And since a man dresses finely and
1401b24	wanders about at night, he is an adulterer; for such men do this. It is similar to say that
1401b25	beggars sing and dance in the temples, and
1401b26	that exiles may live wherever they wish; for since
1401b27	these things belong to those who seem to be happy, those
1401b28	to whom they belong would seem to be happy too; but there is a difference in the
1401b29	manner; that is why this also falls under omission. Another is from taking
1401b30	what is not the cause as the cause: for instance, because something happened at the same time or after it;
1401b31	for they take “after this” as “because of this,” and above all
1401b32	those in political life, as Demades took Demosthenes'
1401b33	policy to be the cause of all the evils, since the war came
1401b34	after it. Another is from the omission of when and how:
1401b35	for instance, that Alexander took
1401b36	Helen justly, for the choice was given to her by her
1401b37	father. For not, perhaps, always, but the first time;
1401b38	for the father's authority extends only so far.
1402a1	Or if one were to say that striking free men is
1402a2	outrage; for not in every case, but only when one starts the
1402a3	unjust blows. Further, as in eristic arguments, from what is
1402a4	so absolutely and what is not absolutely but in some respect, an apparent
1402a5	syllogism arises, as in dialectic, that what is not
1402a6	is, for what is not is not-being; and that the unknown
1402a7	is knowable, for the unknown is knowable as unknown; so
1402a8	in rhetoric too there is an apparent enthymeme from what is not
1402a9	probable absolutely but probable in some respect. This is not universal, as
1402a10	Agathon also says: “Perhaps one might say that this very thing
1402a11	is probable, that many things happen to mortals
1402a12	that are not probable.” For what is contrary to probability happens,
1402a13	so that what is contrary to probability is probable too; and if so,
1402a14	what is improbable will be probable. But not absolutely;
1402a15	rather, as in eristic arguments, leaving out the in what respect, the in relation to what and
1402a16	the in what way makes the trickery, so here it comes from
1402a17	the probable's being probable not absolutely but in some respect. Of this
1402a18	topic the art of Corax is composed: “For if a man is not liable to the
1402a19	charge, for instance if a weak man is prosecuted for assault (it is not probable), and if he is liable,
1402a20	for instance if he is strong (it is not probable, because it was going to seem probable).”
1402a21	And likewise in the other cases; for a man must either be liable or not
1402a22	liable to the charge; both, then, appear probable,
1402a23	but one is probable, the other not absolutely but as has been said;
1402a24	and this is what making the weaker argument the stronger is. And
1402a25	that is why people were justly offended by
1402a26	Protagoras' professed teaching; for it is a lie, and
1402a27	not true but an apparent probability, and found in
1402a28	no art except rhetoric and eristic.
