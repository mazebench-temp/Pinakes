1359a30	First, then, we must grasp what sorts of goods or evils the adviser
1359a31	advises about, since he does not advise about everything but about what
1359a32	can either happen or not. Whatever of necessity is or
1359a33	will be, or cannot be or come about, about these
1359a34	there is no advice. Nor indeed about all that is possible;
1359a35	for among things that can come about or not, some goods
1359a36	come about by nature and some by chance, about which it is of no
1359a37	use to give advice. It is clear that advice concerns whatever
1359a38	admits of deliberation. Such are the things that can naturally be traced back
1359a39	to us, and whose coming into being has its origin in us;
1359b1	for we go on considering until we find out whether they are possible
1359b2	or impossible for us to do. To enumerate precisely one by one,
1359b3	and to divide into species, the matters people usually transact in public, and
1359b4	moreover to define them as far as possible according to the truth,
1359b5	is not what we should seek on the present occasion, because it is neither
1359b6	the business of the rhetorical art but of one more intelligent and more
1359b7	true, and because even now far more than its own
1359b8	proper subjects of study have been given to it. For what we said
1359b9	before is true: rhetoric is composed
1359b10	of analytical science and of the political science that concerns
1359b11	characters, and it is like dialectic in some respects and in others like
1359b12	sophistic arguments. But the more one tries to build dialectic or rhetoric
1359b13	not into capacities, as they are, but into sciences,
1359b14	the more he will unawares obliterate their nature, by passing over,
1359b15	as he rebuilds them, into sciences of certain underlying
1359b16	subjects, and not of arguments alone. Still, whatever it is
1359b17	to the purpose to distinguish, while leaving the inquiry
1359b18	to political science, let us state now too. Of the things
1359b19	about which all people deliberate, and about which
1359b20	advisers speak in public, the most important are, roughly, five
1359b21	in number: revenue, war and
1359b22	peace, the defense of the country, imports
1359b23	and exports, and legislation. So, on revenue, one who
1359b24	means to give advice would need to know the city's sources of income,
1359b25	what and how many they are, so that any that is neglected may be added
1359b26	and any that is too small increased; and also all the city's
1359b27	expenses, so that any that is superfluous may be removed and any that is too great
1359b28	reduced; for people become richer not only by adding to what they have
1359b29	but also by cutting their
1359b30	expenses. These things cannot be surveyed only from experience
1359b31	of one's own affairs; to give advice about them one must also be informed
1359b32	about what has been found out among others.
1359b33	On war and peace one must know the power
1359b34	of the city: how great it already is and how great it could
1359b35	become, and of what kind both the existing power is and any
1359b36	that could be added; and further, what wars it has fought
1359b37	and how. And one must know these things not only about one's own city but also
1359b38	about its neighbors, and about those with whom war is
1359b39	to be expected, so that peace may be kept with the stronger
1360a1	and war with the weaker may be the city's own choice; and their forces,
1360a2	whether they are like or unlike one's own; for here too one can gain an advantage
1360a3	or suffer a loss. For this too one must have studied not only one's own
1360a4	wars but also those of others, how
1360a5	they turn out; for like results naturally come from
1360a6	like causes. Further, on the defense of the country, one must not be ignorant of how
1360a7	it is guarded, but must know the size of the garrison and its
1360a8	kind and the places of the guard posts (this is impossible
1360a9	for one who does not know the country), so that if the garrison is too small
1360a10	it may be increased, and any that is superfluous removed, and the
1360a11	suitable places watched more closely. Further, on food,
1360a12	how much [expense] is enough for the city and of what kind, both the food
1360a13	produced at home and what is imported, and what exports
1360a14	the citizens need and what imports, and from whom, so that
1360a15	agreements and trade treaties may be made with them; for there are two
1360a16	peoples toward whom the citizens must be kept free of complaint:
1360a17	the stronger, and those useful for these purposes. For
1360a18	security one must be able to consider all these things,
1360a19	and not least understand legislation; for in the laws
1360a20	lies the safety of the city, so that one must know how many
1360a21	kinds of constitution there are, what is advantageous to each, and
1360a22	by what each is naturally destroyed, both by things proper to the constitution
1360a23	and by things opposed to it. By being destroyed by what is proper to it I mean that, apart from
1360a24	the best constitution, all the others are destroyed both by being relaxed and
1360a25	by being overstrained: democracy, for instance, not only becomes weaker
1360a26	when relaxed, so that it will end in oligarchy,
1360a27	but also when overstrained; just as a hooked nose and a
1360a28	snub nose not only come to the mean when relaxed, but also,
1360a29	when they become very hooked or very snub, are so disposed that
1360a30	they do not even seem to be noses. It is useful for legislation
1360a31	not only to understand which constitution is advantageous, studying it from past
1360a32	events, but also to know the constitutions of other peoples,
1360a33	which kinds suit which kinds of people. So it is clear that for
1360a34	legislation descriptions of the earth are useful (for from them
1360a35	one can learn the laws of the nations), and for political
1360a36	advice the histories of those who write about men's actions. All
1360a37	these things, however, are the work of politics, not of rhetoric. The subjects
1360a38	on which one who means to give advice must have premises,
1360b1	the most important ones, are these; from what we must
1360b2	exhort or dissuade, both on these and on the rest,
1360b3	let us now say in turn.
