1417b21	The proofs should be demonstrative; and since the dispute
1417b22	is about one of four things, one should demonstrate by bringing the demonstration
1417b23	to bear on the point disputed: for example, if what is in dispute is that the thing
1417b24	did not happen, in the judgment the demonstration must be brought above all on this point;
1417b25	if that he did no harm, on that; and if that the harm was not so great,
1417b26	or that it was just, likewise, as when the dispute is about whether the thing
1417b27	happened. It must not escape us that only in this
1417b28	dispute must one of the two be wicked; for ignorance is not
1417b29	the cause, as it would be if people were disputing about what is just;
1417b30	so one should dwell on this point, but not in the other cases. In
1417b31	epideictic speeches, for the most part, the amplification should be that the deeds are noble and
1417b32	beneficial; for the facts must be taken on trust; speakers seldom bring demonstrations
1417b33	of these too, only if they are incredible, or if someone else has the
1417b34	credit. In speeches to the assembly one might contend either that a thing will not
1417b35	happen, or that what the speaker urges will happen if they do it, but
1417b36	that it is not just, or not beneficial, or not so great. One should also
1417b37	see whether the speaker tells any lie outside the subject; for these
1417b38	appear to be tekmēria that he lies about the rest too.
1418a1	Examples are better suited to speaking to the assembly,
1418a2	enthymemes to judicial speech; for the one is about the
1418a3	future, so that examples must be drawn from
1418a4	past events; the other is about what is or is not, where there is more
1418a5	demonstration and necessity; for what has happened has necessity.
1418a6	Enthymemes should not be stated in a row, but mixed in;
1418a7	otherwise they harm one another. For there is a limit
1418a8	to quantity too: “My friend, since you have said all that a wise man would,”
1418a9	not “such things as”. And do not look for enthymemes about everything;
1418a10	otherwise you will do what some philosophers do,
1418a11	who prove by syllogism what is better known and more credible than the premises
1418a12	they argue from. And when you are rousing emotion, do not state an enthymeme (for either
1418a13	it will drive out the emotion, or the enthymeme will have been stated
1418a14	in vain; for movements that occur together drive each other out, and either
1418a15	cancel or weaken each other); nor, when the speech is to show character,
1418a16	should one look for an enthymeme at the same time; for demonstration has neither
1418a17	character nor choice. Maxims should be used both
1418a18	in narration and in proof, for they show character: “And I gave it,
1418a19	though I knew one should not trust.” Or with emotion:
1418a20	“And I do not regret it, though I have been wronged; his
1418a21	is the profit, mine the justice.” Speaking to the assembly
1418a22	is harder than pleading in court, reasonably enough, because it concerns the
1418a23	future, while pleading concerns the past, which is already knowable even to
1418a24	seers, as Epimenides the Cretan said (for he did not divine
1418a25	about what was to be, but about what had happened
1418a26	and was obscure); and in judicial speeches the law is the starting point,
1418a27	and with a starting point it is easier to find a demonstration. And speaking to the assembly does not allow many
1418a28	digressions, such as against the opponent or about oneself, or arousing
1418a29	emotion; it allows them least of all, unless the speaker leaves the subject. So
1418a30	when at a loss one should do what the orators at Athens do,
1418a31	and Isocrates: for even when giving advice he accuses,
1418a32	the Lacedaemonians in the Panegyricus and Chares in the Symmachicus.
1418a33	In epideictic speeches one should vary the speech with episodes
1418a34	of praise, as Isocrates does; for he is always bringing someone in. And
1418a35	what Gorgias used to say, that he never ran short of things to say, comes to the same
1418a36	thing; for if he speaks of Achilles he praises Peleus, then Aeacus,
1418a37	then the god, and likewise courage, which does this and that
1418a38	or is of such a kind. If you have demonstrations, then, you should speak both so as to show character
1418a39	and demonstratively; if you have no enthymemes, so as to show character;
1418a40	and it suits a decent man better
1418b1	to appear good than to make his speech precise. Of
1418b2	enthymemes, the refutative are better liked than the demonstrative,
1418b3	because whatever produces a refutation is more plainly a syllogism;
1418b4	for opposites are more easily known side by side.
1418b5	Replies to the opponent are not a separate kind; they belong to
1418b6	the proofs, refuting some points by objection and others by syllogism.
1418b7	Both in advice and in a lawsuit, the one who speaks first should state
1418b8	his own proofs first, and afterward meet the opposing arguments,
1418b9	refuting them and tearing them apart in advance. But if the opposing case
1418b10	has many arguments, deal with these first, as Callistratus did
1418b11	in the Messenian assembly: he first demolished what they would say, and only
1418b12	then made his own case. The one who speaks second should first speak against the opposing
1418b13	speech, refuting it and drawing counter-syllogisms, especially
1418b14	if it has been well liked; for just as the mind does not accept a man
1418b15	who has been slandered beforehand, so it does not accept a speech either, if
1418b16	the opponent seems to have spoken well. One must therefore make room
1418b17	in the hearer for the speech to come; and this will happen if you demolish the opponent's;
1418b18	so after fighting against all his arguments, or the greatest, or those well liked, or those
1418b19	easily refuted, one should then make one's own case credible:
1418b20	“First I will be an ally of the goddesses; for I …
1418b21	Hera …”: in these lines the speaker first takes up the
1418b22	silliest point. So much for proofs.
1418b23	As for character: since to say some things about oneself
1418b24	brings envy, or long-windedness, or contradiction,
1418b25	and to say them about another brings abuse or boorishness, one should make
1418b26	someone else speak, as Isocrates does in the
1418b27	Philippus and in the Antidosis, and as Archilochus blames: he makes
1418b28	the father speak about his daughter in the
1418b29	iambic poem, “Nothing is beyond hope, nothing can be sworn impossible,”
1418b30	and Charon the carpenter in the iambic poem that begins
1418b31	“Not for me the wealth of Gyges,” and as Sophocles makes Haemon speak
1418b32	for Antigone to his father as if others were saying it.
1418b33	One should also sometimes change enthymemes
1418b34	and make maxims of them, as “Men of sense should make
1418b35	peace when they are prosperous;
1418b36	for in this way they would gain the most,”
1418b37	and as an enthymeme: “For if, when reconciliations are most beneficial
1418b38	and bring the most gain, one should then
1418b39	make peace, one should make peace when prosperous.”
