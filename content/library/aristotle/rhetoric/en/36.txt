1394a19	As for the use of maxims, once it has been said what a maxim is, it will be
1394a20	clearest about what kinds of things, and when, and for whom it is fitting
1394a21	to use maxims in speeches. A maxim is
1394a22	a statement, not, however, about particulars, for instance what sort of man
1394a23	Iphicrates is, but about universals; nor about all things, for instance that the
1394a24	straight is opposite to the curved, but about the things that are matters of action,
1394a25	and ⟨what⟩ is to be chosen or avoided with a view to
1394a26	action; so that, since the enthymeme is the syllogism about such
1394a27	things, the conclusions
1394a28	and the premises of enthymemes, once the
1394a29	syllogism is removed, are pretty much maxims: for instance,
1394a30	“No man who is sound of mind should ever
1394a31	have his children taught to be too wise.”
1394a32	This, then, is a maxim; but when the cause
1394a33	and the reason why are added, the whole is an enthymeme:
1394a34	for instance, “For besides the idleness they are charged with,
1394a35	they win hostile envy from the citizens”;
1394b1	and “There is no man
1394b2	who is happy in all things,”
1394b3	and “There is no man
1394b4	who is free”
1394b5	is a maxim, but together with what follows it is an enthymeme: “for
1394b6	he is a slave either of money or of fortune.” If a maxim is what
1394b7	has been said, there must be four kinds of maxim; for it
1394b8	will be either with a supplement or without a supplement. Those that
1394b9	need demonstration are all that say something paradoxical
1394b10	or disputed; those that say nothing paradoxical are without
1394b11	a supplement. Of these, some must need no supplement because they are known
1394b12	beforehand: for instance, “For a man
1394b13	health is best, as it seems to me at least” (for it seems so
1394b14	to most people); others are clear as soon as they are spoken
1394b15	to anyone who looks at them: for instance,
1394b16	“No one is a lover who does not love always.”
1394b17	Of those with a supplement, some
1394b18	are part of an enthymeme, like
1394b19	“No man who is sound of mind should ever,”
1394b20	and others are enthymematic, but not part of an enthymeme;
1394b21	these are the ones most admired. They are those in
1394b22	which the cause of what is said is apparent: for instance,
1394b23	“Do not keep your anger immortal, being mortal”; for to
1394b24	say “one should not keep it” is a maxim, and the addition
1394b25	“being mortal” is the reason why. Likewise, “Mortal things
1394b26	a mortal should think, not immortal things.” It is clear,
1394b27	then, from what has been said, how many kinds of maxim there are, and to what
1394b28	each is suited: about what is disputed
1394b29	or paradoxical, not without a supplement; rather, one should either put the
1394b30	supplement first and use the conclusion as a maxim (for instance, if one were to say,
1394b31	“For my part, since one should neither be envied nor be idle,
1394b32	I say one should not be educated”), or say this first and add
1394b33	what came before; about what is not paradoxical but is unclear, one should add
1394b34	the reason as tersely as possible. In such cases
1394b35	Laconic sayings and riddling ones are also suitable,
1395a1	for instance if one says what Stesichorus said at Locri, that they
1395a2	should not be given to outrage, so that the cicadas may not sing from the ground. It is fitting
1395a3	to speak in maxims for those older in age, and about matters in which
1395a4	one is experienced; so that for one not of that age speaking in maxims
1395a5	is unbecoming, as telling stories is, and about matters in which one is inexperienced
1395a6	it is silly and uneducated. A sufficient sign: rustics
1395a7	are the greatest coiners of maxims and readily declare their views.
1395a8	To speak universally of what is not universal is most fitting in
1395a9	protest and exaggeration, and in these either at the beginning or
1395a10	after the demonstration. One should also use hackneyed
1395a11	and common maxims, if they are useful; for because
1395a12	they are common, as if everyone agreed, they seem
1395a13	right; for instance, for one exhorting men to face danger without
1395a14	having sacrificed, “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land,”
1395a15	and if they are weaker, “Enyalius is impartial,”
1395a16	and for destroying the enemies' children
1395a17	though they have done no wrong, “A fool, who kills the father
1395a18	and leaves the children.” Further, some
1395a19	proverbs are maxims too, for instance the proverb
1395a20	“an Attic neighbor.” One should also speak maxims that go against
1395a21	sayings that have become public property (I mean by public property, for instance, “Know yourself”
1395a22	and “Nothing too much”), when either one's character is going to appear
1395a23	better or the maxim is spoken with emotion. An emotional maxim is, for instance, if
1395a24	someone in anger were to say that it is false that one should know oneself:
1395a25	this man at any rate, if he had known himself, would never have thought himself fit to
1395a26	be general; character appears better if one says that one should not, as they say, love as though
1395a27	one will hate, but rather hate as though one will love. One should
1395a28	make one's choice clear by the diction, or if not, add the reason:
1395a29	for instance, by saying, “One should love, not as they say,
1395a30	but as though one will love always; for the other belongs to a schemer,” or
1395a31	thus: “I do not like the saying; for the
1395a32	true friend should love as though he will love always,” and “Nor
1395a33	do I like ‘Nothing too much’; for the bad, at least, one should hate too much.”
1395b1	Maxims are of great help in speeches, first because of
1395b2	the vulgarity of the hearers; for they are pleased if someone speaking in general terms
1395b3	hits upon the opinions they hold about particular cases. What
1395b4	I mean will be clear from this, and at the same time how one should hunt for maxims.
1395b5	A maxim, as has been said, is a general statement,
1395b6	and people are pleased when something is said in general terms that they
1395b7	already happen to suppose in particular; for instance, if someone happens to have bad neighbors or
1395b8	bad children, he would welcome one who said that nothing
1395b9	is more troublesome than neighbors, or nothing more foolish than having children;
1395b10	so one must guess what sort of things they happen to suppose already,
1395b11	and then speak about these in general terms. This is
1395b12	one use of speaking in maxims, and there is another, greater: it makes speeches
1395b13	show character. Speeches have character when
1395b14	the choice is clear; and all maxims do this, because
1395b15	the one who speaks a maxim makes a general statement about
1395b16	choices, so that, if the maxims are good, they
1395b17	make the speaker appear of good character too. About
1395b18	the maxim, then, what it is, how many kinds of it there are, how
1395b19	it should be used, and what benefit it has, let this be enough.
