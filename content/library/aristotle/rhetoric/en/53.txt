1413b3	It must not escape our notice that a different style suits each kind.
1413b4	The written style and the style of debate are not the same, nor are the style of speaking to the assembly
1413b5	and the judicial. One must know both: the one is
1413b6	knowing how to speak good Greek, the other is not being forced to keep silent
1413b7	when one wants to share something with others, which is
1413b8	what happens to those who do not know how to write. The written style
1413b9	is the most precise, and the style of debate the most suited to delivery
1413b10	(it has two kinds, the one that shows character and the emotional);
1413b11	that is why actors seek out plays of this kind,
1413b12	and poets actors of this kind. But poets who are good to read
1413b13	are carried about in people's hands, like Chaeremon (for he is precise, like a speechwriter),
1413b14	and Licymnius among the writers of dithyrambs. When compared,
1413b15	the speeches of writers seem thin in debates,
1413b16	and those of orators, though they were well spoken, seem amateurish when held in
1413b17	the hand. The reason is that in debate what suits delivery is fitting;
1413b18	that is why, when delivery is taken away, such things do not do their
1413b19	work and seem silly: asyndeta, for example, and saying the same thing
1413b20	many times are rightly rejected in the written style, but in
1413b21	the style of debate they are not, and orators use them, for they are
1413b22	suited to delivery. When saying the same thing one must vary it, which, as it were,
1413b23	paves the way for delivery: “This is the man who robbed you,
1413b24	this is the man who deceived you, this the man who in the end tried
1413b25	to betray you”; so the actor Philemon did in
1413b26	Anaxandrides' Old Men's Madness whenever he said “Rhadamanthus
1413b27	and Palamedes”, and with the “I” in the prologue of the Pious;
1413b28	for if one does not act such things, one becomes “the man carrying
1413b29	the beam”. Likewise with asyndeta: “I came, I met him,
1413b30	I begged”; for they must be acted, and not said as one thing with the
1413b31	same character and tone. Asyndeta have a further peculiarity: in
1413b32	the same time many things seem to have been said; for the connective makes many things
1413b33	one, so that if it is taken out, clearly the opposite will happen:
1413b34	the one will be many. So it has amplification: “I came, I talked with him, I entreated”
1414a1	(it seems many things), “he disregarded all I said”. This is
1414a2	what Homer too wants to do in “Nireus from Syme,
1414a3	Nireus, son of Aglaea, Nireus, the most handsome.” For a man about whom
1414a4	much is said must be named often;
1414a5	so if he is named often, much seems to be said; and so Homer has amplified him,
1414a6	though he mentions him only once, through this false inference, and has made him
1414a7	memorable, though he never speaks of him again anywhere afterward.
1414a8	The style of speaking to the assembly is exactly like
1414a9	shadow-painting: the larger the crowd, the more distant the
1414a10	view, so that precision is wasted and looks worse in
1414a11	both; the judicial style is more precise, and still more so the style before
1414a12	a single judge, for there is very little room for rhetoric in it: what
1414a13	belongs to the case and what is foreign to it are more easily seen, and
1414a14	there is no contest, so that the judgment is unclouded. That is why the same
1414a15	orators are not well liked in all of these: where
1414a16	delivery counts most, there precision is least present; and this
1414a17	is where voice is needed, and above all a loud voice. The
1414a18	epideictic style is the most suited to writing, for its function
1414a19	is to be read; second comes the judicial. To distinguish style further,
1414a20	saying that it should be pleasant and magnificent,
1414a21	is superfluous; for why that rather than self-controlled and generous, or any
1414a22	other virtue of character? What has been said will clearly make it
1414a23	pleasant, if the excellence of style has been rightly defined;
1414a24	for why else should it be clear and not mean but
1414a25	appropriate? If it is verbose, it is not clear, nor if
1414a26	it is curt; clearly the mean is what suits. And what has been said
1414a27	will make it pleasant, if it is well mixed: the usual and the
1414a28	unfamiliar, and rhythm, and the persuasive that comes from the appropriate.
1414a29	So much, then, for style, both in general for all kinds
1414a30	and in particular for each; it remains to speak of arrangement.
