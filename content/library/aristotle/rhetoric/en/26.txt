1388a31	In what state people feel emulation, and for what sort of things, and toward whom, will be
1388a32	clear from the following. If emulation is a kind of pain at the apparent
1388a33	presence of goods that are held in honor and that one might obtain oneself, in
1388a34	those who are like one by nature, not because another has them but because one does not have them
1388a35	oneself (that is why emulation is decent and belongs to the decent, while envying
1388a36	is bad and belongs to the bad; for the one prepares himself through emulation
1388a37	to obtain the goods, the other through envy to keep his neighbor from having them),
1388b1	then those must be given to emulation who think themselves worthy of goods they do not
1388b2	have ⟨that it is possible for them to obtain⟩; for no one thinks himself worthy of what appears
1388b3	impossible (that is why the young and the great-souled are like this). And those who
1388b4	have such goods as are worthy of men held in honor; these are
1388b5	wealth, many friends, offices and all such things; for, as
1388b6	it befits them to be good, they emulate the kind of goods that befit those who are well
1388b7	off. And those whom others think
1388b8	worthy. And those whose ancestors or kinsmen or household or people or
1388b9	city are held in honor are given to emulation about these things; for they think these
1388b10	their own, and themselves worthy of them. If the goods held in honor are
1388b11	objects of emulation, then the virtues must be such, and whatever
1388b12	is useful and beneficial to others (for people honor
1388b13	benefactors and the good), and those goods whose enjoyment
1388b14	neighbors share, for instance wealth and beauty more than health. It is clear
1388b15	too who are the objects of emulation: those who have these and similar things
1388b16	are objects of emulation; these are the things mentioned, for instance courage,
1388b17	wisdom, office (for those in office can benefit many),
1388b18	generals, orators, all who have such powers. And those
1388b19	whom many wish to be like, or who have many acquaintances, or many
1388b20	friends, or whom many admire, or whom the emulators themselves admire.
1388b21	And those whose praises and encomia are spoken either by poets or by
1388b22	prose writers. People despise those in the opposite case; for the opposite
1388b23	of emulation is contempt, and of emulating, despising.
1388b24	Those who are so disposed as to emulate or be emulated
1388b25	must be disposed to despise those, and for those things,
1388b26	who have the evils opposite to the goods that are emulated;
1388b27	that is why they often despise the fortunate, when
1388b28	their fortune comes to them without the goods held in honor. By what
1388b29	means, then, the emotions arise and are dissolved, from which
1388b30	the proofs about them come, has been said.
