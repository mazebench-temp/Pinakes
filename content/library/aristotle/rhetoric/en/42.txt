1403b6	There are three things that must be dealt with concerning speech:
1403b7	first, from what sources the proofs will come; second, the
1403b8	style; and third, how the parts of the speech should be arranged.
1403b9	The proofs have been discussed: from how many sources they come, that they come from three,
1403b10	what these are, and why there are only so many (for it is either because
1403b11	the judges themselves are affected in some way, or because they take the speakers
1403b12	to be of a certain character, or because something has been demonstrated, that all are persuaded);
1403b13	and it has been said where enthymemes should be drawn from
1403b14	(for some are species of enthymemes, and others topics). About
1403b15	style it is next to speak. For it is not enough to
1403b16	have what one should say; one must also say it as one should,
1403b17	and this contributes much to the speech's appearing to be of a certain
1403b18	kind. Now the first thing inquired into was, in the order of nature, what naturally comes
1403b19	first: from what the facts themselves get what is persuasive;
1403b20	second, setting them out in style; and third, a matter
1403b21	that has the greatest power but has not yet been taken in hand, the things
1403b22	that concern delivery. For it came late into tragedy and rhapsody too,
1403b23	since at first the poets acted their tragedies
1403b24	themselves. So it is clear that there is something of this kind in rhetoric
1403b25	as in the art of poetry, which
1403b26	certain others have treated, and Glaucon of Teos among them. It
1403b27	lies in the voice: how it should be used for
1403b28	each emotion, when loud and when soft and when middling,
1403b29	and how to use the pitches, high, low and middle, and
1403b30	what rhythms suit each. For there are three things that they consider,
1403b31	and these are magnitude, melody and rhythm. Those who
1403b32	master these carry off nearly all the prizes in the contests; and just as
1403b33	there the actors now count for more than the poets,
1403b34	so it is in political contests too, because of the depravity of the
1403b35	citizens. No art of these matters has yet been composed,
1403b36	since even the study of style made progress only late;
1404a1	and delivery is thought vulgar, and rightly so considered. But since the whole
1404a2	business of rhetoric is directed to opinion, we must give it attention, not as
1404a3	something right but as something necessary; for what is
1404a4	just is to seek nothing more from a speech than that it should neither
1404a5	pain nor delight; it is just to contend with the facts
1404a6	themselves, so that everything outside the demonstration is superfluous.
1404a7	Still, delivery has great power, as has been said, because of the
1404a8	depravity of the hearer. Yet the matter of style does have
1404a9	some small necessary place in all teaching; for it makes some difference
1404a10	for making a thing clear whether one says it this way or that, though not so great a difference;
1404a11	all these things are appearance, and aimed at the hearer.
1404a12	That is why no one teaches geometry this way. When delivery
1404a13	comes, it will do the same as the actor's art; some have tried
1404a14	to say a little about it, such as Thrasymachus in his
1404a15	Appeals to Pity. Being good at delivery is a gift of nature, and
1404a16	rather outside the art, while style is within the art. That is why
1404a17	those who are capable in it win prizes in their turn, as do
1404a18	the orators who excel in delivery; for written speeches
1404a19	owe more of their strength to their style than to their thought.
1404a20	It was the poets, naturally enough, who first set it in motion;
1404a21	for words are imitations, and the voice too was there,
1404a22	the most imitative of all our parts; that is how
1404a23	the arts were formed, rhapsody, acting and
1404a24	others too. And since the poets, though what they said was silly,
1404a25	seemed to have won their reputation through their style, the first style
1404a26	was poetic, like that of Gorgias; and even now
1404a27	most of the uneducated think that such speakers speak
1404a28	most beautifully. This is not so; the style of prose and that of
1404a29	poetry are different. What happens shows it: for not even
1404a30	the writers of tragedy use it in the same way any longer;
1404a31	just as they changed from tetrameters to the iambic,
1404a32	because of all the meters it is the most like prose,
1404a33	so too they have given up all the words that are outside
1404a34	ordinary conversation, with which the early poets adorned their verse, and which even
1404a35	now the writers of hexameters use. So it is laughable
1404a36	to imitate those who no longer use that manner themselves.
1404a37	It is clear, then, that we need not go precisely into everything that can be said
1404a38	about style, but only into what concerns the kind of which we
1404a39	are speaking. The other has been discussed in the books on poetics.
