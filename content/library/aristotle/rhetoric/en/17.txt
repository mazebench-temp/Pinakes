# Aristotle, Rhetoric, Book II, Chapter 2 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.

1378a30	Let anger be a longing, accompanied by pain, for revenge
1378a31	[that is apparent], because of an apparent slight against
1378a32	oneself or ⟨something⟩ of one's own, when the slight is not
1378a33	warranted. If this is what anger is, the angry man must
1378a34	always be angry with some particular
1378a35	individual, for instance with Cleon but not with man in general,
1378b1	and because he has done or was going to do something to him or to one of his own; and every anger must
1378b2	be followed by a certain pleasure, that which comes from the hope of revenge. For it is pleasant
1378b3	to think one will get what one aims at, and no one aims at things
1378b4	that appear impossible for him, while the angry man aims at what is possible for him. That is why
1378b5	it has been well said of temper, “which, far
1378b6	sweeter than dripping honey, swells in the
1378b7	breasts of men”; for a certain pleasure attends it,
1378b8	both for this reason and because people dwell in
1378b9	their thoughts on taking revenge; and the image that arises then
1378b10	produces pleasure, as the images of dreams do. Since
1378b11	a slight is the actualization of an opinion about what appears worth nothing
1378b12	(for we think that bad things and good ones are worth taking seriously,
1378b13	and so are the things that tend toward them; but whatever is nothing or small
1378b14	we suppose worth nothing), there are three kinds of slight:
1378b15	contempt, spite and outrage. For the one who despises
1378b16	slights (for people despise what they think worth nothing,
1378b17	and they slight what is worth nothing), and the one who spites appears
1378b18	to slight. For spite is the thwarting of another's wishes, not so that
1378b19	something may come to oneself but so that it may not come to him; since, then, it is not for any gain of his own, he slights;
1378b20	for clearly he does not suppose that the other will harm him, or he would be afraid
1378b21	and not slight him, nor that he could benefit him in anything
1378b22	worth mentioning, or he would take care to be his friend. And
1378b23	the one who commits outrage slights; for outrage is doing
1378b24	and saying things at which the victim feels shame, not
1378b25	so that anything may come to oneself other than what has happened, but for the pleasure of it;
1378b26	for those who retaliate do not commit outrage but take revenge. The cause
1378b27	of the pleasure for those who commit outrage is that they think that by ill-treating others
1378b28	they show their own superiority the more (that is why the young and the rich are given to outrage:
1378b29	they think they are superior when they commit outrage); and dishonor belongs to outrage, and
1378b30	the one who dishonors slights; for what is worth nothing
1378b31	has no honor, either for good or
1378b32	for bad. That is why Achilles says in his anger,
1378b33	“has dishonored me: he has taken my prize and keeps it himself,”
1378b34	and “as if I were some dishonored vagrant,”
1378b35	as being angry for these reasons. People think it fitting to be highly regarded by their inferiors in birth, in power, in virtue,
1379a1	and in general in whatever they themselves are far superior in: for instance in
1379a2	money the rich man over the poor, and in speaking the rhetorical man
1379a3	over one who cannot speak, and the ruler over the ruled, and the one who
1379a4	[thinks himself] worthy to rule over one who deserves to be ruled. That is why it has been said,
1379a5	“Great is the spirit of kings whom Zeus has nurtured,” and “still
1379a6	he keeps a grudge thereafter”; for they are vexed
1379a7	because of their superiority. Further, people are angry with those by whom they think they should be well treated;
1379a8	these are the people whom one has benefited or is benefiting, oneself or
1379a9	someone on one's behalf or someone of one's own, or wishes or has wished to benefit.
1379a10	From this, then, it is already clear in what state people are angry themselves,
1379a11	with whom, and for what sort of reasons. They themselves are angry when they are pained;
1379a12	for one who is pained is aiming at something; if, then, someone directly
1379a13	opposes him in anything, for instance a thirsty man in drinking, or if
1379a14	not directly, he appears to do the same thing all the same; and if someone works against him,
1379a15	or does not work with him, or troubles him in some other way when he is in this state, he is angry
1379a16	with them all. That is why the sick, the poor, ⟨those at war,⟩ lovers, the thirsty,
1379a17	in general those who desire something and do not succeed, are irascible and easily roused,
1379a18	above all against those who slight their present condition: the sick man against those who slight
1379a19	his illness, the poor man against those who slight his poverty, the man at war against those who slight
1379a20	his war, the lover against those who slight his love,
1379a21	and so with the others ⟨and if not that, then if anyone
1379a22	slights anything else⟩; for each has his path
1379a23	to his own anger prepared by the emotion
1379a24	present in him. Again, if one happens to expect the opposite;
1379a25	for what is far from expectation pains more, just as what is far from expectation
1379a26	delights more if what one wishes comes about. That is why
1379a27	seasons and times and dispositions and ages are clear from this,
1379a28	which are easily moved to anger, and where and when;
1379a29	and the more people are in these conditions, the more easily they are moved.
1379a30	Those, then, who are in this state are easily moved to anger; and they are angry
1379a31	with those who laugh at them, mock them and jeer at them
1379a32	(for they commit outrage), and with those who do them the kind of harm that
1379a33	is a sign of outrage; such acts must be done neither in return for anything
1379a34	nor for the benefit of those who do them; for then they seem to be done through outrage. And
1379a35	with those who speak ill of and despise what they themselves are most serious about: for instance
1379a36	those who pride themselves on philosophy, if someone speaks against philosophy, and those who pride themselves on their
1379a37	looks, if someone speaks against their looks, and likewise in the other cases. And this is far
1379a38	more so if they suspect that these things are not theirs, either at all or not strongly, or not in others' opinion;
1379b1	for when they are quite sure that they excel in the things
1379b2	at which they are jeered, they do not care. And with friends more
1379b3	than with those who are not friends; for they think it more fitting
1379b4	to be well treated by them than not. And with those who are used to honoring them or
1379b5	paying attention to them, if they no longer behave so toward them; for they think that by these too
1379b6	they are despised, or they would do the same as before. And with those
1379b7	who do not return their benefits nor repay them equally. And with those
1379b8	who act against them, if they are their inferiors; for all such people
1379b9	appear to despise them, the one as though they were inferiors, the other
1379b10	as though the benefits came from inferiors. And with those of no account, if they
1379b11	slight them at all, all the more; for anger at a slight is assumed to be against
1379b12	those for whom slighting is not fitting, and it is fitting for inferiors not to slight.
1379b13	And with friends, if they do not speak or act well toward them, and
1379b14	still more if they do the opposite, and if they do not perceive their needs,
1379b15	as Plexippus in Antiphon's play was with Meleager; for not perceiving is a sign
1379b16	of slight, since what we care about does not
1379b17	escape our notice. And with those who rejoice at their misfortunes and in general
1379b18	are cheerful in the midst of their misfortunes; for this is a sign either of an enemy or of one who slights.
1379b19	And with those who do not care if they cause them pain;
1379b20	that is why people are angry with those who bring bad news. And with those who either listen
1379b21	to stories about their faults or look on at them; for they are like
1379b22	either those who slight them or enemies; for friends share the pain, and all who look on at
1379b23	their own faults are pained. Further, with those who slight them before
1379b24	five kinds of people: those with whom they compete for honor, those whom they admire, those
1379b25	by whom they wish to be admired, those before whom they feel shame, or those who
1379b26	feel shame before them; if anyone slights them before these, they are
1379b27	angrier. And with those who slight such things as it would be shameful for them
1379b28	not to defend: parents, children, wives, subjects.
1379b29	And with those who do not return a favor; for the slight is
1379b30	contrary to what is fitting. And with those who use irony against people who are serious;
1379b31	for irony is contemptuous. And with those who do good to others,
1379b32	if not to them as well; for this too is contemptuous, not to
1379b33	think a man worthy of what everyone else is thought worthy of. Forgetfulness, too, produces anger,
1379b34	even forgetting names, trivial as it is;
1379b35	for forgetfulness too seems to be
1379b36	a sign of slight: for it comes about
1379b37	through neglect, and
1379b38	neglect is a kind of slight.
1380a1	With whom people are angry, then, and in what state, and for what reasons, has
1380a2	been said together; and it is clear that the speaker would need to bring the hearers by his speech into
1380a3	the state of those who are prone to anger, and to show his opponents
1380a4	liable for the things at which people are angry, and of the kind with whom
1380a5	people are angry.
