1393a23	It remains to speak about the proofs common to all, since
1393a24	the particular ones have been discussed. The common proofs are of two kinds:
1393a25	example and enthymeme; for the maxim is a part of an enthymeme.
1393a26	First, then, let us speak about example;
1393a27	for example is like induction, and induction is a starting point.
1393a28	There are two kinds of examples: one kind of example
1393a29	is to tell of things that have happened before, another is to
1393a30	make them up oneself. Of the latter, one kind is comparison, another fables, such as the Aesopic and
1393a31	Libyan fables. Telling of things that have happened is something like this: as if one
1393a32	were to say that one must prepare against the king and not allow him to subdue Egypt;
1393a33	for in the past Darius did not cross over before he had taken Egypt,
1393b1	but having taken it he crossed; and again Xerxes did not
1393b2	set about it before he had taken it, but having taken it he crossed; so that
1393b3	this king too, if he takes it, will cross over; and so it must not be allowed.
1393b4	Comparison is the Socratic sort: for instance, if one were to say that
1393b5	officials should not be chosen by lot; for it is as if one were to
1393b6	choose athletes by lot, not those able to compete but those on whom the lot falls,
1393b7	or to choose by lot which of the sailors should steer, as though it should be
1393b8	the one on whom the lot falls and not the one who knows how. A fable is like the fable of
1393b9	Stesichorus about Phalaris and ⟨that⟩ of Aesop on behalf of the demagogue.
1393b10	For Stesichorus, when the people had chosen a general with full powers,
1393b11	Phalaris, at Himera, and were about to give him a bodyguard,
1393b12	after discussing other matters told them a fable: a horse had
1393b13	a meadow to himself; a stag came and spoiled
1393b14	the pasture, and wishing to take revenge on the stag the horse asked a
1393b15	man whether he could help him take revenge on the stag,
1393b16	and the man said yes, if the horse would take a bit and let him mount him
1393b17	with javelins in his hand; the horse agreed, and when the man had mounted, instead of
1393b18	taking revenge the horse became the man's slave. “So you too,”
1393b19	he said, “see that in wishing to take revenge on your enemies
1393b20	you do not suffer the same as the horse; for you already have the bit,
1393b21	having chosen a general with full powers; but if you give him a bodyguard and
1393b22	let him mount you, you will at once be slaves to Phalaris.” Aesop,
1393b23	speaking to the assembly at Samos when a demagogue was on trial for his life, said
1393b24	that a fox crossing a river was swept into a ravine and,
1393b25	being unable to get out, was in distress for a long time, and many dog-ticks
1393b26	clung to her; a hedgehog, wandering by, saw her and
1393b27	took pity, and asked whether he should take the ticks off her;
1393b28	but she would not let him; and when he asked why, “because these,” she said, “are already
1393b29	full of me and draw little blood; but if you take
1393b30	these off, others will come hungry and drink up
1393b31	the rest of my blood.” “So you too, men
1393b32	of Samos: this man will do you no more harm
1393b33	(for he is rich); but if you put him to death,
1394a1	others will come who are poor, who will use you up, stealing what is left.”
1394a2	Fables suit speeches to the assembly, and they have this
1394a3	advantage, that it is hard to find similar things that have happened,
1394a4	but fables are easier; for one must make them up, as one does comparisons, if one
1394a5	can see the likeness, which is easier with philosophy.
1394a6	So what is supplied through fables is easier to provide, but more useful
1394a7	for deliberation is what comes through actual events; for for the most part
1394a8	the future is like the past. One should
1394a9	use examples, when one has no enthymemes,
1394a10	as demonstrations (for proof comes through them), and when one has them, as
1394a11	testimony, using them as a supplement to the enthymemes; for when they are put
1394a12	first they resemble induction, and induction is not appropriate
1394a13	to rhetoric except in a few cases; when they are added after, they resemble testimony, and
1394a14	a witness is persuasive everywhere; that is why one who puts them first must
1394a15	give many, but for one who adds them after, even one is
1394a16	enough; for a good witness, even one, is useful.
1394a17	How many kinds of examples there are, then, and
1394a18	how and when they should be used, has been said.
