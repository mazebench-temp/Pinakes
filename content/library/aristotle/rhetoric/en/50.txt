1409a24	Style must be either strung-on
1409a25	and made one by the connective, like the preludes in dithyrambs,
1409a26	or compact and like the antistrophes of the ancient poets.
1409a27	The strung-on style is the ancient one:
1409a28	“[This is the setting forth of the inquiry of Herodotus of Thurii]” (for formerly
1409a29	everyone used it, but now not many do). By strung-on
1409a30	I mean the style that has no end in itself, unless the matter
1409a31	being told is finished. It is unpleasant because it is unlimited; for
1409a32	everyone wants to see the end. That is why runners pant and collapse
1409a33	at the finishing posts: while they see the finish ahead they do not
1409a34	tire before it. This, then, is the strung-on style;
1409a35	the compact is the one in periods. By
1409a36	a period I mean a stretch of speech that has a beginning and an end
1409a37	in itself and a magnitude easily taken in at a glance.
1409b1	Such a style is pleasant and easy to learn: pleasant because
1409b2	it is the opposite of the unlimited, and because the hearer always thinks
1409b3	he has hold of something and that something has been brought to a limit for him, while
1409b4	to foresee nothing and get nowhere is unpleasant; easy to learn
1409b5	because it is easy to remember, and that is because the style in periods has number,
1409b6	which is the easiest of all things to remember. That is why
1409b7	everyone remembers verse better than loose prose; for it has a number
1409b8	by which it is measured. The period must be complete †also in thought†,
1409b9	and not be broken off like the iambics of Sophocles,
1409b10	“This land is Calydon, of Pelops' country”; for by the division one may
1409b11	take the opposite of what is meant, as in
1409b12	the line cited, that Calydon is in the Peloponnese.
1409b13	A period is either in clauses or simple. Style in
1409b14	clauses is complete and divided and
1409b15	easily spoken in one breath, not in its division †as the period too†
1409b16	but as a whole (a clause is one of its two parts); by simple
1409b17	I mean a period of one clause. Clauses and
1409b18	periods should be neither stunted nor long. For a short one
1409b19	often makes the hearer stumble (for when
1409b20	he is still pressing on toward what lies ahead, and toward the measure whose limit he has in himself,
1409b21	and is pulled back by the speaker's stopping, there must be a kind of stumble
1409b22	from the check); long ones make him lag behind,
1409b23	like those who turn wide of the post; for these too leave behind
1409b24	their companions on the walk. In the same way
1409b25	long periods become a speech in themselves, like a prelude,
1409b26	so that what Democritus of Chios mocked comes true, jibing at
1409b27	Melanippides, who wrote preludes instead of antistrophes:
1409b28	“A man contrives evil for himself who contrives evil for another,
1409b29	and a long prelude is worst for its maker.”
1409b30	For such a jibe can fittingly be made against long-claused periods
1409b31	too. And those whose clauses are very short do not make a period,
1409b32	and so they carry the hearer headlong. Of the style in
1409b33	clauses, one kind is divided and the other opposed:
1409b34	divided, as “I have often wondered at those
1409b35	who convened the festivals and established the athletic
1409b36	contests”; opposed, the one in which, in each of the two clauses, opposite
1409b37	is set beside opposite, or the same word is yoked to opposites,
1410a1	as “They benefited both, those
1410a2	who stayed behind and those who followed: for the
1410a3	one they won more land than they had at home,
1410a4	and to the other they left enough at home.”
1410a5	The opposites are staying and following, enough and more. “So that both
1410a6	those who need money and those who wish to enjoy it”:
1410a7	enjoyment is opposed to acquisition. And again, “It often happens
1410a8	in these that the wise fail and
1410a9	fools succeed.” “At once they were judged worthy of the prize of valor, and not
1410a10	long after they took the rule of the sea.” “To sail
1410a11	through the mainland and march through the sea,
1410a12	bridging the Hellespont and digging through Athos.” “And
1410a13	men who are citizens by nature are deprived of their city by law.” “For some
1410a14	of them perished miserably, others were saved shamefully.”
1410a15	“To use barbarians as servants in private, but in public
1410a16	to look on while many of our allies are slaves.” “Either to possess it
1410a17	living, or to leave it behind at our death.” And what someone said against Peitholaus
1410a18	and Lycophron in the court: “These men sold you
1410a19	when they were at home, and now that they have come to you they have bought you.”
1410a20	All these do what has been described. Such a style is pleasant
1410a21	because opposites are most easily known, and more easily known
1410a22	side by side, and because it is like a syllogism;
1410a23	for refutation is a bringing together of opposites.
1410a24	This, then, is antithesis. Parisōsis is when
1410a25	the clauses are equal; paromoiōsis is when each clause has its
1410a26	extremities alike. This must come at the beginning or at the end:
1410a27	at the beginning it is always the words; at the end, the
1410a28	last syllables, or inflections of the same word, or the same
1410a29	word. At the beginning, such things as “For he got from him a field
1410a30	left fallow”, and “they could be won with gifts and swayed
1410a31	by words”; at the end, “You would have thought he had not
1410a32	fathered a child but become a child himself”,
1410a33	“in the greatest anxieties and the smallest
1410a34	hopes”. Inflections of the same word: “Does he think he should be set up
1410a35	in bronze, when he is not worth a bronze coin?” The same word: “You spoke
1410a36	ill of him alive, and now you write ill of him.” From one syllable: “What
1410a37	terrible thing would you have suffered if you had seen a man idle?” The same sentence can have all of these at once:
1410b1	the same thing can be antithesis, parisōsis and
1410b2	homoeoteleuton. The beginnings of periods have been listed nearly in full
1410b3	in the Theodectea. There are also
1410b4	false antitheses, like those Epicharmus made:
1410b5	“At one time I was in their house, at another I was with them.”
