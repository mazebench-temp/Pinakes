1385b11	So much for doing favors and being ungracious. What
1385b12	things are pitiable, and whom people pity, and in what state they themselves are,
1385b13	let us now say. Let pity be a kind of pain at an apparent evil, destructive
1385b14	or painful, befalling one who does not deserve it, which one might expect oneself
1385b15	to suffer, or one of one's own, and this when it appears
1385b16	near; for clearly one who is going to feel pity must be
1385b17	such as to think that he might suffer some evil, either himself or
1385b18	one of his own, and an evil such as was stated in the definition,
1385b19	or like it, or nearly so; that is why neither those who are utterly ruined
1385b20	feel pity (for they think they can suffer nothing more; they have suffered),
1385b21	nor those who think themselves supremely happy, but they commit outrage instead; for if
1385b22	they think they have all good things, clearly they think they have also the
1385b23	impossibility of suffering any evil; for this too is one of the good things. Those
1385b24	are such as to think they might suffer who have suffered already
1385b25	and escaped, and older men, through good sense and
1385b26	experience, and the weak, and those who are rather cowardly,
1385b27	and the educated; for they reason well. And those who have parents
1385b28	or children or wives; for these are their own, and such as to suffer the
1385b29	evils mentioned. And those who are neither in an emotion of courage, such as anger
1385b30	or confidence (for these take no thought of the future), nor in a
1385b31	disposition given to outrage (for these too take no thought of suffering
1385b32	anything), but those between these; nor again those who are very much afraid;
1385b33	for the panic-stricken do not pity, because they are taken up with
1385b34	their own emotion. And if they think that some people are decent; for
1385b35	one who thinks no one is will think that all deserve evil.
1386a1	And in general when one is in a state to remember
1386a2	that such things have happened to oneself or ⟨to one⟩ of one's own, or
1386a3	to expect them to happen to oneself or to one of one's own. In what state,
1386a4	then, people feel pity has been said; what they pity is clear from
1386a5	the definition: all painful and distressing things that are
1386a6	destructive are pitiable, and all that are ruinous, and all
1386a7	evils of some magnitude of which fortune is the cause. Distressing and
1386a8	destructive are deaths, maltreatment of the body, afflictions, old age,
1386a9	diseases and lack of food; evils of which fortune is the cause are
1386a10	friendlessness, fewness of friends (that is why being torn from friends and
1386a11	companions is pitiable), ugliness, weakness, disability, and
1386a12	evil coming from where some good should have come, and
1386a13	this happening often, and some good coming after one has suffered,
1386a14	as the king's gifts were sent down to Diopeithes after he was dead,
1386a15	and either no good having come at all, or there being no
1386a16	enjoyment of the good things that came. The things at which people feel pity, then,
1386a17	are these and the like; and people pity
1386a18	those they know, if they are not very close to them in kinship
1386a19	(for these they feel as for themselves when they are about to suffer;
1386a20	that is why the son of Amasis, they say, did not weep over his son when he was led
1386a21	to his death, but did weep over his friend begging;
1386a22	for this was pitiable, but that was terrible; for the terrible is
1386a23	different from the pitiable and drives out pity, and is often
1386a24	useful to the opposite; ⟨for⟩ people no longer pity when the terrible
1386a25	is near themselves), and people pity those like them in age, in
1386a26	character, in states, in rank, in birth; for in all
1386a27	these it appears more that the thing might happen to oneself too; for in general here too
1386a28	one must take it that whatever people fear for themselves, they
1386a29	pity when it happens to others. And since sufferings that appear near
1386a30	are pitiable, while those ten thousand years past or to come,
1386a31	neither expecting nor remembering them, people either do not pity at all or not
1386a32	in the same way, those who add to the effect with gestures and voice
1386a33	and dress and delivery in general must be more pitiable (for they
1386a34	make the evil appear near, putting it before our eyes either
1386a35	as about to happen or as having happened;
1386b1	and what has just happened, or is soon to happen, is more pitiable), ⟨and⟩
1386b2	for this reason signs too, for instance the clothes of those who have suffered and
1386b3	the like, and the actions and words and all else of those in
1386b4	suffering, for instance of those already dying. And most pitiable of all is that men of worth should be
1386b5	in such critical moments; for all these
1386b6	make pity greater by appearing near, and because the
1386b7	suffering is undeserved and appears before our eyes.
