1366a23	After this, let us speak about virtue and vice, the noble
1366a24	and the shameful; for these are the targets of one who praises and blames.
1366a25	In speaking about these we shall at the same time make clear the means by
1366a26	which we shall be taken to be of a certain character, which was
1366a27	the second proof; for by the same means we shall be able to make both ourselves and another
1366a28	worthy of belief in respect of virtue. Since it often happens that people praise,
1366a29	both in jest and in earnest, not only
1366a30	a human being or a god but also lifeless things and any animal
1366a31	that comes along, premises about these too must be taken in the same way;
1366a32	so let us speak about these too, just enough
1366a33	for illustration. The noble, then, is what, being chosen for itself,
1366a34	is praiseworthy, or what, being good, is pleasant because it is good. If
1366a35	this is the noble, virtue must be noble,
1366a36	for being good it is praiseworthy. Virtue is, it seems, a capacity
1366a37	for providing and preserving goods, and a capacity
1366a38	for conferring many great benefits, indeed all kinds of benefits in all things.
1366b1	The parts of virtue are justice, courage, self-control,
1366b2	magnificence, greatness of soul, generosity, practical wisdom, wisdom.
1366b3	The greatest virtues must be those most useful
1366b4	to others, if virtue is a capacity for doing good;
1366b5	and that is why people honor the just and the courageous
1366b6	most, for the one is useful in war, the other in war and in peace,
1366b7	to others. Next comes generosity; for the generous give freely
1366b8	and do not compete for money, which others desire
1366b9	most. Justice is the virtue by which each has
1366b10	his own, and as the law prescribes; injustice, that by which one has what belongs to others,
1366b11	not as the law prescribes. Courage is that by which men do
1366b12	noble deeds in dangers, as the law commands, and
1366b13	serve the law; cowardice is the opposite. Self-control is
1366b14	the virtue by which people are disposed toward the pleasures of the body
1366b15	as the law commands; licentiousness is the opposite. Generosity
1366b16	does good with money; meanness is the opposite.
1366b17	Greatness of soul is the virtue that produces great benefits
1366b18	[smallness of soul is the opposite]; magnificence is the virtue that produces greatness
1366b19	in expenditure; smallness of soul and shabbiness
1366b20	are the opposites. Practical wisdom is the virtue of the intellect by
1366b21	which people are able to deliberate well about the goods and evils
1366b22	mentioned in relation to happiness. About
1366b23	virtue and vice in general, and about their parts, enough has been said
1366b24	for the present occasion, and the
1366b25	rest is not hard to see. For it is clear that what produces
1366b26	virtue must be noble (for it tends to virtue), and so must what comes from
1366b27	virtue; such are the signs of virtue and its
1366b28	works. And since the signs, and such things as are the works of a good man
1366b29	or what he undergoes, are noble, it follows that the works of courage, or the signs of courage,
1366b30	or what has been done courageously are noble, and just things and
1366b31	works done justly (but not what is suffered justly; for in this alone of the
1366b32	virtues is what is just not always noble: in punishment,
1366b33	to be punished justly is more shameful than unjustly); and
1366b34	likewise with the other virtues. And deeds whose prize
1366b35	is honor are noble; and those rewarded with honor rather than money. And
1366b36	whatever desirable things one does not for one's own sake; and
1366b37	things good absolutely, such as what one has done for one's country, neglecting
1366b38	one's own interest; and things good by nature, and things not good for oneself,
1367a1	for those are for one's own sake. And whatever can belong to a man
1367a2	dead rather than alive; for what belongs to him alive
1367a3	is more for his own sake. And whatever deeds are done for others' sake,
1367a4	for they are less for one's own. And successes that concern others but
1367a5	not oneself, and those that concern one's benefactors,
1367a6	for that is just. And benefits conferred, for they are not for oneself.
1367a7	And the opposites of what people are ashamed of; for people are ashamed of shameful things
1367a8	when they say them, when they do them, and when they are about to,
1367a9	as Sappho too has shown in her poem, when Alcaeus said,
1367a10	“I want to say something, but shame prevents me”:
1367a11	“If you had a longing for good or noble things, and your tongue
1367a12	were not stirring up some evil to say, shame would not
1367a13	hold your eyes, but you would speak of what is right.”
1367a14	And things people are anxious about without being afraid; for
1367a15	this is how they feel about the goods that lead to
1367a16	reputation. And the virtues and works of those who are better by nature
1367a17	are nobler, those of a man, for instance, than those of a woman.
1367a18	And virtues that others enjoy rather than
1367a19	their possessors; that is why the just and justice are noble.
1367a20	And to take vengeance on one's enemies and not
1367a21	be reconciled; for paying back is just, and
1367a22	the just is noble, and not to be beaten belongs to a courageous man.
1367a23	Victory and honor too are among noble things; for they are desirable
1367a24	though they bring no profit, and they show superiority in virtue. And things
1367a25	worth remembering, and the more so the more they are. And what does not attend a man
1367a26	while he lives, and what honor follows, and what is extraordinary, and what
1367a27	belongs to one alone, are nobler, for they are more memorable. And
1367a28	possessions that bring no profit, for they are more fitting for a free man. And what is
1367a29	peculiar to each people is noble, and whatever signs there are of what each people
1367a30	praises: in Lacedaemon, for instance, wearing long hair is noble, for it is the sign
1367a31	of a free man, since it is not easy for a man with long hair to do any
1367a32	hired laborer's work. And to practice no vulgar craft;
1367a33	for it is the mark of a free man not to live at another's beck. We should also take
1367a34	qualities close to a man's actual ones as if they were the same, both for praise
1367a35	and for blame: calling the cautious man cold and
1367a36	scheming, and the simpleton good-natured, or the insensible
1367a37	gentle; and describing each from the qualities that go with it, always
1367a38	in the best light: the irascible and the frenzied
1367a39	as frank, the arrogant as magnificent and dignified,
1367b1	and those in the excesses as if they had the virtues,
1367b2	the rash man as courageous and the prodigal as generous;
1367b3	for so it will seem to most people, and at the same time a fallacy
1367b4	can be drawn from the motive. For if a man runs risks where there is no need,
1367b5	much more would he seem likely to where it is noble; and if he is lavish
1367b6	to anyone who comes along, so too to his friends; for to do good to all
1367b7	is an excess of virtue. Consider also before whom the praise is given;
1367b8	for as Socrates used to say, it is not hard to praise Athenians
1367b9	among Athenians. One must speak of what is honored among each audience
1367b10	as present, as among Scythians, or Laconians, or philosophers.
1367b11	And in general one should bring the honored under the noble, since
1367b12	they seem to be neighbors. And whatever is fitting, for instance, if deeds are worthy
1367b13	of one's ancestors and of one's past achievements; for it brings happiness and
1367b14	is noble to add to one's honor. And if, beyond what is fitting,
1367b15	a man goes toward the better and the nobler: if, for instance, he was
1367b16	moderate in good fortune and great-souled in bad, or, growing greater,
1367b17	became better and more conciliatory. Such is
1367b18	Iphicrates' saying, “From what to what,” and the
1367b19	Olympic victor's, “Once, with a rough yoke on my shoulders,”
1367b20	and Simonides', “She whose father, husband
1367b21	and brothers were tyrants.” Since praise is drawn from
1367b22	actions, and acting by choice is the mark of the good man,
1367b23	we must try to show that he acted by choice;
1367b24	and it is useful that he should seem to have done it often.
1367b25	That is why coincidences and chance events must be treated as if they were
1367b26	chosen; for if many similar instances are brought forward, it will seem a sign
1367b27	of virtue and choice. Praise is
1367b28	speech that sets forth greatness of virtue. One must therefore show
1367b29	that a man's actions are of that kind. Encomium is concerned with deeds
1367b30	(the surrounding circumstances help belief, such as good birth and education;
1367b31	for it is probable that the good come from the good, and that one brought up so
1367b32	is of that kind), which is why we compose encomia of those who have acted. Deeds
1367b33	are signs of a man's state, since we would praise even one who had not
1367b34	acted, if we believed him to be such. Calling a man blessed and
1367b35	calling him happy are the same as each other, but not the same as these;
1367b36	rather, just as happiness includes virtue,
1367b37	calling a man happy includes these. Praise and advice
1367b38	have a common form; for what you would suggest in giving advice
1367b39	becomes encomium when the wording is changed.
1368a1	Since, then, we know what one should do and what sort of man
1368a2	one should be, when we state these as suggestions we must change
1368a3	and turn the wording; for instance, that one should not pride oneself
1368a4	on what is due to fortune but on what is due to oneself.
1368a5	Said in this way it amounts to a suggestion; said thus, to praise: “Priding
1368a6	himself not on what came to him by fortune but on what he owed
1368a7	to himself.” So when you want to praise, see what you would suggest; and when
1368a8	you want to suggest, see what you would praise. The wording will necessarily be
1368a9	opposite when the prohibition and the non-prohibition are changed around.
1368a10	One should also use many means of amplification: for instance, if
1368a11	he did something alone, or first, or with a few, or more than anyone;
1368a12	for all these are noble. And the topic drawn from times
1368a13	and occasions, that is, if it was beyond what was to be expected. And if
1368a14	he has succeeded in the same thing often; for it is great, and would seem due not to
1368a15	fortune but to himself. And if the incentives
1368a16	and honors were invented and established on his account, and if
1368a17	the first encomium was composed for him, as for Hippolochus, and
1368a18	the setting up of a statue in the marketplace for Harmodius and Aristogeiton;
1368a19	and likewise in the opposite cases. And if you have not much to say about the man himself,
1368a20	compare him with others, as Isocrates used to do because of his
1368a21	unfamiliarity with pleading in court. And you should compare him with famous men;
1368a22	for it amplifies, and it is noble, if he is better than men of worth. Amplification
1368a23	falls naturally under praise; for it lies in superiority,
1368a24	and superiority is one of the noble things. That is why, if not with famous men,
1368a25	you should still compare him with others, since superiority
1368a26	seems to reveal virtue. In general, of the forms common to all
1368a27	speeches, amplification is best suited to epideictic
1368a28	(for these take the actions as agreed, so that what remains
1368a29	is to invest them with greatness and beauty); examples to
1368a30	deliberative speeches (for we judge the future by divining it
1368a31	from the past); and enthymemes to judicial speeches
1368a32	(for what has happened most admits of a cause and demonstration,
1368a33	because it is unclear). The sources, then, from which nearly all praise and
1368a34	blame is spoken, what one should look to
1368a35	in praising and blaming, and from what encomia
1368a36	and reproaches are made, are these; for once we have these, their
1368a37	opposites are clear, since blame comes from the opposites.
