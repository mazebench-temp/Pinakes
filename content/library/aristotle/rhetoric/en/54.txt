1414a31	A speech has two parts: for one must state the subject
1414a32	it is about, and demonstrate it. So it is impossible to state it and not
1414a33	demonstrate it, or to demonstrate it without stating it first; for whoever
1414a34	demonstrates demonstrates something, and whoever states something first does so in order
1414a35	to demonstrate it. Of these, the one is the statement and the other
1414a36	the proof, as if one were to divide them into the problem and
1414a37	the demonstration. As things are, people divide them laughably; for narration surely belongs to judicial
1414a38	speech alone; how can there be in an epideictic speech or a speech to the assembly
1414a39	a narration of the kind they mean, or a reply to the opponent,
1414b1	or an epilogue in demonstrative speeches? Proem,
1414b2	comparison and review occur in speeches to the assembly only
1414b3	when there is controversy. For there is often accusation and
1414b4	defense there, but not insofar as it is advice. And
1414b5	the epilogue does not even belong to every judicial speech, for example if the speech is
1414b6	short or the matter easy to remember; for what it does
1414b7	is to cut down the length. The necessary parts, then, are statement and
1414b8	proof. These are the proper parts; at most there are proem,
1414b9	statement, proof and epilogue; for the reply to the opponent is part of
1414b10	the proofs, and comparison is an amplification of one's own case, so that
1414b11	it is a part of the proofs (for whoever does this demonstrates
1414b12	something); but the proem is not, nor the epilogue: it reminds.
1414b13	If one makes such divisions, then, as the followers of
1414b14	Theodorus did, narration will be one thing, and supplementary narration
1414b15	and preliminary narration others, and refutation and further refutation. One should
1414b16	give a name only when speaking of a species and a difference; otherwise
1414b17	it becomes empty and silly, as Licymnius does in his
1414b18	Art, naming “wafting”, “wandering off” and “branches”.
