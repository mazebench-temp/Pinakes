1363b5	Since people often agree that two things are both advantageous but dispute about which
1363b6	is more so, we should next speak about the
1363b7	greater good and the more advantageous. Let
1363b8	the exceeding be as much and more, and the exceeded what is
1363b9	contained in it; greater and more are always relative to less,
1363b10	while great and small, and much and little, are relative to the size of
1363b11	most things, the great exceeding it and the small
1363b12	falling short, and much and little likewise. Since, then, we call good
1363b13	what is chosen for its own sake and not for the sake of another,
1363b14	and what all things aim at, and what anything would choose that acquired intelligence and
1363b15	practical wisdom, and what produces and preserves, or what
1363b16	such things follow upon [and that for whose sake is the end]; and the end
1363b17	is that for whose sake the rest are, and good for a particular person is what
1363b18	stands in this relation to him: it follows that more goods than one, or than
1363b19	fewer, when the one or the fewer are counted among them, are the greater
1363b20	good; for they exceed, and what is contained in them is exceeded.
1363b21	And if the greatest of one class exceeds the greatest of another, the classes themselves
1363b22	do too; and if the classes do, so does the greatest of the one the greatest of the other:
1363b23	for instance, if the biggest man is bigger than the biggest woman,
1363b24	men in general are bigger than women; and if men
1363b25	in general are bigger than women, the biggest man is bigger than the
1363b26	biggest woman; for the superiority of classes is proportional
1363b27	to that of the greatest members in them. And when this
1363b28	follows that, but that does not follow this; and it follows either by
1363b29	being simultaneous, or subsequent, or potential; for the use
1363b30	of what follows is contained in the use of the other. Life follows being healthy
1363b31	simultaneously, but not health life; knowing follows learning subsequently,
1363b32	and defrauding follows temple-robbing potentially,
1363b33	for one who has robbed a temple would also defraud. And things that exceed
1363b34	the same thing by a greater amount are greater, for they must exceed the
1363b35	lesser too. And things productive of a greater good are greater, for that
1363b36	is what being productive of the greater meant. And likewise that whose producer
1363b37	is greater; for if the wholesome is preferable to
1363b38	the pleasant and a greater good, health is also greater than pleasure.
1364a1	And what is chosen for itself is preferable to what is not, as strength is to
1364a2	the wholesome; for the latter is not for its own sake, the former is, which
1364a3	was what the good is. And if one is an end and the other not; for the one
1364a4	is for another's sake, the other for its own, as exercise is for a body in
1364a5	good condition. And what is less in need than the other
1364a6	of other things, for it is more self-sufficient; and that needs less which needs fewer
1364a7	or easier things. And when this cannot exist without that,
1364a8	or cannot come into being, but the other can without this,
1364a9	what does not need the other is more self-sufficient, and so seems the greater good.
1364a10	And if one is a principle and the other not, or one a cause and the other not,
1364a11	for the same reason; for without a cause and a principle nothing can be
1364a12	or come into being. And of two principles, what comes from the greater principle
1364a13	is greater; and of two causes, what comes from the greater cause
1364a14	is greater. And conversely, of two principles, the principle of the greater
1364a15	is greater, and of two causes the cause of the greater is greater.
1364a16	It is clear from what has been said, then, that a thing can be made to seem greater both ways:
1364a17	for if one is a principle and the other not, it will seem greater, and also if
1364a18	it is not a principle and the other is; for the end is greater, and not the principle;
1364a19	as Leodamas, accusing Callistratus, said that the one who planned
1364a20	did more wrong than the one who did the deed, for it would not have been done had he not
1364a21	planned it; and again, accusing Chabrias, that the doer did more wrong than the
1364a22	planner, for it would not have happened had there been no one to do it; for
1364a23	people plot for this purpose, that someone may act. And the
1364a24	rarer is greater than the plentiful, as gold than iron, though it is less
1364a25	useful; for its possession is greater because it is harder to get.
1364a26	(In another way the plentiful is greater than the rare, because
1364a27	its use exceeds; for often exceeds seldom;
1364a28	hence it is said, “Best is water.”) And in general
1364a29	the harder is greater than the easier, for it is rarer. In another
1364a30	way the easier is greater than the harder, for it is as we wish.
1364a31	And that whose opposite is greater, and that whose loss is greater. And
1364a32	virtue is greater than non-vice, and vice than non-virtue; for the former are
1364a33	ends, the latter not. And things whose works are nobler or more shameful are
1364a34	themselves greater; and of things whose vices and virtues are greater, the works
1364a35	are greater, since as causes and principles are, so are their results,
1364a36	and as the results are, so are the causes and principles.
1364a37	And things whose superiority is preferable or nobler: seeing keenly, for instance,
1364a38	is preferable to smelling keenly (as sight is to smell),
1364b1	and being fonder of one's companions nobler than being fonder of money,
1364b2	so that love of companions is nobler than love of money. And
1364b3	conversely, the excesses of better things are better, and
1364b4	those of nobler things nobler. And things for which the desires are nobler or
1364b5	better; for greater longings are for greater things. And
1364b6	desires for nobler or better things are better and
1364b7	nobler, for the same reason. And things whose sciences are nobler or
1364b8	more serious are themselves nobler and more serious;
1364b9	for as the science is, so is its truth, and each science prescribes
1364b10	what is its own. And the sciences of more serious and nobler things
1364b11	are proportionally so, for the same reason. And what the wise would judge, or
1364b12	have judged, to be the greater good, or all, or most, or the majority, or
1364b13	the best, must be so, either absolutely
1364b14	or in the respect in which they judged by practical wisdom. This holds generally
1364b15	of other things too; for what a thing is, and how much, and of what sort, are
1364b16	as science and practical wisdom would say. But we have said it
1364b17	of goods; for good was defined as what each thing, if it acquired
1364b18	practical wisdom, would choose; so it is clear that
1364b19	that is greater which practical wisdom pronounces more so. And what belongs to the better,
1364b20	either absolutely or insofar as they are better, as courage is greater than strength.
1364b21	And what the better man would choose, absolutely or insofar as he is better: for instance,
1364b22	to suffer wrong rather than to do it; for that is what the juster man would
1364b23	choose. And the more pleasant than the less pleasant; for all things
1364b24	pursue pleasure, and they long for being pleased for its own sake, and it is by these
1364b25	marks that the good and the end were defined; and more pleasant is what is
1364b26	freer from pain, and pleasant for longer. And the nobler
1364b27	than the less noble; for the noble is either what is pleasant or what is
1364b28	chosen for itself. And whatever people more wish to bring about
1364b29	for themselves or their friends is a greater good, and whatever
1364b30	they less wish to, a greater evil. And longer-lasting things than
1364b31	shorter-lived ones, and more secure things than less secure;
1364b32	for the use of the former exceeds in time, of the latter in wish:
1364b33	for whenever people wish, the use of what is secure is more at hand.
1364b34	And as with one of a set of coordinates or of like inflections,
1364b35	so the rest follow: for instance, if “courageously” is nobler and
1364b36	preferable to “with self-control,” courage too is preferable to self-control,
1364b37	and being courageous to having self-control. And what all
1364b38	choose is greater than what not all choose, and what the majority choose than what the minority choose;
1365a1	for good was what all desire, so that the greater is what
1365a2	they desire more. And what opponents or enemies choose, or judges,
1365a3	or those whom these judge; for in the one case it is as if all said so,
1365a4	in the other those in authority and those who know. And sometimes what all
1365a5	share in is greater, for not to share is a disgrace; sometimes what
1365a6	no one or few share in, for it is rarer. And what is more praiseworthy,
1365a7	for it is nobler. And likewise things whose honors are greater; for
1365a8	honor is a sort of valuation. And things whose penalties are greater.
1365a9	And what is greater than things agreed or seen to be great.
1365a10	And the same things, divided into their parts, seem
1365a11	greater, for there seems to be more to exceed. That is why
1365a12	the poet says that what persuaded Meleager to rise and fight was
1365a13	“all the ills that come to men whose city is taken: the people
1365a14	perish, and fire levels the city, and strangers
1365a15	lead away the children.” And combining and
1365a16	building up, as Epicharmus does, both for the same reason
1365a17	as division (for combination shows great
1365a18	superiority) and because it seems to be the origin and cause of great things.
1365a19	And since what is harder and rarer is greater,
1365a20	occasions and ages and places and times
1365a21	and powers make things great; for if a thing is done beyond one's power,
1365a22	beyond one's age, beyond one's equals, and if in such a way
1365a23	or in such a place or at such a time, it will have the greatness of noble and good
1365a24	and just things, and of their opposites. Hence too the epigram on the
1365a25	Olympic victor: “Once, with a rough yoke on my shoulders,
1365a26	I carried fish from Argos to
1365a27	Tegea”; and Iphicrates
1365a28	praised himself by saying from what beginnings he had come to this. And
1365a29	what is natural is greater than what is acquired, for it is harder.
1365a30	Hence the poet says, “I am self-taught.” And
1365a31	the greatest part of something great: as Pericles, delivering the funeral oration,
1365a32	said that the youth had been taken from the city as if the spring had been
1365a33	taken out of the year. And things useful in greater need,
1365a34	such as those useful in old age and sickness. And of two things, the one nearer
1365a35	the end. And what is good for oneself rather than absolutely. And the possible
1365a36	rather than the impossible; for the one is good for oneself, the other not. And what lies at
1365a37	the end of life; for what is near the end is more of an end.
1365b1	And what aims at reality rather than at appearance; the definition of what aims at appearance
1365b2	is what one would not choose if one were going to go unnoticed. That is why receiving benefits
1365b3	would seem preferable to conferring them; for one will choose the first even if
1365b4	unnoticed, but no one, it seems, would choose to confer benefits unnoticed.
1365b5	And whatever people wish to be rather than to seem; for that aims more
1365b6	at reality. That is why they say that justice is a small thing,
1365b7	because it is preferable to seem just than to be just; but with being healthy
1365b8	it is not so. And what is more useful for many purposes, such as for
1365b9	life and living well and pleasure and doing noble deeds;
1365b10	that is why wealth and health seem to be the greatest goods, for they have
1365b11	all these. And what is freer from pain and accompanied by pleasure;
1365b12	for there is more than one good, since there is both pleasure and
1365b13	freedom from pain. And of two things, the one which, added to the same thing, makes the
1365b14	whole greater. And things whose presence is noticed rather than those not noticed;
1365b15	for these tend toward reality. That is why being rich would appear
1365b16	a greater good than seeming rich. And what is cherished, whether to some
1365b17	it is the only one and to others one among others. That is why the penalty is not the same
1365b18	if one blinds a one-eyed man and a man with two eyes;
1365b19	for what was cherished has been taken away. From what, then, proofs must
1365b20	be drawn in exhorting and dissuading has, roughly, been said.
