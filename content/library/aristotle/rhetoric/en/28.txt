1389b13	Such, then, is the character of the young; older men
1389b14	and those past their prime have characters made up mostly of the opposites
1389b15	of these; for because they have lived many years and
1389b16	have been deceived more often and made more mistakes, and because most things
1389b17	turn out badly, they are positive about nothing, and
1389b18	they do everything far less than they should. They think, but know nothing; and,
1389b19	being in two minds, they always add “perhaps” and “maybe,” and they say everything
1389b20	this way, nothing firmly. And they are ill-natured; for
1389b21	ill-nature is supposing everything to be for the worse. Further,
1389b22	they are suspicious because of their distrust, and distrustful because of their experience.
1389b23	And for these reasons they neither love nor hate strongly, but in keeping with
1389b24	Bias' precept they both love as though they will hate and hate
1389b25	as though they will love. And they are small-souled because they have been humbled by
1389b26	life; for they desire nothing great or out of the ordinary, but only what
1389b27	is needed for life. And they are ungenerous; for property is one of the
1389b28	necessities, and at the same time they know from experience how
1389b29	hard it is to acquire and how easy to lose. And they are cowardly
1389b30	and afraid of everything in advance; for they are disposed in the opposite way to the young:
1389b31	they are chilled, the young are warm, so that old age has prepared the way
1389b32	for cowardice; for fear too is a kind of chill.
1389b33	And they love life, and more so on their last day, because
1389b34	appetite is for what is absent, and what people lack they
1389b35	desire most. And they love themselves more than they should; for this too is a kind of smallness of soul. And
1389b36	they live for the advantageous and not for the noble, more than they should, because they love themselves;
1390a1	for the advantageous is good for oneself, the noble good absolutely. And
1390a2	they are shameless rather than prone to shame; for because they do not care
1390a3	as much for the noble as for the advantageous, they slight how they seem.
1390a4	And they are despondent because of their experience (for most of what happens
1390a5	is bad; most things turn out for the worse), and
1390a6	also because of their cowardice. And they live by memory rather than by
1390a7	hope; for what is left of their life is short and what is past
1390a8	long, and hope is of the future and memory
1390a9	of the past; this is the cause of their garrulousness too;
1390a10	for they keep talking of what has happened, since they enjoy
1390a11	remembering. And their fits of temper are sharp but weak, and their
1390a12	appetites have either left them or are weak, so that they are neither
1390a13	given to appetite nor apt to act on their appetites, but for
1390a14	gain; that is why men of that age appear self-controlled; for their
1390a15	appetites have slackened and are slaves to gain. And they
1390a16	live by calculation more than by character; for calculation is
1390a17	of the advantageous, character of virtue. And the wrongs
1390a18	they commit are from malice, not from outrage. Old men too
1390a19	are prone to pity, but not for the same reasons as the young; for the young pity through
1390a20	love of mankind, the old through weakness; for they think every suffering
1390a21	near to themselves, and this, we saw, makes one prone to pity;
1390a22	hence they are given to lamentation, and are not witty or
1390a23	fond of laughter; for lamenting is the opposite of loving laughter.
1390a24	Such, then, are the characters of the young and of older men,
1390a25	so that, since everyone accepts speeches spoken in their own character
1390a26	and by people like themselves, it is not unclear how, by using
1390a27	speeches, both the speakers and their speeches will appear such.
