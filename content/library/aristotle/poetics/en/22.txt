1458a18	The excellence of diction is to be clear and not mean. The clearest
1458a19	diction is the one made of standard words, but it
1458a20	is mean: an example is the poetry of Cleophon and that
1458a21	of Sthenelus. Dignified and free of the commonplace is the diction
1458a22	that uses unfamiliar words; by unfamiliar I mean a rare word and
1458a23	metaphor and lengthening and everything that departs from the standard.
1458a24	But if someone makes everything like this, the result will be either a riddle or
1458a25	barbarism: a riddle if it is made of metaphors, and if it is made of
1458a26	rare words, barbarism. For this is the form of a riddle:
1458a27	to say things that are real by joining impossibilities. This cannot be done
1458a28	by combining the other words, but it
1458a29	can by combining metaphors, as in “I saw a man who with fire had welded bronze
1458a30	upon a man”, and the like. And what is made of
1458a31	rare words is barbarism. So diction must be a blend of these: the one kind
1458a32	will keep it from being commonplace or mean, such as
1458a33	the rare word and metaphor and ornament and the other
1458a34	kinds we have named, and the standard word will give clarity. No small part
1458b1	in making diction clear and not commonplace is played by
1458b2	the lengthenings and curtailments and alterations of words:
1458b3	by being other than the standard form, departing from
1458b4	the usual, they will make it uncommon, and by
1458b5	sharing in the usual they will keep it clear. So those are wrong
1458b6	who find fault with this manner of speech
1458b7	and make fun of the poet, like Euclides the elder,
1458b8	who said that composing would be easy if one were allowed to lengthen syllables as far as
1458b9	one liked, and lampooned it in the very diction: “I saw Epichares
1458b10	walking to Marathon”, and “†not having mixed† his
1458b11	hellebore”. To be seen using this
1458b12	manner obtrusively is ridiculous; moderation is common to all the
1458b13	kinds: for one who used metaphors and rare words and the other
1458b14	kinds inappropriately and on purpose to raise a laugh would
1458b15	achieve the same effect. How much the fitting use makes a difference
1458b16	may be seen in epic verse, when the standard words are put into the
1458b17	meter. With the rare word, with metaphors and
1458b18	with the other forms, if one substituted the standard words
1458b19	one would see that what we say is true. For example, the same
1458b20	iambic line was composed by Aeschylus and by Euripides, but with only one word
1458b21	changed, a rare word in place of a standard, usual one, and the one line
1458b22	seems beautiful, the other trivial. Aeschylus in his
1458b23	Philoctetes wrote “the ulcer that eats the flesh of my foot”,
1458b24	and Euripides put “feasts on” in place of “eats”. Or take
1458b25	“and now one who is small and of no account
1458b26	and unseemly”, if one were to say it with the standard words
1458b27	substituted: “and now one who is little and
1458b28	weakly and ugly”; or “setting down
1458b29	an unseemly stool and a small table”,
1458b30	“setting down a wretched stool and a little table”; or
1458b31	“the shores roar” as “the shores shriek”. Again, Ariphrades
1458b32	made fun of the tragedians because they use expressions no one would use in
1458b33	conversation, such as dōmatōn apo (“halls from”) instead of
1458b34	apo dōmatōn (“from halls”), and sethen (“of thee”), and egō de nin (“and I him”), and
1459a1	Achilleōs peri (“Achilles about”) instead of peri Achilleōs, and all other such
1459a2	things. Because they are not among the standard words, all such expressions
1459a3	make the diction uncommon; but he
1459a4	did not know this. It is a great thing to use each of the kinds we have named
1459a5	fittingly, double words and rare words too, but by far
1459a6	the greatest thing is to have a gift for metaphor. This alone
1459a7	cannot be learned from another, and it is a sign of natural gifts: for
1459a8	to make good metaphors is to see likeness. Of words,
1459a9	double words suit dithyrambs best,
1459a10	rare words heroic verse, and metaphors iambics.
1459a11	In heroic verse all the kinds we have named are useful, but
1459a12	in iambics, because they imitate speech as closely as possible,
1459a13	the fitting words are those one would also use in prose:
1459a14	the standard word, metaphor and ornament.
1459a15	About tragedy, then, and imitation by
1459a16	acting, let what has been said be enough.
