1460b6	As for problems and their solutions, of how many and what
1460b7	kinds they are would become clear if we consider them as follows. Since
1460b8	the poet is an imitator, just like a painter or any
1460b9	other maker of images, he must always imitate one of three
1460b10	things: things as they were or are, or as people say and
1460b11	think they are, or as they ought to be. These are expressed in a diction in which there are
1460b12	rare words and metaphors and many modifications of diction,
1460b13	for we allow these to the poets. Besides, correctness
1460b14	is not the same in the art of politics and in the art of poetry, nor in any other
1460b15	art and in poetry. Within the art of poetry itself there are two kinds of error:
1460b16	one that belongs to the art itself, the other incidental. If
1460b17	the poet chose to imitate … through incapacity, the error belongs to the art itself; but if
1460b18	the error lies in a wrong choice, such as a horse with both right legs
1460b19	thrown forward at once, or an error in some particular art,
1460b20	such as medicine or some other art [or impossibilities have been composed]
1460b21	of any kind, it does not belong to the art itself. So the criticisms in
1460b22	the problems must be met by examining them on these grounds. First,
1460b23	those that concern the art itself. Impossibilities have been composed: that is an error;
1460b24	but it is correct if it achieves the end of the art (the
1460b25	end has been stated), that is, if in this way it makes this or another part
1460b26	more astonishing. An example is the pursuit of Hector. But if the
1460b27	end could have been achieved better, or no less well, even by keeping to
1460b28	the art concerned with these things, it is not correct; for one should,
1460b29	if possible, make no error at all. Again, of which kind is the
1460b30	error, one that concerns the art or incidental to something else?
1460b31	For it is a lesser fault not to know that a female deer has no horns than
1460b32	to paint one unrecognizably. Besides, if the criticism is that
1460b33	something is not true, perhaps it is as it ought to be: as Sophocles said
1460b34	that he made people as they ought to be, and Euripides as they are; that is how
1460b35	to answer it. If it is neither, say that this is what people say, as with stories about the gods;
1460b36	for perhaps it is neither better to tell them so nor true, but, as may well be,
1461a1	as Xenophanes thought; but at any rate people say so. Other things are perhaps not better
1461a2	but were so at the time, as with the arms: “and their spears
1461a3	upright on their butt-spikes”; for that was the custom then,
1461a4	as it is still now among the Illyrians. As to whether something has been
1461a5	said or done well or not well by someone, one must look not only at
1461a6	the deed or saying itself, to see whether it is good or
1461a7	bad, but also at the one who does or says it, to whom, or
1461a8	when, or by what means, or for what purpose: whether to bring about a greater
1461a9	good, or to avert a greater evil. Other problems must be resolved
1461a10	by looking at the diction: by a rare word, as in “the mules
1461a11	first”; for perhaps he means not the mules but the
1461a12	guards; and of Dolon, “who was ill-favored
1461a13	to look at”, meaning not that his body was misshapen but that his face was ugly,
1461a14	for the Cretans call a fair face eueides, “well-favored”; and
1461a15	“mix the wine stronger” means not undiluted, as for drunkards, but
1461a16	quicker. Other things are said by metaphor, for example “All
1461a17	the gods and men slept all night long”, while at the same time
1461a18	he says “Whenever he looked out over the Trojan plain, the din of flutes
1461a19	and pipes”; for “all” is said instead of “many” by
1461a20	metaphor, since all is a kind of many. And “she alone has
1461a21	no share” is by metaphor, for the best known is alone. By
1461a22	accent, as Hippias of Thasos solved “and we grant him
1461a23	to win glory” and “part of which rots in the rain”. Others
1461a24	by punctuation, as in Empedocles: “At once things grew mortal that before
1461a25	had learned to be immortal, and things unmixed before were mixed”. Others by ambiguity:
1461a26	“the greater part of the night has gone”, for “greater” is ambiguous.
1461a27	Others by the custom of the language. Mixed wine is still called
1461a28	wine, and so the poet composed “a greave of newly wrought tin”;
1461a29	and those who work iron are called bronze-smiths, and so it is said that
1461a30	Ganymede “pours wine for Zeus”, though the gods do not drink wine. This
1461a31	could also be by metaphor. And when a word
1461a32	seems to signify a contradiction, one must consider in how many ways it might signify
1461a33	this in the passage: as in “there the bronze
1461a34	spear was stopped”, in how many senses it is possible to be stopped there, this way or that, as
1461a35	one would best suppose; the opposite of the way
1461b1	Glaucon describes, when some people make an unreasonable assumption and,
1461b2	having passed judgment themselves, argue from it, and, as though the poet had said whatever
1461b3	they think, find fault if it contradicts their own notion.
1461b4	This has happened over Icarius. They think he was a Laconian,
1461b5	so it is absurd that Telemachus did not meet him when he went to
1461b6	Lacedaemon. But perhaps it is as the Cephallenians
1461b7	say: they say that Odysseus married among them, and that
1461b8	the man was Icadius, not Icarius; so it is through a mistake that the
1461b9	problem arises, †it is likely†. In general, the impossible must be referred to
1461b10	poetry, or to what is better, or to opinion.
1461b11	For poetry, a convincing impossibility is preferable
1461b12	to what is unconvincing though possible; … that people should be such as Zeuxis
1461b13	painted them, but it is better so: for the model should surpass the reality.
1461b14	The irrational should be referred to what people say; so too by showing that sometimes it is not irrational,
1461b15	for it is probable that things should happen even contrary to probability. Things
1461b16	said in contradiction should be examined as refutations are in
1461b17	arguments: whether it is the same thing, in relation to the same thing, and in the same way; so that
1461b18	†he himself† contradicts either what he himself says or what a sensible person would assume.
1461b19	Criticism of irrationality and of wickedness is right when, there being
1461b20	no necessity, the poet makes use of the irrational, as Euripides
1461b21	does of Aegeus, or of baseness, as in the Orestes that of Menelaus.
1461b22	Criticisms, then, are brought on five grounds: that things are
1461b23	impossible, or irrational, or harmful, or contradictory, or
1461b24	contrary to correctness in the art. The solutions must be looked for
1461b25	under the headings we have listed. They are twelve.
