1448a1	Since those who imitate imitate people in action, and these
1448a2	must be either serious or inferior (for characters almost always
1448a3	follow these alone, since it is by badness and virtue that all people
1448a4	differ in character), they imitate people either better than we are, or worse,
1448a5	or like us, as the painters do: Polygnotus
1448a6	portrayed people better, Pauson worse, and Dionysius like us.
1448a7	It is clear that each of the imitations we have mentioned will also have
1448a8	these differences, and will be different by imitating different objects
1448a9	in this way. For in dancing too, and in playing the pipe and
1448a10	the lyre, these dissimilarities can arise, and
1448a11	in prose and in verse without music: Homer, for instance,
1448a12	imitates better people, Cleophon people like us, and Hegemon of Thasos, who
1448a13	first composed parodies, and Nicochares, the author of the Deiliad,
1448a14	worse people. It is the same with dithyrambs and
1448a15	nomes: one might portray †…† Cyclopes, as Timotheus and Philoxenus
1448a16	did. And this is the very difference that sets
1448a17	tragedy apart from comedy: for comedy
1448a18	aims to imitate people worse, and tragedy better, than those of today.
