1452b28	What poets must aim at and what they must avoid in constructing
1452b29	their plots, and how the effect of tragedy will be produced,
1452b30	should be discussed next, after what has now been said. Since, then,
1452b31	the structure of the finest tragedy must be not simple
1452b32	but complex, and it must imitate fearful and pitiable
1452b33	things (for this is the distinctive feature of such imitation),
1452b34	first, it is clear that decent men must not be shown
1452b35	passing from good fortune to misfortune, for this is
1452b36	neither fearful nor pitiable but repugnant; nor
1452b37	wicked men passing from misfortune to good fortune, for this is the most untragic
1452b38	thing of all: it has nothing it should have, being neither satisfying to human feeling
1453a1	nor pitiable nor fearful; nor again an utterly villainous man
1453a2	falling from good fortune into misfortune: such a structure
1453a3	might satisfy human feeling, but would bring neither pity nor
1453a4	fear, for the one is felt for a man who suffers undeservedly, the other
1453a5	for one like ourselves, pity for the undeserving, fear
1453a6	for one like us; so neither pitiable nor fearful will be
1453a7	what happens. There remains, then, the man between these. He is one
1453a8	who is neither outstanding in virtue and justice, nor falls
1453a9	into misfortune through badness and wickedness, but through
1453a10	some error, one of those who stand in great repute and good fortune,
1453a11	such as Oedipus and Thyestes and the men of such families
1453a12	who are famous. A plot that is well made, then, must
1453a13	be single rather than double, as some say it should be, and
1453a14	change not to good fortune from misfortune but the opposite,
1453a15	from good fortune to misfortune, not through wickedness but through
1453a16	a great error, of a man such as we have described, or better rather than
1453a17	worse. What actually happens is evidence of this: at first the
1453a18	poets ran through any stories that came to hand, but now
1453a19	the finest tragedies are composed about a few houses, as
1453a20	about Alcmaeon and Oedipus and Orestes and Meleager
1453a21	and Thyestes and Telephus and all the others who happened either
1453a22	to suffer or to do terrible things. The finest tragedy according to the art,
1453a23	then, comes from this structure. That is why
1453a24	those who blame Euripides make the same mistake, for doing this
1453a25	in his tragedies, and because many of his plays end
1453a26	in misfortune. For this, as has been said, is right.
1453a27	The greatest evidence: on the stage and in the competitions
1453a28	such plays appear the most tragic, if they are well done,
1453a29	and Euripides, even if he does not manage other things well,
1453a30	at least appears the most tragic of the poets. Second comes the structure
1453a31	that some call first, the one that has a double
1453a32	structure, like the Odyssey, and ends in opposite ways
1453a33	for the better and the worse people. It seems to be first because of the
1453a34	weakness of the audiences; for the poets follow along, composing
1453a35	to suit the wishes of the spectators. But this is not the pleasure
1453a36	that comes from tragedy; it belongs rather to comedy:
1453a37	there, those who are the bitterest enemies in the story, such as
1453a38	Orestes and Aegisthus, go off at the end as friends,
1453a39	and nobody is killed by anybody.
