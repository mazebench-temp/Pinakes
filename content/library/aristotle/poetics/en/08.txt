1451a16	A plot is not one, as some think, if it is about one
1451a17	man; for many things, indeed countless things, happen to one man, some of which
1451a18	make no unity at all; so too one man performs many actions,
1451a19	from which no single action results. So all those poets seem
1451a20	to have gone wrong who have composed a Heracleid, a Theseid and
1451a21	such poems; for they think that since
1451a22	Heracles was one, the plot too must be one. But
1451a23	Homer, as he excels in everything else, seems to have seen this too
1451a24	clearly, whether by art or by nature: in composing the Odyssey
1451a25	he did not put in everything that happened to him, for example
1451a26	his being wounded on Parnassus and his feigning madness at
1451a27	the mustering, of which neither, when the one had happened, made it necessary or
1451a28	probable that the other should happen; but around one action of the kind we mean
1451a29	he built the Odyssey, and the Iliad likewise.
1451a30	So, just as in the other imitative arts a single imitation
1451a31	is of one thing, so the plot too, since it is an imitation of action,
1451a32	must be of one action, and that a whole, and the parts
1451a33	of the incidents must be so constructed that if any part is moved or
1451a34	removed, the whole is disjointed and disturbed. For a thing whose presence
1451a35	or absence makes no visible difference is no part of the whole.
