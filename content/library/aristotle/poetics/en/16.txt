1454b19	What recognition is has been said before. As for the kinds
1454b20	of recognition, first is the least artistic, and the one most
1454b21	used for want of resource: recognition through signs. Of these, some
1454b22	are congenital, such as “the spearhead which the Earth-born bear”, or stars
1454b23	such as Carcinus uses in his Thyestes; others are acquired, and of these
1454b24	some are on the body, such as scars, and others external, such as
1454b25	necklaces, and as in the Tyro, through the little boat. These
1454b26	too can be used better or worse: Odysseus, for example,
1454b27	was recognized through his scar in one way by the nurse and in another
1454b28	by the swineherds. Recognitions made for the sake of proof are less artistic,
1454b29	and so are all such, while those that come out of a reversal, like
1454b30	the one in the Bath, are better. Second come those
1454b31	contrived by the poet, and therefore inartistic. For example,
1454b32	Orestes in the Iphigenia makes it known that he is
1454b33	Orestes: she is recognized through the letter, but he
1454b34	himself says what the poet wants, and
1454b35	not the plot; so it comes close to the fault just mentioned,
1454b36	for he might as well have brought some tokens too. So too in Sophocles' Tereus
1454b37	the voice of the shuttle. The third kind is through memory, when someone feels something
1455a1	on seeing something, as in the Cyprians of Dicaeogenes, where he saw
1455a2	the painting and wept, and the one in the tale told to Alcinous, where, hearing
1455a3	the lyre player, he remembered and shed tears, and so they were recognized.
1455a4	The fourth comes from reasoning, as in the Libation Bearers: that
1455a5	someone like her has come; no one is like her but Orestes;
1455a6	so he has come. And that of Polyidus the sophist about
1455a7	Iphigenia: for it was probable, he said, that Orestes would reason
1455a8	that his sister had been sacrificed and that it was his lot to be sacrificed too. And in
1455a9	the Tydeus of Theodectes, that having come to find his son he himself
1455a10	is to die. And the one in the Phinidae: on seeing the place
1455a11	the women inferred their fate, that it was destined they would die
1455a12	there, for they had also been exposed there. There is also a
1455a13	composite kind, made from a false inference by the audience, as in Odysseus
1455a14	the False Messenger: that he strings the bow and no one else does is contrived by the poet, a premise, and so is his saying he would recognize the bow, which he had not
1455a15	seen; but to make it seem that he would be recognized by that means, through this,
1455a16	is a false inference. Best of all recognitions is the one
1455a17	that arises from the incidents themselves, when the astonishment comes about through
1455a18	probable events, as in Sophocles' Oedipus and in the Iphigenia,
1455a19	for it is probable that she should wish to send a letter. Only recognitions of this kind
1455a20	do without contrived signs and
1455a21	necklaces. Second are those from reasoning.
