1459a17	As for the art of imitation in narrative and in meter, the
1459a18	plots should be constructed, as in tragedies,
1459a19	dramatically, about one action that is whole and complete,
1459a20	with a beginning, middle parts and an end, so that, like one whole living creature,
1459a21	it may produce its proper pleasure; this is clear. Nor should the compositions be like histories,
1459a22	in which one must set out not one action
1459a23	but one period of time, all that happened in it to one man
1459a24	or more, each of these events related to the others as chance would have it. For just as
1459a25	the sea battle at Salamis was fought at the same time
1459a26	as the battle with the Carthaginians in Sicily, though they in no way
1459a27	tended to the same end, so too in successive
1459a28	times one thing sometimes happens after another, without any single
1459a29	end coming from them. Yet most poets, one might say, do
1459a30	this. That is why, as we have already said, in this respect too
1459a31	Homer would seem divine beside the others, in not trying to make even the war, though
1459a32	it had a beginning and an end, into a whole poem; for the plot
1459a33	would have been too large and not easily taken in at a glance,
1459a34	or, if moderate in magnitude, entangled by its variety.
1459a35	As it is, he has taken one part and used many of the others
1459a36	as episodes, such as the catalogue of ships and other episodes,
1459a37	with which he divides up his poem. The others compose about one man and
1459b1	one period and one action of many parts, like the poet of the
1459b2	Cypria and of the Little Iliad. So from
1459b3	the Iliad and the Odyssey one tragedy is made from each,
1459b4	or two at most, but many from the Cypria, and from the Little
1459b5	Iliad [[more than] eight, such as the Judgment of Arms, the Philoctetes,
1459b6	the Neoptolemus, the Eurypylus, the Beggary, the Laconian Women,
1459b7	the Sack of Troy and the Sailing Away [and the Sinon and the Trojan Women]].
