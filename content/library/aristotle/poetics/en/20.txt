1456b20	Diction as a whole has these parts: letter, syllable,
1456b21	connective, noun, verb, joint, inflection, statement. A letter
1456b22	is an indivisible sound; not every such sound, but one from which
1456b23	a composite sound can naturally arise, for the sounds of animals too are
1456b24	indivisible, and I call none of them a letter. Its
1456b25	parts are the vowel, the semivowel and the mute.
1456b26	A vowel is what has a sound that can be heard without
1456b27	contact; a semivowel, what has a sound that can be heard with contact,
1456b28	such as S and R; a mute, what with
1456b29	contact has no sound of its own, but becomes audible
1456b30	together with letters that have some sound, such as G
1456b31	and D. These differ by the shapes of the mouth and
1456b32	the places of articulation, by aspiration and its absence, by length and shortness,
1456b33	and also by high, low and middle pitch; each of these
1456b34	should be studied in works on meter. A syllable
1456b35	is a sound without meaning, composed of a mute and a letter that has sound;
1456b36	for GR without A is †a syllable, and† so it is with A,
1456b37	as GRA. But to study the differences between these too belongs
1456b38	to the art of meter. A connective is a sound without meaning that neither
1457a1	prevents nor produces a single significant sound out of several
1457a2	sounds, that is naturally placed both at the ends and in the
1457a3	middle, and that it is not fitting to put by itself at the beginning of a statement,
1457a4	such as men, ētoi, de; or a sound without meaning that, out of several
1457a5	sounds that are each significant, naturally produces one significant
1457a6	sound. A joint is a sound without meaning that shows the beginning or
1457a7	end or division of a statement, such as amphi and peri and
1457a8	the rest; or a sound without meaning that neither prevents nor produces
1457a9	a single significant sound out of several sounds, and is naturally placed
1457a10	both at the ends and in the middle. A noun is a composite,
1457a11	significant sound, without time, no part of which is
1457a12	significant by itself; for in compounds we do not use a part as
1457a13	having a meaning of its own: in Theodorus, for example,
1457a14	the -dōros (“gift”) means nothing. A verb is a composite significant sound with
1457a15	time, no part of which has meaning by itself, as with
1457a16	nouns: “man” or “white” does not signify
1457a17	when, but “walks” or “has walked” signifies in addition, the one
1457a18	the present time, the other the past. An inflection
1457a19	of a noun or a verb is either one that signifies “of this” or “to this”
1457a20	and the like, or one that signifies one or many, such as “men”
1457a21	or “man”, or one that concerns delivery, such as question or command:
1457a22	“Did he walk?” or “Walk!” is an inflection of a verb of these kinds.
1457a23	A statement is a composite significant sound some of whose parts signify something
1457a24	by themselves (not every statement is composed of verbs and nouns, the
1457a25	definition of man, for example; a statement can be without
1457a26	verbs, but it will always have some part
1457a27	that signifies something), such as “Cleon” in “Cleon walks”.
1457a28	A statement is one in two ways: either by signifying one thing,
1457a29	or by being bound together out of several: the Iliad
1457a30	is one by being bound together, the definition of man by signifying one thing.
