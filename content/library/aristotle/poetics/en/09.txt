1451a36	It is clear from what has been said, too, that to tell what has happened
1451a37	is not the task of a poet, but what could happen,
1451a38	things possible by probability or necessity. For the
1451b1	historian and the poet do not differ by speaking in verse or in prose
1451b2	(the works of Herodotus could be put into verse,
1451b3	and they would be history no less with meter than without
1451b4	it); the difference is this, that the one tells what has happened,
1451b5	the other what could happen. That is why poetry is more philosophical
1451b6	and more serious than history: for
1451b7	poetry speaks rather of universals, history of particulars.
1451b8	A universal is the sort of thing that a person of a certain sort will happen
1451b9	to say or do by probability or necessity, which is what
1451b10	poetry aims at, though it attaches names; a particular
1451b11	is what Alcibiades did or what he suffered. In
1451b12	comedy this has already become clear: the poets construct the
1451b13	plot through probable events and only then supply whatever names they like,
1451b14	and do not, like the iambic poets, write about the particular
1451b15	individual. In tragedy they keep to the names of people
1451b16	who existed. The reason is that what is possible is convincing:
1451b17	what has not happened we do not yet believe to be possible, but
1451b18	what has happened is evidently possible, for it would not have happened if it were impossible.
1451b19	Yet even in tragedies, in some there are one
1451b20	or two of the familiar names, and the rest are invented,
1451b21	and in some none at all, as in Agathon's Antheus; for in this play
1451b22	the incidents and the names alike are invented,
1451b23	and it gives no less pleasure. So one should not seek at all costs
1451b24	to keep to the traditional stories around which tragedies are made;
1451b25	indeed it would be ridiculous to seek this, since even the
1451b26	familiar stories are familiar to few, and yet they give pleasure to all.
1451b27	It is clear from this that the poet must be a maker of plots
1451b28	rather than of verses, inasmuch as he is a poet by virtue of
1451b29	imitation, and what he imitates is actions. And even if he happens
1451b30	to make a poem of things that happened, he is no less a poet; for nothing prevents
1451b31	some things that have happened from being such as would probably
1451b32	happen [and could possibly happen], and it is in this respect that he is their maker.
1451b33	Of simple plots and actions, the episodic
1451b34	are the worst. By an episodic plot I mean one in which the
1451b35	episodes follow one another with neither probability nor necessity.
1451b36	Such plots are made by bad poets because of themselves,
1451b37	and by good poets because of the actors: for in composing
1451b38	show pieces for competition, and stretching the plot beyond its capacity,
1452a1	they are often forced to distort the sequence. But since
1452a2	the imitation is not only of a complete action but also of fearful
1452a3	and pitiable things, and these arise most of all [and
1452a4	more] when things happen contrary to expectation but because of one another (for
1452a5	so they will have more of the marvelous than if they happened spontaneously and
1452a6	by chance, since even among chance events the most marvelous
1452a7	seem to be those that look as if they had happened on purpose, as when the
1452a8	statue of Mitys at Argos killed the man responsible for
1452a9	the death of Mitys by falling on him as he looked at it; for such things seem
1452a10	not to happen at random), plots of this kind
1452a11	must necessarily be the finer.
