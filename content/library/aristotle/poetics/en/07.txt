1450b21	These things defined, let us next say what kind of
1450b22	structure the incidents must have, since this
1450b23	is the first and most important thing in tragedy. We have laid it down
1450b24	that tragedy is an imitation of an action that is complete and whole
1450b25	and has some magnitude; for a thing can be whole and have no
1450b26	magnitude. A whole is what has a beginning and a middle and
1450b27	an end. A beginning is what does not itself necessarily follow something else,
1450b28	but after which something else naturally is or comes to be;
1450b29	an end, on the contrary, is what itself naturally follows something else, either
1450b30	by necessity or as a rule, but has nothing else after it;
1450b31	and a middle is what itself follows something else and has something else after it.
1450b32	Well-constructed plots, then, must neither begin wherever it happens
1450b33	nor end wherever it happens, but must follow the
1450b34	patterns we have described. Further, since a beautiful thing, whether a living creature or anything
1450b35	that is composed of parts, must not only have these parts
1450b36	in order but also have a magnitude that is not arbitrary (for
1450b37	beauty lies in magnitude and order; so neither could a very small
1450b38	creature be beautiful, for our view of it is blurred as it comes close to
1450b39	an imperceptible moment of time, nor a very large one, for
1451a1	the view does not come all at once, and its unity
1451a2	and wholeness are lost to the viewers, as if there were a creature ten thousand stades long),
1451a3	so, just as with bodies and with
1451a4	creatures there must be magnitude, but one that can be taken in at a glance, so
1451a5	with plots there must be length, but one that can easily
1451a6	be remembered. The limit of length set by the competitions
1451a7	and the attention of the audience is no concern of the art: if a hundred
1451a8	tragedies had to compete, they would compete by the water clock,
1451a9	†as they say happened once before†. The limit set by the very nature
1451a10	of the thing is this: the greater, so long as it remains clear as a whole,
1451a11	is always the more beautiful in its magnitude; and to give a simple
1451a12	definition, the magnitude in which, as events follow one after another by probability or
1451a13	necessity, it comes about that there is a change to good fortune from
1451a14	misfortune, or from good fortune to misfortune, is an adequate
1451a15	limit of magnitude.
