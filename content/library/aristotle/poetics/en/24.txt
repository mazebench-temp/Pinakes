1459b8	Further, epic must have the same kinds as
1459b9	tragedy: it must be simple or complex, of character or
1459b10	of suffering; and it has the same parts, apart from song and spectacle,
1459b11	for it needs reversals and recognitions and sufferings;
1459b12	and its thoughts and diction must also be good. All these
1459b13	Homer used, both first and sufficiently. Indeed
1459b14	each of his poems is constructed so: the Iliad simple
1459b15	and full of suffering, the Odyssey complex (there is recognition
1459b16	throughout) and full of character; and besides, in diction and thought
1459b17	he has surpassed everyone. Epic differs in the length of its
1459b18	structure and in its meter. For length, the limit
1459b19	we have stated is adequate: one must be able to take in the beginning
1459b20	and the end together. This would be so if the structures were shorter than those of the
1459b21	old epics, and reached the length of the number of tragedies
1459b22	presented at a single hearing. Epic has a great
1459b23	and special capacity for extending its magnitude,
1459b24	because in tragedy it is not possible to imitate many parts
1459b25	being done at the same time, but only the part on the stage and
1459b26	among the actors; but in epic, because it is narrative,
1459b27	one can present many parts being completed at once, and by these,
1459b28	if they belong to the story, the bulk of the poem is increased. So epic has
1459b29	this advantage for grandeur, and for giving variety to the
1459b30	hearer and diversifying with episodes unlike each other; for
1459b31	sameness quickly sates and makes tragedies fail. The
1459b32	heroic meter has proved fitting by experience. For if someone were to compose
1459b33	a narrative imitation in some other meter, or in many,
1459b34	it would seem unfitting: for the heroic is the most stately and
1459b35	weighty of the meters (which is why it most readily admits rare words and
1459b36	metaphors; for narrative imitation too is extraordinary
1459b37	beyond the others), while the iambic and the tetrameter
1460a1	are lively, the one suited to dancing, the other to action. Still more
1460a2	absurd would it be to mix them, as Chaeremon did. So
1460a3	no one has made a long structure in any meter but the heroic; rather,
1460a4	as we said, nature itself teaches us to choose what
1460a5	fits it. Homer deserves praise for many other things, and
1460a6	especially because he alone of the poets is not unaware of what he himself should do.
1460a7	The poet himself should say very little, for it is not
1460a8	in this capacity that he is an imitator. The other poets appear in their own person throughout
1460a9	and imitate little and seldom; but he, after a brief
1460a10	prelude, at once brings on a man or a woman or some other
1460a11	character, and none without character, but each with a character. In tragedies, then,
1460a12	one should produce the marvelous, but more admissible in
1460a13	epic is the irrational, through which the marvelous most of all
1460a14	comes about, because we do not see the person acting. The scenes of
1460a15	Hector's pursuit would seem ridiculous on the stage, with
1460a16	the men standing still and not pursuing, and Achilles waving them back; but in
1460a17	epic it goes unnoticed. The marvelous is pleasant: evidence of this is that everyone
1460a18	adds something when telling a story, thinking to please. It is
1460a19	Homer above all who has taught the others how to tell lies as they should be told.
1460a20	This is the false inference. People think that when
1460a21	this thing is, that one is, or when this happens, that happens, then if the latter is,
1460a22	the former too is or happens; but this is false. So,
1460a23	if the first thing is false, but something else would necessarily be or
1460a24	happen if it were so, the poet must add that; for knowing this to be true,
1460a25	our mind falsely infers that the first is true as well. An example
1460a26	of this is the passage from the Bath. One should prefer
1460a27	probable impossibilities to unconvincing possibilities; and stories
1460a28	should not be made up of irrational parts, but above all
1460a29	should have nothing irrational, or failing that, it should be outside the plot, as
1460a30	with Oedipus' not knowing how Laius died, and not in
1460a31	the drama, like those who report the Pythian games in the Electra,
1460a32	or the man in the Mysians who comes without a word from Tegea to Mysia. So
1460a33	to say that the plot would have been ruined otherwise is ridiculous: such plots should not
1460a34	be constructed in the first place. †If he does put it in, and it seems
1460a35	more reasonable, he may admit even the absurd†. Even the irrational things in the Odyssey
1460a36	about Odysseus' being put ashore would clearly be intolerable,
1460b1	if a bad poet had made them; as it is,
1460b2	the poet conceals the absurdity, sweetening it with his other virtues. The
1460b3	diction should be elaborated in the slack parts, where there is neither character
1460b4	nor thought; for diction that is too brilliant
1460b5	obscures, in turn, both characters and thoughts.
