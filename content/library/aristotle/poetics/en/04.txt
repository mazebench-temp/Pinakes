1448b4	Two causes, it seems, gave birth to the art of poetry as a whole,
1448b5	and both of them natural. For imitating
1448b6	is innate in human beings from childhood, and in this they differ
1448b7	from the other animals, that man is the most imitative of them and learns
1448b8	his first lessons through imitation; and so is the pleasure
1448b9	that everyone takes in imitations. Evidence of this is what happens
1448b10	in practice: things that are painful for us to see in themselves, of these
1448b11	we enjoy looking at the most exact images,
1448b12	such as the shapes of the lowliest animals and of corpses. The reason
1448b13	for this too is that learning is most pleasant not only for philosophers
1448b14	but for everyone else alike, though they
1448b15	have only a brief share in it. That is why people enjoy images
1448b16	when they see them: because in looking they come to learn and to infer
1448b17	what each thing is, for example that this man is that one. For if one happens not
1448b18	to have seen the subject before, the pleasure will come not from the imitation as such but from
1448b19	the workmanship or the color or some other such cause.
1448b20	Since imitation is natural to us, and so are melody
1448b21	and rhythm (for that meters are sections of rhythms
1448b22	is evident), those who from the beginning had the greatest gift for these things,
1448b23	advancing little by little, gave birth to poetry out of their
1448b24	improvisations. And poetry split apart according to the poets' own characters:
1448b25	the more dignified imitated noble actions and
1448b26	the actions of noble people, the more trivial those of inferior people,
1448b27	composing invectives at first, as the others composed hymns and encomia.
1448b28	We cannot name a poem of this kind by anyone before Homer,
1448b29	though there were probably many such poets; but beginning from Homer
1448b30	we can, for example his Margites and the like. In these
1448b31	the iambic meter too came in, as was fitting —which is why it is still called
1448b32	iambic today, because it was in this meter that they lampooned (iambizon) one another.
1448b33	And so of the old poets some became poets of heroic verse, others
1448b34	of lampoons. And just as in serious things Homer was the poet above all
1448b35	(for he alone composed not only well but also made his imitations
1448b36	dramatic), so too the form of comedy
1448b37	he was the first to point out, dramatizing not invective but the ridiculous;
1448b38	for the Margites is analogous: as the Iliad
1449a1	and the Odyssey are to tragedies, so is this poem to
1449a2	comedies. When tragedy and comedy had appeared,
1449a3	those drawn to each kind of poetry by their own
1449a4	nature became, some, makers of comedies instead of lampoons,
1449a5	and others producers of tragedies instead of epics, because
1449a6	these forms were greater and more esteemed than those.
1449a7	Whether tragedy is by now fully developed in its
1449a8	kinds or not, judged both in itself and in relation to
1449a9	the theaters, is another question. At any rate, having arisen from an improvised
1449a10	beginning (both tragedy itself and comedy, the one
1449a11	from those who led off the dithyramb, the other from those who led the
1449a12	phallic songs that even now remain in many of the cities
1449a13	as a custom), it grew little by little, as poets developed whatever
1449a14	of it came to light; and after passing through many changes
1449a15	tragedy stopped, once it had attained its own nature. The
1449a16	number of actors was first raised from one to two
1449a17	by Aeschylus, who also reduced the part of the chorus and made speech
1449a18	take the leading role; three actors and scene painting
1449a19	came with Sophocles. Then there was magnitude: from slight plots and
1449a20	comic diction, since it had changed from the satyric, tragedy
1449a21	was late to acquire its dignity, and its meter changed from tetrameter to iambic.
1449a22	At first they used the tetrameter because the poetry was satyric
1449a23	and more suited to dancing; but when speech came in,
1449a24	nature itself found the meter that belonged to it: for of all meters
1449a25	the iambic is the most like speech. The evidence for
1449a26	this is that we speak iambics most often in conversation
1449a27	with one another, but hexameters seldom, and only
1449a28	when we depart from the tone of speech. Then there was the number
1449a29	of episodes. As for the other things, and how each is said to have been embellished,
1449a30	let them be taken as said; for it would perhaps be a long
1449a31	task to go through them one by one.
