1454a16	In characters there are four things to aim at.
1454a17	One, and the first, is that they be good. The play will have character
1454a18	if, as was said, the speech or the action makes evident
1454a19	a choice, of whatever kind it is, and good character if
1454a20	the choice is good. This is possible in every class: a woman can be good,
1454a21	and so can a slave, though perhaps of these the one is inferior, and the other
1454a22	wholly worthless. Second, that they be appropriate: a character may be
1454a23	manly, but it is not appropriate for a woman to be so manly
1454a24	or so clever. Third, that they be like. This is different
1454a25	from making the character good and appropriate, as described above.
1454a26	Fourth, that they be consistent. For even if the person
1454a27	being imitated is inconsistent, and such a character is the premise, still he must be consistently
1454a28	inconsistent. An example of baseness of
1454a29	character that is not necessary is Menelaus in the Orestes; of
1454a30	the unseemly and inappropriate, the lament of Odysseus in the
1454a31	Scylla and the speech of Melanippe; of the inconsistent,
1454a32	Iphigenia at Aulis, for the girl who pleads for her life is nothing like the later one.
1454a33	In characters, just as in the
1454a34	structure of the incidents, one must always seek the necessary or the probable,
1454a35	so that it is necessary or probable for such a person to say or do such things,
1454a36	and necessary or probable for this to happen after that.
1454a37	It is evident, then, that the resolutions of plots too must come about from the
1454b1	plot itself, and not, as in the Medea, from the machine,
1454b2	or as with the sailing away in the Iliad. The machine
1454b3	should be used for what lies outside the drama, either what happened before,
1454b4	which no human being can know, or what happens later, which
1454b5	needs to be foretold and announced; for we grant
1454b6	the gods the power to see everything. There should be nothing irrational in the
1454b7	incidents, or if there is, it should lie outside the tragedy, as in
1454b8	Sophocles' Oedipus. Since tragedy is an imitation
1454b9	of people better than we are, poets must imitate good
1454b10	portrait painters: they too render the particular shape,
1454b11	making their subjects like, yet paint them more beautiful. So too the poet,
1454b12	when he imitates people who are irascible or easygoing or have other such
1454b13	traits of character, must make them, being such, decent,
1454b14	†as Homer made Achilles good, though an example of harshness†.
1454b15	These things must be watched, and besides them, what concerns the
1454b16	perceptions that necessarily attend the art of poetry; for
1454b17	there too one can often go wrong. But enough has been said
1454b18	about them in my published works.
