1449b21	Of the art of imitation in hexameters, and of
1449b22	comedy, we shall speak later. Let us speak of tragedy,
1449b23	gathering from what has been said the definition
1449b24	of its essence that emerges. Tragedy, then, is an imitation of an action that is serious
1449b25	and complete and has magnitude, in embellished language, with each
1449b26	of its kinds used separately in the parts; by people acting and not through narration;
1449b27	accomplishing through pity and fear the
1449b28	catharsis of such feelings. By “embellished language” I mean language
1449b29	that has rhythm and melody [and song]; and by “with its kinds
1449b30	separately”, that some parts are accomplished through meters alone, and others
1449b31	again through song. Since it is people acting who produce the imitation,
1449b32	first, of necessity, one part of tragedy would be the adornment
1449b33	of spectacle; then song and diction, for in these
1449b34	they produce the imitation. By diction I mean the actual
1449b35	composition of the verses; by song, something whose power
1449b36	is entirely evident. Since it is an imitation of action, and action is done
1449b37	by certain people acting, who must be of a certain kind in
1449b38	both their character and their thought (for it is through these that we say
1450a1	actions too are of a certain kind [the causes of actions are by nature two,
1450a2	thought and character], and it is by their actions that
1450a3	all people succeed or fail), the imitation of the
1450a4	action is the plot; for by plot I mean here the
1450a5	arrangement of the incidents; by characters, that in virtue of which
1450a6	we say the people acting are of a certain kind; and by thought, all the places where in speaking
1450a7	they demonstrate something or declare an opinion. So
1450a8	every tragedy must have six parts, by which it is a tragedy of a certain kind:
1450a9	these are plot, characters, diction,
1450a10	thought, spectacle and song. The media in which they imitate are two
1450a11	parts, the manner is one, the objects are three, and
1450a12	there is nothing besides these. These kinds, then, †not a few of them†,
1450a13	so to speak, have used; for †spectacle has everything†, and
1450a14	character and plot and diction and song and thought alike.
1450a15	The most important of these is the structure of the incidents.
1450a16	For tragedy is an imitation not of people but of actions
1450a17	and of life [and happiness and unhappiness lie in action,
1450a18	and the end is a kind of action, not a quality;
1450a19	people are of a certain kind according to their characters, but according to their
1450a20	actions they are happy or the opposite]. So it is not in order to imitate characters
1450a21	that they act; rather they take in the characters
1450a22	for the sake of the actions. So the incidents and the plot are the end
1450a23	of tragedy, and the end is the most important thing of all. Again,
1450a24	without action there could be no tragedy, but without characters
1450a25	there could; for the tragedies of most of the moderns are without character,
1450a26	and in general many poets are like this, just as among
1450a27	painters Zeuxis stands to Polygnotus: for
1450a28	Polygnotus is a good painter of character, while the painting of Zeuxis has
1450a29	no character at all. Again, if someone sets in a row speeches full of character, well made
1450a30	in diction and in thought, he will not achieve what was
1450a31	the work of tragedy; far better will be the tragedy that uses these
1450a32	less well but has a plot and a structure
1450a33	of incidents. Besides this, the most powerful means by which tragedy sways the soul
1450a34	are parts of the plot, reversals and
1450a35	recognitions. Another sign: those who attempt to compose
1450a36	can achieve precision in diction and in characters before
1450a37	they can construct the incidents, as was true of the first poets
1450a38	almost without exception. The plot, then, is the first principle and, as it were, the soul of
1450a39	tragedy, and characters come second (it is much the same
1450b1	in painting: if someone smeared on the most beautiful
1450b2	colors at random, he would not give as much pleasure as one who outlined
1450b3	an image in white). It is an imitation of action, and it is because of the action
1450b4	above all that it imitates the people acting. Third is thought: this
1450b5	is being able to say what the matter admits and what fits it,
1450b6	which in speeches is the task of the art of politics and of rhetoric:
1450b7	the old poets made their characters speak like statesmen, the poets
1450b8	of today like rhetoricians. Character is what
1450b9	reveals choice, of what kind it is [in cases where it is not clear whether
1450b10	someone chooses or avoids]; so speeches have no character in which there is nothing at all that the speaker chooses or avoids;
1450b11	and thought is found where people demonstrate that something is or is not,
1450b12	or declare something universal. Fourth †of the verbal parts†
1450b13	is diction. By diction I mean, as was said before,
1450b14	expression through words, which has the same power in verse and
1450b15	in prose. Of the remaining parts,
1450b16	song is the greatest of the embellishments; spectacle sways the soul,
1450b17	but it is the least artistic and the least proper to the art of poetry:
1450b18	for the power of tragedy exists even without a performance and
1450b19	actors, and besides, for the production
1450b20	of spectacle the art of the costume-maker is more decisive than that of the poets.
