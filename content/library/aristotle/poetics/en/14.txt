# Aristotle, Poetics, Chapter 14 — English translation by Claude (Anthropic), 2026.

1453b1	The fearful and the pitiable can arise from the spectacle,
1453b2	but they can also arise from the very structure of the incidents,
1453b3	which comes first and is the mark of a better poet. For
1453b4	even without seeing it, the plot should be so constructed that the one
1453b5	who hears the incidents as they happen both shudders and feels pity
1453b6	at what comes about, which is what one would feel on hearing the
1453b7	story of Oedipus. To produce this effect through spectacle
1453b8	is less a matter of art and needs a sponsor's money. Those who produce through spectacle
1453b9	not the fearful but only the monstrous
1453b10	have nothing in common with tragedy: for one should not seek every
1453b11	pleasure from tragedy, but the one that belongs to it. And since
1453b12	the pleasure that comes from pity and fear through imitation
1453b13	must be produced by the poet, it is evident that he must build this into the incidents
1453b14	themselves. Let us consider, then, which happenings seem terrible or which
1453b15	pitiable. Such actions must be done either by those who are dear
1453b16	to each other, or by enemies, or by those who are neither.
1453b17	If an enemy acts against an enemy, there is nothing pitiable, neither in the deed
1453b18	nor in the intention, except in the suffering itself; nor if
1453b19	they are neither. But when it is within close relationships that
1453b20	sufferings arise, as when brother kills brother, or son father, or mother son,
1453b21	or son mother, or is about to, or does something else of the kind,
1453b22	these are what one must look for. The stories that have been handed down
1453b23	cannot be undone, I mean such as the death of Clytemnestra
1453b24	at the hands of Orestes, and of Eriphyle at the hands of Alcmaeon;
1453b25	but the poet must find out for himself how to use the traditional material
1453b26	well. What we mean by “well”, let us say more clearly. The
1453b27	action may happen as the old poets
1453b28	made it, with people acting knowingly and in full awareness, as Euripides too
1453b29	made Medea kill her children. Or
1453b30	people may do the terrible deed, but do it in ignorance, and then
1453b31	recognize the relationship later, as Sophocles' Oedipus does;
1453b32	here, it is true, the deed is outside the drama, but within the
1453b33	tragedy itself there is Astydamas' Alcmaeon, or Telegonus
1453b34	in Odysseus Wounded. There is a third way besides these: when one
1453b35	is about to do something irreparable through ignorance, to recognize the truth
1453b36	before doing it. Besides these there is no other way: for people must either act
1453b37	or not, and knowingly or unknowingly. Of these, to be
1453b38	about to act knowingly and not to do it is the worst: for it is
1453b39	repugnant, and not tragic, for there is no suffering. So no one
1454a1	composes in this way, except rarely, as in the Antigone, where Creon is threatened by
1454a2	Haemon. To do the deed is second. Better is to do it in ignorance
1454a3	and to recognize after doing it: for the repugnant
1454a4	is absent, and the recognition is astonishing. Best of all
1454a5	is the last, as in the Cresphontes, where Merope
1454a6	is about to kill her son, and does not kill him, but
1454a7	recognizes him; and in the Iphigenia, the sister her brother; and
1454a8	in the Helle, the son recognizes his mother when he is about to give her up.
1454a9	This is the reason, as was said long ago, why tragedies are not about many
1454a10	families. For in their search it was not by art
1454a11	but by chance that the poets discovered how to produce this in their
1454a12	plots; so they are forced to resort to those houses
1454a13	in which such sufferings have happened. About the
1454a14	structure of the incidents, then, and what kind of plots there should be,
1454a15	enough has been said.
