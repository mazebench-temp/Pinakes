1449a32	Comedy, as we said, is an imitation of people who are worse,
1449a33	though not in respect of every kind of badness: rather, the ridiculous is a part
1449a34	of the ugly. For the ridiculous is
1449a35	an error or ugliness that causes no pain and does no harm; to take
1449a36	the obvious example, the comic mask is something ugly and distorted
1449a37	without pain. Now the changes that tragedy went through, and
1449a38	those who brought them about, are not unknown; but comedy, because it was not
1449b1	taken seriously at first, escaped notice. Indeed, a chorus of comedians
1449b2	was granted by the archon only late; before that they were volunteers. Comedy
1449b3	already had some of its forms when those called its poets
1449b4	are first remembered. Who introduced masks or prologues or
1449b5	the number of actors and all such things is not known. The making of plots
1449b6	[Epicharmus and Phormis] came originally from
1449b7	Sicily; of the poets at Athens, Crates was the first to give up
1449b8	the iambic form and compose stories and plots of a general kind.
1449b9	Epic agrees with tragedy so far as being an imitation
1449b10	of serious people in speech with meter; but in having
1449b11	a single meter and being narrative, in these respects
1449b12	they differ; and in length too: tragedy tries as far as possible
1449b13	to keep within a single circuit of the sun, or to exceed it a little, while
1449b14	epic is unlimited in time, and differs in this; and yet
1449b15	at first they did the same in tragedies as
1449b16	in epics. Of their parts, some are the same and others belong
1449b17	to tragedy alone; so whoever knows about tragedy, good
1449b18	and bad, knows about epics too: for what epic
1449b19	has, tragedy has too, but not all that tragedy has is in
1449b20	epic.
