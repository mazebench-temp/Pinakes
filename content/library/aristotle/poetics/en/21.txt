1457a31	Nouns are of two kinds: simple, by which I mean not composed
1457a32	of significant parts, such as gē (“earth”), and double. Of the double,
1457a33	some are composed of a significant and a non-significant part (though not significant and non-significant within the noun), and some of significant parts
1457a34	only. There could also be triple and quadruple nouns and
1457a35	multiple ones, like most of those of the Massaliots,
1457b1	Hermocaicoxanthus… Every noun is either standard, or a rare word,
1457b2	or a metaphor, or an ornament, or coined, or lengthened, or
1457b3	shortened, or altered. By standard I mean the word that
1457b4	each people uses, and by rare word one that others use; so it is evident that
1457b5	the same word can be both rare and standard, but not for the same
1457b6	people: sigynon (“spear”) is standard for the Cypriots, but a rare word for us.
1457b7	Metaphor is the transfer of a word that belongs to something else, either from genus
1457b8	to species, or from species to genus, or from species
1457b9	to species, or by analogy. By “from genus to
1457b10	species” I mean, for example, “Here my ship stands”; for lying at anchor is
1457b11	a kind of standing. From species to genus: “Truly Odysseus has done countless
1457b12	good deeds”; for countless is many, and he has used it here instead
1457b13	of many. From species to species, for example “with the bronze
1457b14	drawing off his life” and “cutting with the long-edged bronze”; for here
1457b15	he has said “drawing off” for “cutting” and “cutting” for “drawing off”: both
1457b16	are kinds of taking away. By analogy I mean when
1457b17	the second term stands to the first as the fourth to the
1457b18	third: then the poet will say the fourth instead of the second, or instead of the
1457b19	fourth the second. And sometimes they add the term to which the word replaced
1457b20	is related. I mean, for example, that the cup stands to
1457b21	Dionysus as the shield to Ares; so the poet will call the
1457b22	cup “the shield of Dionysus” and the shield “the cup
1457b23	of Ares”. Or: as old age is to life, so evening is to day;
1457b24	so he will call evening “the old age of the day”, or as
1457b25	Empedocles does, and old age “the evening of life” or “the sunset of life”. For some
1457b26	terms of an analogy there is no established word, but it will be expressed
1457b27	in the same way none the less: to scatter seed is to sow, but the sun's scattering of its
1457b28	flame has no name; yet this stands to the sun
1457b29	as sowing to the seed, which is why it has been said: “sowing the god-created
1457b30	flame”. This kind of metaphor can also be used
1457b31	in another way: by applying the alien name and then denying something of what properly belongs
1457b32	to it, as if one were to call the shield a cup, not “of Ares” but “wineless”…
1457b33	A coined word is one that nobody at all uses and the poet
1457b34	himself creates; there seem to be some such words, for example
1457b35	ernyges (“sprouts”) for horns, and arētēr (“one who prays”) for priest. A word is lengthened
1458a1	or shortened: lengthened if it uses a longer vowel
1458a2	than its own, or has a syllable inserted; shortened if
1458a3	something has been taken away from it. Lengthened: as poleōs
1458a4	becomes polēos, and Pēleidou Pēlēiadeō; shortened, as
1458a5	kri and dō, and “one sight (ops) comes from both”. A word is altered
1458a6	when the poet keeps part of the usual form and
1458a7	makes up part, as “in the right breast”, with dexiteron for dexion.
1458a8	Of nouns themselves, some are masculine, some feminine, and some
1458a9	in between: masculine are those that end in N and R and S
1458a10	and in the letters compounded with S (these are two, Ps and X),
1458a11	feminine those that end in the vowels that are always long, such as
1458a12	Ē and Ō, and, of those that can be lengthened, in A; so it turns out that
1458a13	masculine and feminine have an equal number of endings, for Ps and X
1458a14	are compounds. No noun ends in a mute, nor in
1458a15	a short vowel. In I there are only three,
1458a16	meli (“honey”), kommi (“gum”), peperi (“pepper”). In Y there are five.
1458a17	The in-between nouns end in these and in N and S.
