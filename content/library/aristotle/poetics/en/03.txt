1448a19	There is, further, a third difference: the manner in which one might imitate
1448a20	each of these objects. For in the same media one may imitate the same objects
1448a21	at times by narrating, either becoming someone else,
1448a22	as Homer does, or remaining oneself without change,
1448a23	or by presenting all as acting and doing,
1448a24	†those who imitate†. Imitation, then, lies in these three differences,
1448a25	as we said at the beginning: the media, the objects and the manner. So
1448a26	in one respect Sophocles would be the same kind of imitator as Homer, since both
1448a27	imitate serious people, and in another the same as Aristophanes, since both imitate people
1448a28	acting and doing. This, some say, is why such works are called
1448a29	dramas, because they imitate people doing (drōntes). This is also why
1448a30	both tragedy and comedy are claimed by the
1448a31	Dorians (comedy by the Megarians, both those here, who say it began
1448a32	when they had their democracy, and those from
1448a33	Sicily, for the poet Epicharmus came from there, who lived long before
1448a34	Chionides and Magnes; and tragedy by some
1448a35	of those in the Peloponnese), taking the names as their evidence. They
1448a36	say that they call the outlying villages kōmai, while the Athenians
1448a37	call them dēmoi, on the grounds that comedians were named not from revelling (kōmazein)
1448a38	but from wandering from village to village when they were scorned and kept out of the city;
1448b1	and that for “doing” they themselves say dran, while the Athenians
1448b2	say prattein. About the differences of imitation, then,
1448b3	how many they are and what they are, let this be enough.
