1456a33	The other parts have now been discussed; it remains to speak of diction
1456a34	and thought. What concerns thought may be left to
1456a35	my discussions of rhetoric, for it belongs more properly to that
1456a36	inquiry. Under thought fall all the effects that must be produced
1456a37	by speech. Their parts are
1456a38	proving and refuting and arousing feelings (such as
1456b1	pity or fear or anger and the like), and also magnifying
1456b2	and minimizing. It is clear that in the incidents too
1456b3	the same principles must be used when things must be made pitiable or terrible
1456b4	or great or probable; except that there is this
1456b5	difference, that the one must be apparent without explanation, while the other
1456b6	must be produced in speech by the speaker and arise from
1456b7	the speech. For what would the speaker's work be,
1456b8	if things appeared as they should even without the speech? Of matters concerning
1456b9	diction, one subject of study is the forms of utterance,
1456b10	knowledge of which belongs to the art of delivery and to whoever
1456b11	has the master art of it: for instance, what a command is, and a prayer, and
1456b12	a statement, and a threat, and a question, and an answer, and anything
1456b13	else of the kind. For on the ground of knowing these or not knowing them
1456b14	no criticism is brought against the art of poetry that is worth
1456b15	taking seriously. For who would suppose it a fault, what
1456b16	Protagoras criticizes, that thinking to pray, Homer gives an order
1456b17	when he says “Sing, goddess, the wrath”? For to tell someone,
1456b18	he says, to do something or not to is an order. So let it be passed over
1456b19	as a subject of another art and not of poetry.
