1447a8	Concerning the art of poetry itself and its kinds, what
1447a9	power each of them has, and how plots must be put together
1447a10	if the poetry is to turn out well; and further, of how many and
1447a11	of what sort of parts it consists; and likewise about the other things that belong to the
1447a12	same inquiry, let us speak, beginning, as is natural,
1447a13	first with what comes first. Epic, then, and the composition of tragedy,
1447a14	and comedy too, and dithyrambic poetry, and
1447a15	most of the music of the pipe and of the lyre, all happen to be,
1447a16	taken as a whole, imitations. But they differ from one another
1447a17	in three ways: by imitating in different media, or different objects, or
1447a18	in a different manner and not in the same way. For just as with colors
1447a19	and shapes some people imitate many things by making likenesses of them (some
1447a20	by art, others by habit), and others through the voice,
1447a21	so too in the arts we have mentioned: all of them produce
1447a22	their imitation in rhythm and speech and melody, and use these
1447a23	either separately or mixed. Melody and rhythm alone are used
1447a24	by the music of the pipe and of the lyre, and by any
1447a25	others that happen to have the same kind of power, such as that of the
1447a26	panpipes; rhythm by itself, without melody, by the art
1447a27	of the dancers (for they too, through rhythms given shape in movement,
1447a28	imitate characters and feelings and actions). But the art [epic] that uses
1447a29	bare speech alone, and the art that uses meters, whether these
1447b8	are mixed with one another or it uses a single kind of
1447b9	meter, happen to have no name up to now. For we would have no
1447b10	common name to give the mimes of Sophron and Xenarchus
1447b11	and the Socratic dialogues, nor to an imitation that someone made in trimeters
1447b12	or elegiacs or some other such meters.
1447b13	People, it is true, join the “making” to the meter,
1447b14	and call some elegiac poets and others epic poets, naming them poets not
1447b15	for their imitation but indiscriminately for their meter;
1447b16	for even if someone sets out something of medicine or natural science in
1447b17	meter, they are accustomed to call him that. Yet there is nothing in common
1447b18	between Homer and Empedocles except the meter: so it is right to call
1447b19	the one a poet, and the other a natural philosopher rather than
1447b20	a poet. In the same way, even if someone mixes all the meters
1447b21	in making his imitation, as Chaeremon composed
1447b22	the Centaur, a mixed rhapsody in all the meters, he too
1447b23	must be called a poet. On these matters, then, let the distinctions be drawn
1447b24	in this way. There are some arts that use all the media
1447b25	we have mentioned, I mean rhythm and song and meter,
1447b26	such as the composition of dithyrambs and of nomes,
1447b27	and tragedy and comedy; they differ in that some
1447b28	use them all at once, and others by turns. These, then, I call
1447b29	the differences of the arts in the media in which they produce their imitation.
