1455b24	Every tragedy has a complication and a resolution.
1455b25	Events outside the play, and often some of those inside it, are the complication, and the
1455b26	rest is the resolution. By complication I mean everything from the beginning
1455b27	up to the last part, the one from which the change comes
1455b28	to good fortune or to misfortune; and by resolution everything from the beginning of the
1455b29	change to the end. Thus in the Lynceus of Theodectes
1455b30	the complication is what was done before, and the seizure of the child, and
1455b31	again their own …; the resolution runs from the accusation of murder
1455b32	to the end. There are four kinds of tragedy (for
1455b33	that is also the number of the parts we named): the complex, which is all
1455b34	reversal and recognition; the tragedy of suffering, such as the
1456a1	Ajaxes and the Ixions; the tragedy of character, such as the Women of Phthia and
1456a2	the Peleus; the fourth †…†, such as the Daughters of Phorcys and
1456a3	the Prometheus and all plays set in Hades. One should try above all
1456a4	to have every quality, or failing that, the most important and the most, especially
1456a5	as people now carp at the poets: since there have been
1456a6	good poets in each part, they expect one poet to surpass each
1456a7	in his own excellence. It is right to call a tragedy
1456a8	different or the same by nothing so much as by the plot, that is,
1456a9	by having the same complication and resolution. Many who tie the knot well
1456a10	untie it badly; but both must always be mastered. One should
1456a11	remember what has often been said, and not make an epic
1456a12	structure into a tragedy —by epic I mean one with many stories—
1456a13	as if someone were to make a tragedy of the whole story of the Iliad. There,
1456a14	because of its length, the parts take on their proper magnitude, but in
1456a15	dramas the result falls far short of expectation. Evidence of this:
1456a16	all who have made the whole Sack of Troy, and not a part of it
1456a17	as Euripides did, or Niobe, and not as Aeschylus did, either
1456a18	fail or fare badly in the competition; even Agathon failed
1456a19	in this alone. In reversals and in
1456a20	simple incidents poets aim at what they want with marvelous skill,
1456a21	for this is tragic and satisfies human feeling. This happens when
1456a22	the clever but villainous man is deceived, like Sisyphus,
1456a23	or the brave but unjust man is defeated. This
1456a24	is even probable, as Agathon says, for it is probable that many things should happen
1456a25	even contrary to probability. The chorus too should be treated as one
1456a26	of the actors; it should be a part of the whole and
1456a27	share in the action, not as in Euripides but as in Sophocles. In
1456a28	the other poets the songs belong no more to the plot than to any other
1456a29	tragedy; that is why they sing interludes, a practice first begun
1456a30	by Agathon. Yet what difference is there
1456a31	between singing interludes and fitting a speech from one play
1456a32	into another, or a whole episode?
