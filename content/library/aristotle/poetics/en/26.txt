1461b26	Whether epic or tragic imitation is the better,
1461b27	one might well ask. If the less vulgar is the better, and that
1461b28	is always the one addressed to better spectators, it is all too clear that
1461b29	the one that imitates everything is vulgar: for, as if the audience would not notice unless
1461b30	the performer adds something himself, they move about a great deal, like bad
1461b31	pipers whirling round if they have to imitate a discus, and dragging
1461b32	the chorus leader about if they are piping the Scylla. Tragedy, then,
1461b33	is like this, just as the earlier actors thought of those who came after them;
1461b34	for overdoing it, Mynniscus used to call
1461b35	Callippides an ape, and there was the same opinion about
1462a1	Pindarus; and as these actors stand to their predecessors, so the whole art of tragedy stands to
1462a2	epic. Epic, they say, is addressed to decent spectators
1462a3	who have no need of gestures, and tragedy to
1462a4	inferior ones; if, then, it is vulgar, it would clearly be the worse. First,
1462a5	the charge is not against the art of poetry but against the art of acting, since
1462a6	it is possible to overdo the signs in reciting epic too, as
1462a7	Sosistratus did, and in singing, as Mnasitheus of Opus used to do.
1462a8	Next, not all movement is to be rejected, any more than
1462a9	all dancing, but only that of inferior people, which was the fault found with Callippides, and now
1462a10	with others, for imitating women who are not free. Besides, tragedy
1462a11	does its work even without movement, just as epic does: for
1462a12	by reading it, its quality is evident. If, then, it is
1462a13	superior in other respects, this fault need not be present in it.
1462a14	Then, tragedy is superior because it has everything that epic has (it can even use its
1462a15	meter), and besides it has music, no small part,
1462a16	[and spectacle], through which pleasures are produced most vividly;
1462a17	next, it has vividness both in reading and in
1462a18	performance; further, because the end of the imitation is achieved in less space
1462b1	(for what is more concentrated gives more pleasure than what is diluted over a long
1462b2	time: I mean, for example, if someone were to put Sophocles' Oedipus
1462b3	into as many verses as the Iliad has); further, the imitation of the epic poets
1462b4	is less unified (the evidence: from any epic imitation several
1462b5	tragedies come), so that if they make a single plot,
1462b6	it either seems curtailed if it is presented briefly, or
1462b7	watery if it follows the length of the meter; I mean, for example, if
1462b8	the epic is composed of several actions, as the Iliad
1462b9	and the Odyssey have many such parts, which even by
1462b10	themselves have magnitude; and yet these poems are constructed
1462b11	as well as possible, and are as nearly as can be the imitation of a single action.
1462b12	If, then, tragedy excels in all these respects, and also in the work of the art
1462b13	(for they should produce not any chance pleasure but
1462b14	the one we have described), it is evident that it would be the superior, since it achieves its
1462b15	end better than epic. About
1462b16	tragedy and epic, then, both themselves and
1462b17	their kinds and parts, how many there are and how
1462b18	they differ, and what makes them succeed or fail, and about
1462b19	criticisms and their solutions, let this be enough…
