---
summary: Thought is the third part of tragedy, the speakers' power to prove, refute, rouse feeling and state general truths; its study belongs to rhetoric.
grc: [διανο*]
en: [thought, thoughts]
---

The people acting are of a certain kind in their character and their thought (1449b36–38), and thought is found “all the places where in speaking they demonstrate something or declare an opinion” (1450a6–7). It comes third, after plot and character:

{{quote:26a03b2e-e114-5f69-b629-f1f1a2972b7f}}

Character shows what someone chooses; thought, “where people demonstrate that something is or is not, or declare something universal” (1450b11–12). In chapter 19 Aristotle sends the subject to his treatise on rhetoric, “for it belongs more properly to that inquiry” (1456a34–36; see [[rhetoric-of-aristotle]]): its parts are proving and refuting, rousing feelings such as pity, fear and anger, and magnifying and minimizing (1456a36–b2). The same principles govern the incidents, except that these must be evident without explanation, while thought is produced by the speaker in speech (1456b2–8).

Epic needs good thoughts as tragedy does, and in thought and diction Homer “has surpassed everyone” (1459b11–17); diction should be elaborated only where there is neither character nor thought, for diction that is too brilliant obscures both (1460b2–5).
