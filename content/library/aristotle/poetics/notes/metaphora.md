The examples: from genus to species, “My ship stands over there”, since lying at anchor is a kind of standing (see [[here-my-ship-stands]]); from species to genus, “Truly Odysseus has done countless good deeds”, countless being a kind of many (see [[countless-good-deeds]]); from species to species, Empedocles' “with the bronze drawing off his life” and “cutting with the long-edged bronze”, drawing off and cutting being kinds of taking away (see [[drawing-off-life-with-bronze]]); by analogy, the cup as the shield of Dionysus and the shield as the cup of Ares, evening as the old age of the day and old age as the evening of life, and the sun “sowing the god-created flame” (1457b9–30; see [[the-cup-of-ares]], [[the-evening-of-life]] and [[the-god-created-flame]]). A metaphor of analogy can also deny something proper to the borrowed word, as if the shield were called a cup “not of Ares” but “wineless” (1457b30–32).

Diction made of metaphors becomes a riddle (1458a25–30; see [[ainigma]]). But metaphor is the great thing:

{{quote:3a944e90-ec3a-5172-8501-b88b74da8b10}}

Metaphors suit iambics (1459a10), and heroic verse admits them most (1459b35–36). In chapter 25 several problems in Homer are solved “by metaphor”: “all the gods and men slept” means many, the Bear “alone” has no share in Ocean because she is the best known, and Ganymede's pouring wine for gods who drink none may be a metaphor too (1461a16–31; see [[all-the-gods-slept]], [[alone-without-a-share]] and [[aristotle-wine-of-the-gods]]).
