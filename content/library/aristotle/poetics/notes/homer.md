Aristotle names Homer more often than any other poet, and nearly always to praise him.

**The poet of serious things.** Homer imitates people better than we are (1448a11–12; see [[better-worse-or-like-us]]) and narrates while “becoming someone else”, speaking in the persons of his characters (1448a20–22; see [[modes-of-imitation]]); in one respect Sophocles is the same kind of imitator, since both imitate serious people (1448a25–27). No poem of the comic kind can be named before Homer, but beginning from him one can, his *Margites* (1448b28–30; see [[margites]]):

**Unity.** Homer “as he excels in everything else” saw that a plot is not one because it is about one man: the Odyssey leaves out the wound on Parnassus and the feigned madness, and the Iliad likewise is built around one action (1451a22–30; see [[unity-of-action]]). In chapter 23 he “would seem divine beside the others” for not trying to make the whole war into a poem, but taking one part and using others as episodes, like the catalogue of ships (1459a30–37; see [[catalogue-of-ships]]); from each of his poems one or two tragedies can be made, from the *Cypria* and the *Little Iliad* many (1459b2–7).

**The model of epic.** Homer used all the kinds and parts of epic, “both first and sufficiently”: the Iliad simple and full of suffering, the Odyssey complex and full of character, and in diction and thought “he has surpassed everyone” (1459b12–17). He alone knows what part the poet himself should play:

{{quote:8aa12ce2-e2ff-51cf-99a4-a57d6b2086b2}}

He above all “has taught the others how to tell lies as they should be told” (1460a18–19; see [[homer-taught-lies]] and [[paralogismos]]), and he hides the absurdities of the Odyssey with his other virtues (1460a35–b2). The Iliad and Odyssey are “constructed as well as possible, and are as nearly as can be the imitation of a single action” (1462b10–11).

**Problems.** Chapter 25 defends him against critics: his spears driven upright, the mules of Apollo, Dolon's looks, Achilles' stronger wine, all the gods asleep, the dream's message, the greave of tin, and the spear that stopped (1461a1–35; see [[problems-and-solutions]]). Protagoras' complaint about the first line is dismissed (1456b15–19; see [[protagoras-on-the-first-line]]). The corrupt sentence at 1454b14 may have said that Homer made Achilles good though an example of harshness, or, in a common emendation, named Agathon with Homer (see [[achilles]]).
