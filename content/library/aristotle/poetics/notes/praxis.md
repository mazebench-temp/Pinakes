summary: Tragedy imitates action, not people; the plot is the imitation of the action, and the action must be one, whole and complete.

Imitation is of “people in action” (1448a1), and tragedy is “an imitation of an action that is serious and complete and has magnitude” (1449b24–25; see [[definition-of-tragedy]]). The plot is the imitation of the action:

{{quote:8d393dd5-34db-5ffa-b4f3-85b6c90d7142}}

The bracketed words in Kassel's text, that happiness and unhappiness lie in action and that the end of life is an action, not a quality, may be a later addition, but they state the point exactly. Characters are included for the sake of the actions; a tragedy can exist without character, never without action (1450a23–25). The action must be one: a plot is not one because it is about one man, for one man performs many actions from which no single action results (1451a16–19; see [[unity-of-action]]). A simple action is one in which the change of fortune comes without reversal or recognition, a complex one with them (1452a12–18; see [[simple-and-complex-plots]]).

The deed itself may be done knowingly or in ignorance, or be about to be done and not done; Aristotle ranks the four possibilities in chapter 14 (1453b27–1454a9; see [[the-best-tragic-plot]]). Epic too must be built “about one action that is whole and complete” (1459a18–20), and the Iliad and Odyssey are “as nearly as can be the imitation of a single action” (1462b10–11).
