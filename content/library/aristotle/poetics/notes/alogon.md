“There should be nothing irrational in the incidents, or if there is, it should lie outside the tragedy, as in Sophocles' Oedipus” (1454b6–8), for Oedipus does not know how Laius died (1460a29–30; see [[oedipus-the-king]]). Epic admits more of it because we do not see the person acting (1460a12–17; see [[thaumaston]]). Chapter 24 gives the rule with examples:

{{quote:40c724aa-ac3d-5af8-9eac-d4a2c07571d1}}

The irrational things in the Odyssey about Odysseus' being put ashore would be intolerable if a bad poet had made them; Homer conceals the absurdity, “sweetening it with his other virtues” (1460a35–b2; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). In chapter 25 the irrational should be referred to what people say, or shown not to be irrational, “for it is probable that things should happen even contrary to probability” (1461b14–15), and criticism is right when the poet uses it without necessity, as Euripides does of Aegeus (1461b19–21; see [[aegeus]]).
