Magnitude is part of the definition of tragedy (1449b24–25), and one of the stages of its history: from slight plots and comic diction tragedy grew late to dignity (1449a19–21). Chapter 7 explains why it matters:

{{quote:56a494f2-07f2-5772-a686-d7552fc10524}}

A creature too small blurs; one ten thousand stades long cannot be seen as one (see [[the-animal-of-ten-thousand-stades]]). The limit set by competitions and the attention of the audience “is no concern of the art”; the natural limit is that the greater is the more beautiful, so long as it stays clear as a whole, and “the magnitude in which, as events follow one after another by probability or necessity, it comes about that there is a change to good fortune from misfortune, or from good fortune to misfortune, is an adequate limit” (1451a6–15; see [[metabasis]]).

Epic has a special capacity for extending its magnitude, since it can narrate many parts happening at once (1459b22–28); but the whole war would have made the Iliad's plot “too large and not easily taken in at a glance” (1459a30–34). An epic made of several actions has parts, like those of the Iliad and the Odyssey, “which even by themselves have magnitude” (1462b7–10).
