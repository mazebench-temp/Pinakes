summary: The Oedipus is cited more often than any other play: for its reversal, its finest recognition joined with reversal, its deed done in ignorance, its irrationality outside the drama, its recognition from the incidents, and its concentrated plot.
grc: [Οἰδίποδι, Οἰδίπουν θείη]
en: [the Oedipus, Sophocles' Oedipus, Oedipus]
---

The play supplies Aristotle's chief examples: the reversal brought by the messenger who comes to cheer Oedipus (1452a24–26; see [[the-discovery-of-oedipus]]); the finest recognition, the one that comes together with a reversal (1452a32–33; see [[anagnorisis]]); the terrible deed done in ignorance and recognized afterwards, “outside the drama” (1453b29–32); the irrational element kept “outside the tragedy”, that Oedipus does not know how Laius died (1454b6–8, 1460a29–30; see [[alogon]]); and the best recognition, which arises from the incidents themselves (1455a16–18). The story alone, merely heard, makes one shudder (1453b3–7), and the play's concentration is a reason why tragedy is superior to epic: what would it be “if someone were to put Sophocles' Oedipus into as many verses as the Iliad has”? (1462b1–3; see [[epic-and-tragedy]]).
