---
summary: Wickedness rules a man out as a tragic hero; criticism of wickedness is right when the poet uses it without necessity.
grc: [μοχθηρ*]
except: [1458b30]
en: [wicked, wickedness]
---

The wicked man passing from misfortune to good fortune “is the most untragic thing of all” (1452b36–1453a1), and the hero falls “not through badness and wickedness but through some error” (1453a8–10, 1453a15; see [[hamartia]] and [[the-tragic-hero]]). In chapter 25 criticism of irrationality and of wickedness is right “when, there being no necessity, the poet makes use of” them (1461b19–21). (At 1458b30 the same word is the “wretched” stool of Aristotle's rewording of the Odyssey: see [[the-unseemly-stool]].)
