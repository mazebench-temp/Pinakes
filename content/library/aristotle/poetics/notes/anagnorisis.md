Recognitions are parts of the plot and, with reversals, the strongest means by which tragedy sways the soul (1450a33–35). The definition:

{{quote:864c7613-3301-5203-bb9f-13b8c8d98a32}}

There are recognitions of objects and of whether someone has done something, but the one that belongs most to the plot is of persons, and it may be one-sided or mutual: Iphigenia is recognized by Orestes from the letter she sends, and he needs another means to be recognized by her (1452a33–b8; see [[the-recognition-of-orestes-and-iphigenia]]). In chapter 14 recognition is what makes the best plots possible, when the terrible deed is about to be done in ignorance and is recognized in time (1454a4–9; see [[the-best-tragic-plot]]).

Chapter 16 sets out the kinds, from worst to best:

- by signs, congenital or acquired, on the body or outside it, “the least artistic”: the birthmark of the Earth-born, the stars in Carcinus' *Thyestes*, scars, necklaces, the little boat in the *Tyro*; Odysseus is recognized by his scar one way by the nurse and another by the swineherds (1454b19–30; see [[semeion]]);
- contrived by the poet: Orestes in the *Iphigenia* says who he is; the voice of the shuttle in Sophocles' *Tereus* (1454b30–37);
- through memory, when something seen or heard stirs a feeling: the painting in Dicaeogenes' *Cyprians*, the song of the lyre player in the tale told to Alcinous (1454b37–1455a4);
- from reasoning, as in the *Libation Bearers*: someone like me has come; no one is like me but Orestes; so Orestes has come (1455a4–12);
- a composite kind built on the audience's false inference, as in *Odysseus the False Messenger* (1455a12–16; see [[paralogismos]]);
- best of all, the recognition “that arises from the incidents themselves, when the astonishment comes about through probable events”, as in Sophocles' *Oedipus* and the *Iphigenia* (1455a16–21).

The Odyssey is complex because “there is recognition throughout” (1459b15).
