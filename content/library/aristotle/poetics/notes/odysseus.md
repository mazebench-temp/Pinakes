---
summary: Odysseus is everywhere in the Poetics: in the outline of the Odyssey, in its scenes of recognition, in tragedies and a dithyramb about him, and in a Homeric problem about his marriage.
heading: In the Poetics
---

Homer did not put into the Odyssey everything that happened to Odysseus, such as his wounding on Parnassus and his feigned madness (1451a24–28; see [[the-boar-hunt-on-parnassus]] and [[odysseus-feigns-madness]]), and the whole story can be told in a sentence (1455b17–23; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). His recognitions supply examples: by the scar, in one way by the nurse and in another by the swineherds (1454b26–30; see [[odysseus-recognized-by-his-nurse]]); by memory, when he weeps at the song in the tale to Alcinous (1455a2–3; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]); and by a false inference in *Odysseus the False Messenger* (1455a13–16; see [[odysseus-the-false-messenger]]). His being set ashore asleep on Ithaca is irrational, but Homer sweetens it (1460a35–b2; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). Tragedies show his death at his son's hands (1453b33–34; see [[telegonus-kills-odysseus]]) and his entry into Troy as a beggar (1459b6; see [[odysseus-enters-troy-as-a-beggar]]); Timotheus' *Scylla* gave him an unseemly lament (1454a30–31; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). He is the subject of a metaphor, “truly Odysseus has done countless good deeds” (1457b11–13; see [[countless-good-deeds]]), and of a problem: whom did he marry? (1461b4–9; see [[the-icarius-problem]]).
