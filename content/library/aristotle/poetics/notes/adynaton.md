The poet's business is “what could happen, things possible by probability or necessity” (1451a36–38). Tragedy keeps historical names because what is possible is convincing:

{{quote:6472c0d5-d4fa-52cd-a3b5-75939c92c469}}

A riddle is made by joining impossibilities to say what is real (1458a26–27; see [[ainigma]]). Chapter 24 states the paradox: “one should prefer probable impossibilities to unconvincing possibilities” (1460a26–27; see [[probable-impossibilities]]). In chapter 25 impossibility is the first of the five grounds of criticism (1461b22–23); to compose impossibilities is an error, but it is correct if it achieves the end of the art (1460b23–26), and the impossible must be referred to poetry, to what is better, or to opinion: “for poetry, a convincing impossibility is preferable to what is unconvincing though possible” (1461b9–12; see [[problems-and-solutions]]).
