The treatise opens with its subject:

{{quote:b4a821f5-f021-59d7-bb25-fb432fd9211b}}

Aristotle defines the art by what it does, not by the form it takes. Epic, tragedy, comedy, the dithyramb and most music for the pipe and the lyre are all [[mimesis|imitations]] (1447a13–16); people, he complains, name poets after their meter, elegiac poets and epic poets, “not for their imitation but indiscriminately for their meter”, though Homer and the natural philosopher [[Empedocles]] have nothing in common but the hexameter (1447b13–20). Two natural causes gave birth to poetry, the instinct to imitate and the instinct for melody and rhythm (1448b4–24; see [[origins-of-poetry]]), and it split at once according to the character of its makers (1448b24–27).

The comparison with history uses the same word: ποίησις is “more philosophical and more serious than history” (1451b5–7; see [[poetry-and-history]]). Its correctness is its own, for “correctness is not the same in the art of politics and in the art of poetry” (1460b13–15), and the faults that belong to the art must be told apart from incidental ones (1460b15–21; see [[problems-and-solutions]]). The art “belongs to the gifted or to the mad” (1455a32–34; see [[the-gifted-or-the-mad]]). Spectacle, by contrast, is “the least artistic and the least proper to the art of poetry” (1450b16–20; see [[opsis]]).
