The arts that use bare speech or meter “happen to have no name up to now” (1447a28–b9), and calling poets after their meter is a mistake: “there is nothing in common between Homer and Empedocles except the meter” (1447b13–20; see [[poietes]]). Meters are “sections of rhythms” (1448b21–22; see [[rhythmos]]). The history of poetry is partly a history of meters finding their genres: the iambic came into lampoon “as was fitting” (1448b30–32), tragedy changed from the tetrameter to the iambic once speech came in, and “nature itself found the meter that belonged to it” (1449a21–28; see [[iambic-trimeter]] and [[trochaic-tetrameter]]). Epic differs from tragedy in having a single meter (1449b11) and keeps the heroic meter, which “has proved fitting by experience” (1459b31–1460a5; see [[hexameter]]).

The distinction between poetry and history is “not by speaking in verse or in prose (the works of Herodotus could be put into verse, and they would be history no less with meter than without it)” (1451b1–4; see [[poetry-and-history]]). The poet must be a maker of plots rather than of verses (1451b27–28).
