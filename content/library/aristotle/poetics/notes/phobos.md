heading: In the Poetics — pity and fear
grc: [φοβ*, φρίττειν]

The definition of tragedy names fear beside pity (1449b27; see [[pity]] and [[katharsis]]). In chapter 13 Aristotle separates them: pity is felt “for the undeserving”, fear “for one like us” (1453a3–6), which is why the hero must be neither a paragon nor a villain (see [[the-tragic-hero]]). The fearful should come from the structure of the incidents, not from spectacle:

{{quote:8e2a4d47-1628-5343-917f-86e993de69fa}}

To produce “not the fearful but only the monstrous” through spectacle is to have nothing in common with tragedy (1453b8–10; see [[opsis]]). Oedipus's fear about his mother, which the messenger from Corinth comes to remove and instead confirms, is Aristotle's example of reversal (1452a24–26; see [[the-discovery-of-oedipus]]).
