summary: Character comes second among the parts of tragedy, after plot: it reveals choice, it is included for the sake of the action, and it must be good, appropriate, like and consistent.
grc: [ἦθος, ἤθη, ἠθῶν, ἤθεσι*, ἤθους, ἠθικ*, ἀήθ*, ἠθογράφος]
en: [character, characters, of character, without character]

Those who act “must be of a certain kind in both their character and their thought”, and it is by these that actions are of a certain kind (1449b36–1450a3); by characters Aristotle means “that in virtue of which we say the people acting are of a certain kind” (1450a5–6). Character is the second part of tragedy, after plot, as in painting a mere outline in white pleases more than the finest colors smeared at random (1450a38–b3). It is subordinate: “they do not act in order to imitate characters; rather they take in the characters for the sake of the actions”, and the tragedies of most of the moderns are without character, as [[Zeuxis]] painted without it where [[Polygnotus]] was a good painter of character (1450a20–29). Chapter 15 sets out four aims: characters should be good, appropriate, like, and consistent (1454a16–28; see [[four-aims-of-character]]), with examples of each failure from [[Euripides]] and [[Timotheus]]: Menelaus in the *Orestes*, the lament of Odysseus in the *Scylla*, the speech of Melanippe, and Iphigenia at Aulis (1454a28–33). As in plot, so in character one must seek “the necessary or the probable” (1454a33–36), and the poet, like a good portrait painter, must make people better than they are while keeping their likeness (1454b8–14).

Some tragedies are “tragedies of character”, like the *Women of Phthia* and the *Peleus* (1456a1–2; see [[four-kinds-of-tragedy]]); the Odyssey is “full of character”, the Iliad full of suffering (1459b15–16). Homer is praised because after a brief prelude he at once brings on “a man or a woman or some other character, and none without character” (1460a10–11).
