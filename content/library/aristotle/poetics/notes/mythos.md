heading: In the Poetics — plot

Aristotle gives the word a technical sense and says so: “the imitation of the action is the plot; for by plot I mean here the arrangement of the incidents” (1450a3–5; see [[systasis]] and [[praxis]]). The plot is the first of the six parts of tragedy and the most important (1450a15; see [[six-parts-of-tragedy]]):

{{quote:bd25722b-605c-579f-b6b5-2d2dfa297def}}

The plot is “the end of tragedy, and the end is the most important thing of all” (1450a22–23); reversals and recognitions, by which tragedy most sways the soul, are parts of it (1450a33–35). A plot must be whole and of a magnitude that can be remembered (chapter 7; see [[megethos]]), one (chapter 8; see [[unity-of-action]]), and built by probability or necessity (chapter 9; see [[eikos]]); it is simple or complex (chapter 10; see [[simple-and-complex-plots]]), it has a complication and a resolution (1455b24–32; see [[desis-and-lysis]]), and tragedies are best judged the same or different by their plots (1456a7–9). The poet is “a maker of plots rather than of verses” (1451b27–28; see [[poietes]]).

The older sense survives beside the new one. The finest tragedies are composed about a few houses, as “about Alcmaeon and Oedipus and Orestes” (1453a17–22; see [[the-few-houses]]), and the poet need not keep at all costs to “the traditional stories around which tragedies are made” (1451b23–25; see [[traditional-stories]]); in a comedy “the bitterest enemies in the story”, Orestes and Aegisthus, go off as friends (1453a37–39). One hears “the story of Oedipus” and shudders (1453b6–7). Comedy took its plots from Sicily (1449b5–7), and Crates was the first at Athens to compose “stories and plots of a general kind” (1449b7–8).
