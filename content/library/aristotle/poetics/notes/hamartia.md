---
summary: The hero falls “through some error”; the same family of words names the poet's faults, the critic's mistakes, and the error that makes comedy laughable.
grc: [ἁμαρτ*, ἡμαρτ*]
en: [error, fault, mistake, go wrong, gone wrong, the same mistake]
---

The word's famous use is in chapter 13. After ruling out the decent man falling and the wicked man rising, Aristotle describes the man between:

{{quote:83a41d99-b039-5d80-bea1-c243c9a1c3e5}}

The plot should change “from good fortune to misfortune, not through wickedness but through a great error” of such a man, “or better rather than worse” (1453a13–17; see [[the-tragic-hero]]). The examples, [[Oedipus]] and [[Thyestes]], are men who did terrible things in ignorance. Chapter 14 prefers the deed done in ignorance and recognized afterwards, or recognized just in time (1453b27–1454a9; see [[the-best-tragic-plot]]).

The word family runs through the treatise. The ridiculous is “an error or ugliness that causes no pain and does no harm” (ἁμάρτημα, 1449a34–35; see [[geloion]]). Poets of Heracleids “go wrong” (1451a19–20), and the critics of Euripides “make the same mistake” (1453a24). A recognition in which the character simply says what the poet wants “comes close to the fault” Aristotle has just described (1454b34–36). In chapter 25 poetry has two kinds of error, one that belongs to the art and one incidental to it, such as a horse painted with both right legs forward (1460b15–21; see [[problems-and-solutions]]), and the Icarius problem arises “through a mistake” (1461b8; see [[the-icarius-problem]]).
