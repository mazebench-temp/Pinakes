The first sentence of the treatise asks “how plots must be put together” (1447a9), and the structure of the incidents is the most important of the six parts of tragedy (1450a15; see [[six-parts-of-tragedy]]). A tragedy that uses character, diction and thought less well but has a plot and “a structure of incidents” is far better than a string of fine speeches (1450a29–33), and beginners can manage diction and character before they can construct the incidents (1450a35–38).

Chapters 7 to 14 are about the structure: its wholeness and magnitude (7), its unity (8), its probability (9), simple and complex plots (10–11), and the plots that best produce pity and fear (13–14), which should arise “from the very structure of the incidents”, not from spectacle:

{{quote:741061f9-474d-545b-b027-f5f69954d1da}}

The finest tragedy has a complex, not a simple, structure (1452b31–32); a double structure, ending happily for the good and unhappily for the bad, belongs rather to comedy (1453a30–39; see [[the-double-plot]]). Epic plots too must be constructed “dramatically” (1459a18–19), and the length of the structure is one of the ways epic differs from tragedy (1459b17–22).
