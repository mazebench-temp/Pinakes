summary: Epic is treated alongside tragedy throughout: the same kinds and parts except song and spectacle, a single meter, narration, greater length; Homer is its model; and in the last chapter it is ranked below tragedy.
grc: [ἐποποι*, διηγηματικ*]
en: [epic, epics, epic poets, narrative]
---

Epic is the first art named in the treatise (1447a13), and Aristotle compares it with tragedy at every stage. It agrees with tragedy in being an imitation “of serious people in speech with meter”, but differs in having a single meter, in being narrative, and in length, since “epic is unlimited in time” (1449b9–16); whoever knows tragedy knows epic, for epic's parts are all found in tragedy (1449b16–20). An epic structure, with many stories, should not be made into a tragedy (1456a10–19).

Chapters 23 and 24 are on epic. Its plots should be dramatic, about one action, not like histories (1459a17–29; see [[unity-of-action]]); it has the same kinds as tragedy, simple or complex, of character or of suffering (1459b8–16); it can be longer, and narrate many parts at once, which gives it grandeur and variety (1459b17–31); its meter is the heroic (1459b31–1460a5; see [[hexameter]]); and it admits more of the irrational and the marvelous, “because we do not see the person acting” (1460a11–17; see [[thaumaston]]). Homer is the model throughout (1459a30–b7, 1460a5–11; see [[homer]]). The last chapter weighs the claim that epic is better, being addressed to better spectators, and rejects it (1461b26–1462b15; see [[epic-and-tragedy]]).
