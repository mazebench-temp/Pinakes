---
summary: Diction is the fourth part of tragedy, “expression through words”; chapters 20–22 analyze its parts, the kinds of words, and its excellence, to be clear and not mean.
grc: [λεξι*, λεξε*, λεκτικ*]
en: [diction, speech]
---

By diction Aristotle means “the actual composition of the verses” (1449b34–35) and, more generally, “expression through words, which has the same power in verse and in prose” (1450b13–15). It is the fourth part of tragedy (1450b12–13). When “speech came in”, tragedy found its natural meter, the iambic, “the most like speech” of all meters (1449a23–28; see [[iambic-trimeter]]).

Chapter 19 sets aside the “forms of utterance”, command and prayer, statement and threat, question and answer, as the business of the art of delivery (1456b8–19; see [[forms-of-utterance]] and [[delivery]]). Chapter 20 divides diction into its parts, from the letter to the statement (see [[stoicheion]] and the entries it links), and chapter 21 sorts words into standard and rare, metaphor and ornament, coined, lengthened, shortened and altered (1457b1–3; see [[kyrion]], [[glotta]] and [[metaphora]]). All unfamiliar words make a riddle or a barbarism (1458a23–26; see [[ainigma]] and [[barbarismos]]); the art is in the blend (see [[excellence-of-diction]]). Diction should be labored only in the slack parts of a poem, where there is neither character nor thought (1460b2–5), and many problems in Homer are solved by looking at the diction (1461a9–31; see [[problems-and-solutions]]).
