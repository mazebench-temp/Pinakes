The power of tragedy exists “even without a performance and actors” (1450b18). The limit of a play's length set by the competitions and the attention of the audience is no concern of the art: “if a hundred tragedies had to compete, they would compete by the water clock” (1451a6–9; see [[water-clock]]). Good poets compose “show pieces for competition” and distort the sequence of the plot (1451b37–1452a1). Plays ending in misfortune seem most tragic “on the stage and in the competitions” (1453a27–28), and those who stage a whole Sack of Troy “either fail or fare badly in the competition” (1456a16–19).
