---
summary: Aristotle quotes Agathon's paradox twice, for the tragedy in which the clever villain is deceived, and in defense of the irrational.
grc: [εἰκὸς γὰρ γίνεσθαι πολλὰ, καὶ παρὰ τὸ εἰκός]
en: [it is probable that many things should happen, it is probable that things should happen even contrary to probability, even contrary to probability]
---

At the end of chapter 18 Aristotle says that in reversals and in simple plots poets achieve what they aim at, an effect that is tragic and satisfies human feeling, when the clever but villainous man is deceived, like Sisyphus, or the brave but unjust man is defeated. “This is even probable, as Agathon says” (1456a19–25; see [[philanthropon]] and [[sisyphus]]). In chapter 25 the saying becomes one of the answers to critics: the irrational should be referred to what people say, and it can be shown that it is sometimes not irrational, “for it is probable that things should happen even contrary to probability” (1461b14–15; see [[alogon]] and [[problems-and-solutions]]). In both places the paradox lets the poet keep a surprising event without giving up the rule that a plot should move by probability or necessity (see [[eikos]]).
