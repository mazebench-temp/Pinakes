Tragedy is named in the first sentence of the treatise with epic, comedy and the dithyramb (1447a13–14), and differs from comedy in imitating people better than we are (1448a16–18; see [[better-worse-or-like-us]]) and from epic in being enacted and not narrated (1448a19–28; see [[modes-of-imitation]]).

**Origins (chapter 4).** Tragedy arose “from those who led off the dithyramb” and grew little by little, “and after passing through many changes tragedy stopped, once it had attained its own nature” (1449a9–15). Aeschylus brought in a second actor, Sophocles a third and scene painting; it grew to dignity from slight plots and comic diction and changed its meter from tetrameter to iambic (1449a15–28; see [[the-origins-of-tragedy]]). The Dorians of the Peloponnese claimed it (1448a29–35).

**Definition (chapter 6).** Tragedy is “an imitation of an action that is serious and complete and has magnitude … accomplishing through pity and fear the catharsis of such feelings” (1449b24–28; see [[definition-of-tragedy]]), and it has six parts, plot, character, thought, diction, song and spectacle (see [[six-parts-of-tragedy]]). It is “an imitation not of people but of actions and of life” (1450a16–17).

**Plot (chapters 7–14, 18).** The plot must be whole, of the right magnitude, one, and probable (see [[beginning-middle-end]], [[megethos]], [[unity-of-action]], [[eikos]]); it is simple or complex (see [[simple-and-complex-plots]]); the best is complex, ends in misfortune through error, and sets the terrible deed among kin (see [[the-best-tragic-plot]] and [[the-tragic-hero]]). Every tragedy has a complication and a resolution, and there are four kinds of tragedy (1455b24–1456a3; see [[desis-and-lysis]] and [[four-kinds-of-tragedy]]). The finest tragedies are about a few houses (1453a17–22; see [[the-few-houses]]).

**The quantitative parts (chapter 12):** prologue, episode, exodos and choral songs (see [[prologue]]).

**Tragedy and epic (chapters 5, 24, 26).** Epic has what tragedy has, but not all that tragedy has (1449b16–20); in the last chapter Aristotle answers those who call tragedy vulgar and concludes that it is superior, since it achieves the end of the art better (1461b26–1462b15; see [[epic-and-tragedy]]).
