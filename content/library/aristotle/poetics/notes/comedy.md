Comedy aims to imitate “people worse … than those of today” (1448a16–18), and it differs from tragedy in its objects but resembles it in its manner: Sophocles is like Aristophanes in that both imitate people acting (1448a25–28). Its name was claimed for the Dorians: the Megarians and the Sicilians, among whom Epicharmus lived, said the comedians took their name from villages, κῶμαι, not from reveling (1448a29–b2; see [[komos]], [[megarians]] and [[epicharmus]]). Homer's Margites first showed the form of comedy, “dramatizing not invective but the ridiculous” (1448b36–1449a2; see [[margites]]), and comic poets replaced the lampooners (1449a2–5).

Its history is obscure because it was not taken seriously at first: the archon granted a chorus only late, and the inventors of masks, prologues and actors are unknown; plot-making came from Sicily, and at Athens Crates first gave up the iambic form for plots of a general kind (1449a37–b9; see [[the-origins-of-comedy]], [[crates]] and [[iambus]]). In comedy names are supplied after the plot is built, unlike tragedy (1451b11–15). The pleasure of the double plot belongs to comedy, where enemies like Orestes and Aegisthus go off friends and “nobody is killed by anybody” (1453a35–39; see [[the-double-plot]]). The fuller discussion of comedy announced at 1449b21–22 belonged to the lost second book (see [[the-lost-book-on-comedy]]).
