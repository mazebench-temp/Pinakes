Tragedy accomplishes “through pity and fear the catharsis of such feelings” (1449b27–28; see [[definition-of-tragedy]] and [[katharsis]]). It imitates not only a complete action but “fearful and pitiable things”, and these arise most when things happen contrary to expectation but because of one another (1452a1–4); a recognition joined with a reversal “will bring pity or fear” (1452a38–b1). Chapter 13 defines the two emotions by their objects:

{{quote:5de44c03-3d12-52fd-862b-68c3f98bbda6}}

So a decent man falling into misfortune is repugnant rather than pitiable, a wicked man rising is the most untragic thing of all, and a villain's fall satisfies human feeling but moves neither emotion; the remaining case is the man between (1452b34–1453a12; see [[the-tragic-hero]]). Chapter 14 asks which events seem terrible or pitiable, and answers: those within close relationships, “as when brother kills brother, or son father, or mother son” (1453b14–22; see [[the-best-tragic-plot]]). The pleasure proper to tragedy is “the pleasure that comes from pity and fear through imitation” (1453b10–13; see [[tragic-pleasure]]). Rousing pity, fear and anger is also one of the tasks of thought in speeches (1456a38–b1; see [[dianoia]]).
