The treatise begins from a confusion in ordinary speech. People “join the making to the meter” and call some elegiac poets and others epic poets, naming them by their verse; but Homer and [[Empedocles]] share only the meter, so the one is rightly called a poet and the other a natural philosopher (1447b13–20). Even [[Chaeremon]], who mixed all the meters in his *Centaur*, “must be called a poet” (1447b20–23).

The play on the word comes to a point in chapter 9:

{{quote:58e2573b-0b2e-5301-a5cc-2e00d8396132}}

A poet makes plots; if the plot is drawn from things that happened, he is no less their maker, since what happened may also be what would probably happen (1451b29–32). The same thought governs the advice of chapter 17, to set out the story in general terms before giving it names and episodes (1455a34–b2), and of chapter 24, that the poet should speak in his own voice as little as possible, “for it is not in this capacity that he is an imitator” (1460a5–8; see [[the-poet-should-say-little]]).

Aristotle distinguishes good and bad poets constantly: bad poets make episodic plots because of themselves, good ones because of the actors (1451b36–1452a1), and the irrational things in the Odyssey would be intolerable “if a bad poet had made them” (1460a35–b2). The poets of his own day make their characters speak like rhetoricians, the old poets like statesmen (1450b7–8).
