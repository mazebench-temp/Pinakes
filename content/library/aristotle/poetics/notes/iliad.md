summary: The Iliad is Aristotle's model of epic: like tragedy in being about one action, simple and full of suffering, too large to make into one tragedy, with episodes like the catalogue of ships.

As the Iliad and the Odyssey are to tragedies, so the *Margites* is to comedies (1448b38–1449a2; see [[margites]]). Homer built the Iliad, like the Odyssey, around one action (1451a29; see [[unity-of-action]]), and did not try to tell the whole war, but “has taken one part and used many of the others as episodes, such as the catalogue of ships” (1459a30–37; see [[catalogue-of-ships]]). So “from the Iliad and the Odyssey one tragedy is made from each, or two at most” (1459b2–4), and to make a tragedy of “the whole story of the Iliad” would fail (1456a10–15). The poem is “simple and full of suffering” (1459b14–15; see [[simple-and-complex-plots]]). The sailing away in Book 2, stopped by Athena, is a resolution from outside the plot like the machine (1454b1–2; see [[the-test-of-the-army]]). The Iliad is “the Iliad is one by being bound together” (1457a28–30; see [[syndesmos]]). It is constructed as well as possible, as nearly as can be the imitation of a single action, yet has many parts that have magnitude in themselves (1462b8–11), and its length is the measure for Sophocles' Oedipus diluted into epic (1462b1–3). Chapter 25 answers a series of critics' problems in it (see [[problems-and-solutions]]).
