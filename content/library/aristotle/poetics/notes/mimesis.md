The opening chapters build the theory of poetry on the word. Epic and tragedy, comedy and dithyramb, and most music for the pipe and the lyre “all happen to be, taken as a whole, imitations” (1447a13–16), and they differ in three ways:

{{quote:5a4b0980-0627-50ed-8f77-16482ce0b83e}}

The media are rhythm, speech and melody, used alone or together (1447a21–b29); the objects are people in action, better than we are, worse, or like us (1448a1–18; see [[better-worse-or-like-us]]); and the manner is narration or enactment (1448a19–28; see [[modes-of-imitation]]). The three differences are recalled at the end of chapter 3: “the media, the objects and the manner” (1448a24–25).

Imitation is natural. Man is “the most imitative” of animals and learns his first lessons through imitation, and everyone takes pleasure in imitations, even of things painful to see (1448b5–19; see [[most-imitative-of-animals]] and [[pleasure-of-imitation]]). Tragedy is “an imitation of an action” (1449b24; see [[definition-of-tragedy]]), and the plot is “the imitation of the action” (1450a3–4). The poet is a poet by imitation, and so must be a maker of plots (1451b27–29; see [[poietes]]); he should say very little in his own voice, “for it is not in this capacity that he is an imitator” (1460a7–8). Like a painter, he must imitate things as they were or are, as people say they are, or as they ought to be (1460b8–11; see [[problems-and-solutions]]).
