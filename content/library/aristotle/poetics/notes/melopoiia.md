---
summary: Song is the sixth part of tragedy and the greatest of its embellishments; Aristotle names it, defines it in a phrase, and says little more.
grc: [μελοποιι*, μέλος, μέλει, μέλους, μελῶν]
en: [song, songs]
---

Language with “rhythm and melody and song” is the “embellished language” of the definition of tragedy, and some parts of a tragedy are carried out in meter alone, others in song (1449b25–31; see [[definition-of-tragedy]]). Spectacle, song and diction follow from the fact that tragedy is enacted (1449b31–34). Of song he says only that its power is “entirely evident” (1449b35–36) and that it is “the greatest of the embellishments” (1450b15–16).

The songs of the chorus are the parodos, the stasimon and the kommos (1452b17–25; see [[parodos]], [[stasimon]], [[kommos]]). Aristotle wants them to belong to the play: the chorus should be “one of the actors”, sharing in the action as in Sophocles and not as in Euripides, while the other poets sing interludes that belong no more to their plot than to any other tragedy, a practice begun by [[Agathon]] (1456a25–32; see [[chorus]] and [[embolima]]). Epic has all the parts of tragedy “apart from song and spectacle” (1459b10), and tragedy is superior partly because it has music too, “no small part” (1462a14–17).
