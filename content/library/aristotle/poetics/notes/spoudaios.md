So comedy “aims to imitate people worse, and tragedy better, than those of today” (1448a16–18; see [[better-worse-or-like-us]]). Poetry split by the character of its makers: “the more dignified imitated noble actions”, the more trivial “those of inferior people” (1448b24–26). Homer was the poet of serious things above all (1448b34); epic agrees with tragedy in being an imitation “of serious people” in meter (1449b9–10); and tragedy imitates “an action that is serious” (1449b24; see [[definition-of-tragedy]]). Comedy imitates “people who are worse, though not in respect of every kind of badness” (1449a32–33).

The same words judge quality: whoever knows about tragedy “good and bad” knows about epic too (1449b17–18), episodic plots are made by bad poets (1451b36), and poetry is “more serious than history” (1451b6; see [[poetry-and-history]]). In chapter 15 a woman and a slave can be good in character, “though perhaps of these the one is inferior, and the other wholly worthless” (1454a20–22).
