summary: Orestes figures in more of Aristotle's examples than anyone but Oedipus: the head of one of the few houses of tragedy, the son whose killing of his mother cannot be undone, and the brother recognized by Electra's reasoning and by Iphigenia's letter.
heading: In the Poetics
grc: [Ὀρέστης, Ὀρέστην, Ὀρέστου]
---

Orestes' house is one of the few on which the finest tragedies are composed (1453a20; see [[the-few-houses]]), and his killing of Clytemnestra is a traditional story that the poet may not undo; only in comedy would Orestes and Aegisthus go off as friends (1453a37–39, 1453b22–24; see [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]]). His recognitions give Aristotle examples of three kinds: by reasoning, in the *Libation Bearers* (1455a4–6; see [[electra-recognizes-orestes]]); contrived by the poet, when he declares himself to Iphigenia (1454b31–35); and by the probable reflection that Polyidus gave him (1455a6–8; see [[the-recognition-of-orestes-and-iphigenia]]). His madness and his rescue through the purification are the proper episodes of the *Iphigenia* (1455b14–15). Euripides' *Orestes*, in which his uncle Menelaus refuses to help him, is the example of baseness that the plot does not need (1454a28–29, 1461b21; see [[orestes-play]]).
