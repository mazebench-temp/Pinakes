The third part of the plot:

{{quote:2f8d03fc-d58c-5388-a45c-04224d15cbaf}}

Within close relationships “sufferings arise” that are worth seeking (1453b19–22), and poets are forced to return to the houses “in which such sufferings have happened” (1454a12–13; see [[the-few-houses]]). To be about to act knowingly and not act is untragic, “for there is no suffering” (1453b38–39). Dancers imitate “characters and feelings and actions” (1447a28), and the definition of tragedy ends with “the catharsis of such feelings” (1449b27–28; see [[katharsis]]). The poet should feel what he writes, for those in the grip of feeling are the most convincing (1455a30–32), and thought includes the rousing of feelings (1456a38).

The tragedy of suffering is one of the four kinds, like the *Ajaxes* and the *Ixions* (1455b34–1456a1; see [[four-kinds-of-tragedy]]); epic can be “of suffering” too, and the Iliad is “simple and full of suffering” (1459b9–15).
