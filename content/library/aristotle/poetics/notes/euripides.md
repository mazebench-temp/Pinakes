Aristotle's verdict comes in chapter 13, after he has argued that the best plot ends in misfortune:

{{quote:bdd5e54f-2d6e-59fb-bfd0-b0df980d86fe}}

His plays supply examples of every kind. He made Medea kill her children knowingly, as the old poets did (1453b27–29; see [[medea-kills-her-children]]); his *Cresphontes* and *Iphigenia among the Taurians* have the best kind of plot, the deed recognized in time (1454a4–7; see [[cresphontes-play]] and [[iphigenia-among-the-taurians]]), and the *Iphigenia* is twice praised for its recognitions (1452b5–8, 1455a16–19) and outlined in chapter 17 (1455b2–12). But Menelaus in the *Orestes* is baseness without necessity (1454a28–29, 1461b20–21; see [[orestes-play]]), Melanippe's speech is unseemly (1454a31; see [[melanippe-the-wise]]), Iphigenia at Aulis is inconsistent (1454a31–32; see [[iphigenia-at-aulis]]), the *Medea* ends by the machine (1454b1; see [[the-machine]]), and the use of Aegeus is irrational (1461b19–21; see [[aegeus]]). He made a part of the sack of Troy, not the whole (1456a16–17), and Euripides changed one word in a line of Aeschylus to make it beautiful (1458b19–24; see [[the-ulcer-that-eats-my-foot]]). The chorus should share in the action “not as in Euripides” (1456a27). Sophocles said that he made people as they ought to be, Euripides as they are (1460b33–35; see [[as-they-ought-to-be]]).
