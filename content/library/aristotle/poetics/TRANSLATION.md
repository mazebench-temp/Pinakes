# Poetics — translation conventions

The English is a new line-for-line translation by Claude (Anthropic), made from Rudolf Kassel's
Oxford text in `grc/`. Every English row carries the Bekker line of the Greek it renders, so
quotations, the index and the reader can address any passage in either language. Greek prose does not
break where English would, so English syntax often runs across a line break; when it does, the words
stay within the same one or two lines as the Greek, and names stay on the line where the Greek names
them. American spelling is used throughout.

## The text and its numbering

The Greek is R. Kassel, *Aristotelis De arte poetica liber* (Oxford Classical Texts, 1965), as
digitized by the Perseus Digital Library (CC BY-SA 4.0). The Poetics is cited by the page, column and
line of Immanuel Bekker's Berlin edition of Aristotle (1831): it runs from 1447a8 to 1462b19. Kassel
prints every fifth Bekker line in his margin, and so does the digitized text. The lines between are
divided here in proportion to their length, with two further anchors: each chapter begins on the line
where editions place it (chapter 6 at 1449b21, chapter 9 at 1451a36), and each column ends where the
length of its text puts it (both columns of every page come out the same length, which they should).
The every-fifth lines are Kassel's; a line between them may begin a word or two earlier or later than
in Bekker's own print.

Kassel prints, as his lines 1450b10a, 1455a14a–b and 1457a33a, words that Bekker did not have (most
come from the manuscript in Florence, Riccardianus 46). They are kept within the Bekker line they fall
in. The chapter divisions are those of the Renaissance editions, used by every modern text.

### Editorial signs

| Sign | Meaning | In the English |
|---|---|---|
| ⟨ ⟩ | words Kassel adds to the manuscripts | translated without comment |
| [ ] | words Kassel deletes as not Aristotle's | translated in square brackets where they carry content |
| [[ ]] | a later addition inside the text (the list of plays from the Little Iliad, 1459b5–7) | likewise |
| † † | a corrupt passage Kassel cannot mend | translated as literally as possible between daggers, or marked †…† |
| … | a gap in the text | marked … |

## Quotations

Aristotle quotes Homer from memory or from texts that differ from ours. Where a quotation agrees with
our Iliad, the English keeps the wording of the Iliad translation (“Sing, goddess, the wrath”, 1456b17 =
Il. 1.1; “Truly Odysseus has done countless good deeds”, 1457b11 = Il. 2.272; “mix the wine stronger”,
1461a15 = Il. 9.203; “the greater part of the night has gone”, 1461a26 = Il. 10.252). Where it differs,
the English follows Aristotle: “All the gods and men slept all night long” (1461a16–17), where our
Iliad has “the other gods” (2.1); “and we grant him to win glory” (1461a22–23), words that our text of
2.15 does not have. The index gives each quotation its source and its context.

## Names

Names have their Latin forms, as in the Iliad (Aeschylus, Oedipus, Clytemnestra). Ἀλκμέων is
“Alcmaeon”. Plays are named in English when the title is a description (the Libation Bearers, Women of
Phthia, Daughters of Phorcys, Odysseus Wounded, the Judgment of Arms) and transliterated when it is a
name (the Phinidae, the Cyprians). When Aristotle names a play after its hero, the English adds the
article: “in the Oedipus”, “in the Antigone”. Ἰλίου πέρσις is “the Sack of Troy”.

## Places where the Greek is disputed

- **1447a28.** Kassel deletes ἐποποιία, “epic”: the art that uses bare speech or meters has no name, and
  epic would be a name. The English keeps it in brackets.
- **1448a15.** †γᾶς† Κύκλωπας: the word before “Cyclopes” is corrupt. Proposals include a name (Argas,
  a poet of nomes) and Πέρσας, “Persians” (the subject of a famous nome by Timotheus).
- **1449b6.** Kassel deletes the names of Epicharmus and Phormis as a gloss on “the making of plots”.
- **1450a12–13.** †οὐκ ὀλίγοι αὐτῶν†, †ὄψις ἔχει πᾶν†: the sense is that nearly all poets use all six
  parts, but the words are corrupt.
- **1450b9–10.** The words Kassel brackets are a doublet of the definition that follows; his line 10a
  carries the version he keeps.
- **1454b14.** †…οἷον τὸν Ἀχιλλέα ἀγαθὸν καὶ Ὅμηρος†: the manuscripts read ἀγαθόν, “good”, and the
  translation renders it. Many editors read Ἀγάθων, “Agathon”: “as Agathon and Homer portrayed Achilles”.
- **1455a14.** The account of Odysseus the False Messenger is the most damaged passage of the treatise;
  Kassel's lines 14a and 14b come from the Riccardianus.
- **1456a2.** †οης†: the fourth kind of tragedy is lost in the corruption. The likeliest restoration is
  ὄψις, “spectacle”, which the examples (the Daughters of Phorcys, the Prometheus, plays in Hades) fit.
- **1459b5–7.** The list of tragedies made from the Little Iliad is bracketed as a later addition;
  “more than” and “the Sinon and the Trojan Women” are bracketed again within it.
- **1461b8–9.** †εἰκός ἐστιν† at the end of the Icarius problem is corrupt.
- **1462b19.** The treatise breaks off: the second book, on comedy, which Aristotle promises at 1449b21,
  is lost.
