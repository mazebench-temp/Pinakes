## The poet

The Greeks told many stories about Homer: that he was blind, that he came from Smyrna or Chios (seven cities claimed him), that he lost a riddle contest with fishermen. The Suda's life of Homer records, among many parentages, that some made him the son of Telemachus and of Nestor's daughter Polycaste, and, among many birthplaces, Ithaca (Suda ο 251). None of this has any historical value. The Iliad and the Odyssey were probably composed in Ionia in the eighth or early seventh century BCE, at the end of a long tradition of oral poetry. Whether one poet made both, and how writing entered the process, is the "Homeric Question", debated since F. A. Wolf's *Prolegomena* (1795).

## The poem's voice

The poet speaks in his own voice only through the Muse: "Sing, goddess" (1.1), and in the Odyssey "Tell me, Muse, of the man" (*Odyssey* 1.1). He names no author, gives no date, and addresses no patron. The Odyssey shows singers at work in the houses of the great, Phemius among the suitors on Ithaca and Demodocus among the Phaeacians, singing the deeds of men and gods to the lyre (*Odyssey* 1.153–155, 1.337–338, 8.43–45; see [[phemius]]). See [[muses]], [[proem]] and [[in-medias-res]].

## Oral poetry

The recurring epithets and formulas that fill the poems ("swift-footed Achilles", "rosy-fingered Dawn", "in answer to him said…") were explained by Milman Parry in the 1920s and 1930s as the tools of oral composition. They are ready-made phrases that fit the hexameter and let a singer compose in performance. See [[winged-words]] and the index of [[swift-footed|epithets]].
