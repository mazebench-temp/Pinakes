## Life and work

Empedocles was a democrat of Acragas, a physician and a wonder-worker, of whom many legends were told, the most famous that he leapt into the crater of Etna to make men think he had become a god. He wrote two hexameter poems, known as *On Nature* and *Purifications*, of which several hundred lines survive in quotations and on a papyrus from Strasbourg. Aristotle, in his lost dialogue *On Poets*, called him “Homeric and skilled in diction, a maker of metaphors” (Diogenes Laertius 8.57; see [[on-poets]]).

## In the Poetics

The first mention denies that he is a poet at all: people call writers of verse on medicine or nature poets, “yet there is nothing in common between Homer and Empedocles except the meter: so it is right to call the one a poet, and the other a natural philosopher rather than a poet” (1447b16–20; see [[poietes]]). But in the chapters on diction Aristotle quotes his lines as poetry: two metaphors from species to species, “with the bronze drawing off his life” and “cutting with the long-edged bronze” (1457b13–16; see [[drawing-off-life-with-bronze]]); old age as “the sunset of life” (1457b24–25; see [[the-evening-of-life]]); a shortened word, ὄψ, in “one sight comes from both” (1458a5; see [[one-sight-from-both]]); and a problem solved by punctuation, “at once things grew mortal that before had learned to be immortal” (1461a23–25; see [[empedocles-immortal-and-mortal]]).
