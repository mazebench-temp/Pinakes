summary: Roman poet (65–8 BCE) whose Ars Poetica praised Homer for rushing his listener "into the middle of things", and who twice turned the opening lines of the Odyssey into Latin.
---

See [[in-medias-res]]. In the *Epistles* (1.2) Horace also reads the Iliad as a moral lesson on the follies of kings: "whatever madness the kings commit, the Achaeans pay for it" (quidquid delirant reges plectuntur Achivi, 1.2.14). That line is a fair summary of the Iliad's first book.

The Odyssey is the other half of the same letter. Ulysses, who saw the cities and the ways of many men and endured hardships on the wide sea while winning a return for himself and his companions, is the example of what virtue and wisdom can do, and the suitors of Penelope are idlers like ourselves, “born to consume the fruits of the earth” (*Epistles* 1.2.17–31). In the *Satires* Tiresias, teaching Ulysses to hunt for old men's wills, explains Penelope's chastity by the meanness of her suitors, who came sparing of gifts and keener on the kitchen than on love (*Satires* 2.5.75–83; see [[horace-on-the-suitors]]). In the *Ars Poetica* the first lines of the Odyssey, *Dic mihi, Musa, virum*, are the model of a modest beginning, set against the cyclic poet's boast (*Ars Poetica* 136–152). See [[horace-on-the-opening-of-the-odyssey]].

Rome, Horace says, counts its poets “from the age of the writer Livius”, and he remembered the poems of Livius that his schoolmaster Orbilius dictated to him as a boy (*Epistles* 2.1.60–62, 2.1.69–72). Livius Andronicus' Latin Odyssey began *Virum mihi, Camena, insece versutum*, but Horace does not say which of his poems he was taught (see [[odusia]]).
