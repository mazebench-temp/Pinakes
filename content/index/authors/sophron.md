Sophron's mimes, written in rhythmic Doric prose, were divided into “men's mimes” and “women's mimes”; titles like *The Women Who Say They Will Drive Out the Goddess* and *The Tunny-fisher* survive with fragments. Plato is said to have brought them to Athens and kept them under his pillow (Diogenes Laertius 3.18). Aristotle names him with his son Xenarchus to show that there is no common name for imitations in prose: “the mimes of Sophron and Xenarchus and the Socratic dialogues” (1447b9–11; see [[mime]], [[xenarchus]] and [[socratic-dialogues]]).
