Plato's Socrates calls Homer "the first teacher and leader" of the tragedians (*Republic* 10.595b–c), and then banishes him from the ideal city. The *Republic* uses Iliad 1 four times:

- the prose retelling of Chryses' plea, to define narration: [[plato-chryses-diegesis]];
- the gods' unquenchable laughter: [[plato-homeric-laughter]];
- Hephaestus hurled by his father: [[plato-hephaestus-hurled]];
- Achilles' insults to Agamemnon: [[plato-achilles-insults]];
- the terrors of Hades, among them the soul of Patroclus leaving manhood and youth: [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]];
- the laments of gods and heroes, among them Zeus' grief for Sarpedon: [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]];
- Achilles' choice, in Socrates' defense at his trial and in Phaedrus' praise of love: [[plato-the-choice-of-achilles]];
- the anger “far sweeter than dripping honey”, a pain of the soul mixed with pleasure: [[plato-the-pleasure-of-anger]].

From the Odyssey's first book the *Republic* takes Telemachus' saying that men praise most the newest song, and warns against reading it as praise of a new manner of song, since the modes of music are never changed without the laws of the city (*Republic* 4.424b–c, on *Odyssey* 1.351–352): [[plato-the-newest-song]]. The *Ion* names Phemius, “the rhapsode of Ithaca”, with Olympus, Thamyras and Orpheus (*Ion* 533b–c; see [[phemius]]).
