Plato's Socrates calls Homer "the first teacher and leader" of the tragedians (*Republic* 10.595b–c), and then banishes him from the ideal city. The *Republic* uses Iliad 1 four times:

- the prose retelling of Chryses' plea, to define narration: [[plato-chryses-diegesis]];
- the gods' unquenchable laughter: [[plato-homeric-laughter]];
- Hephaestus hurled by his father: [[plato-hephaestus-hurled]];
- Achilles' insults to Agamemnon: [[plato-achilles-insults]];
- the terrors of Hades, among them the soul of Patroclus leaving manhood and youth: [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]];
- the laments of gods and heroes, among them Zeus' grief for Sarpedon: [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]];
- Achilles' choice, in Socrates' defense at his trial and in Phaedrus' praise of love: [[plato-the-choice-of-achilles]];
- the anger “far sweeter than dripping honey”, a pain of the soul mixed with pleasure: [[plato-the-pleasure-of-anger]].

From the Odyssey's first book the *Republic* takes Telemachus' saying that men praise most the newest song, and warns against reading it as praise of a new manner of song, since the modes of music are never changed without the laws of the city (*Republic* 4.424b–c, on *Odyssey* 1.351–352): [[plato-the-newest-song]]. The *Ion* names Phemius, “the rhapsode of Ithaca”, with Olympus, Thamyras and Orpheus (*Ion* 533b–c; see [[phemius]]).

In the *Phaedo* the soul that philosophy has set free will not bind itself again to pleasures and pains, doing “the endless work of a Penelope handling her web the opposite way” (*Phaedo* 84a; see [[penelopes-web-in-later-writers]]). The *Republic* would have no poet tell of gods who take on all sorts of forms, “in the likeness of strangers from abroad” (*Republic* 2.381d, quoting *Odyssey* 17.485–486), and the *Laws* quotes the counsel that Athena, in the likeness of Mentor, gives Telemachus at Pylos (*Laws* 7.803e–804a, quoting *Odyssey* 3.26–28): [[athena-as-mentor-in-later-writers]]. Nestor is his type of the wise old speaker. The *Phaedrus* speaks of the handbooks of speech that Nestor and Odysseus wrote in their leisure at Troy, the *Laws* of Nestor's strength in speech and greater self-control, and in the *Hippias Major* Hippias' showpiece has Nestor advise the young Neoptolemus after the fall of Troy (*Phaedrus* 261b–c; *Laws* 4.711d–e; *Hippias Major* 286a–b): [[platos-nestor]]. The *Alcibiades I* calls the Odyssey and the Iliad poems about a difference over the just and the unjust, which brought the battles and deaths at Troy and those of the suitors of Penelope and Odysseus (*Alcibiades I* 112a–b; see [[the-suitors]]). The critic Longinus, as Proclus reports, found a phrase of the *Timaeus* as strange as Homer's “the sacred strength of Telemachus” (*Timaeus* 19e; see [[longinus-on-the-sacred-strength-of-telemachus]]).

The Proteus of *Odyssey* 4 is Socrates' image of a speaker who slips away from the question: the brothers of the *Euthydemus* imitate “Proteus the Egyptian sophist”, Euthyphro is not to be let go, like Proteus, until he tells, and Ion, like Proteus, takes every shape until he escapes as a general; the *Republic* forbids the poets to tell lies about Proteus and Thetis (*Euthydemus* 288b–c; *Euthyphro* 15d; *Ion* 541e; *Republic* 2.381d): [[platos-proteus]]. The *Gorgias* sends the souls of the just to the Isles of the Blessed, with Rhadamanthys, whom the Odyssey places in the Elysian plain, among their judges (*Gorgias* 523a–526c): [[elysium-and-the-isles-of-the-blessed]].
