## The pattern

A dream in Homer comes to the sleeper from outside. It is a visitor, and the scene that tells its visit has fixed parts.

1. **The sleeper.** Someone lies asleep, usually at night: Agamemnon “sleeping in his hut, and ambrosial sleep was poured around him” (2.19).
2. **The station.** The figure “stood above his head” (στῆ δʼ ἄρʼ ὑπὲρ κεφαλῆς), the fixed half-line of the scene (2.20, 2.59; Iliad 23.68; Odyssey 4.803, 6.21).
3. **The likeness.** It comes in the shape of someone the sleeper knows and trusts: Nestor for Agamemnon (2.20–22), Penelope's sister Iphthime for Penelope (Odyssey 4.795–798), a friend of Nausicaa's for Nausicaa (Odyssey 6.22). The ghost of Patroclus comes as himself, like him in stature, eyes and voice (Iliad 23.65–67).
4. **The speech.** It opens by reproaching the sleeper for sleeping, εὕδεις, “you are asleep” (2.23; Iliad 23.69; Odyssey 4.804), and then gives its message.
5. **The departure and the waking.** It goes, and the sleeper wakes with the dream still present to him. The divine voice is poured around Agamemnon (2.41); Achilles springs up (Iliad 23.101); Penelope's heart is warmed, so clear was the dream that came to her in the dead of night (Odyssey 4.839–841).

The half-line occurs eight times in the two poems, always of someone who comes and stands over a person lying in bed. The same scene serves when a god comes to a sleeper in person. Athena stands above the head of Nausicaa (Odyssey 6.21), and above the head of Odysseus, who is lying awake, so that her reproach is turned around: why is he awake again? (Odyssey 20.32–33). Hermes stands above the head of the sleeping Priam in the Achaean camp, asks how he can go on sleeping among enemies, and wakes him to take him home (Iliad 24.682–684). And the old nurse Eurycleia stands above Penelope's head in the same words to wake her with the news that Odysseus is home (Odyssey 23.4).

E. R. Dodds took the pattern as the mark of the Homeric dream (*The Greeks and the Irrational*, 1951, ch. 4). The Greeks, he observed, spoke of seeing a dream, not of having one, and in Homer the dream-figure is an objective visitor: it comes, takes its stand, speaks and leaves. The sleeper does not make the message; he receives it. See also [[onar]] and [[dream]].

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates, much as he praises Homer, will not praise the sending of the dream by Zeus to Agamemnon, since a god does not deceive (*Republic* 2, 383a; see [[plato-lying-dream]]).

Herodotus tells of a dream that stood over a king and sent him to war. A tall and handsome man appeared to Xerxes in his sleep and forbade him to give up the invasion of Greece; the dream came back, and appeared to his uncle Artabanus as well (Herodotus 7.12–18).
