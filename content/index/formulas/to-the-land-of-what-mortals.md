## The pattern

The first verse is a cry and a question. ὤ μοι ἐγώ, “Ah me,” is the cry of a man alone (as in the storm, *Odyssey* 5.299, 5.465); τέων is the epic genitive plural of τίς, “of what (men)”; and αὖτε, “this time, again,” makes the arrival one of many. The other two verses set out the only alternatives as a double question, ἦ ῥα … ἦε: men who are ὑβρισταί, “arrogant” ([[hybris]]), ἄγριοι, “savage” ([[wild]]), and not δίκαιοι, “just” ([[themis]]), or men who are φιλόξεινοι, “kind to strangers,” with a νόος θεουδής, “a mind that fears the gods” ([[xenia]]). Merry takes the second half of the last verse as explaining the first: men who are kind to strangers are those who fear the wrath of Zeus, the god of strangers (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.121, comparing *Iliad* 13.625, where Menelaus reproaches the Trojans for not fearing it). θεουδής is “god-fearing,” not, as it was taken before Buttmann, a form of θεοειδής, “godlike” (Merry on 6.121; see the Odyssey translation's key words).

{{quote:6640e6be-5081-54fe-8f25-deb6178e4e78}}

The three verses are spoken twice, and the last two once more, always by Odysseus, and the answer each time decides what kind of land he has come to:

- **Scheria.** He wakes in the bushes at the cry of the girls at play, asks the question, and wonders whether the voices are nymphs' or men's (*Odyssey* 6.117–126). The answer comes from Nausicaa: the Phaeacians are very dear to the immortals, and “they are all from Zeus, / strangers and beggars” (*Odyssey* 6.201–208; [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]]).
- **The land of the Cyclopes.** He tells his companions that he will go with his own ship and find out who the men on the mainland are, whether arrogant, savage and unjust, or kind to strangers and god-fearing (*Odyssey* 9.172–176). The Cyclops gives the answer: “You are a fool, stranger, or you have come from far away,” for the Cyclopes care nothing for aegis-bearing Zeus nor for the blessed gods (*Odyssey* 9.273–276).
- **Ithaca.** He wakes in his own land and does not know it, for Athena has poured a mist about, so that everything looks strange to him, and he asks the same three verses (*Odyssey* 13.187–202). Athena answers with the Cyclops' verse, “You are a fool, stranger, or you have come from far away,” and tells him that the land is Ithaca (*Odyssey* 13.237–249).
