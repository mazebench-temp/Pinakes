## The pattern

κινήσας (“moving, shaking”) + κάρη (“head”), then προτὶ ὃν μυθήσατο θυμόν, “spoke to his own heart.” The verse is used four times, always of a god who sees a mortal's plight and speaks a soliloquy, and its first half, the gesture, is what sets it apart from the soliloquies of men, which have the verse of vexation, “and deeply troubled he spoke to his own proud heart” ([[deeply-troubled]]).

In the Iliad the speaker is Zeus, and his feeling is pity. He sees Hector putting on the armor of Achilles, “shook his head,” and pities the man who does not know that his death is near (*Iliad* 17.198–208); he sees the horses of Achilles weeping for Patroclus and asks why the gods gave immortal horses to a mortal (*Iliad* 17.441–455; [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Both speeches begin with ἆ δειλ-, “Ah, wretch” ([[wretch]]). In the Odyssey the speaker is Poseidon, and his feeling is anger: he sees Odysseus on his raft near the land of the Phaeacians and swears to give him his fill of misery (*Odyssey* 5.282–290), and when the raft is broken he bids him wander over the sea (*Odyssey* 5.375–379). The Odyssey has the gesture in another verse, of a man who will not answer: “in silence he shook his head, brooding evils” (ἀκέων κίνησε κάρη, κακὰ βυσσοδομεύων), said of Odysseus when Antinous strikes him with a footstool and when Melanthius taunts him, and of Telemachus when he sees his father struck (*Odyssey* 17.462–465, 17.489–491, 20.182–184).
