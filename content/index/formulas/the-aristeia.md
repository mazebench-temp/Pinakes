## The pattern

ἀριστεία, “excellence”, is formed from ἄριστος, “best” (see [[aristos]]), through the verb ἀριστεύω, “be the best, be foremost in the fight”. The noun is not Homeric; the verb is. Hippolochus sent his son Glaucus to Troy with the charge “always to be the best and to excel above the others” (αἰὲν ἀριστεύειν καὶ ὑπείροχον ἔμμεναι ἄλλων, 6.208), and Peleus gave Achilles the same charge in the same words (11.784).

The noun became the name of the episodes in which one hero dominates the fighting. In the titles by which the books of the Iliad were known in antiquity, Book 5 is Διομήδους ἀριστεία, “the aristeia of Diomedes”, Book 11 is the aristeia of Agamemnon, and Book 17 the aristeia of Menelaus. Herodotus quotes lines from what is now Book 6 (6.289–292) as standing “in the aristeia of Diomedes” (Herodotus 2.116): the episode had that name by the fifth century BC, and it ran on past the later book division (see [[title-of-book-5]]).

The parts recur, though no aristeia has them all. Tilman Krischer described the sequence (*Formale Konventionen der homerischen Epik*, 1971), and Bernard Fenik studied the battle scenes it is made of (*Typical Battle Scenes in the Iliad*, 1968):

1. **The shining start.** The hero arms, or a god arms him, and he blazes. Athena kindles fire from Diomedes' helmet and shield like the star of late summer (5.4–7). Agamemnon's bronze shines to the sky (11.44–46). Athena sets a tireless fire above Achilles' head in the words she used for Diomedes (ἀκάματον πῦρ, 18.225–227), and his shield and helmet shine like the moon and a star (19.374–383).
2. **The rout.** He breaks into the enemy ranks and kills man after man, often in pairs and in quick lists, and the enemy gives way before him.
3. **The wound.** He is hit, often by an archer: Diomedes by Pandarus (5.95–100) and later by Paris (11.369–378), Agamemnon by Coon's spear (11.248–253).
4. **Prayer and help.** He prays, and a god gives him new strength. Athena answers Diomedes, makes his limbs light, and takes the mist from his eyes (5.114–132).
5. **The great opponent.** He meets an enemy of rank, and the gods take part. Aphrodite and Apollo save Aeneas from Diomedes (5.297–346, 5.432–453); Patroclus kills Sarpedon, the son of Zeus (16.419–507); Achilles meets Aeneas, whom Poseidon saves, and Hector, whom Apollo saves (20.158–352, 20.419–454).
6. **Against the gods.** At the height of his success the hero goes past the limit set for men. Diomedes wounds Aphrodite and Ares. Three times Diomedes, Patroclus and Achilles rush on, and at the fourth a god stops them (5.436–442, 16.702–709, 16.784–789, 20.445–449; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]). Achilles fights the river Scamander (21.211–382).
7. **The end.** The aristeia ends in withdrawal or in death. Agamemnon, Diomedes and Odysseus leave the battle wounded in Book 11; Patroclus is struck by Apollo and killed by Hector (16.787–867); Achilles' great day ends with the death of Hector (Book 22).

Similes of fire, flood and storm, and above all of the lion, mark the hero's progress (5.87–92, 5.136–143, 5.161–164; 11.113–121, 11.155–159; 20.164–175, 20.490–494). The star of late summer that blazes from Diomedes' armor at the start of his day returns at the end of Achilles' career. Priam sees him coming across the plain like the star that rises at the end of summer (ὀπώρης), which men call the dog of Orion: the brightest of the stars, and an evil sign (22.26–31).
