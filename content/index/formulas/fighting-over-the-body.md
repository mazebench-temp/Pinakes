## The pattern

When a man falls, his body lies between the lines with his armor on it, and both sides want it. The enemy wants the armor as a prize and the body to shame (see [[stripping-the-dead]]); his friends want to save it and give it burial. The Iliad opens with the fear of what can become of the dead: the wrath made the heroes themselves “the spoil of dogs and of every bird” (1.4–5). The fight that follows has recurring parts:

1. **The attempt.** The killer, or another man, runs up to strip the body or to drag it off by the feet (ἐρύω, “drag”).
2. **The defense.** A friend stands over it. The verbs are ἀμφιβαίνω and περιβαίνω, “bestride, stand around”, and the defender holds his spear and his round shield in front of the body, ready to kill whoever comes, in two verses used for Aeneas over Pandarus and for Menelaus over Patroclus (5.300–301 = 17.7–8). A simile often gives him an animal's courage or devotion: a lion trusting in its courage (5.299), a cow standing over her first calf (17.4–5), a lion with its cubs (17.133–136).
3. **The price.** To stoop over a body is to open oneself to the spears. Elephenor is stabbed in the side his shield leaves bare as he drags Echepolus by the feet (4.463–469), and Leucus is hit in the groin as he drags Simoeisius away, and falls on the body (4.489–493).
4. **The outcome.** The attacker is driven back by a ring of spears, in a pair of verses used for Thoas and for Ajax (4.534–535 = 5.625–626), or the body is carried to the rear and given to the dead man's companions (4.506, 5.573–575).

A god can take part. Zeus has Apollo carry Sarpedon's body out of the missiles, wash and anoint it, and give it to Sleep and Death to take home to Lycia for burial (16.666–675). Apollo covers Hector's body with his aegis to keep it from harm while Achilles drags it (24.18–21).

The fight grows with the poem. Over Sarpedon the Lycians and the Myrmidons fight until he is hidden under missiles, blood and dust (16.532–683); over Cebriones, Hector holds the head and Patroclus the foot like two lions over a slain deer (16.756–764). The whole of Book 17 is a fight over the body of Patroclus. Hector strips the armor and would cut off the head and give the body to the dogs of Troy (17.125–127); Menelaus, the Ajaxes and Meriones fight their way back to the ships with it (17.717–761); and Achilles, unarmed, saves it at last with his shout from the trench, so that the Achaeans can draw it out of the missiles and lay it on a bier (18.203–233).
