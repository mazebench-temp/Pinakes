## The pattern

The sentence is an unreal condition in two parts. The first, usually opened by καί νύ κεν (“and now … would”) or ἔνθά κεν (“then … would”), says what was about to happen; the second, εἰ μή (“had not”), names who or what prevented it, and the story goes on from there. The second verse is often a whole-line formula, εἰ μὴ ἄρʼ ὀξὺ νόησε, “had not … been quick to see it”, followed by the name of the one who saw (see [[divine-rescue]]).

The first two in the poem are the turns of Books 2 and 3. The army would have gone home “beyond what was fated” (ὑπέρμορα), had not Hera spoken to Athena (2.155–156). Menelaus would have dragged Paris off by his helmet and won untold glory, had not Aphrodite been quick to see it (3.373–374). In both a god saves the plot of the poem, and in both the thing prevented is one the audience knows cannot happen: the war does not end in Book 2, and Paris lives to kill Achilles.

What is prevented is most often a death or an ending: the fall of Troy before its time (16.698–701, 21.544–545), the death of a hero whom the tradition keeps alive (5.311–312, 7.104–108, 8.90–91, 20.288–291), the burning of the ships (8.217–218), a quarrel or a contest carried too far (23.490–491, 23.733–734). The one who prevents it is most often a god, but it can be a man: Odysseus calling to Diomedes (11.310–312), Polydamas advising Hector (13.723–725), Antilochus claiming his prize (23.540–541). The narrator lets his listeners see the other story for a moment, the one the tradition did not tell, and then keeps to his own.

Irene de Jong called these “if not” situations and studied them as a device of the narrator (*Narrators and Focalizers: The Presentation of the Story in the Iliad*, 1987); Heinz-Günther Nesselrath traced such almost-episodes through ancient epic (*Ungeschehenes Geschehen*, 1992). James V. Morrison read them as the poet's way of showing where his story could have gone and did not (“Alternatives to the Epic Tradition”, *Transactions of the American Philological Association* 122, 1992).
