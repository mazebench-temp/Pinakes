## The pattern

Three meetings of the Iliad are built the same way: Diomedes and Glaucus (6.119–236), Achilles and Aeneas (20.158–352), Achilles and Asteropaeus (21.139–204).

1. **The meeting.** Two men step out “between the two armies, eager to fight” (ἐς μέσον ἀμφοτέρων συνίτην μεμαῶτε μάχεσθαι, 6.120 = 20.159). They come together, in the whole-line formula “and when they had come close, advancing against each other”, and one speaks first (τὸν πρότερος προσέειπε, 6.121–122, 20.176–177, 21.148–149; see [[when-they-came-close]]).
2. **The challenge.** The first speaker asks who the other is, or mocks what he knows of him: “Who are you, good sir, among mortal men?” (6.123), “Who are you, and from where, that you have dared to come against me?” (21.150). A threat follows: “It is the sons of unhappy men who meet my might” (6.127 = 21.151). Achilles does not ask Aeneas his name. He taunts him with Priam's throne, which he will never win (20.178–198).
3. **The answer.** The other questions the question: “why do you ask about my lineage?” (τί ἢ γενεὴν ἐρεείνεις, 6.145 = 21.153); Aeneas says that the two of them know each other's lineage and parents already, from the report of men (20.203–204). Then he offers it anyway, in a couplet that Glaucus and Aeneas share: “But if you wish to learn this too, so that you may know well our lineage, and many men know it” (6.150–151 = 20.213–214). The genealogy runs down from its source, a god, a hero or a river: Aeolus, Sisyphus and Bellerophon for Glaucus (6.152–206), Zeus and Dardanus for Aeneas (20.215–240), the river Axius and Pelegon for Asteropaeus (21.157–160).
4. **The close.** “This is the lineage and the blood I claim” (ταύτης τοι γενεῆς τε καὶ αἵματος εὔχομαι εἶναι, 6.211 = 20.241). εὔχομαι is to declare a thing solemnly: to a god it is a prayer, of oneself a claim (see [[euchomai]]).
5. **The outcome.** The fight follows. Aeneas ends by calling for spears instead of words (20.244–258), and Asteropaeus with “now let us fight” (21.160). Glaucus' genealogy alone ends in friendship, for Diomedes finds in it a guest-friend of his grandfather's (6.212–236; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]] and [[xenia]]).

Lineage is a warrior's title to be heard and feared. Diomedes, the youngest of the leaders, claims the right to speak in council from his father Tydeus: no one could say that he was of base or cowardly birth (14.110–127). Tlepolemus, a son of Heracles, tells Sarpedon that men lie when they call him a son of Zeus (5.633–646), and Idomeneus, over a man he has killed, tells Deiphobus to come and see what kind of offspring of Zeus has come to Troy: Zeus fathered Minos, Minos Deucalion, and Deucalion him (13.448–454). When Achilles has killed Asteropaeus, he answers the genealogy the dead man gave him: a river's son cannot contend with the sons of Zeus, and Achilles is a grandson of Aeacus, the son of Zeus (21.184–199).

The long genealogies also keep the memory of the older generations. Glaucus' carries the story of Bellerophon (see [[bellerophon]]), and Aeneas' the history of the Dardanian house, from Dardanus through Tros and the horses of Erichthonius to Priam and himself (see [[dardanus]] and [[tros]]).
