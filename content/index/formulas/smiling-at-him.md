## The pattern

ἐπιμειδήσας, aorist participle of ἐπιμειδιάω, “smile at” (ἐπί, “at, upon”, + μειδιάω, “smile”), takes the place of ἀπαμειβόμενος in the reply formula (see [[answering-him]]), like the participles of anger, trouble and grief: [[looking-darkly]], [[deeply-troubled]], [[groaning-heavily]]. The speaker smiles at the one he answers, and the smile tells how the words that follow are meant.

Its first use is in Agamemnon's review. He has rebuked Menestheus and Odysseus for standing back, and Odysseus has answered him “looking at him darkly”: what a word has escaped the barrier of his teeth, and he will see the father of Telemachus among the champions (4.349–355). Then:

The smile answers the glare. It comes “when he saw that he was angry”, and the king takes his word back (πάλιν … λάζετο μῦθον), calls Odysseus by his full titles, and promises to make it good later (see [[the-review]] and [[odysseus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same opening brings in Zeus' answer when Athena asks leave to help the Achaeans with counsel (8.38), and Odysseus' answer to the captured Dolon, whose life he has just seemed to promise (10.400). The smile is always that of the stronger speaker; it can reassure, as Agamemnon's and Zeus' do, or it can be the smile of a man who already knows how the scene will end.
