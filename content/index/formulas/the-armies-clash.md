## The pattern

{{quote:20721753-adff-5b3f-84b5-52cc3ce37f4e}}

The six verses move from the mass to the sound and then to the blood. The armies come “into one place”; shields, spears and the μένεα, “the fury”, of men are thrown together, the repeated σύν (“together”) sounding at the head of two clauses (σύν ῥʼ ἔβαλον … σὺν δʼ ἔγχεα); the bossed shields press against one another (see [[shield]] and [[bossed]]); and a great din rises. Then the verses pair opposites: οἰμωγή, “cries of pain”, and εὐχωλή, “cries of triumph” (the word of boasting and of prayer; see [[prayer]]), from men ὀλλύντων τε καὶ ὀλλυμένων, “killing and being killed”, the active and passive of one verb (see [[polyptoton]]). The last half-verse is the earth running with blood.

No one is named. The passage shows the whole battle before the poem turns to single men, and it is followed by a simile of sound, two torrents meeting in a ravine, whose roar a shepherd hears far off in the mountains (4.452–456; see [[torrents-meeting]]). Then comes the first killing (4.457; see [[androktasia]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same six verses begin the fighting of the next day of battle in Book 8, where they are followed not by a simile but by Zeus weighing the fates of the two armies in his golden scales at midday (8.60–65, 8.66–74).
