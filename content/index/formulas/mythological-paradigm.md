## The pattern

In a paradigm (παράδειγμα, “example”) a speaker tells a story from the past, his own or a famous one, to show the listener what to do. The story is shaped to fit the present case, and it is often framed by the exhortation it supports ([[ring-composition]]). M. M. Willcock (“Mythological Paradeigma in the Iliad,” *Classical Quarterly* 1964) argued that the poet adapts such stories to their moment, and at times invents them.

## In Book 1

**Nestor.** To persuade Achilles and Agamemnon to listen to him, Nestor tells how the greatest heroes of the past listened to him ([[catalogue-of-lapiths]], [[nestor-and-the-centaurs]]). The logic runs from the greater to the lesser: if they obeyed, so should you.

{{quote:df86957a-826b-5d1d-9e84-52c77f03902f}}

The example fails. Agamemnon agrees in words and refuses in substance, and Achilles breaks in on him ({{ref:Iliad 1.285-303}}).

**Achilles and Thetis.** To show that Zeus owes his mother a favor, Achilles recalls her boast that she alone saved Zeus when the other Olympians wished to bind him, by summoning the Hundred-Hander to sit beside him ([[binding-of-zeus]], [[hundred-handed]]).

{{quote:1cb90b7f-276f-5b07-9054-f481d9240c99}}

The story is told nowhere else. Willcock suggested that it may have been invented for this context. Others, notably Laura Slatkin (*The Power of Thetis*, 1991), have seen in it a trace of a tradition in which Thetis held real cosmic power. Either way it is fitted to its purpose: the goddess who now needs Zeus' help once gave him hers.

**Hephaestus.** To persuade his mother to yield, Hephaestus tells how Zeus once threw him from heaven when he tried to defend her ([[fall-of-hephaestus]]).

{{quote:3afcccd5-835c-5ec4-a51d-ff600fc78bc4}}

This example works. Hera smiles and takes the cup, and the quarrel dissolves in laughter ([[unquenchable-laughter]]). The three stories make a progression: the human example fails, the divine claim is honored, and the comic example ends the quarrel of the gods.
