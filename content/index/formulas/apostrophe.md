## The pattern

ἀποστροφή, “turning away”, is the rhetoricians' term for a speaker who turns from his audience to address someone else. In Homer the narrator does it in two ways.

**To a character.** In the middle of the story he speaks to one of his characters as “you”. Menelaus is addressed seven times (4.127, 4.146, 7.104, 13.603, 17.679, 17.702, 23.600), and Patroclus eight times, all in Book 16, from “groaning heavily you answered him, horseman Patroclus” (16.20; see [[groaning-heavily]]) to the moment of his death (16.787, 16.812, 16.843). Achilles, Melanippus and Apollo are addressed once or twice each (20.2, 15.582, 15.365, 20.152). In the Odyssey the swineherd Eumaeus is addressed many times.

**To the listener.** The narrator speaks to his hearer as “you”, usually with a potential optative: “you would not have seen” (4.223), “you would not have said” (4.429), as for the son of Tydeus, “you could not have told which side he was on” (5.85). A related device imagines an onlooker in the third person: a man who came into the fighting unhurt, led by Athena, “could make light of it” no longer (4.539–542).

## Why

The addresses to characters have been explained in two ways. The vocatives Μενέλαε, Πατρόκλεες and Εὔμαιε fit the verse in places where the nominative would not, and the formulas were built on them. Or the address marks the narrator's sympathy: the three men so addressed are gentle, loyal and vulnerable, and Patroclus is addressed most as he nears his death. Adam Parry argued for the second view (“Language and Characterization in Homer”, *Harvard Studies in Classical Philology* 76, 1972), and Elizabeth Block followed the figure from Homer to Virgil (“The Narrator Speaks: Apostrophe in Homer and Vergil”, *Transactions of the American Philological Association* 112, 1982). The addresses to the listener draw him into the scene as a witness; Irene de Jong studied them with the narrator's other ways of seeing (*Narrators and Focalizers*, 1987).

## Outside Homer

Virgil turns to his characters at moments of pity: to Nisus and Euryalus as they die together, “Fortunate both!” (*Aeneid* 9.446), to Pallas, “O grief and great glory to your father” (10.507), and to Lausus (10.791–793).
