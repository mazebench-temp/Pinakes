## The pattern

νύκτα διʼ ὀρφναίην (or ἀμβροσίην), ὅτε θʼ εὕδουσι βροτοὶ ἄλλοι: “through the dark (or ambrosial) night, when other mortals are asleep”. The first half of the verse names the night with one of its epithets (see [[dark]] and [[ambrosial]]), and the second half sets the one who is abroad against everyone else, asleep. The verse ends a question about his errand, “why are you coming like this …?” or “where are you driving …?”, and the one who asks has him at his mercy, or seems to.

It is used three times. Nestor, raised on his elbow in his bed, challenges the unknown visitor at his hut (10.82–85; the visitor is Agamemnon). Odysseus puts the question to the captured Dolon (10.385–386). And Hermes, in the likeness of a young prince, asks Priam where he is driving his horses and mules “through the ambrosial night, when other mortals are asleep” (24.362–363), when Priam and his herald, terrified, have thought of flight or of supplication (24.354–360).

Homer opens three nights with the same contrast. The others, gods and men or the chiefs of the Achaeans, slept all night long, but sleep did not hold Zeus, or Agamemnon, or Hermes, each of them pondering what to do (2.1–4, 10.1–4, 24.677–681; see [[hypnos]]). Two of those nights are the nights of this question.
