## The pattern

οἳ δʼ ὅτε δὴ σχεδὸν ἦσαν (“and when they were close”) + ἐπʼ ἀλλήλοισιν ἰόντες (“going against each other”). The verse marks the moment before the first blow, and what follows is a speech or a throw: the one who strikes or speaks first is often named πρότερος, “first”.

It first brings the two armies together in Book 3, where Paris steps out to challenge the best of the Achaeans (3.15). Book 5 uses it three times, each time for a duel:

- Phegeus and Idaeus, the sons of Dares, charge Diomedes, and “Phegeus was the first to let fly his long-shadowed spear” (5.14–15).
- Sarpedon and Tlepolemus, the son and the grandson of Zeus, meet, and Tlepolemus speaks first (5.630–632; see [[tlepolemus-and-sarpedon]]).
- Ares and Diomedes, with Athena in his chariot, meet, and “Ares lunged first” (5.850–851).

It returns for Agamemnon and Iphidamas (11.232), Menelaus and Peisander (13.604), Patroclus and Sarpedon (16.462), Achilles and Aeneas (20.176), Achilles and Asteropaeus (21.148), and Achilles and Hector (22.248); a variant opens the armed fight of Diomedes and Ajax at the funeral games (23.816). A god, a man with a god's help, or the greatest of mortals: the line is the same for each.
