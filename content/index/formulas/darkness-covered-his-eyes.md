## The pattern

τὸν δέ (“and him”) + σκότος (“darkness”) + ὄσσε (“the two eyes”, a dual) + κάλυψε (“covered”, from καλύπτω). The phrase fills the second half of the verse after the caesura, and it comes where the blow has done its work: the weapon goes in, the verse tells where it went or what it broke, and darkness covers the eyes. A formula of falling often follows in the next verse (see [[fell-with-a-thud]]).

Death is told as the end of sight. To live is to “see the light of the sun” (ὁρᾷ φάος ἠελίοιο, 18.61, 18.442; see [[while-i-live]]), and the dead go into the dark. Night can take the place of darkness: “murky night covered his eyes” (τὸν δὲ κατʼ ὀφθαλμῶν ἐρεβεννὴ νὺξ ἐκάλυψε), of Tlepolemus killed by Sarpedon (5.659). The same image is used of fainting. When Andromache sees Hector's body dragged behind Achilles' chariot, dark night covers her eyes and she falls back (22.466), and when Hector is struck by a stone, black night covers his eyes and he faints (14.438–439). Other formulas put death in the eyes too: “his eyes were seized by purple death and mighty fate” (5.82–83; see [[purple-death]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The whole blow of the first killing of the poem, three verses from the ridge of the helmet to the darkness, is used again when Ajax kills the Thracian leader Acamas at the beginning of Book 6 (6.9–11 ~ 4.459–461).
