## The pattern

Each contest of the games is framed by the same steps, and most of them are told in verses that recur.

1. **The prizes.** Achilles brings out the prizes and names them: “And at once the son of Peleus set out other prizes” (Πηλεΐδης δʼ αἶψʼ ἄλλα … ἄεθλα, 23.700, 23.740), or “Then the son of Peleus brought a long-shadowed spear … and set it down in the gathering” (αὐτὰρ Πηλεΐδης κατὰ μὲν δολιχόσκιον ἔγχος … θῆκʼ ἐς ἀγῶνα φέρων, 23.798–799, 23.884–886; [[long-shadowed]]). There is a prize for the loser too, or for each place in the chariot race (23.262–270).
2. **The call.** “And he stood upright and spoke among the Argives” (στῆ δʼ ὀρθὸς καὶ μῦθον ἐν Ἀργείοισιν ἔειπεν) introduces the call six times (23.271, 23.657, 23.706, 23.752, 23.801, 23.830), and once Idomeneus' report from his lookout (23.456). The call may open with “Son of Atreus, and you other well-greaved Achaeans” (23.272, 23.658; [[well-greaved]]) and “for these we bid two men, the very best” (23.659, 23.802), or be a single verse: “Rise up, you who will make trial of this prize too” (23.707, 23.753, 23.831).
3. **The rising.** “So he spoke, and then … rose” (ὣς ἔφατʼ, ὦρτο δʼ ἔπειτα, 23.708, 23.811, 23.836, 23.859) or “So he spoke, and at once … rose” (23.664, 23.754), and each contestant is named with his epithets in a verse that begins ἂν δʼ, “and up rose” (23.709, 23.755, 23.812, 23.837–838, 23.860, 23.887–888; [[so-he-spoke]]).
4. **The contest.** The boxers and the wrestlers gird themselves and go “into the middle of the gathering” (ζωσαμένω … ἐς μέσσον ἀγῶνα, 23.685, 23.710). The contest itself is told afresh each time, with its similes and the help of the gods ([[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]); twice the verse of the onlookers follows its decisive moment, Odysseus' throw in the wrestling and the fall of the dove in the archery: “and the people gazed and were amazed” (λαοὶ δʼ αὖ θηεῦντό τε θάμβησάν τε, 23.728, 23.881).
5. **The prize.** “So saying, he put it in his hands, and he received it with joy” (23.624, 23.797; the shorter verse of 23.565). When neither can win, Achilles or the Achaeans bid the two “take equal prizes” (ἀέθλια … ἶσʼ ἀνελόντες, ἀνελέσθαι, 23.736, 23.823).

The frame is followed most closely in the boxing, the wrestling, the footrace, the armed duel and the weight throw. The chariot race, the first and longest contest, expands it with Nestor's advice, the lots and the lookout at the turning post (23.287–361; [[turning-post]]); the archery and the javelin shorten it, and in the javelin Achilles gives the prize to Agamemnon before anyone throws (23.884–897).
