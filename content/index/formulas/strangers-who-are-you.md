## The pattern

The question has four verses. The first asks who the strangers are and where they sail from, with the sea as a road, πόθεν πλεῖθʼ ὑγρὰ κέλευθα, “From where do you sail the watery ways?” ([[watery-ways]]). The second asks why: ἤ τι κατὰ πρῆξιν ἦ μαψιδίως ἀλάλησθε, “Is it on some business, or do you wander at random,” where κατὰ πρῆξιν is a trading voyage and μαψιδίως “idly,” without a purpose ([[prexis]]; see the translation's key words). The third and fourth say what such wanderers are: οἷά τε ληιστῆρες ὑπεὶρ ἅλα, τοί τʼ ἀλόωνται / ψυχὰς παρθέμενοι κακὸν ἀλλοδαποῖσι φέροντες, “like pirates, who wander / staking their lives and bringing evil to foreigners” ([[piracy]]). Merry takes κατὰ πρῆξιν of a trading enterprise, comparing the πρηκτῆρες, “traders,” of *Odyssey* 8.162, and joins οἷά τε ληιστῆρες to μαψιδίως ἀλάλησθε, “idly rove,” on which κατὰ πρῆξιν also depends by a kind of zeugma, since a voyage on business is not a roving (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.72, 3.73).

{{quote:590ed5d2-9825-54ff-a369-15c99a3e423a}}

The verses are spoken twice in the Odyssey. Nestor asks them of Telemachus and the goddess in the likeness of Mentor, when they have eaten at the feast on the shore, as is fitting (*Odyssey* 3.69–74), and Telemachus answers that his business is private, not public (*Odyssey* 3.82; see [[visit-scene]] and [[xenia]]). The Cyclops asks them of Odysseus and his companions in his cave, as soon as he sees them by the light of his fire, and Odysseus answers that they are Achaeans driven off their course from Troy (*Odyssey* 9.252–262; [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). The same words serve a host who has fed his guests and a giant who will eat them. Compare the question asked of a single stranger, [[who-are-you-among-men]].

## Outside Homer

In the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* the god, back on the Cretan sailors' ship in the likeness of a young man in his prime, asks them the same four verses (*Hymn to Apollo* 452–455), and their leader answers in the verse of [[tell-me-this-truly]] (*Hymn to Apollo* 467). Thucydides cites the old poets' question as evidence that piracy brought no shame in early Greece ([[thucydides-and-the-pirates]]).
