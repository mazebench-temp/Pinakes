## The pattern

ἔπεα, plural of ἔπος (“word”) + πτερόεντα (“winged, feathered,” from πτερόν, “feather, wing”) + προσηύδα (“addressed,” from προσαυδάω). The phrase fills the second half of the verse after the main caesura, and it usually completes a whole-line formula: καί μιν φωνήσας ἔπεα πτερόεντα προσηύδα, “and he spoke and addressed him with winged words.” It always introduces a speech and never closes one. It occurs more than a hundred times in the Iliad and the Odyssey together.

ἔπεα once began with a consonant, the digamma (ϝέπεα). That is why a short syllable before it can count as long, as it does after φωνήσας in the whole-line formula.

The image is disputed. πτερόεις also describes arrows, which are feathered (πτερόεντες ὀϊστοί), so the words may be pictured as fletched shafts that fly straight to their mark; or as birds, flying from the speaker's mouth to the listener. The Odyssey has a counterpart for a word that is received in silence: “her word was wingless” (ἄπτερος ἔπλετο μῦθος).

Whether the formula means anything in its context has been argued at length. For Milman Parry it was a fixed phrase chosen to fill the verse. Others have observed that it is not scattered at random: it tends to introduce speech that bursts out in response to a sight or a strong feeling (so P. Vivante, “On Homer's Winged Words,” *Classical Quarterly*, 1975).

## In Book 1

It occurs once:

The moment is the height of the book's first crisis. Achilles has begun to draw his sword; Athena has come up behind him and seized him by the hair; he turns and knows her. The speech that follows is abrupt and angry: “Why have you come now, child of aegis-bearing Zeus? Is it to see the arrogance of Agamemnon?” If the formula marks speech that comes on an impulse, it could not be better placed.

## Outside Homer

“Winged words” passed into many languages. In German, *geflügelte Worte* became the name for familiar quotations after Georg Büchmann's collection of that title (1864), which took the phrase from Johann Heinrich Voss's translation of Homer.
