## The pattern

λίσσεσθαι δέ μιν αὐτός, “and you yourself must beg him,” with a clause of purpose, ὅπως νημερτέα εἴπῃ or ἵνα νημερτὲς ἐνίσπῃ, “to tell you truly”; then ψεῦδος δʼ οὐκ ἐρέει, “he will not tell a lie,” and the reason, μάλα γὰρ πεπνυμένος ἐστί, “for he is very wise.” The infinitive gives the order, and αὐτός is emphatic: the young man must now act for himself and not depend on the one who sends him (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.19). The reason for the truth is the wisdom of the man who will tell it. Merry observes that in Homer wickedness and folly are one, and compares Clytemnestra, whose fall was long delayed “for her mind was good” (*Odyssey* 3.266; Merry on 3.20); he finds the same thought in Athena's delight at Peisistratus, “the wise and just man” (*Odyssey* 3.52; Merry on 3.52). See [[wise]] and [[pseudos]].

{{quote:2de5d582-0985-5d51-9e2a-2dae943df88e}}

The couplet is used twice, both times in Book 3 of the Odyssey, and it passes the young man from one wise king to the next. Athena, in the likeness of Mentor, says it of Nestor as Telemachus steps ashore at Pylos (*Odyssey* 3.19–20), and Nestor says it of Menelaus as he sends him on to Sparta (*Odyssey* 3.327–328), with ἵνα νημερτὲς ἐνίσπῃ for ὅπως νημερτέα εἴπῃ ([[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). In Sparta the truth comes from further off still: Menelaus tells what he heard from the old man of the sea, γέρων ἅλιος νημερτής, “the unerring old man of the sea” (*Odyssey* 4.349, 4.384). Telemachus asks for it in the words of other formulas: “tell me truly” (*Odyssey* 3.101) and “tell me the truth” (*Odyssey* 3.247; compare [[tell-me-this-truly]] and [[give-me-an-exact-account]]).
