## The pattern

Homer's verbs for it are ὀτρύνω (“urge on”), κελεύω (“order”), θαρσύνω (“encourage”) and, for its harsh form, νεικέω (“rebuke”). Its parts are few and recur:

1. **Address.** The men are called by their people's name, often with praise or blame: “Argives”, “horse-taming Trojans”, “friends”.
2. **Command.** Imperatives: fight, stand, do not give way, and the standing phrase for courage, θοῦρις ἀλκή, “furious courage”: “do not yet let go of your furious courage” (4.234). Hector's version is a whole line, “be men, friends, and remember your furious courage” (ἀνέρες ἔστε φίλοι, μνήσασθε δὲ θούριδος ἀλκῆς, 6.112), which Ajax also uses (15.734).
3. **Reasons.** Shame before others and before oneself (see [[aidos]]); what a man owes for the honor he receives; what the enemy will do, or is unable to do; the will of the gods; the reward and the penalty; and the example of fathers and of men of old (see [[mythological-paradigm]]).

The rebuke is the same speech turned round. It shames a man into fighting by naming what he is failing to be, and it is often unjust.

Gods exhort too, openly or in disguise. Apollo shouts to the Trojans from the citadel of Troy (4.507–513), and Poseidon, in the likeness of Calchas, goes round the Achaean ranks (13.43–124).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Sarpedon's speech to Glaucus gives the reason from honor its classic form: they are honored in Lycia with the best seats, the best meat and full cups, and so they must stand in the front line and face the blazing battle (12.310–321).

## Outside Homer

The exhortations of the Iliad have close kin in the war poems of Callinus and Tyrtaeus, the elegists of the seventh century BC: “How long will you lie idle? When will you have a brave heart, young men?” (Callinus, fragment 1 West; Tyrtaeus, fragments 10–12 West). Joachim Latacz compared the three, and argued from the exhortations that the Iliad's battles are fought by masses of men in close order, not by champions alone (*Kampfparänese, Kampfdarstellung und Kampfwirklichkeit in der Ilias, bei Kallinos und Tyrtaios*, 1977; see [[battle-order]]).
