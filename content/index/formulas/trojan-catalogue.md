## The pattern

Iris warns the Trojans that the Achaean army is coming across the plain, and tells Hector that the allies in the city speak many tongues, so each leader should command his own people (2.802–806). The Trojans and their allies are marshaled at the mound called Batieia (2.811–815), and the poet names them.

{{quote:6b37cb01-e030-5a84-a6e6-90706c2d6c42}}

Each entry names a people and its leaders, usually with a verb of leading: ἦρχε, ἡγεμόνευε, ἡγεῖτο, ἡγήσατο (“led”) or ἄγε, ἦγε (“brought”). Most begin with the people, as “The Dardanians were led by … Aeneas” (Δαρδανίων αὖτʼ ἦρχεν, 2.819), or with the leader, the people being his object, as “Pyraechmes led the Paeonians” (2.848). Only the three entries from the Troad begin as those of the Catalogue of Ships do, with the places in a relative clause: “Those who lived in Zeleia … were led by …” (οἳ δὲ … ἔναιον … τῶν … ἦρχε, 2.824–826; so also 2.828–830, 2.835–837). Short words of transition, αὖ, αὖτε, αὐτάρ (“in turn”, “and”), carry the list on. Five pairs of brothers lead a contingent or share in leading one: the sons of Antenor, of Merops, of Lethus, of Talaemenes and of Nomion.

There are no numbers. The Catalogue of Ships gives the ships of every contingent, more than a thousand in all; the Trojan Catalogue counts neither ships nor men. It is also much shorter, sixty-two lines against the two hundred and sixty-six of the Achaean list (see [[catalogue-of-ships]]). The two lists share some of their verses and half-verses. Hector's Trojans, like Agamemnon's men, are “by far the most and the best” (2.817–818, 2.577–578); Aeneas, like Polypoetes, is “not alone” (οὐκ οἶος, 2.822, 2.745); and an Antiphus and an Amphimachus lead on each side, in the same verse-frames (2.678 and 2.864; 2.620 and 2.870).

## Order

The list starts at Troy and moves outward. First come Hector's Trojans, Aeneas' Dardanians and the peoples of the Troad (2.816–843). Then it follows four lines out from Troy, like spokes from a hub:

- north-west across the Hellespont, into Thrace and on to the Axius: Thracians, Cicones, Paeonians (2.844–850);
- north-east, to the Paphlagonians on the coast of the Black Sea and the Halizones of Alybe, a place that has been located in several ways (2.851–857);
- inland: Mysians, Phrygians (2.858–863);
- south, down the Aegean coast of Asia Minor: Maeonians, Carians, Lycians (2.864–877).

Each line ends with a people from far away: the Paeonians (τηλόθεν, “from far away”, 2.849), the Halizones (τηλόθεν, 2.857), the Phrygians (τῆλʼ, “far off”, 2.863) and the Lycians (τηλόθεν, 2.877). The last verse of the catalogue, and of the book, names the farthest: “from far-off Lycia, from the eddying Xanthus” (2.877).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The allies' many tongues are heard again when the armies meet. The Trojans come on with a clamor like cranes, the Achaeans in silence (3.1–9). In the next battle the Trojans' war cry is like the bleating of ewes, for they had no one speech or language, but their tongues were mixed, men called from many lands (4.433–438).

Several of the allies named here matter later. Pandarus breaks the truce with his bow in Book 4. Sarpedon has a large part in Books 5, 12 and 16, and Glaucus meets Diomedes in Book 6. Rebuking Hector, Sarpedon says that he came from very far, for Lycia is far away, by the eddying Xanthus (τηλοῦ γὰρ Λυκίη Ξάνθῳ ἔπι δινήεντι, 5.478–479).

The catalogue and the later narrative do not always agree. Pylaemenes, the Paphlagonian leader, is killed by Menelaus (5.576–579), yet he follows the body of his son Harpalion, weeping, in Book 13 (13.643–659). Ennomus the augur is still among Hector's allies in Book 17 (17.218), and neither his death nor that of the Carian leader “in the river” (2.860–861, 2.874–875) is told when Achilles fights in the river in Book 21. (An Ennomus is killed by Odysseus at 11.422, but he is not called an augur.)

## Outside Homer

Demetrius of Scepsis, a scholar from the Troad, wrote a commentary on these sixty-odd lines in thirty books, and Strabo drew on it for his account of the Troad and its neighbors (see [[strabo-and-the-catalogue]]).

Where the list's knowledge comes from is disputed, as is the location of several of its peoples. It places no Greek settlement on the coast of Asia Minor, and its Miletus belongs to the Carians (2.868). Some have seen in this a memory of the time before the Greeks settled in Asia, others the poet's care to keep later cities out of the heroic age.
