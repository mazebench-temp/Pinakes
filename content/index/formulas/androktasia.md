## The pattern

ἀνδροκτασίη, “the slaying of men”, is Homer's own word, most often in the plural: Hector boasts, “I know battles well, and the killing of men” (μάχας τʼ ἀνδροκτασίας τε, 7.237), and Achilles tells Priam of the battles and slayings of men around his city (24.548). Modern scholars use it for the scene, repeated many times over in the Iliad's battles, in which one warrior kills another. Bernard Fenik set out its recurring parts (*Typical Battle Scenes in the Iliad*, 1968):

1. **Killer and victim.** The killer is named, often as “first” (πρῶτος), and then the man he kills.
2. **The victim.** His father and his home, and sometimes a few lines of his history: where he was born, what he left behind, who will not see him again. Many victims enter the poem only to die.
3. **The blow.** Throw or thrust, spear, sword or stone, and the exact place where the weapon went in and where it came out, told with a precision that has been studied as anatomy (W.-H. Friedrich, *Verwundung und Tod in der Ilias*, 1956).
4. **The fall and the death.** A formula of falling and a formula of death end the man: [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]], [[loosened-his-limbs]], [[fell-with-a-thud]], “his spirit left him”, he fell “in the dust”. A simile may mark the fall.
5. **The body.** The killer strips the dead man's armor, if he can, while the victim's companions fight to save the body. Stripping and rescue lead to new killings.
6. **The boast.** The killer may taunt his dead enemy, as Idomeneus taunts Othryoneus (13.374–382).

A single killing may use all these parts or only one or two, and killings are linked in chains: a spear thrown at one man kills another, and a friend avenges a friend.

## Meaning

The small biographies of the victims have been read as the poem's way of giving each death its weight. Jasper Griffin wrote of them as brief obituaries, which set the life a man leaves beside the moment he loses it (*Homer on Life and Death*, 1980).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod names Ἀνδροκτασίαι, “Slayings of Men”, among the children of Strife, with Battles, Fightings and Murders (*Theogony* 226–232; see [[strife]]).
