## The pattern

ᾤμωξεν (“groaned aloud”) + πεπλήγετο, the reduplicated aorist of πλήσσω (“strike”), with μηρώ, the dual of μηρός (“thigh”), and in the next verse χερσὶ καταπρηνέσσʼ, “with the flat of his hands,” and ὀλοφυρόμενος δʼ ἔπος ηὔδα, “and lamenting he spoke a word.” The gesture comes at the moment a man sees that things have gone against him, and a speech follows. Homer uses it five times.

In the Iliad Asius strikes his thighs when the Lapiths hold the gate of the Achaean wall against him, and calls Zeus “a lover of lies” (*Iliad* 12.162–166; see [[lover-of-lies]]). Ares strikes “both his sturdy thighs” when Hera tells him that his son Ascalaphus has been killed, and asks the gods not to be indignant if he goes to avenge him, even if he is struck by the thunderbolt of Zeus (*Iliad* 15.113–118; see [[ascalaphus]]). Patroclus strikes his in the two verses that Odysseus will use, when he sees the Trojans storm the wall (*Iliad* 15.397–398), and Achilles, with the participle μηρὼ πληξάμενος, when the fire takes the stern of Protesilaus' ship and he sends Patroclus out (*Iliad* 16.124–126). In the Odyssey the gesture is Odysseus', when he wakes on Ithaca and does not know it (*Odyssey* 13.197–199). See [[groaning-heavily]] and [[lament]].
