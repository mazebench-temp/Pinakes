## The pattern

σέβας, “awe,” the feeling that stops a man before something greater than himself ([[sebas]]), + μʼ ἔχει, “holds me,” + εἰσορόωντα, “as I look” (εἰσορόωσαν when a woman speaks). The translation keeps “awe” for the word in this verse; the Iliad's one σέβας is a reproach, “shame” (*Iliad* 18.178; see the translation's key words). Merry calls the feeling “amazement,” and in Nestor's use of the verse it is amazement at a resemblance, which the next lines trace (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.123).

It ends the verse five times, always in the first person. Two of the uses are about the same likeness. Nestor, hearing Telemachus speak, says “awe holds me as I look at you,” for his words are so like his father's (*Odyssey* 3.123–125), and in Sparta Helen, before she is told, sees in the young man the son of Odysseus (*Odyssey* 4.142–144). Telemachus himself says it to Peisistratus of the gleaming palace of Menelaus, like the court of Zeus (*Odyssey* 4.71–75). Odysseus says it to Nausicaa, whom he has never seen the like of, man or woman, and Merry notes that a few verses later the same feeling is ἐτεθήπεα θυμῷ, “I marveled in my heart” (*Odyssey* 6.160–161, 6.166; Merry on 3.123); and he says it again to Alcinous after the Phaeacian dancing (*Odyssey* 8.382–384).
