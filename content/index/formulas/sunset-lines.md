## The pattern

ἠέλιος (“the sun”) + καταδύω (“go down, sink”). The phrase comes in several shapes to fill different parts of the verse:

- ἦμος δ’ ἠέλιος κατέδυ καὶ ἐπὶ κνέφας ἦλθε, “when the sun went down and darkness came on,” a whole line, followed by sleep (475);
- ἅμα δ’ ἠελίῳ καταδύντι, “as the sun was setting” (592);
- πρόπαν ἦμαρ ἐς ἠέλιον καταδύντα, “all day long until the sun went down” (601), measuring a day of feasting;
- αὐτὰρ ἐπεὶ κατέδυ λαμπρὸν φάος ἠελίοιο, “but when the bright light of the sun had set” (605).

## In Book 1

{{quote:8d860252-abe7-5bd0-a344-75b781785151}}

{{quote:7a46619c-ff85-5614-aaf0-9ecaee0077d3}}

The sunset lines close the book's two days of feasting, the Achaeans' at Chryse and the gods' on Olympus, and both are followed by sleep. Between them stands a darker use. Hephaestus tells how Zeus threw him from heaven, and he fell “all day long” until sunset, landing on Lemnos with little life left in him ({{ref:Iliad 1.590-594}}; [[fall-of-hephaestus]]). The span of a god's fall is the span of a god's feast: from morning to the setting of the sun. The companion formula for daybreak is [[dawn-line]].
