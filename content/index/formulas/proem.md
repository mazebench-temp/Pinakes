## The pattern

An epic proem calls on the Muse, names the subject, and sets the point where the song begins. The Iliad and the Odyssey open the same way.

- **The subject first.** The poem's first word is its subject, in the accusative: μῆνιν, “wrath”, here, and ἄνδρα, “the man”, in the Odyssey (ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, Μοῦσα, Odyssey 1.1).
- **The invocation.** A goddess is asked to sing: ἄειδε θεά, “sing, goddess”. The singer presents the song as hers. The Iliad does not name her; the Odyssey calls her Muse ([[muses]]).
- **The expansion.** An epithet and a relative clause unfold what the subject did: οὐλομένην, ἣ μυρίʼ Ἀχαιοῖς ἄλγεʼ ἔθηκε, “ruinous, which laid countless sorrows on the Achaeans”.
- **The starting point.** ἐξ οὗ δὴ τὰ πρῶτα, “from the time when first”. The Odyssey leaves the choice to the goddess: “from some point (ἁμόθεν) in these things, goddess, daughter of Zeus, tell us too” (Odyssey 1.10).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Iliad calls on the Muses again before the Catalogue of Ships (2.484–493) and at three turning points of the battle (11.218, 14.508, 16.112). See [[invocation-of-the-muses]].

## Outside Homer

Aristotle quotes the first lines of both poems to show what a proem does. It gives “a sample of the subject”, so that the audience knows in advance what the work is about (*Rhetoric* 3.14, 1415a).
