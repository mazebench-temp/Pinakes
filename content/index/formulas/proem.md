## The pattern

An epic proem calls on the Muse, names the subject, and sets the point where the song begins. The Iliad and the Odyssey open the same way.

- **The subject first.** The poem's first word is its subject, in the accusative: μῆνιν, “wrath”, here, and ἄνδρα, “the man”, in the Odyssey (ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, Μοῦσα, Odyssey 1.1).
- **The invocation.** A goddess is asked to sing: ἄειδε θεά, “sing, goddess”. The singer presents the song as hers. The Iliad does not name her; the Odyssey calls her Muse ([[muses]]).
- **The expansion.** An epithet and a relative clause unfold what the subject did: οὐλομένην, ἣ μυρίʼ Ἀχαιοῖς ἄλγεʼ ἔθηκε, “ruinous, which laid countless sorrows on the Achaeans”.
- **The starting point.** ἐξ οὗ δὴ τὰ πρῶτα, “from the time when first”. The Odyssey leaves the choice to the goddess: “from some point (ἁμόθεν) in these things, goddess, daughter of Zeus, tell us too” (Odyssey 1.10).

## In Book 1

{{quote:86460625-f50f-58a7-990b-6330c318d18b}}

The proem is one sentence. It moves out from the wrath to its effects and the plan behind them, then back to where it began.

1. **The wrath and its owner.** μῆνιν is the first word, and Achilles ends the line.
2. **The verdict.** οὐλομένην, “ruinous”, stands first in the second line ([[menis]]).
3. **The cost.** Countless sorrows for the Achaeans, many souls hurled to Hades, the men themselves left as spoil for dogs and birds ([[zenodotus-feast-for-birds]]). The victims are Achilles' own side. The proem says nothing of Troy.
4. **The plan.** Διὸς δʼ ἐτελείετο βουλή, “and the plan of Zeus was being fulfilled”. The imperfect shows the plan unfolding through all of it ([[plan-of-zeus]]).
5. **The beginning.** ἐξ οὗ: the moment the two men “stood apart in strife” (διαστήτην ἐρίσαντε, a dual). The last line names them, and like the first line it ends with Achilles.

**Where does “from the time when” belong?** It is usually taken with ἄειδε: “sing, beginning from the time when”. It can also go with the plan of Zeus, which “was being fulfilled from the time when”. The Greek allows both; the point is disputed.

**From proem to story.** Line 8 asks which god set the two to quarrel, and line 9 answers at once: “The son of Leto and of Zeus.” Then the narrative steps back from effect to cause, with γάρ (“for”) and οὕνεκα (“because”). Apollo was angry with the king and sent the plague, because Agamemnon dishonored the priest; for Chryses had come to the ships.

{{quote:1c12bf05-3dde-55ca-879b-95de01c0d210}}

The story then begins with Chryses' coming, one step before the quarrel that the proem named ([[in-medias-res]]).

**The end of the book.** The poem begins by asking a goddess to sing. Book 1 ends at the feast of the gods, where the Muses “sang (ἄειδον), answering one another with lovely voices” ({{ref:Iliad 1.604}}).

## Outside Book 1

The Iliad calls on the Muses again before the Catalogue of Ships: “Tell me now, Muses who have your homes on Olympus, for you are goddesses, and are present, and know all things” (Iliad 2.484–486). The first of these lines returns at three turning points of the battle (11.218, 14.508, 16.112).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle quotes the first lines of both poems to show what a proem does. It gives “a sample of the subject”, so that the audience knows in advance what the work is about (*Rhetoric* 3.14, 1415a).
