## The pattern

An epic proem calls on the Muse, names the subject, and sets the point where the song begins. The Iliad and the Odyssey open the same way.

- **The subject first.** The poem's first word is its subject, in the accusative: μῆνιν, “wrath”, in the Iliad, and ἄνδρα, “the man”, in the Odyssey (ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, μοῦσα, *Odyssey* 1.1).
- **The invocation.** A goddess is asked to sing: ἄειδε θεά, “sing, goddess”. The singer presents the song as hers. The Iliad does not name her; the Odyssey calls her Muse ([[muses]]).
- **The expansion.** An epithet and a relative clause unfold what the subject did: οὐλομένην, ἣ μυρίʼ Ἀχαιοῖς ἄλγεʼ ἔθηκε, “ruinous, which laid countless sorrows on the Achaeans”; in the Odyssey, πολύτροπον, ὃς μάλα πολλὰ / πλάγχθη, “of many turns, who was driven / far astray” (*Odyssey* 1.1–2).
- **The starting point.** ἐξ οὗ δὴ τὰ πρῶτα, “from the time when first”. The Odyssey leaves the choice to the goddess, with ἁμόθεν, “from some point”: “Of these things, goddess, daughter of Zeus, beginning where you will, tell us too” (*Odyssey* 1.10).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Iliad calls on the Muses again before the Catalogue of Ships (2.484–493) and at three turning points of the battle (11.218, 14.508, 16.112). The Odyssey never calls on the Muse again after its proem. See [[invocation-of-the-muses]].

## Outside Homer

Aristotle quotes the first lines of both poems to show what a proem does. It gives “a sample of the subject”, so that the audience knows in advance what the work is about (*Rhetoric* 3.14, 1415a12–17; see [[prooimion]]). Livius Andronicus began his Latin Odyssey with *Virum mihi, Camena, insece versutum* (see [[odusia]]), and Horace twice turned the Odyssey's opening into Latin, once as the model of a modest beginning (see [[horace-on-the-opening-of-the-odyssey]]). For Horace's praise of Homer's beginning, see [[in-medias-res]]; for Protagoras' objection to the command ἄειδε, [[menis]]; for the first line of the Iliad in English, [[first-line-in-english]].
