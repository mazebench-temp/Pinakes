## The pattern

A Homeric voyage is told as a series of practical steps, each with its own formula.

- **Launching.** The ship is dragged down to the sea (ἅλα δὲ προέρυσσεν). Rowers are chosen (ἐν δʼ ἐρέτας ἔκρινεν), cargo and passengers go aboard, the commander embarks (ἐν δʼ ἀρχὸς ἔβη), and they sail “over the watery ways” (ἐπέπλεον ὑγρὰ κέλευθα; [[watery-ways]]).
- **Under sail.** A god may send a fair wind (ἴκμενον οὖρον ἵει). The crew set up the [[mast|mast]] and spread the [[sails|sails]] (ἱστὸν στήσαντʼ ἀνά θʼ ἱστία λευκὰ πέτασσαν). The wind fills the sail, and the wave roars at the [[cutwater|cutwater]] as the ship runs.
- **Arrival.** Inside the harbor they furl the sails (ἱστία μὲν στείλαντο), lower the mast by the [[forestays|forestays]] into its [[mast-crutch|mast-crutch]], and row to the anchorage (εἰς ὅρμον προέρεσσαν ἐρετμοῖς). They cast out the [[mooring-stones|mooring-stones]], make fast the [[stern-cables|stern-cables]] (ἐκ δʼ εὐνὰς ἔβαλον, κατὰ δὲ πρυμνήσιʼ ἔδησαν), and step out “where the sea breaks upon the shore” (ἐπὶ ῥηγμῖνι θαλάσσης).
- **Beaching.** For a long stay the ship is hauled up on the land (ἐπʼ ἠπείροιο ἔρυσσαν), high on the sand, and set on [[props|props]]. The crew scatter to their huts (ἐσκίδναντο).

## In Book 1

**Launching.** Agamemnon gives the order step by step: a black ship, oarsmen, the hecatomb, Chryseis, and “one man of counsel” in command ({{ref:Iliad 1.141-146}}). The narrative carries it out in the same order and nearly the same words, and names the commander, Odysseus.

{{quote:a2ac1dff-c337-51bc-9afc-7d4e41b9e2f3}}

The loading is told with the preverbs for “in”, “into” and “up”: ἐν δʼ ἐρέτας, ἐς δʼ ἑκατόμβην, ἀνὰ δὲ Χρυσηΐδα, ἐν δʼ ἀρχός. Then the voyage out is passed over. The story stays in the camp and meets the ship again at Chryse.

**Arrival at Chryse.**

{{quote:94ec89f5-486b-534b-bb2d-734da559202d}}

The landing mirrors the loading. Four lines in a row begin with ἐκ, “out”: the mooring-stones, the men, the hecatomb, and last Chryseis, the reason for the voyage ({{ref:Iliad 1.436-439}}). The ship is moored, not beached; the stay is short. The crew sleep beside the stern-cables ({{ref:Iliad 1.476}}).

**The voyage home.** At dawn ([[dawn-line]]) they put out, and “far-working Apollo sent them a following wind” ([[far-worker]]). The god who sent the plague now speeds the ship. This is the only time the story of the Iliad shows a ship under sail.

{{quote:ca68adaf-e1f6-553f-8abf-c22143e3780b}}

**Beaching.** At the camp the ship is drawn up and propped like the rest of the fleet.

{{quote:53225e7b-28ab-5525-b070-9b63d6c6f189}}

The next line turns to Achilles, “sitting beside his swift-sailing ships” ({{ref:Iliad 1.488}}). The ships he threatened to sail home in stay on the beach ({{ref:Iliad 1.169-170}}). See [[ships|ships]] and [[return-of-chryseis]].

## Outside Book 1

When Telemachus sails from Ithaca, Athena sends “a following wind” in the words of 1.479, with her name in place of Apollo's (Odyssey 2.420). The lines on the wind in the sail, the wave at the cutwater and the running ship follow almost word for word (Odyssey 2.427–429 = Iliad 1.481–483). The harbor of the island off the land of the Cyclopes is so sheltered that there is no need “to cast out mooring-stones or tie stern-cables” (Odyssey 9.136–137).
