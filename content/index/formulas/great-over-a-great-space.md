## The pattern

μέγας (“great”) beside μεγαλωστί, an adverb of the same stem: the man's size laid out over the ground he covers (see [[polyptoton]]). Homer uses the phrase three times, always of a hero lying in the dust.

**Cebriones.** Patroclus has killed Hector's charioteer with a stone and mocked his fall as a diver's (16.737–750; see [[cebriones]] and [[like-a-diver]]). Hector holds the head and Patroclus the foot, the armies fight around them, and spears, arrows and stones fly around the body (16.762–775). The dead man lies in the middle, “forgetful of his horsemanship” (λελασμένος ἱπποσυνάων, 16.776): ἱπποσύνη, the art of driving, was his skill, and death has taken it from him (see [[chariot]]).

**Achilles in grief.** When Antilochus brings the news that Patroclus is dead, Achilles pours dust on his head and lies stretched out in the dust, μέγας μεγαλωστὶ τανυσθείς, tearing his hair (18.22–27). The words of a fallen warrior are used of the living mourner.

**Achilles dead.** In the Underworld the shade of Agamemnon tells Achilles how he died at Troy: the best of the Trojans and the Achaeans were killed around him, fighting over him, “and you in a whirl of dust / lay, great over a great space, forgetful of your horsemanship” (κεῖσο for κεῖτο, *Odyssey* 24.37–40). They fought the whole day, and would not have stopped had Zeus not stopped them with a storm (*Odyssey* 24.41–42). The same two verses tell of the fight over Cebriones in the Iliad and of the fight over Achilles in the Odyssey (see [[fighting-over-the-body]]).
