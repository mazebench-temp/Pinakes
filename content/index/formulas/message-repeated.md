## The pattern

When a god or a king sends a message, Homer often gives it twice: once when it is given, and again when it is delivered, in the same words. The sender may even ask for this, as Zeus asks the Dream to say “everything exactly as I command” (πάντα μάλʼ ἀτρεκέως ἀγορευέμεν ὡς ἐπιτέλλω, 2.10).

The changes are of three kinds.

1. **Grammar.** Pronouns and persons shift to the new listener. “Bid him arm” becomes “he bids you arm” (2.11, 2.28).
2. **Frame.** The messenger adds an address and an introduction, often naming the sender, and sometimes a closing word of his own (2.23–27, 2.33–34).
3. **Choice.** The messenger may leave out what would not serve, or change what he repeats, so that the listener hears a different message from the one that was sent.

The audience hears the message twice and knows the original, so every change is noticed. For the errand itself (the sending, the journey, the arrival), see [[messenger-scene]]; for a character's repetition of an earlier event, see [[retelling]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

The best-known choice is made in Book 9. Odysseus repeats Agamemnon's list of gifts to Achilles almost line for line (9.264–299, from 9.122–157), but he leaves out the king's last words, that Achilles should yield to him as the more kingly and the elder (9.158–161), and puts an appeal of his own in their place (9.300–306).

## Outside Homer

Ancient critics asked whether the lie in Agamemnon's dream was Zeus' or the Dream's. Aristotle reports that Hippias of Thasos cleared Zeus of lying by reading a line of his instructions as an order to the Dream, so that the false promise became the Dream's to speak and not Zeus' own (*Poetics* 25, 1461a21–23; *Sophistical Refutations* 4, 166b6–8).
