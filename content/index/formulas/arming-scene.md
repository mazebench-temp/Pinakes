## The pattern

A warrior arms in a fixed order, and each piece has its own verse, which returns from scene to scene with few changes. Paris' arming gives the plain form:

1. **Greaves** ([[greaves]]): κνημῖδας μὲν πρῶτα περὶ κνήμῃσιν ἔθηκε / καλάς, ἀργυρέοισιν ἐπισφυρίοις ἀραρυίας, “First he put the greaves around his shins, fine ones, fitted with silver ankle-pieces” (3.330–331).
2. **Corselet** ([[corselet]]): δεύτερον αὖ θώρηκα περὶ στήθεσσιν ἔδυνεν, “second, he put on the corselet around his chest” (3.332).
3. **Sword** ([[sword]]): ἀμφὶ δʼ ἄρʼ ὤμοισιν βάλετο ξίφος ἀργυρόηλον / χάλκεον, “and around his shoulders he slung his silver-studded sword of bronze” (3.334–335).
4. **Shield** ([[shield]]): αὐτὰρ ἔπειτα σάκος μέγα τε στιβαρόν τε, “and then his shield, great and heavy” (3.335).
5. **Helmet** ([[helmet]]): κρατὶ δʼ ἐπʼ ἰφθίμῳ κυνέην εὔτυκτον ἔθηκεν / ἵππουριν· δεινὸν δὲ λόφος καθύπερθεν ἔνευεν, “and on his mighty head he set a well-made helmet with a horsehair crest, and the plume nodded terribly above it” (3.336–337).
6. **Spear** ([[spear]]): εἵλετο δʼ ἄλκιμον ἔγχος, ὅ οἱ παλάμηφιν ἀρήρει, “and he took up a strong spear that fitted his grip” (3.338).

The order is the practical one. The greaves go on while the body can still bend freely; the sword and the shield hang from straps over the shoulders; then the helmet, and last the spear, taken in the hand as the man goes out. The [[dressing-scene]], its peaceful twin, shares the sword verse (2.45) and also ends with something taken up in the hand, a staff or a spear.

The Iliad tells four arming scenes in full: Paris before his duel (3.328–338), Agamemnon on the morning of his great day in battle (11.15–46), Patroclus in Achilles' armor (16.130–144), and Achilles in the armor Hephaestus made for him (19.364–391). The verses for the greaves and the corselet recur in all four (3.330–332, 11.17–19, 16.131–133, 19.369–371), and those for the sword and the shield in the armings of Paris, Patroclus and Achilles (3.334–335, 16.135–136, 19.372–373).

What changes is what the story needs, and each full scene gives one piece a history or a meaning. Agamemnon's corselet was a guest-gift from Cinyras of Cyprus, his shield bears the Gorgon's face, and Athena and Hera thunder to honor him (11.19–46). Patroclus puts on all of Achilles' armor except the spear, the ash from Pelion that no other Achaean could wield (16.140–144). Achilles' shield shines like the moon and his helmet like a star, and when he tries the armor on it lifts him like wings (19.373–386); last he takes up his father's spear (19.387–391).

The scene can also shrink to a verse, as for Menelaus in Book 3 (3.339), or change its pieces for the occasion. Teucer, when his bowstring breaks, puts down the bow and takes a shield, a helmet and a spear (15.461–482). Diomedes and Odysseus arm for their night raid with a leather cap and a helmet of boar's tusks (10.254–271).

Walter Arend described the arming scene among Homer's recurring scenes (*Die typischen Scenen bei Homer*, 1933). J. I. Armstrong showed how the four full scenes grow in scale and how each prepares a decisive fight (“The Arming Motif in the Iliad”, *American Journal of Philology* 79, 1958).
