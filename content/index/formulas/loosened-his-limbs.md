## The pattern

λῦσε (“loosened”, from λύω, “loosen, undo, set free”) + δέ + γυῖα (“limbs”, above all the legs and knees, on which a man stands). The strength that holds a man upright is imagined as a binding, and death undoes it. The first use in the poem is the death of Elephenor, killed by Agenor as he stoops to drag a body away:

{{quote:118b8e20-94d4-56d8-9fd6-3fee41c4159d}}

The next verse gives the other half of the picture: ὣς τὸν μὲν λίπε θυμός, “so his spirit left him” (4.470). The limbs are loosened and the θυμός, the spirit that moves them, goes away (see [[thymos]]). Book 4 has other ways of saying it: Diores dies “breathing out his life” (θυμὸν ἀποπνείων, 4.524), and Thoas, killing Peirous, “took away his life” (ἐκ δʼ αἴνυτο θυμόν, 4.531).

## Related phrases

The same verb and the same parts of the body describe weariness, fear and death. When Agamemnon leaves his chariot he tells his driver to keep it near, “for when weariness should take his limbs” (γυῖα λάβῃ κάματος, 4.230). A killer “loosens the knees” (γούνατʼ ἔλυσεν) of many men: Aeneas says so of Diomedes, and Sarpedon of Patroclus (5.176, 16.425). And a man's knees and heart are loosened by fear, as Lycaon's are before Achilles (λύτο γούνατα καὶ φίλον ἦτορ, 21.114) and Odysseus' in the storm (Odyssey 5.297). λύω is also the verb of undoing armor: Machaon “loosened the gleaming belt” to heal Menelaus (λῦσε δέ οἱ ζωστῆρα, 4.215; see [[belt]]). From the same image comes the adjective λυσιμελής, “limb-loosening”, which the Odyssey gives to sleep (Odyssey 20.57, 23.343).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod calls Eros λυσιμελής (*Theogony* 121), and the lyric poets give the word to desire: “Eros the limb-loosener shakes me again” (Sappho, fragment 130), and “limb-loosening longing overpowers me, friend” (Archilochus, fragment 196 West).
