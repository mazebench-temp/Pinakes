## The pattern

τέλος (“end, completion, fulfillment”) + θανάτοιο (“of death”, the old genitive in -οιο): death as the end that completes a life (see [[thanatos]]). The phrase stands in either order, τέλος θανάτοιο in the middle of the verse and θανάτοιο τέλος at its end, and the verb around it says which of three things it is.

**Fated.** Priam leaves the field before the duel of Paris and Menelaus, saying that Zeus and the other immortal gods know “for which of the two the end of death is fated” (3.309; see [[foresight]]). Achilles says that two fates are carrying him “toward the end of death” (θανάτοιο τέλος δέ, 9.411; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]), and the narrator says of Peisander, going straight for Menelaus, that “an evil fate was leading him toward the end of death” (13.602; see [[moira]]).

**Coming on a man.** The end of death can be put off, or it can be too quick. If Achilles goes home, “the end of death will not come on me quickly” (9.416); Odysseus tells the dying Socus that “the end of death was too quick for you and caught you, and you did not escape” (11.451). In the Odyssey it is what Odysseus wishes on Antinous, the end of death “before his marriage” (*Odyssey* 17.476).

**Covering.** In battle the end of death covers the dying, as darkness covers their eyes (see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). Of the twin sons of Diocles, killed by Aeneas, “there the end of death covered them both” (5.553; see [[crethon]]). Three times the verse closes a speech with the speaker's death, “As he spoke, the end of death covered him” (ὣς ἄρα μιν εἰπόντα τέλος θανάτοιο κάλυψε): Sarpedon dies with it after charging Glaucus to fight for his body (16.502), Patroclus after foretelling Hector's death (16.855), and Hector after foretelling the death of Achilles (22.361). For Patroclus and Hector it is followed by the two verses of the soul's flight to Hades (16.856–857 = 22.362–363; see [[leaving-manhood-and-youth]] and [[so-he-spoke]]).
