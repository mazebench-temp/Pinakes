## The pattern

ἀλλʼ ἄγε, “but come”, + μοι τόδε εἰπέ, “tell me this”, + καὶ ἀτρεκέως κατάλεξον, “and give me an exact account”. ἀτρεκέως is “precisely, truly”, and καταλέγω is “recount point by point”. The verse comes after the speaker has said something of his own, and the question follows in the next verse, often as a choice between two or three possibilities, “whether … or …”. The answer may take up the words: τοὶ γὰρ ἐγώ τοι ταῦτα μάλʼ ἀτρεκέως καταλέξω, “Well then, I will tell you all this very exactly” (10.413, 10.427). The Odyssey has the answer with ἀγορεύσω, “I will declare”, in place of καταλέξω, eight times (Od. 1.179, 1.214, 4.383, 4.399, 14.192, 15.266, 15.352, 16.113).

In the Iliad the question verse occurs four times, always at night, and always in a meeting across the lines of the war. Odysseus uses it twice in his questioning of the captured Dolon (10.384, 10.405). Hermes, in the likeness of a young prince, uses it when he meets Priam on the plain, asking whether the old king is sending his treasures away to safety or the Trojans are all abandoning Ilios (24.380–385), and Achilles, in his hut, uses it to ask Priam how many days he needs for Hector's funeral (24.656–658). The Odyssey has it thirteen times, most often when a host or a stranger asks who the other is and where he comes from (Od. 1.169–170, 1.206–207, 24.256–258), and in the underworld, when Odysseus questions the dead (Od. 11.140, 11.170, 11.370, 11.457).

The demand for exactness has a counterpart in the sending of a message. Zeus tells the Dream to say “everything exactly as I command” (πάντα μάλʼ ἀτρεκέως, 2.10; see [[message-repeated]]).
