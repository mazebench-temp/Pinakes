## The pattern

ἀποσιώπησις (“falling silent,” from ἀποσιωπάω) is the ancient rhetoricians' name for a sentence left unfinished. Most often it is a condition whose “then” clause is suppressed. The effect is of feeling or menace too strong, or too obvious, to put into words. The name is later than Homer; the figure is not.

## In Book 1

**Agamemnon.**

{{quote:583415ea-0425-517c-bd62-404ac0c77774}}

“If the great-hearted Achaeans will give me a prize, suiting it to my heart so that it will be of equal worth —.” The main clause never comes; the translation supplies “well and good.” Agamemnon moves at once to the other case, which he does complete: “but if they will not give it, then I myself will take one.”

**Achilles.**

{{quote:1c174520-9ee1-599b-acc8-b761493056ba}}

Achilles calls the heralds to witness “if ever again there comes a need of me to ward off shameful ruin from the others —” and does not say what then (see [[shameful-ruin]]). He turns instead to the cause: “for truly he rages with a ruinous mind.” The unspoken consequence is the Achaeans' disaster, which the rest of the poem will supply.

**Hephaestus.**

{{quote:d5008e12-8ea8-596d-ace3-b1daecf36150}}

“For if the Olympian lord of the lightning should wish to blast us from our seats —.” What would follow is plain: he could. Hephaestus gives the reason instead, “for he is by far the strongest.” Here the silence is tact. The son will not describe his father's violence against his mother ([[lord-of-the-lightning]]).

## Outside Homer

The most famous example in Latin is Neptune's threat to the winds, *quos ego —* (“whom I —”), in Virgil's *Aeneid* (1.135), broken off so that he can calm the sea.
