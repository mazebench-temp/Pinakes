## The pattern

τρίς (“three times”) … ἀλλʼ ὅτε δὴ τὸ τέταρτον (“but when the fourth time”). A hero attacks three times and three times is thrown back or fails, and at the fourth a god intervenes. The verse of the fourth attack is fixed, ἀλλʼ ὅτε δὴ τὸ τέταρτον ἐπέσσυτο δαίμονι ἶσος, “but when for the fourth time he rushed on like a god”, and it is followed by a god's terrible shout (δεινὰ δʼ ὁμοκλήσας) or blow.

Its first use is Diomedes' attack on Aeneas, whom Apollo is shielding:

{{quote:c6f743f1-bf92-5222-9e94-25279a5e608e}}

Then Diomedes “drew back a little way, avoiding the wrath of Apollo who shoots from afar” (5.443–444).

The same frame is used three more times in the Iliad, always at the height of a hero's success:

- **Patroclus at the wall.** Three times he climbed the angle of the wall of Troy, and three times Apollo thrust him back, striking his shield with his immortal hands; at the fourth, “like a god”, the god shouted that it was not his portion to sack the city, nor even Achilles'. Patroclus drew back “far”, in the verse that ended Diomedes' attack, where Diomedes drew back “a little way” (16.702–711; 16.710–711 ~ 5.443–444).
- **Patroclus' last charge.** Three times he charged the Trojans, “equal to swift Ares”, and three times he killed nine men; at the fourth, “like a god”, “then for you, Patroclus, the end of life appeared”: Apollo met him in the battle and struck him (16.784–792).
- **Achilles and Hector.** Apollo snatched Hector away in mist, and three times Achilles sprang at him and three times stabbed the empty mist; at the fourth, “like a god”, he shouted after him that he would find him again (20.443–454).

Looser forms of the rhythm mark other turning points: Hector three times seizes the feet of Patroclus' body and three times the Ajaxes beat him off (18.155–158); Achilles shouts three times at the trench and three times the Trojans are thrown into confusion (18.228–229); Asteropaeus tries three times to pull Achilles' spear from the bank (21.176–179); Achilles chases Hector three times around the city, and at the fourth circuit Zeus lifts his scales (22.165, 22.208–213). In the Odyssey, Telemachus tries three times to string his father's bow and would have done it at the fourth, had Odysseus not stopped him with a nod (Odyssey 21.125–129).

The three show how far a man can go; the fourth shows where he must stop. δαίμονι ἶσος, “like a god”, is said of the hero at each fourth attack (5.438, 16.705, 16.786, 20.447): at the moment he is most like a god, a god reminds him that he is not one. The warning to Diomedes says so in as many words, and Diomedes obeys and lives. See [[the-aristeia]], [[gods-and-mortals]] and [[daimon]].
