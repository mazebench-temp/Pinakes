## The pattern

ὀδάξ (“with the teeth”) + ἐν χείλεσι φύντες (“fastening on their lips”): Merry derives ὀδάξ from the root of δάκνω, “bite,” with a prefixed ὀ-, renders the adverb “with teeth set,” and takes φύντες ἐν χείλεσι literally as “fastening on their lips” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.381). The phrase belongs to a pair of verses that follow a speech: ὣς ἔφαθʼ, οἱ δʼ ἄρα πάντες ὀδὰξ ἐν χείλεσι φύντες / Τηλέμαχον θαύμαζον, ὃ θαρσαλέως ἀγόρευεν, “So he spoke, and they all, biting their lips with their teeth, / marveled at Telemachus, that he spoke so boldly.”

The two verses come three times, always after Telemachus has spoken out to the suitors in his own house: when he tells them that he will call an assembly and order them out (*Odyssey* 1.381–382), when he tells them to go home to bed now that they have feasted (*Odyssey* 18.405–411), and when he has seated the beggar at a table in the hall and warns them to keep their insults and their hands from him (*Odyssey* 20.257–269). Each time the suitors are silenced, and then one of them answers: Antinous twice, and once Amphinomus (*Odyssey* 1.383, 18.412, 20.270). See [[so-he-spoke]].
