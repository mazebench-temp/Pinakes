## The pattern

The sender gives the message in direct speech and sends the messengers off. They go (βάτην), arrive (ἱκέσθην), and find the one they seek, often in the middle of something (τὸν δʼ εὗρον …). The messenger delivers the message, often in the sender's exact words. The addressee answers, and the messengers go back.

## In Book 1

{{quote:27366c73-2e5f-5ac0-9dce-142c14c490cf}}

Agamemnon sends Talthybius and Eurybates, his [[ready-attendants|ready attendants]], with an order and a threat: if Achilles refuses, he will come himself with more men. He sends them off with “a stern command” ([[stern-command]]).

{{quote:34ac6799-621d-586c-9840-ac207a71fbdc}}

The journey, the arrival and the finding follow in order. The heralds walk the shore “unwilling” (ἀέκοντε), reach the huts and ships of the Myrmidons, and find Achilles sitting by his hut. Then the pattern breaks. The heralds stand in fear and awe and say nothing, and the message is never spoken. Achilles “understood in his heart” and speaks instead. He greets them as “messengers of Zeus and of men” ([[messengers-of-zeus]]), clears them of blame, and has Patroclus bring Briseis out.

{{quote:1258be00-b64f-5332-92cc-3cfd81b31a44}}

The heralds go back, and “the woman went with them, unwilling” (ἀέκουσα). The scene begins and ends with unwillingness. There is no report to Agamemnon; the narrative stays with Achilles, who weeps. See [[taking-of-briseis]] and [[heralds|heralds]].

## Outside Book 1

The Dream repeats Zeus' orders to Agamemnon almost word for word (Iliad 2.8–34). The embassy of Book 9 arrives in the same words: “they came to the huts and ships of the Myrmidons, and found him” delighting his heart with the lyre (Iliad 9.185–186). Its walk along the shore is told in dual verbs (9.182), though the envoys are more than two. The duals are a much-discussed puzzle.
