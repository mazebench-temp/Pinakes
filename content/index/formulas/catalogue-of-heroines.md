## The pattern

Persephone sends the women, and they gather about the blood; Odysseus, his sword drawn, lets them drink one at a time, and each declares her own descent (*Odyssey* 11.225–234; see [[persephone]] and [[odysseus-in-the-land-of-the-dead]]). They are introduced as “all who had been the wives and daughters of chieftains”, ἀριστήων ἄλοχοι … ἠδὲ θύγατρες, and dismissed as “all the wives and daughters of heroes that I saw”, with ἡρώων, “of heroes”, for ἀριστήων (*Odyssey* 11.227, 11.329; see the translation's key-word row for ἀριστήων ἄλοχοι ἠδὲ θύγατρες). Each entry is built from the same parts, in varying order:

1. **The sight**, in a fixed verse-opening: “There the first I saw” (*Odyssey* 11.235), “And after her I saw”, τὴν δὲ μετʼ … ἴδον (*Odyssey* 11.260, 11.266, 11.305), “And I saw”, καὶ … ἴδον (*Odyssey* 11.271, 11.281, 11.298, 11.321, 11.326; see the translation's formula row for these verses).
2. **The name and the family**: the woman with an epithet, and her father or her husband, “daughter of a noble father”, “the daughter of Asopus”, “the wife of Amphitryon”, “the wife of Tyndareus”.
3. **The union**: most of the women bore their sons to a god, and what the poem reports is what they said: Tyro “said” she was the offspring of Salmoneus, Antiope “claimed” to have slept in the arms of Zeus, Iphimedeia “declared” that she lay with Poseidon (φάτο, εὔχετο, φάσκε, *Odyssey* 11.236, 11.261, 11.306; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.305).
4. **The children and their story**: the sons and what they did, a city founded, a kingdom ruled, a threat against heaven; or the woman's own deed or fate.

The entries vary greatly in length. Tyro has twenty-five verses and Chloris, with the story of her daughter, seventeen, while Phaedra and Procris share a verse with Ariadne, and Maera and Clymene one with Eriphyle (*Odyssey* 11.321, 11.326).

The close is the verse with which the poet refused to name the multitude of the Achaeans before the Catalogue of Ships, and Helen the deeds of Odysseus: “The multitude I could not tell or name” (*Iliad* 2.488; *Odyssey* 4.240, 11.328; see [[catalogue-of-ships]]). Zeus' list of the women he loved, a catalogue in seven names, includes Alcmene (*Iliad* 14.315–328; see [[the-loves-of-zeus]]).

## Order

The list follows families, not time. It begins with the house of Aeolus, Tyro and her sons, and comes back to it with Chloris, Tyro's daughter-in-law, and her daughter Pero, whom the seer Melampus won for his brother; and the last woman, Eriphyle, was, as Merry tells her story, the wife of Amphiaraus, the great-grandson of Melampus (*Odyssey* 15.225–247, Greek only; Merry on 11.326; see [[amphiaraus]]). Between them stand the women of Thebes: Antiope, whose sons first walled the city, Alcmene and Megara, the mother and the wife of Heracles, and Epicaste. Merry observes that if the Attic names of *Odyssey* 11.321–325 are left out, all the characters come from the legends of the Minyans and the Thebans, and he accepts Lauer's argument for a Boeotian origin of the passage: the Boeotians had a peculiar fondness for lists of noble women, as the Hesiodic catalogue of women shows, and the Catalogue of Ships was itself called the Βοιωτία (Merry on 11.225). In his view Leda's passage, if genuine, breaks the run of Boeotian and Thessalian legends, the Attic women Phaedra, Procris and Ariadne are a later insertion from the time of the Peisistratean recension, and with Maera, Clymene and Eriphyle the list returns to Boeotia (on 11.298, 11.321, 11.326; see the translation's notes on *Odyssey* 11.298–304 and 11.321–325).

## Outside Homer

The Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* told many of the same women and their children: Tyro and her five sons (fr. 13 Evelyn-White), Iphimedeia and the Aloadae (fr. 6), Castor and Polydeuces, whom it made sons of Zeus (fr. 66), Amphion and Zethus, who built the wall of Thebes with a lyre (fr. 96), and Ariadne (fr. 76); a poem called the *Great Eoiae* told of Melampus and the cattle of Iphiclus (fr. 12). The *Shield of Heracles* ascribed to Hesiod opens as though in the middle of such a list, ἢ οἵη, “Or like her who left her home and her native land and came to Thebes with warlike Amphitryon”, Alcmene, daughter of Electryon (*Shield* 1–3; see [[hesiod]]).
