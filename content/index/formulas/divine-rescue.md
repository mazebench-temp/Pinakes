## The pattern

1. **The danger.** A warrior is about to be killed or dragged away.
2. **“Had not.”** The narrator says what would have happened and names the god who prevented it: “And now he would have dragged him off and won untold glory, had not Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, been quick to see it” (3.373–374). The second verse returns when the same goddess saves Aeneas from Diomedes (5.311–312), and with Poseidon's name when Achilles is about to kill him (20.290–291).
3. **The rescue.** The god breaks the hold or shields the man, and snatches him away (ἐξαρπάζω) “very easily, as a god can”, covered in mist: τὸν δʼ ἐξήρπαξʼ Ἀφροδίτη / ῥεῖα μάλʼ ὥς τε θεός, ἐκάλυψε δʼ ἄρʼ ἠέρι πολλῇ (3.380–381). The same two verses, with Apollo's name, save Hector from Achilles (20.443–444).
4. **Safety.** The god sets the man down somewhere safe: Paris in his own fragrant bedroom (3.382), Aeneas in Apollo's temple on Pergamus, where Leto and Artemis heal him (5.445–448).
5. **The attacker.** The man robbed of his victory is left searching or baffled. Menelaus ranges through the crowd like a wild beast (3.449–450). Diomedes rushes at Aeneas three times, though he knows that Apollo is shielding him, until the god warns him off (5.432–444). Achilles cannot believe what he has seen (20.344–352).

The gods rescue the men they favor, and the men whose time has not come. Poseidon saves Aeneas because he is fated to survive and to rule the Trojans (20.293–308). Zeus thinks of snatching his son Sarpedon alive out of the battle, and later of saving Hector, and gives way when Hera and Athena object (16.431–461, 22.166–187). Bernard Fenik studied such recurring patterns of battle narrative (*Typical Battle Scenes in the Iliad*, 1968).

## Outside Homer

Virgil remembers the rescue of Aeneas from Achilles. Neptune tells Venus that when her son met Achilles, unequal in strength and in the gods' favor, he snatched him away “in a hollow cloud” (nube cava rapui, *Aeneid* 5.808–811).
