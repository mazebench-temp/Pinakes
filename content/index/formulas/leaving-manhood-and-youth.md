## The pattern

Patroclus has told Hector that death and mighty fate stand close beside him (16.851–854), and “the end of death” covers him as he speaks (16.855; see [[the-end-of-death]]). The narrator then follows the soul. The ψυχή, the life that leaves a man at death (see [[psyche]]), goes ἐκ ῥεθέων, “from his limbs” (ῥέθεα, a rare word, again in Priam's plea, 22.68), πταμένη, “flying”, and goes to Hades (Ἄϊδος δέ, “to Hades'”, with the suffix of direction printed apart; see [[hades]]). It goes ὃν πότμον γοόωσα, “lamenting its fate”, as the living lament the dead (see [[lament]]), λιποῦσʼ ἀνδροτῆτα καὶ ἥβην, “leaving manhood and youth”.

The two verses are used twice, at the death of Patroclus (16.856–857) and at the death of Hector (22.362–363). Each comes after the same closing verse and after a dying speech that foretells the death of the killer: Patroclus foretells Hector's at the hands of Achilles, and Hector foretells Achilles' at the hands of Paris and Apollo (22.358–360). Hector dies in the words of Patroclus' death. In Book 23 the soul of Patroclus comes back to Achilles in his sleep, and Achilles says that all night it stood over him “lamenting and weeping” (γοόωσά τε μυρομένη τε, 23.106).

ἀνδροτής, “manhood”, is found only here and at 24.6, where Achilles longs for Patroclus' “manhood and noble might” (ἀνδροτῆτά τε καὶ μένος ἠΰ). In ἀνδροτῆτα the first syllable must be short, which the cluster -νδρ- does not allow (see the translation's note on 16.855–857).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates names the two verses among those to be struck out of the poems, with the words of Achilles' shade, the house of Hades and the soul of Patroclus going down like smoke (23.100–101): they are not true, and they will not help men who must fight; and the more poetic they are, the less they should be heard by boys and men who are to be free and to fear slavery more than death (*Republic* 3.386c–387b; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]). His text has Ἄϊδόσδε for Ἄϊδος δέ. The Neoplatonist Proclus explains the verses by the state of the soul: a soul that has lived the life of action is distressed to be parted from the body that served it, “like the soul of Patroclus, leaving manhood and youth”, and in Hades longs for its company (*On the Republic* I 119 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).
