## The pattern

A man gets up, at night or at dawn, to act on something that will not wait. He sits up (ἕζετο δʼ ὀρθωθείς, 2.42) and dresses in a fixed order, one item to a verse or half-verse, most of them with an epithet:

1. the tunic (χιτών), put on (ἔνδυνε; see [[tunic]]);
2. the cloak (φᾶρος or χλαῖνα), thrown around him (περὶ … βάλλετο; see [[cloak]]);
3. the sandals, in a whole-line formula: ποσσὶ δʼ ὑπὸ λιπαροῖσιν ἐδήσατο καλὰ πέδιλα, “and under his shining feet he bound his fine sandals” (see [[sandals]]);
4. the sword, slung from the shoulders (ἀμφὶ δʼ ἄρʼ ὤμοισιν βάλετο ξίφος; see [[sword]]);
5. last, a staff or a spear, taken up (εἵλετο), with which he goes out.

The dressing scene is the civilian twin of the arming scene, in which a warrior puts on greaves, corselet, sword, shield and helmet, and takes up his spear last (Paris, Iliad 3.328–338; Agamemnon, 11.17–46; Patroclus, 16.130–144; Achilles, 19.364–391). The two share the sword verse. “And around his shoulders he slung his silver-studded sword” (2.45) is also the verse for the sword when Paris, Patroclus and Achilles arm (3.334, 16.135, 19.372). The last item tells what the dressing is for: a spear for a night errand in the camp, a scepter for a king going to the assembly (2.46).

Walter Arend's *Die typischen Scenen bei Homer* (1933) is the classic study of Homer's recurring scenes.

## Elsewhere in Homer

Book 10 has a run of night dressings. Agamemnon, unable to sleep, puts on his tunic and sandals, throws a lion's skin over his shoulders and takes a spear (Iliad 10.21–24). Nestor, woken by him, does the same with a crimson cloak (10.131–135).

The fullest dressing in the Iliad is Hera's, when she makes herself beautiful to beguile Zeus. She bathes and anoints herself, braids her hair, puts on robe, girdle, earrings and veil, and binds on her sandals in the same verse (14.170–186). Her veil is νηγάτεος, the rare word used of Agamemnon's tunic; it occurs only in these two scenes (2.43, 14.185).

In the Odyssey Telemachus dresses, slings his sword and binds on his sandals to go to the assembly of Ithaca (Odyssey 2.2–5), as Menelaus does at Sparta (4.307–309), and Telemachus again on the morning of the contest of the bow (20.124–127). When a god sets out on an errand, the sandals are “immortal, golden”, and carry him over sea and land (Hermes, Iliad 24.340–342 and Odyssey 5.44–46; Athena, Odyssey 1.96–98). The sandal verse occurs twelve times in the two poems, seven of them in its full form (Iliad 2.44, 10.22, 10.132, 14.186; Odyssey 2.4, 4.309, 20.126).
