## The pattern

The scene is made of fixed verses, of which any meeting may use some or all:

1. **The coming.** ἦλθε δʼ ἐπὶ ψυχή, “and there came the soul of …”, with the dead man's name or relation: of Patroclus to the sleeping Achilles (*Iliad* 23.65), and four times in the Odyssey's underworld. The soul keeps the look of the living man, “like him in all things, in stature and in the fair eyes / and the voice” (*Iliad* 23.66–67; see [[psyche]]).
2. **Pity.** The living man weeps at the sight: “When I saw him I wept, and pitied him in my heart” (*Odyssey* 11.55; see [[pity]] and [[tears]]).
3. **Recognition.** The soul knows the living man. In the Odyssey's underworld the souls must drink the blood of the victims before they know him and speak truly (*Odyssey* 11.147–149; see [[dark-blood]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]); in the poem's second scene among the dead, the soul of Agamemnon knows a newly dead suitor, his host in Ithaca (*Odyssey* 24.102–104, a plain rendering).
4. **Address.** The speeches open with a lament or a question: the dead ask the newcomer how he has come down (*Odyssey* 24.106, a plain rendering; see [[misty]]), the living asks what death brought the dead man down (see [[that-lays-men-low]]), and the soul often speaks “lamenting” (see [[winged-words]]).
5. **The failed embrace.** The living man reaches out and cannot hold the soul. Achilles asks Patroclus to stand nearer, so that they may put their arms around each other and take their fill of lament, and his hands close on nothing; the soul goes beneath the earth like smoke (*Iliad* 23.97–101; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]]). Odysseus tries three times to clasp his mother (see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]).
6. **Parting.** A verse closes the exchange, “So we two sat exchanging hateful words” (*Odyssey* 11.81; see [[hateful]]), the soul goes back into the house of Hades or into Erebus ([[hades]], [[erebus]]), and the living man stays: “but I stayed there steadfastly” (*Odyssey* 11.152).

The Iliad's scene comes in a dream, and the soul stands above the sleeper's head as a dream does (*Iliad* 23.68; see [[dream-scene]]). In the Odyssey the living man goes to the dead, awake, and the meetings follow one another at the pit.
