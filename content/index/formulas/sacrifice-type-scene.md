## The pattern

A full sacrifice is told in verses that recur almost word for word in both poems. After the prayer, each phase opens with αὐτὰρ ἐπεί (“but when”) and a clause that sums up the phase before ({{ref:Iliad 1.458}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.464}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.467}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.469}}).

1. **Preparation.** The victims are set in order around the [[altar|altar]] (ἑξείης ἔστησαν). The men wash their hands (χερνίψαντο) and take up the [[barley-grains|barley grains]] (οὐλοχύτας ἀνέλοντο).
2. **Prayer.** The one who sacrifices prays with lifted hands (εὔχετο χεῖρας ἀνασχών; see [[prayer-type-scene]]).
3. **Killing.** “When they had prayed and thrown the barley” (αὐτὰρ ἐπεί ῥʼ εὔξαντο καὶ οὐλοχύτας προβάλοντο), three verbs do the work: αὐέρυσαν (“drew back” the victims' heads), ἔσφαξαν (“cut their throats”), ἔδειραν (“flayed”).
4. **The god's portion.** They cut out the thigh-bones (μηρούς τʼ ἐξέταμον), cover them with fat (κνίσῃ ἐκάλυψαν) in a double fold (δίπτυχα ποιήσαντες), and lay raw flesh on them (ὠμοθέτησαν). These [[thigh-pieces|thigh-pieces]] are burned on split wood, with wine poured over them.
5. **The men's portion.** “When the thigh-pieces were burned and they had tasted the entrails” (σπλάγχνα πάσαντο), they cut up the rest (μίστυλλον), roast it on [[spits|spits]] (ὤπτησαν), and draw it off.
6. **The meal.** “When they had ceased from the work”, they feast (δαίνυντο), each with an equal share ([[equal-feast]]), and then they drink.

The rite divides the animal. The thigh-bones in their fat go up in smoke to the god, and the men eat the meat. The sacrifice ends as a feast ([[feast-type-scene]]). For the ritual itself, see [[sacrifice]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

Nestor's sacrifice to Athena at Pylos is the fullest in Homer (Odyssey 3.430–463). The victim's horns are gilded, hairs from its head are thrown into the fire, the women raise the ritual cry at the blow, and Athena comes in person to receive the rites. There the old man who “burned them on split wood” is Nestor.

When Odysseus' men kill the cattle of the Sun, they have no barley or wine, and use oak leaves and water instead (Odyssey 12.356–365). The fixed verses make the missing things conspicuous.
