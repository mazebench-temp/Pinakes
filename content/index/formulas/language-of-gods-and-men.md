## The pattern

Homer sometimes gives two names for one thing: “which the gods call X, but men call Y.” The divine name is the rarer; the human one is the ordinary word. The device recurs a handful of times in the two epics: the hill that men call Batieia and the gods the tomb of Myrine (Il. 2.813–814); the bird the gods call chalcis and men cymindis (Il. 14.290–291); the river the gods call Xanthos and men Scamander (Il. 20.74); and, in the Odyssey, the plant the gods call moly (Od. 10.305) and the rocks they call the Wanderers (Od. 12.61). Other Indo-European traditions have similar lists of words used by gods and by men, as in the Old Norse *Alvíssmál*.

## In Book 1

{{quote:764f0028-34e3-5fe4-a379-444aefc1f967}}

The Hundred-Hander ([[hundred-handed]], [[briareus]]) is Βριάρεως to the gods and Αἰγαίων to men. Βριάρεως suggests βριαρός (“strong, heavy”), and Αἰγαίων recalls the Aegean Sea; neither connection is certain. The next clause, “for he is mightier even than his father,” is obscure, since his father is not named. Ancient commentators suggested Poseidon, one of the three gods who wanted to bind Zeus, named just before; Hesiod makes Briareus instead Poseidon's son-in-law (*Theogony* 817–819).

The double naming lends the monster an air of ancient lore, fitting for a story that Thetis once told in Peleus' halls ([[binding-of-zeus]], [[mythological-paradigm]]).
