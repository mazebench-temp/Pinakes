## The pattern

ἔνθα τίνα πρῶτον τίνα δʼ ὕστατον ἐξενάριξεν, “then whom first, whom last did he slay”: a whole verse, and a question the narrator puts to himself. The answer is a list of names, two or three to a verse, with hardly more than a name each (see [[androktasia]]). The question gives a rush of killing the shape of a catalogue.

It is used three times:

- **Hector and Ares.** “Then whom first, whom last did they slay, Hector, the son of Priam, and bronze Ares?” (5.703–704). Six names follow, and the last, Oresbius, is given three verses of his own: he lived in Hyle on the shore of the Cephisian lake, much intent on his wealth, among the rich Boeotians (5.705–710; see [[oresbius]]).
- **Hector.** “Then whom first, whom last did he slay, Hector, son of Priam, when Zeus gave him glory?” (11.299–300). Nine names follow in three verses (11.301–303).
- **Patroclus.** The narrator turns to Patroclus himself: “whom first, whom last did you slay, Patroclus, when the gods called you to death?” Nine names again, and the rest of the Trojans turn to flight (16.692–697; see [[apostrophe]]).

The question is a small invocation without the Muse. When he wants to begin a list of the first to fight or to kill, the narrator can ask the Muses instead: “Tell me now, Muses, you who have your homes on Olympus, who was the first to come against Agamemnon” (11.218–220), and who of the Achaeans first took the bloody spoils (14.508–510). See [[invocation-of-the-muses]]. The verb of the formula is ἐξεναρίζω, “strip of his arms”, used here for killing pure and simple (see [[stripping-the-dead]]).
