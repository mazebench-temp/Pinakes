## The pattern

The episode is traditionally called the Epipolesis (ἐπιπώλησις, “the going round”; see [[the-review-of-the-army]]), from the verb of its frame, ἐπιπωλέομαι, “range through”: “he himself, on foot, ranged through the ranks of men” (4.231), and “So, like a master, he ranged through the ranks of men” (4.250). The review lies between the healing of Menelaus and the advance of the armies, and the narrator opens it by addressing the listener: “Then you would not have seen brilliant Agamemnon drowsing, nor cowering, nor unwilling to fight” (4.223–224; see [[apostrophe]]).

1. **On foot.** The king leaves his horses and chariot with his driver [[Eurymedon]], to be kept close for when weariness takes him, and goes on foot (4.226–231).
2. **Two kinds of men.** Those he sees eager he encourages, and those he sees holding back he rebukes (4.232–249). The verbs are iterative, θαρσύνεσκε and νεικείεσκε: he did this again and again. The two speeches that follow are samples of each (see [[exhortation]]).
3. **Five visits.** The poem then follows him to five leaders in turn. Each visit is built from recurring verses: the arrival, “he came to … going through the crowd of men” (4.251, 4.273), “he found” (4.293), or “he found … standing” (4.327–328, 4.365–366); what the leader is doing; the king's response, “seeing them … rejoiced” (4.255, 4.283, 4.311) or “seeing them … rebuked them” (4.336, 4.368); his speech, four times introduced “with winged words” (see [[winged-words]]); an answer, or none; and the departure, “the son of Atreus passed on, glad at heart” (4.272, 4.326), or “So saying he left them there and went on to others” (4.292, 4.364).
4. **Praise, then blame.** The first three visits end in praise: Idomeneus and the Cretans arming (4.251–272), the two Ajaxes, whose battalions come on like a storm cloud (4.273–292), and Nestor marshaling the Pylians (4.293–326). The last two end in rebuke: Menestheus and Odysseus, who stand waiting (4.327–364), and Diomedes and Sthenelus (4.365–421).

The rebukes are unjust. Menestheus and Odysseus are waiting because their men “had not yet heard the war cry” (4.331). Odysseus answers “looking at him darkly”, and Agamemnon smiles and takes his words back (4.349–363; see [[looking-darkly]] and [[smiling-at-him]]). Diomedes says nothing, “in awe of the rebuke of the king he revered”, and Sthenelus answers for him (4.401–410). Of the others, Idomeneus, Nestor and Odysseus answer the king; the Ajaxes, whom he does not urge at all (4.286), and Menestheus say nothing.

## In the poem

The review shows the Achaean leaders one by one before the first battle, as the Catalogue of Ships listed them (2.494–759) and Helen named them for Priam from the wall (3.161–244). Its verb has been heard before: Priam saw Odysseus ranging “like a ram through the ranks of men” (ἐπιπωλεῖται στίχας ἀνδρῶν, 3.196). Like the Catalogue and the view from the wall, the review is often read as part of the poem's retelling of the war's beginning in its tenth year (see [[the-view-from-the-wall]] and [[the-war-in-small]]). It also shows the king as a leader: generous to the men he trusts, quick to blame, and wrong in his blame. See [[agamemnon]], [[the-review-of-the-army]], [[praise-and-blame]] and [[battle-order]].

The Iliad has no second review of this kind. The nearest is Poseidon's round of the Achaean ranks in the likeness of Calchas, rousing first the two Ajaxes and then the others (13.43–124). The verse of the frame returns in Book 11 of Agamemnon and of Hector ranging through the ranks in battle (ἐπεπωλεῖτο στίχας ἀνδρῶν, 11.264, 11.540).
