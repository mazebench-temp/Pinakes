## The pattern

ἔνθʼ αὖτε, “then in turn,” + ἄλλα, “other things,” + ἐνόησε, “thought of, devised” (νοέω; [[noos]]), with the subject's name and epithet in the second half of the verse. What follows is a new act, which carries the story forward where it might have come to a halt. In the *Iliad* it is said twice of Achilles at the funeral of Patroclus, “Then brilliant swift-footed Achilles thought of another thing”: he cuts the lock of hair he had grown for the river Spercheius (23.140–151), and, when the pyre will not burn, he prays to the winds (23.192–198).

In the Odyssey it belongs above all to Athena, θεά, γλαυκῶπις Ἀθήνη: twice in the night of Telemachus' departure (*Odyssey* 2.382, 2.393); when Penelope has fallen asleep in her grief for her son, and the goddess makes a phantom to comfort her (*Odyssey* 4.795–796); when Nausicaa is about to go home, and the goddess contrives that Odysseus should wake and see her (*Odyssey* 6.112–113); when she pours sleep on Penelope before Penelope shows herself to the suitors (*Odyssey* 18.187–188); and on the night of the reunion, when she holds back the dawn and then brings it on (*Odyssey* 23.242–246, 23.344–348). Penelope has it once, when she resolves to show herself to the suitors who are plotting her son's death (*Odyssey* 16.409–411). See [[athena]] and [[gray-eyed]].
