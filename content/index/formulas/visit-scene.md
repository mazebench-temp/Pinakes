## The pattern

Walter Arend counted the arrival among the recurring scenes of Homer (*Die typischen Scenen bei Homer*, 1933). Its parts, of which any scene may use some or all:

1. **Arrival.** The visitor comes to the house, and the house may be described: Hephaestus' house is imperishable, starry and of bronze (18.369–371).
2. **Finding.** He finds (εὗρε) the one he seeks, and the verse says what that person was doing and who was nearby. Iris finds Helen weaving a purple web (3.125–128); the envoys find Achilles delighting his heart with the lyre, with Patroclus sitting opposite in silence (9.186–191); Thetis finds Hephaestus sweating at his bellows (18.372–373); Priam finds Achilles just done with eating and drinking, with two companions busy near him (24.472–476). In battle the finding follows a search: Athena and then Aeneas look for Pandarus, “if she might find him anywhere”, and find the son of Lycaon (4.88–89, 5.168–169).
3. **Reception.** The host rises, takes the visitor by the hand and leads him in (9.193–199, 11.645–646), or a woman of the house greets the visitor, clinging to her hand and calling her by name (18.384; see [[called-him-by-name]]). The host asks why the visitor has come, with τίπτε, “why?” (18.385), seats him (9.200, 18.389–390), and offers food and drink (9.201–221, 18.387).
4. **The errand.** The visitor says why he has come.
5. **Departure.**

The visitor in haste refuses to sit. Nestor rises and bids Patroclus sit, and Patroclus refuses: “There is no time to sit, old sir, nurtured by Zeus, and you will not persuade me” (οὐχ ἕδος ἐστί … οὐδέ με πείσεις, 11.648). He has an errand from Achilles, who is quick to blame. Iris, arriving at the house of the winds, where they are feasting, refuses the seat each of them offers her in the same words, “there is no time to sit”, because she must go on to the Ethiopians (23.200–207).

The Odyssey is built of such scenes, with the full reception of a guest (see [[xenia]]), and Steve Reece studied them as a single pattern (*The Stranger's Welcome*, 1993). In the Iliad visits are few and brief, and the war cuts them short: more than one visitor will not sit down.
