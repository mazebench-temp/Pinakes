## The pattern

δούπησεν, from δουπέω (“make a heavy, dull sound”), + δὲ πεσών (“and falling”), and ἀράβησε, from ἀραβέω (“clatter, rattle”), + δὲ τεύχεʼ ἐπʼ αὐτῷ (“and his armor upon him”). The line is all sound: the dull thud of the body and the rattle of the bronze. It follows the blow, often after [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]], and it closes a killing (see [[androktasia]]).

Its first use is the death of Democoon, the bastard son of Priam whom Odysseus kills to avenge Leucus:

{{quote:83460d51-3560-5029-bf71-98aeb5b16aaa}}

Then the Trojan champions and Hector give ground (4.505).

δοῦπος, the noun of the verb, is also the roar of the battle: in the simile that follows the armies' clash, a shepherd far off in the mountains hears the δοῦπος of two torrents meeting (4.455; see [[torrents-meeting]]). The verb came to mean simply “fall, die”: Mecisteus went to Thebes “when Oedipus had fallen” (δεδουπότος Οἰδιπόδαο, 23.679).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The line returns for Odius, killed by Agamemnon (5.42), for Deicoon, whose belt Agamemnon's spear pierces (5.540), and for Euphorbus, killed by Menelaus (17.50), among others. Its second half closes the fall of Pandarus himself from his chariot, “and he fell headlong from the chariot, and his armor clattered upon him” (ἤριπε δʼ ἐξ ὀχέων, ἀράβησε δὲ τεύχεʼ ἐπʼ αὐτῷ, 5.294): the archer who broke the truce falls with the same sound as the men of the first battle. In the Odyssey the whole line is used once, for Eupeithes, the father of Antinous, whom Laertes kills in the last fight of the poem (Odyssey 24.525).
