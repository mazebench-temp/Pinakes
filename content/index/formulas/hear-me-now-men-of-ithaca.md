## The pattern

κέκλυτε, the reduplicated aorist imperative of κλύω, “hear,” + δὴ νῦν, “now,” + μευ, “me,” + Ἰθακήσιοι, the people addressed ([[ithacans]]), + ὅττι κεν εἴπω, “whatever I say.” It is the Ithacan form of the Iliad's κέκλυτέ μευ, with which a speaker addresses a gathered host by its names, “Hear me, Trojans and well-greaved Achaeans” or “Hear me, Trojans and Dardanians and allies” (*Iliad* 3.86, 3.456, 7.67, 7.348), and Zeus the gods, “Hear me, all you gods and all you goddesses” (*Iliad* 8.5; [[hear-me]]).

It opens five speeches, all in the two assemblies of Ithaca. In *Odyssey* 2 the old hero Aegyptius, the reader of birds Halitherses and Odysseus' companion Mentor speak with it (*Odyssey* 2.25, 2.161, 2.229). At the end of the poem, when the kinsmen of the suitors gather, the herald Medon begins with its first half and goes on, “for it was not against the will of the immortal gods that Odysseus devised these deeds” (*Odyssey* 24.443–444), and Halitherses speaks the whole verse again, brought in by the verses that introduced him in Book 2, the second of them changed (*Odyssey* 24.451–454; 2.157–160; [[with-good-intent]], [[halitherses]]). No suitor uses it.
