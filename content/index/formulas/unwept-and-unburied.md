## The pattern

ἄκλαυτος = ἀ- (“not”) + κλαίω (“weep, lament”): “unwept”; ἄθαπτος = ἀ- + θάπτω (“bury”): “unburied.” The two words stand side by side, once joined by καί, and name together the two things the living owe the dead, the lament and the burial (see [[honoring-the-dead]], [[lament]] and [[tomb]]). Homer has the pair three times, always of the body of a companion that still waits for its rites, and neither word is found anywhere else in the poems, except that ἄκλαυτος alone is said once of the living, “without tears,” when Proteus tells Menelaus, “I do not think / you will be long without tears, when you have heard it all” (*Odyssey* 4.493–494).

The first use is Achilles', over the body of Hector, when he turns from the thought of attacking the city to the dead friend who still waits at the ships:

{{quote:5210dc68-2e8b-54c9-b1ba-77448c3a54d4}}

The dead man so left cannot cross into Hades. The soul of Patroclus comes to Achilles that night and asks to be buried “as quickly as may be,” since the souls keep him away and he wanders through the wide-gated house of Hades (*Iliad* 23.71–74; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]] and [[wide-gated]]). In the Odyssey the pair returns twice in one scene, of Elpenor, the companion who fell from Circe's roof and was left behind in her hall; Merry explains from the ghost of Patroclus why Elpenor's soul is the first to come to Odysseus: unburied, it could not pass within the gates (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.51; see [[elpenor]] and [[the-death-of-elpenor]]).

## Outside Homer

Merry quotes Plutarch's *Table Talk* (book 9, question 5, in his citation) for the same belief: of the souls that Homer names in his Nekyia, Elpenor's, not yet mingled with those in Hades because his body was not buried, wanders as if on a borderland (Merry on 11.51).
