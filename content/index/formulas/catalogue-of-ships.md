## The pattern

After the invocation of the Muses (see [[invocation-of-the-muses]]) the poet names the leaders and their ships, contingent by contingent. Each entry is built from the same three parts, in varying order:

1. **The leaders**, with the verbs of command: ἦρχον, ἡγεμόνευε, ἡγεῖτο (“led”). “The Boeotians were led by Peneleos and Leitus” (2.494).
2. **The places**, in clauses of the form “those who held (ἔχον, εἶχον) X and lived in (ἐνέμοντο, ναῖον, ἀμφενέμοντο) Y”, many with an epithet: “rocky Aulis”, “Thisbe of the many doves”, “grassy Haliartus”.
3. **The ships**: “and with him followed forty black ships” (τῷ δʼ ἅμα τεσσαράκοντα μέλαιναι νῆες ἕποντο), or “thirty hollow ships were ranged in line” (τοῖς δὲ τριήκοντα γλαφυραὶ νέες ἐστιχόωντο). The first formula closes twelve entries.

{{quote:e2e162b2-46f3-51cc-a0e9-69c735652e14}}

Short words of transition, αὖ, αὖτε, αὖθʼ (“in turn”), carry the list from one entry to the next. The whole is framed by the question to the Muses, “who were the leaders and lords of the Danaans” (2.487), and its answer: “These, then, were the leaders and lords of the Danaans” (2.760).

## Order

The list moves through Greece as a traveler would, from neighbor to neighbor, in three great circuits. It begins in Boeotia and goes through central Greece (Phocis, Locris, Euboea, Athens, Salamis), round the Peloponnese (Argos, Mycenae, Lacedaemon, Pylos, Arcadia, Elis) and out to the western islands and Aetolia. Then it turns to the islands of the south and east: Crete, Rhodes, Syme, Cos and its neighbors. Last comes Thessaly and the north, from Achilles' realm to the Magnetes. Why it starts with the Boeotians, who play little part in the rest of the poem, is not said; the usual explanation is that the fleet gathered at Aulis in Boeotia.

The total is the usual modern count. Agamemnon brings the most ships, a hundred, and lends ships to the Arcadians besides (2.612–614); Nireus brings three. Twice the poet gives the crews: a hundred and twenty young men in each Boeotian ship (2.509–510), fifty rowers, all archers, in each of Philoctetes' (2.719–720). Thucydides later worked out the size of the army from these two figures (see below and [[numbers-and-counting]]). Nearly 180 places are named, depending on how one counts.

## Digressions

Some entries open into a story of a few lines. There are births and ancestors: the sons of Ares born to Astyoche (2.512–515), Erechtheus raised by Athena (2.547–551), Tlepolemus' flight after killing his great-uncle (2.653–670), the Lapiths' victory over the Centaurs (2.740–744). There are the Muses' punishment of Thamyris (2.594–600) and the river Titaressus, a branch of the Styx (2.751–755). And there are glimpses of the war itself: Achilles lying idle and soon to rise (2.686–694), Protesilaus, the first to die at Troy (2.698–709), Philoctetes left on Lemnos (2.721–728). Nireus, the most handsome man at Troy after Achilles, gets three lines that begin with his name, and is never mentioned again (2.671–675).

## The Catalogue and the Iliad

The Catalogue counts ships, not men drawn up on the plain, and its verbs of leading and following suit a fleet setting out. It lists a leader who died at the landing and another left behind on Lemnos, and it gives large forces to men who hardly appear in the poem while Odysseus and Ajax bring twelve ships each. Many scholars have concluded that a list first made for the gathering at Aulis has been fitted, with a few added lines, to the tenth year of the war. The frame is the poem's own: the invocation before it, and after it the question who was best, answered with a reminder that Achilles is keeping up his wrath (2.760–779).

Its date and origin have been argued for two centuries. Some scholars hold that it preserves a record of Mycenaean Greece, since many of its places were inhabited in the Late Bronze Age and several were deserted afterwards (D. L. Page, *History and the Homeric Iliad*, 1959; R. Hope Simpson and J. F. Lazenby, *The Catalogue of the Ships in Homer's Iliad*, 1970). Others see it as the work of oral poets of a much later age, put together from traditional names and contemporary knowledge (E. Visser, *Homers Katalog der Schiffe*, 1997), and point to kingdoms that are hard to square with the rest of the Iliad and with the palace states of the Linear B tablets. Most would now agree that it holds old material reworked over a long tradition; how old, and how it was handed down, are still disputed (see [[catalogue-and-mycenae]]).

## Outside Homer

Ancient readers called it the *Boeotia*, after its first entry (see [[the-boeotia]]). It was read as evidence. Thucydides worked out the size of the army from its crews (1.10.4; see [[thucydides-and-the-catalogue]]). In 480 BCE an Athenian envoy cited the praise of Menestheus to claim the command of the Greek fleet (Herodotus 7.161; see [[herodotus-menestheus]]), and one line, on Ajax's ships stationed beside the Athenians (2.558), was suspected in antiquity of having been added to support the Athenian claim to Salamis (see [[solon-and-salamis]]). Strabo followed it place by place in his books on Greece (see [[strabo-and-the-catalogue]]). Aristotle took Nireus' three lines as his example of how repetition makes a man seem great (*Rhetoric* 3.12, 1414a2–7; see [[aristotle-nireus]]).

Poets imitated it. The chorus of Euripides' *Iphigenia at Aulis* describes the heroes at their games and counts the ships (164–302; see [[euripides-catalogue]]), and Virgil opens his catalogue of the Italian forces with an invocation modeled on Homer's (*Aeneid* 7.641–817; see [[virgil-catalogue-of-italians]]).
