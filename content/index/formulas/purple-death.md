## The pattern

τὸν δὲ κατʼ ὄσσε / ἔλλαβε πορφύρεος θάνατος καὶ μοῖρα κραταιή: κατʼ ὄσσε, “down over his two eyes”, + ἔλλαβε, “seized” (λαμβάνω, with the λ doubled for the meter), + πορφύρεος θάνατος, “purple death”, + καὶ μοῖρα κραταιή, “and mighty fate”. The phrase begins at the end of one verse and fills the next. Like [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]], it tells death as something that comes over the eyes.

Its first use ends the last of the single kills that open Book 5. Eurypylus runs down Hypsenor, son of Dolopion, the priest of the Scamander:

The verse comes twice more, and the setting is the same each time: a sword stroke and blood. Ajax son of Oileus cuts Cleobulus' neck, “and the whole sword grew warm with blood” (16.330–334), and Achilles splits Echeclus' head, with the same half-verse about the sword (20.474–477). The color follows the blood in each scene: the arm that falls “bloody” (αἱματόεσσα) to the plain, the sword warm with it. See [[hypsenor]], [[eurypylus]] and [[sword]].

What πορφύρεος means here is not certain. It is the color of fine dyed cloth (3.126), but also of the sea wave (1.482), of the rainbow (17.547) and of a cloud (17.551), and the verb πορφύρω means “surge, heave”, of the sea and of a troubled heart (14.16, 21.551). Some take purple death as the color of blood; others as the darkness that covers a dying man's eyes, like the black night of other formulas; others, from the verb, as death surging over him. See [[purple]].

## Death and mighty fate

The second half-verse, θάνατος καὶ μοῖρα κραταιή, “death and mighty fate”, belongs to a larger family. Patroclus, dying, tells Hector that death and mighty fate already stand close beside him (16.853), and Thetis tells Achilles the same of himself (24.132). Achilles tells Lycaon that over him too hang death and mighty fate (21.110). “Mighty fate” alone rouses Tlepolemus against Sarpedon (5.629), and it spun Hector's thread at his birth (24.209–210). See [[moira]] and [[thanatos]].
