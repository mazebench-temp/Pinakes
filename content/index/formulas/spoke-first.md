## The pattern

τόν, the one addressed, + πρότερος, “the first of the two,” in the feminine προτέρη, + προσέειπε, “spoke to,” and then the speaker's name with its epithets to the end of the verse. The other answers with a verse of reply ([[answering-him]], [[then-answered]]).

In the Iliad the verse comes nine times, and four times it follows the verse of two warriors' approach, “And when they had come close, advancing against each other” ([[when-they-came-close]]): Diomedes speaks first to Glaucus (*Iliad* 6.121–122), Achilles to Aeneas and to Asteropaeus (*Iliad* 20.176–177, 21.148–149), and Hector to Achilles before their last fight (*Iliad* 22.248–249). The son of Lycaon speaks first to Diomedes as he and Aeneas drive up (*Iliad* 5.274–276), and Patroclus to Achilles when he comes out of the hut at his call (*Iliad* 11.604–605). It brings in meetings of friends and kin as well: Paris overtakes his brother Hector just as he turns from his wife (*Iliad* 6.515–517), Menelaus finds Agamemnon arming at night by the stern of his ship (*Iliad* 10.34–36), and Priam speaks first when he and Achilles have had their fill of gazing at each other (*Iliad* 24.633–634).

In the Odyssey the gray-eyed goddess speaks first to Telemachus as they step ashore at Pylos (*Odyssey* 3.12–13). The last book has the same opening with another verb, τὸν προτέρη ψυχὴ προσεφώνεε, “the shade spoke first,” when the shade of Achilles meets Agamemnon's in the underworld, and Agamemnon's the suitor Amphimedon's (*Odyssey* 24.23, 24.105).
