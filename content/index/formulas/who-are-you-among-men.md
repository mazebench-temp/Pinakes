## The pattern

τίς (“who”) + πόθεν (“from where”) + εἰς ἀνδρῶν (“are you among men”): Merry takes the first words not as two distinct questions but as two blended into one (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.170). The second half of the verse asks the same of the stranger's home and family, πόθι τοι πόλις ἠδὲ τοκῆες, “where are your city and your parents?”

The whole verse is asked six times in the Odyssey: by Telemachus of the goddess in the likeness of Mentes (*Odyssey* 1.170), by Circe of Odysseus, whom her drug has not changed (*Odyssey* 10.325), by Eumaeus of the beggar who is Odysseus (*Odyssey* 14.187), by the seer Theoclymenus of Telemachus at Pylos (*Odyssey* 15.264), by Penelope of the beggar (*Odyssey* 19.105), and by Laertes of his son in the orchard (*Odyssey* 24.298). Arete asks the first half with a second of her own, “who gave you these clothes?”, for she knows the clothes (*Odyssey* 7.238).

The question belongs to the welcome of a guest, and the host may join to it other formulas of questioning, [[give-me-an-exact-account]] and [[tell-me-this-truly]], and three verses about the ship that brought the stranger, which end “For I do not think that you came here on foot” (*Odyssey* 1.171–173; Eumaeus has the same verses, *Odyssey* 14.188–190; see [[visit-scene]] and [[xenia]]). The answer is more often a false tale than the truth: the goddess answers as Mentes (*Odyssey* 1.179–212), and Odysseus answers Eumaeus, Penelope and Laertes with invented lives (*Odyssey* 14.192–359, 19.165–202, 24.303–314), while Telemachus tells Theoclymenus the truth (*Odyssey* 15.266–270).

In the Iliad only the first half is found, and in battle. On the bank of the river Achilles asks Asteropaeus, “Who are you among men, and from where, that you have dared to come against me?” (21.150), and the answer is a genealogy ([[genealogy-in-battle]]).
