## The pattern

ὧδε (“thus”) δέ τις (“and someone, a man”) + εἴπεσκεν, the iterative past of εἶπον (“would say, kept saying”) + ἰδὼν ἐς πλησίον ἄλλον (“looking at another, his neighbor”). The suffix -σκ- makes the speech typical rather than single: many said this, or something like it, and one voice speaks for them all. The speaker has no name. His words are few, and they react to something the crowd has just seen: a sign from a god, a punishment, a fallen enemy. A closing verse may round the speech off, either in the singular again, ὣς ἄρα τις εἴπεσκε, “so a man would say” (4.85, 17.423, 22.375), or with a plural verb, ὣς ἄρʼ ἔφαν, “so they spoke” (7.181, 7.206). Book 2 names the group: ὣς φάσαν ἣ πληθύς, “so said the crowd” (2.278), a closing found nowhere else in Homer.

These are among the few places where the Iliad lets the army or the people speak. Besides such speeches, which are reported as spoken, there are imagined ones, in which a hero pictures what “someone will say” of him in time to come (καί ποτέ τις εἴπῃσι: Hector at 6.459–462 and 7.87–91). Irene de Jong studied both kinds together (“The Voice of Anonymity: tis-Speeches in the Iliad”, *Eranos*, 1987).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The whole verse occurs three times in the Iliad. Besides 2.271, it introduces the armies' wonder when Athena comes down among them like a shooting star: someone asks whether war will come again, or whether Zeus is making friendship between the two sides (4.81–84). And it introduces the taunt of the Achaeans as they stab the body of Hector, a speech that opens, like the crowd's in Book 2, with ὢ πόποι (see [[o-popoi]]): Hector is softer to handle now than when he set the ships on fire (22.372–375). With another second half the verse brings in the prayers of both armies at the truce of Book 3 (3.297, 3.319), the army's prayers before the duel of Ajax and Hector (7.178, 7.201), and a speech of the Achaeans over the body of Patroclus, which a Trojan voice answers (17.414–423).

The Odyssey has the whole verse six times. The gods laugh at Ares and Aphrodite caught in Hephaestus' net, and then one says to his neighbor that evil deeds do not prosper (Odyssey 8.326–329): laughter and comment follow each other as they do after the beating of Thersites (2.270–271). The other five are spoken by Odysseus' crew (10.37), by the Phaeacians (13.167) and by the suitors (18.72, 18.400, 21.396).
