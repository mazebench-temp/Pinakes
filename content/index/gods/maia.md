## Name

Μαῖα (Maia); Homer has only the genitive, Μαιάδος. The same letters, lowercase, are a word of address for an old woman of the house, which the translation renders “nurse”: Telemachus calls Eurycleia μαῖα when he bids her draw wine for his voyage (*Odyssey* 2.349), and so do Odysseus and Penelope in the later books (*Odyssey* 19.482, 23.11; see [[eurycleia]]). The goddess is not that word: the index claims only the genitive of the name.

## Family

Mother of [[Hermes]]. Homer gives her no father and no story, and names her only in her son's title, Μαιάδος υἱεῖ, “son of Maia” (*Odyssey* 14.435). Elsewhere the poems name Hermes by his father, “Hermes, son of Zeus” (*Odyssey* 8.335; see [[zeus]]), as they name Apollo by both parents, “the son of Leto and of Zeus” (*Iliad* 1.9; see [[leto]]).

## Outside Homer

[[hesiod|Hesiod]] lists her among the women who bore children to Zeus: “Maia, daughter of Atlas, bore to Zeus glorious Hermes, herald of the immortals, going up into his holy bed” (Ζηνὶ δʼ ἄρʼ Ἀτλαντὶς Μαίη τέκε κύδιμον Ἑρμῆν, *Theogony* 938–939; see [[atlas]] and [[the-loves-of-zeus]]). The *Astronomy* ascribed to him counts her among the seven Pleiades, the daughters of Atlas, and says that on the mountains of Cyllene she bore Hermes, the herald of the gods (*Astronomy* fr. 1 Evelyn-White; see [[pleiades]] and [[cyllene]]).

The *Homeric Hymn to Hermes* begins with her. Maia, “a nymph with lovely tresses”, revered, shunned the company of the blessed gods and lived in a deeply shadowed cave, where Zeus lay with her in the dead of night while sweet sleep held Hera, unseen by gods and men; she bore him on the fourth day of the month (*Hymn to Hermes* 1–9, 19). The shorter hymn to him repeats the verses and calls her “the daughter of Atlas” (*Hymn* 18.3–9). In the Hymns Hermes is Διὸς καὶ Μαιάδος υἱός, “son of Zeus and Maia”, again and again, and often Μαιάδος υἱός alone, the title of Homer's single verse (*Hymn to Hermes* 1, 73, 235, 424, 498, 521, 574, 579; *Hymn* 18.10, 29.7). Tragedy keeps the name for the god's title: he is “Maia's son” in Aeschylus' *Libation Bearers* (812) and in Euripides' *Medea* (759), *Rhesus* (216) and *Orestes* (997), and in the prologue of Euripides' *Ion* he introduces himself: Atlas “begot Maia, who bore me, Hermes, to greatest Zeus” (*Ion* 1–4; see [[aeschylus]] and [[euripides]]).

Cicero's Cotta, counting several Mercuries, makes the third the son of the third Jupiter and of Maia, and the one by whom, they say, Penelope bore Pan (*On the Nature of the Gods* 3.56; see [[cicero]], [[penelope]] and [[pan]]).
