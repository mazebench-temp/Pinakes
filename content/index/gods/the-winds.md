## Etymology

ἄνεμος (anemos), “wind”, is related to Latin *animus* and *anima*, “breath”, “soul”. Homer speaks of the wind's πνοιή, its “breath”, with the verb ἄημι, “blow”, and of its violence in the storm words θύελλα, ἄελλα and λαῖλαψ (see [[the-storm-wind-on-the-sea]]).

## In Homer

**Gods.** Four winds have names: [[boreas|Boreas]], the North Wind; [[zephyrus|Zephyrus]], the West Wind; [[notus|Notus]], the South Wind; and [[eurus|Eurus]], the East Wind. All four fall on Odysseus' raft together in Poseidon's storm: “The East Wind and the South Wind clashed together, and the stormy West Wind / and the North Wind, born of the clear sky, rolling up a great wave” (*Odyssey* 5.295–296). In the Iliad the winds are gods who can be prayed to. When the pyre of Patroclus will not burn, Achilles prays to two of them, the North Wind and the West Wind, promises them fine sacrifices and pours libations from a golden cup (*Iliad* 23.192–197), and Iris goes as messenger to the winds, who are “all together in the house of the stormy West Wind, / feasting at a banquet” (23.199–201):

{{quote:1b141032-8c45-58bc-89fe-8d07c0818e7e}}

They rise “with a wondrous din, driving the clouds in confusion before them”, blow on the pyre all night, and at dawn “the winds went back again to go home / over the Thracian sea” (23.212–230; see [[iris]], [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]] and [[thrace]]).

The gods rouse and still them. Zeus roused the North Wind against Odysseus' ships (*Odyssey* 9.67–68); Poseidon “spurred on all the storms / of winds of every kind” (*Odyssey* 5.292–293), and Odysseus says of him that he “roused the winds against me and bound up my path” (*Odyssey* 7.272). Athena, when Poseidon had gone, “bound up the paths of the other winds, / and bade them all cease and lie down to rest”, and roused the North Wind alone to carry Odysseus on (*Odyssey* 5.383–385); she had sent Telemachus a fresh West Wind (*Odyssey* 2.420–421); Calypso sends Odysseus off with a fair wind (*Odyssey* 5.268), and Circe sends a following wind behind his ship (*Odyssey* 11.6–8, 12.148–150). In *Odyssey* 10 the winds are given into the keeping of [[aeolus|Aeolus]], “dispenser of the winds, / both to still and to rouse whichever he wishes” (*Odyssey* 10.21–22; see [[dispenser-of-the-winds]] and the Book 10 note). W. W. Merry observes that Homer's Aeolus is not the king of the winds that Virgil made him, only their manager by the leave of Zeus, and that other gods send a favoring wind when they please (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.21). Olympus alone is out of their reach: “It is not shaken by winds” (*Odyssey* 6.43; see [[olympus]]).

**Weather.** The similes of the Iliad use the winds as weather. The Danaans stand like clouds set motionless on the mountain tops “while the might of the North Wind sleeps, and of the other / violent winds, which scatter the shadowy clouds / with their shrill blasts when they blow” (*Iliad* 5.524–526; see [[clouds-on-the-mountains]]); “The wind scatters the leaves on the ground” as the generations of men pass (6.147; see [[the-generations-of-leaves]]); the Trojans come on “like a blast of grievous winds” that falls on the sea under the thunder of Zeus (13.795–799; see [[the-storm-and-the-waves]]); the East Wind and the South Wind heap up the waves of the Icarian sea and strive in a mountain forest (2.144–146, 16.765–769; see [[waves-of-the-icarian-sea]] and [[the-winds-in-the-forest]]); and the North Wind and the West Wind, blowing from Thrace, stir up the fish-filled sea as the hearts of the Achaeans are torn (9.4–8; see [[two-winds-from-thrace]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes three of the winds children of the Dawn: “Dawn bore to Astraeus the strong-hearted winds, / clear-blowing Zephyrus, and Boreas of the swift path, / and Notus” (*Theogony* 378–380; see [[eos]] and [[hesiod]]). These three are of the gods' race, a great boon to mortals; from Typhoeus come the other, random winds, which fall on the misty sea, scatter ships and destroy sailors, and on land ruin the works of men with dust and turmoil (*Theogony* 869–880). In the Persian War the Delphians were told by their oracle to pray to the winds, “for they would be great allies of Greece”, and set up an altar to them at Thyia with sacrifices (Herodotus 7.178); the Athenians called on Boreas (7.189; see [[boreas]]); and when a storm wrecked the Persian fleet off Magnesia the Magi stopped it on the fourth day with victims and spells chanted to the wind, “or perhaps it subsided of its own accord” (7.191; see [[herodotus]]). Virgil's Aeolus is a king who holds the struggling winds in a vast cave with chains and a prison; the almighty father hid them under mountains, for fear that they would sweep away sea, land and sky, and gave them a king who would know by a fixed compact how to curb them and how to slacken their reins when bidden. At Juno's request he strikes the hollow mountain with his spear, and the winds rush out to raise the storm against Aeneas (*Aeneid* 1.52–83; see [[virgil-juno-and-aeolus]] and [[virgil]]).
