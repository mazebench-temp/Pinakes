Βορέας (Homer's form is Βορέης), the north wind. The Iliad calls it αἰθρηγενής, “born of the bright upper air” (15.171, 19.358): a cold wind that clears the sky. With Zephyrus it blows “from Thrace” (9.5). The name has no agreed etymology.

## In Homer

The North Wind comes twice in Book 5, first in a simile and then as a breath of life. The Danaans await the Trojans like clouds that Zeus sets motionless on the mountain tops in windless weather, “while the might of the North Wind sleeps, and of the other violent winds” (5.524–525). When the spear is drawn from Sarpedon's thigh and he faints, “the breath of the North Wind blowing on him revived his spirit” (5.697–698; see [[sarpedon]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). The poem does not say that a god sent it.

Boreas and Zephyrus stir up the sea together in a simile for the Achaeans' despair (9.4–8; see [[zephyrus]]). Zeus reminds Hera that she drove Heracles across the sea to Cos with storms and the North Wind (15.26–28; see [[heracles]]). Boreas loved the mares of Erichthonius in the likeness of a dark-maned stallion, and their twelve colts ran over the tips of the grain without breaking it, and over the breakers of the sea (20.221–229). Achilles prays to Boreas and Zephyrus to make the pyre of Patroclus burn; Iris fetches them from a feast in the house of Zephyrus, they fan the fire all night, and at dawn they go home across the Thracian sea (23.192–230).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes Boreas a son of Astraeus and Eos, the Dawn, and a brother of Zephyrus and Notus (*Theogony* 378–380). At Athens he was the husband of Oreithyia, daughter of Erechtheus, and so the Athenians' son-in-law. Before the sea battle at Artemisium they prayed to him to destroy the Persian ships, and afterward they built him a shrine by the Ilissus (Herodotus 7.189).
