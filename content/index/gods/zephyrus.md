Ζέφυρος (Zephyros), the west wind. The name has been connected with ζόφος, the darkness of the west, where the sun sets; the connection is not certain.

{{quote:57a00aad-4926-58f3-aff0-4d594febbe5a}}

λάβρος ἐπαιγίζων (“blowing hard and wild”): ἐπαιγίζω suggests a sudden squall. The deep grain bows all one way, as the assembly moves all at once toward the ships. See [[wind-in-the-wheat]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the similes of the Iliad the West Wind drives a cloud over the sea, raises the waves on the shore, darkens the sea with its ripple and batters the clouds of the South Wind (4.275–276, 4.422–423, 7.63–64, 11.305–306). Zephyrus is the father of Achilles' immortal horses, Xanthus and Balius, by the Harpy Podarge (16.148–151). When Achilles prays to the winds to kindle the pyre of Patroclus, Iris finds them feasting in the house of Zephyrus (23.192–230). In the Odyssey's ideal places he is gentle: his breath ripens the fruit in the orchard of Alcinous (*Odyssey* 7.118–119), and in the Elysian plain Oceanus always sends up his breezes, blowing shrill, to refresh men (*Odyssey* 4.567–568; see [[elysium]]). At his breath Proteus comes out of the sea at midday, hidden in the dark ripple (*Odyssey* 4.402; see [[proteus]]). At sea he is the wind of a voyage when a god sends him: Athena sends “a fresh West Wind” for Telemachus' ship (*Odyssey* 2.420–421), and Aeolus sends his breath to carry Odysseus' ships home (*Odyssey* 10.25–26).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes him a son of Eos and Astraeus (*Theogony* 378–380). In later poetry he became the mild wind of spring, the Latin Favonius.
