## Etymology

νύμφη is also the ordinary word for a young woman of an age to marry, or a bride. Iris, in the likeness of Laodice, calls Helen νύμφα φίλη, “dear bride” (3.130), and the brides led through the streets in the wedding on Achilles' shield are νύμφαι (18.492). The goddesses are the young women of the wild places. The origin of the word is uncertain.

## Descriptions

Homer names kinds of nymphs by the places they live in. A νηΐς or νηϊάς, a Naiad, is a nymph of springs and running water (6.22; *Odyssey* 13.104), a name usually derived from νάω, “flow”; the Suda glosses Ναΐδες as “springs, or nymphs who dwell by the streams” (Suda ν 14). The ὀρεστιάδες are nymphs “of the mountains” (ὄρος), the Oreads; the word occurs only at 6.420. Eumaeus prays to the κρηναῖαι, the nymphs “of the spring” (κρήνη, *Odyssey* 17.240), and the nymphs who play with Artemis are ἀγρονόμοι, “who haunt the fields” (ἀγρός, “field”, + νέμομαι, “range over”, *Odyssey* 6.106). They are ἐυπλόκαμοι, “of the lovely tresses” (*Odyssey* 12.132), like the Trojan women “with lovely tresses” of 6.380 (see [[with-lovely-tresses]]).

## Family

Homer makes them daughters of Zeus: κοῦραι Διὸς αἰγιόχοιο, “daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus” (6.420; *Odyssey* 6.105, 9.154), a title they share with the Muses (2.598; see [[muses]]), and Odysseus and Eumaeus call on them as κοῦραι Διός, “daughters of Zeus” (*Odyssey* 13.356, 17.240). Few are named. Abarbarea, a Naiad, bore twin sons to Bucolion (6.21–22; see [[abarbarea]]). In the Odyssey the nymph [[thoosa|Thoosa]] bore the Cyclops Polyphemus to Poseidon (*Odyssey* 1.71–73), and the nymphs [[phaethusa|Phaethusa]] and [[lampetie|Lampetie]], daughters of Helios, herd their father's cattle (*Odyssey* 12.131–136). [[calypso|Calypso]] is called a nymph again and again (νύμφη πότνια, “the lady nymph”, *Odyssey* 1.14), and [[circe|Circe]] once (*Odyssey* 10.543).

## Elsewhere in Homer

**Mothers of warriors.** In the Iliad nymphs are the mothers of men from the Troad and Asia Minor, born beside rivers and lakes to fathers who kept herds. Satnius was the son of “a noble Naiad nymph” and of Enops, who herded cattle on the banks of the Satnioeis (14.443–445; see [[satnius]], [[enops]] and [[satnioeis]]). Iphition's mother was a Naiad, and he was born beneath snowy Tmolus (20.382–385; see [[tmolus]]). The Gygaean lake bore the Maeonian leaders Mesthles and Antiphus (2.864–865; see [[gygaean-lake]]). All of them fight for Troy, and Satnius and Iphition, like the sons of Abarbarea, fall to Achaean spears.

**Among the gods.** When Zeus calls the gods to assembly in *Iliad* 20, no river stays away but Oceanus, nor any of the nymphs “who dwell in the lovely groves / and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows” (20.7–9). Niobe, turned to stone, broods on her sorrows on Sipylus, where, men say, the nymphs who dance around the Achelous have their resting places (24.614–617).

**Worship.** On Ithaca the Naiads have a cave at the head of the harbor of Phorcys, where Odysseus used to offer them hecatombs and where he prays to them when he comes home (*Odyssey* 13.102–112, 13.347–360; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]] and [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]). Eumaeus sets aside a share of the feast for the nymphs and Hermes (*Odyssey* 14.435–436), and prays to the nymphs at the town's spring, where every traveler made offerings at their altar (*Odyssey* 17.204–211, 17.240–246).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes the mountains “the graceful haunts of the goddess nymphs who dwell in the wooded glens” (*Theogony* 129–130). The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* describes the mountain nymphs who will rear Aeneas. They belong neither with mortals nor with immortals; they live long and dance with the gods, the Sileni and “the keen-eyed slayer of Argus” lie with them in the depths of their lovely caves, and firs and oaks spring up with them when they are born and wither when they die (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 256–273; see [[hermes]]). Verses quoted as Hesiod's reckon their span at ten lives of the phoenix: “and we, the nymphs with lovely hair, daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus, ten phoenixes” (Hesiod, fragment 304 Merkelbach–West, in Plutarch, *On the Obsolescence of Oracles* 11, 415c). Another *Hymn* has them nurse the infant Dionysus at Nysa (*Hymn* 26.3–10; see [[dionysus]]). Their cult was everywhere in Greece, at springs and in caves, often with Pan and Hermes. In the *Hymn to Pan* the mountain nymphs sing and dance with Pan, the son of Hermes, and tell how Hermes came to Arcadia and, god though he was, tended sheep for a mortal man for love of a nymph, the daughter of Dryops (*Hymn* 19.19–37; see [[pan]] and [[hermes]]). In Euripides' *Electra* Aegisthus, on his estate by the horse pastures, is preparing a feast for the Nymphs with the sacrifice of a bull; he invites two passing strangers to share it and prays to the “Nymphs of the rocks”, while one of the strangers, Orestes, prays in silence for the opposite (*Electra* 623–627, 783–810; see [[euripides]]). The Neoplatonist Porphyry read the cave of the Naiads on Ithaca as an image of the cosmos in his essay *On the Cave of the Nymphs* (see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]).
