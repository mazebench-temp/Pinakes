## Etymology

νύμφη is also the ordinary word for a young woman of an age to marry, or a bride. Iris, in the likeness of Laodice, calls Helen νύμφα φίλη, “dear bride” (3.130), and the brides led through the streets in the wedding on Achilles' shield are νύμφαι (18.492). The goddesses are the young women of the wild places. The origin of the word is uncertain.

## Descriptions

Homer names kinds of nymphs by the places they live in. A νηΐς or νηϊάς, a Naiad, is a nymph of springs and running water (6.22; Od. 13.104), a name usually derived from νάω, “flow”. The ὀρεστιάδες are nymphs “of the mountains” (ὄρος), the Oreads; the word occurs only at 6.420. Eumaeus prays to the κρηναῖαι, the nymphs “of the spring” (κρήνη, Od. 17.240), and the nymphs who play with Artemis are ἀγρονόμοι, “who haunt the countryside” (ἀγρός, “field”, + νέμομαι, “range over”, Od. 6.106). They are ἐϋπλόκαμοι, “with lovely tresses” (Od. 12.132), like the Trojan women of 6.380 (see [[with-lovely-tresses]]).

## Family

Homer makes them daughters of Zeus: κοῦραι Διὸς αἰγιόχοιο, “daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus” (6.420; Od. 6.105, 9.154), a title they share with the Muses (2.598; see [[muses]]). Few are named. Abarbarea, a Naiad, bore twin sons to Bucolion (6.21–22; see [[abarbarea]]). In the Odyssey the nymph Thoosa bore the Cyclops Polyphemus to Poseidon (Od. 1.71–73), and the nymphs Phaethusa and Lampetie, daughters of Helios, herd their father's cattle (Od. 12.131–136). Calypso is called a nymph again and again (νύμφη πότνια, “the lady nymph”, Od. 1.14), and Circe once (Od. 10.543).

## Elsewhere in Homer

**Mothers of warriors.** In the Iliad nymphs are the mothers of men from the Troad and Asia Minor, born beside rivers and lakes to fathers who kept herds. Satnius was the son of “a blameless Naiad” and of Enops, who herded cattle on the banks of the Satnioeis (14.443–445; see [[satnioeis]]). Iphition's mother was a Naiad, and he was born beneath snowy Tmolus (20.382–385; see [[tmolus]]). The Gygaean lake bore the Maeonian leaders Mesthles and Antiphus (2.864–865; see [[gygaean-lake]]). All of them fight for Troy, and Satnius and Iphition, like the sons of Abarbarea, fall to Achaean spears.

**Among the gods.** When Zeus calls the gods to assembly in Book 20, no river stays away but Oceanus, nor any of the nymphs “who haunt the lovely groves and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows” (20.7–9). Niobe, turned to stone, broods on her sorrows on Sipylus, where, men say, the nymphs who dance around the Achelous have their resting places (24.614–617).

**Worship.** At the head of the harbor of Ithaca is a cave sacred to the Naiads, with bowls and jars of stone where bees store honey, and stone looms on which the nymphs weave sea-purple cloth; one of its two doors is for men, the other for the gods (Od. 13.102–112). Odysseus used to offer them hecatombs there, and when he comes home he prays to them and promises gifts again (Od. 13.347–360). Eumaeus sets aside a share of the feast for the nymphs and Hermes (Od. 14.435–436), and prays to the nymphs at the town's spring, where every traveler made offerings at their altar (Od. 17.204–211, 17.240–246).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes the mountains “the graceful haunts of the goddess nymphs who dwell in the wooded glens” (*Theogony* 129–130). The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* describes the mountain nymphs who will rear Aeneas. They belong neither with mortals nor with immortals; they live long and dance with the gods, and firs and oaks spring up with them when they are born and wither when they die (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 256–273). Verses quoted as Hesiod's reckon their span at ten lives of the phoenix: “and we, the nymphs with lovely hair, daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus, ten phoenixes” (Hesiod, fragment 304 Merkelbach–West, in Plutarch, *On the Obsolescence of Oracles* 11, 415c). Another *Hymn* has them nurse the infant Dionysus at Nysa (26.3–10; see [[dionysus]]). Their cult was everywhere in Greece, at springs and in caves, often with Pan and Hermes. The Neoplatonist Porphyry read the cave of the Naiads on Ithaca as an image of the cosmos in his essay *On the Cave of the Nymphs*.
