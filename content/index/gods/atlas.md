## Etymology

Ἄτλας (Atlas), genitive Ἄτλαντος. W. W. Merry divides the name ἄ-τλας and renders it “the upholder” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.53); the second part is the root of τλῆναι, “bear, endure”, as in τλήμων, “enduring”.

Father of [[Calypso]]. Homer knows him only as her father, here and when Odysseus tells Arete of “Ogygia, an island that lies far off in the sea, where the daughter of Atlas, crafty Calypso, dwells” (*Odyssey* 7.244–246).

## Descriptions

ὀλοόφρων, “baleful” (*Odyssey* 1.52), = ὀλοός (“destructive”) + φρήν (“mind”); in the Iliad it is said of dangerous beasts, a water snake and a boar (*Iliad* 2.723, 17.21; see [[oloophron]]). In the Odyssey it belongs, besides Atlas, to Aeetes, Circe's brother (*Odyssey* 10.137), and to Minos (*Odyssey* 11.322). Merry takes it as the epithet of one whose powers are feared, like a wizard, and finds the reason for it here in the words that follow: preternatural knowledge makes its possessor suspected of the will, as well as the power, to use it for harm (on 1.52).

ὅς τε θαλάσσης πάσης βένθεα οἶδεν, “who knows the depths of the whole sea” (*Odyssey* 1.52–53): the same words are said of Proteus, the old man of the sea in Egypt, “who knows the depths of the whole sea, the servant of Poseidon” (*Odyssey* 4.385–386), whom Merry compares as a magician, ὀλοφώια εἰδώς, “skilled in wiles” (*Odyssey* 4.460; on 1.52).

ἔχει δέ τε κίονας αὐτὸς / μακράς, αἳ γαῖάν τε καὶ οὐρανὸν ἀμφὶς ἔχουσιν, “and himself holds the tall pillars / that hold earth and heaven apart” (*Odyssey* 1.53–54). Merry gives two readings of ἀμφὶς ἔχουσιν: “support at either side”, as if Atlas held pillars set east and west under the two ends of the vault of heaven, or, more simply, “keep asunder”, with the earth below, the heaven above, and Atlas with his pillars between them, which the translation follows (on 1.53; see the Odyssey translation's note on 1.52–54). αὐτός, “himself”, is emphatic: he holds them all by himself (on 1.53).

## Outside Homer

[[hesiod|Hesiod]] makes Atlas the son of [[Iapetus]] and Clymene, a daughter of Oceanus, and the brother of Menoetius, [[Prometheus]] and Epimetheus (*Theogony* 507–511). Zeus assigned him his lot: “by hard necessity” he holds up the wide sky at the limits of the earth, before the clear-voiced Hesperides, standing, with his head and tireless arms (*Theogony* 517–520); and again he stands before the house of Night, holding the wide sky unmoving, where Night and Day greet each other as they cross the great bronze threshold (*Theogony* 746–750). His daughter Maia bore Hermes to Zeus (*Theogony* 938–939; the shorter *Hymn to Hermes*, 18.3–4; see [[hermes]]).

In [[aeschylus|Aeschylus]]' *Prometheus Bound* Prometheus grieves for his brother Atlas, “who stands toward the western places, propping on his shoulders the pillar of heaven and earth, a burden not easy to hold” (*Prometheus Bound* 347–350). [[herodotus|Herodotus]] heard of a mountain in Libya called Atlas, narrow and round and so high, it is said, that its peaks are never seen for the clouds; the people who live there call it “the pillar of heaven”, and are named Atlantes after it (*Histories* 4.184.3–4). [[aristotle|Aristotle]] rejects the “myth of the ancients” that the heaven needs an Atlas to keep it safe: its makers, he says, supposed, like later men, that the bodies above have weight, and so in the manner of myth set a living necessity beneath the heaven (*On the Heavens* 2.1, 284a18–23). Among the senses of “hold” (ἔχειν) he lists the holding of what would otherwise move, as pillars hold the weights laid on them, “and as the poets make Atlas hold the heaven, as though it would otherwise fall upon the earth” (*Metaphysics* 5.23, 1023a17–21).
