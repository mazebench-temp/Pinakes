“the daughter of Cadmus …, Ino of the lovely ankles, / Leucothea, who was once a mortal with human voice, / but now in the open sea has a share of honor from the gods” (*Odyssey* 5.333–335). Homer brings her into the story once, to Odysseus in the storm.

## Name

Ἰνώ (Inō) is her name as a woman, and Λευκοθέη (Leukotheē), Attic Λευκοθέα, “the white goddess”, her name in the sea. Homer gives the two in one breath, the first with her father and the second at the head of the next verse (*Odyssey* 5.333–334), and calls her Ino again when she takes back her veil (*Odyssey* 5.461). W. W. Merry thought the second name might picture the white foam of the stormy waves, or, more likely, the fair calm, λευκὴ γαλήνη (*Odyssey* 10.94), which a sea goddess had the power to bring (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 5.333).

## Family

Daughter of [[cadmus|Cadmus]] (*Odyssey* 5.333). Hesiod names her first among the children Harmonia bore to Cadmus in Thebes: Ino, Semele, Agave, Autonoe and Polydorus (*Theogony* 975–978; see [[semele]] and [[hesiod]]). Homer names no husband or child of hers.

## Descriptions

καλλίσφυρος, “of the lovely ankles” (*Odyssey* 5.333; see [[of-the-lovely-ankles]]). As a mortal she was βροτὸς αὐδήεσσα, “a mortal with human voice” (*Odyssey* 5.334), a word the ancients disputed: the grammarian Aristophanes explained it as “speaking with human voice”, and the scholia say that Aristotle wrote οὐδήεσσα, “earthly” (Merry's critical note and on *Odyssey* 5.334; see [[with-human-voice]] and the translation's note on *Odyssey* 5.334). Merry calls her “the single instance in Homer of such an apotheosis of a mortal” (on *Odyssey* 5.333).

## Outside Homer

Merry gathers the later story. Her bond with the sea is explained by the legends in which she drowned herself with her son Melicertes, a name he takes to be the Phoenician Melkart, known in Greek as Palaemon. When her husband Athamas, maddened by Hera, killed their other son Learchus, Ino leapt from a sea cliff between Megara and Corinth with Melicertes in her arms, and the gods of the sea saved them both. Pindar says that in the sea, among the daughters of Nereus, an immortal life has been given to Ino for all time (*Olympian* 2.28, as Merry cites and quotes it). She was honored as a goddess at many places on the coasts of Greece, and Roman religion made her Mater Matuta and Palaemon Portunus (Merry on *Odyssey* 5.333; see [[pindar]]). In Euripides' *Iphigenia among the Taurians*, a herdsman who sees two strangers sitting on the shore takes them for gods and prays to her son: “O son of the sea goddess Leucothea, guardian of ships, lord Palaemon, be gracious to us” (*Iphigenia among the Taurians* 270–271, a plain rendering; see [[iphigenia-among-the-taurians]] and [[euripides]]).

The leap was told in other ways. In Euripides' *Medea* the chorus knows of only one woman before Medea who laid hands on her own children: Ino, driven mad by the gods when the wife of Zeus sent her wandering from her house, who for the impious murder of her children fell into the sea, stretching her foot over the sea cliff, and perished with her two sons (*Medea* 1282–1289; see [[medea-play]]). In the Suda it is Athamas who shot both her sons, Learchus and Melicertes, with arrows, and afterwards she was called Leucothea (Suda ι 381). Cicero gives her Roman name himself: “Is not Ino, the daughter of Cadmus, who is called Leucothea by the Greeks, held by our people to be Matuta?”, one of the mortals with whom almost the whole sky is filled (*Tusculan Disputations* 1.28). His Academic Cotta counts “Leucothea, who was Ino, and her son Palaemon”, worshipped by all Greece, among the gods who were once human, and asks why, if Ino is a goddess though she is the daughter of Cadmus, Circe, Pasiphae and Aeetes, children of the Sun, should not be gods too (*On the Nature of the Gods* 3.39, 3.48; see [[cicero]]).

Her double nature, a woman who had died and a goddess, made her cult a puzzle. When the people of Elea asked Xenophanes whether they should sacrifice to Leucothea and lament her, he told them not to lament her if they thought her a goddess, and not to sacrifice to her if they thought her a woman (Aristotle, *Rhetoric* 1400b5–8; see [[xenophanes]] and [[eleates]]).
