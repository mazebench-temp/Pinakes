## The name

Εὐρυνόμη seems to be made of εὐρύς, “wide”, and the root of νέμω, “deal out, hold, dwell” (as in νομός, “pasture”): perhaps “wide-ruling” or “wide-ranging”. The sense of the second part is a guess. The Odyssey gives the same name to a woman of Ithaca, the housekeeper of Odysseus' house (ταμίη, *Odyssey* 17.495), who has nothing to do with the goddess (see [[handmaids]]).

## Family

{{quote:27b0e97e-6618-5bea-ae72-74217d98f965}}

Daughter of [[Oceanus]], “backward-flowing”, the river that runs around the earth (see [[backward-flowing]]). Walter Leaf finds the repetition of the first of the two names very unusual, and thinks Payne Knight right to expel 18.399 as a gloss (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.399; see the translation's note on 18.399). Without the verse the Iliad would not say whose daughter she is.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod names Eurynome among the daughters whom Tethys bore to Oceanus, “the eldest daughters” (*Theogony* 358, 362–363; see [[tethys]]). She is the third of the goddesses whom Zeus took after Metis and Themis, and “Eurynome, daughter of Ocean, with a lovely form”, bore him the three fair-cheeked Graces, Aglaea, Euphrosyne and Thalia (*Theogony* 907–909; see [[graces]] and [[the-loves-of-zeus]]). Pausanias names her as their mother on Hesiod's authority (Pausanias 9.35.5).

Pausanias found her sanctuary near Phigalia in Arcadia, where the Lymax runs into the Neda, not far from the hot springs twelve stades above the town: a place holy from of old, hard to reach for the roughness of the ground, among many cypresses. The people of Phigalia took Eurynome for a title of Artemis, but those who had received the old records said that she was a daughter of Oceanus, whom Homer mentions in the Iliad as receiving Hephaestus together with Thetis. The sanctuary was opened on one day each year, with sacrifices public and private. Pausanias came too late for the festival and did not see the image, but he was told that golden chains bound the wooden figure, a woman down to the buttocks and a fish below; the fish, he reasons, would suit a daughter of Oceanus who lived with Thetis in the depths of the sea, and could not reasonably belong to Artemis (Pausanias 8.41.4–6).
