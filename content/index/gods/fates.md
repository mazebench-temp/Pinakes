## The name

μοῖρα is a share or portion, and so a man's lot, and above all his death (see [[moira]]). Μοῖρα in person is that share as a power: Fate. Only the editor's capital tells the person from the word, as with Sleep, Death and Themis (see [[sleep]], [[death]] and [[themis-the-goddess]]), and the translation follows it. The Iliad has the person four times: Μοῖρα three times, twice beside Zeus or a god (19.87, 19.410) and once spinning at a birth (24.209), and the plural Μοῖραι once, “the Fates” (24.49). The Odyssey prints no capital, but it has the κλῶθες, the “spinners”, who spin a man's lot at his birth (*Odyssey* 7.197–198).

## In Homer

Fate stands with the gods as an author of what befalls men. Agamemnon lays his folly on three powers, “but Zeus and Fate and the Erinys who walks in darkness” (19.87; see [[agamemnon]], [[erinyes]] and [[ate]]). The horse Xanthus tells Achilles that his day is near and that the horses will not be to blame, “but a great god and mighty Fate” (19.409–410; see [[xanthus-the-horse]] and [[mighty-fate]]). In the last book Apollo says that the Fates have given men an enduring heart, and Hecuba that mighty Fate spun Hector's death for him at his birth (24.49, 24.209–210).

Spinning is the image of the allotment. Hecuba's verse, γιγνομένῳ ἐπένησε λίνῳ, “spun for him with her thread at his birth”, is said in Book 20 of αἶσα, the portion that Achilles will suffer hereafter (20.127–128; see [[aisa]]), and in the Odyssey of Odysseus, who at home will suffer whatever his αἶσα and the heavy spinners spun for him when his mother bore him (*Odyssey* 7.196–198). More often the spinners are the gods themselves (ἐπεκλώσαντο θεοί, 24.525; *Odyssey* 1.17, 3.208, 8.579).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod names three Moirai, Clotho, Lachesis and Atropos, who give mortals at their birth good and evil to have. In one passage they are daughters of Night, born with the Keres, the pitiless avengers who pursue the transgressions of men and gods and never cease from their anger until the offender is punished (*Theogony* 217–222; see [[night]] and [[hesiod]]); in another they are daughters of Zeus and Themis, “to whom the counselor Zeus gave the greatest honor” (*Theogony* 904–906; see [[themis-the-goddess]]). In the myth of Er that ends Plato's *Republic* they are the daughters of Necessity. They sit on thrones around her spindle, dressed in white with garlands on their heads, and sing to the music of the Sirens, Lachesis of what has been, Clotho of what is, Atropos of what is to be; Clotho turns the spindle with her right hand. Each soul, when it has chosen its life, is led from Lachesis to Clotho, who ratifies the lot, and to the spinning of Atropos, which makes the thread irreversible (*Republic* 10.617b–d, 620d–e; see [[plato]]).
