The simile gives the pain that comes over Agamemnon when his wound dries (see [[the-woman-in-labor]] and [[agamemnon]]).

## Etymology

Εἰλείθυια (Eileithuia), plural Εἰλείθυιαι. The name is very old: Linear B tablets from Knossos record an offering of honey to e-re-u-ti-ja, Eleuthia, at a-mi-ni-so, Amnisos (KN Gg 705), where the Odyssey puts her cave (*Odyssey* 19.188). It has been connected with the root of ἐλεύσομαι, ἤλυθον, “come”, as the goddess who comes to the woman in labor; the derivation is uncertain.

## Family

The Iliad calls the Eileithyiai Ἥρης θυγατέρες, “the daughters of Hera” (11.271), and names no father (see [[hera]]).

## Descriptions

μογοστόκος (11.270, 16.187, 19.103) = μόγος (“toil, pain”) + τόκος (“birth”): “who brings hard birth” (see [[who-brings-hard-birth]]). The Eileithyiai are πικρὰς ὠδῖνας ἔχουσαι, “who have the bitter pangs in their keeping” (11.271), and what they send is a βέλος, a “dart”, sharp and piercing (11.269–270).

## In Homer

She is plural twice in the Iliad and singular twice. In Book 16 “Eileithyia of the hard births” brings Eudorus, son of Hermes and Polymele, out into the light, “and he saw the rays of the sun” (16.187–188; see [[hermes]]). In Book 19 Agamemnon tells how on the day of Heracles' birth Zeus boasted that “today Eileithyia of the hard births will bring into the light a man” who would rule all who dwell around him (19.103–105), and how Hera, having bound him by an oath, brought Sthenelus' son Eurystheus to birth in the seventh month and “held back the Eileithyiai” from Alcmene (19.114–119; see [[eurystheus]] and [[heracles]]). In the Odyssey the disguised Odysseus tells Penelope that a storm once brought Odysseus to Amnisus in Crete, “where the cave of Eileithyia is” (*Odyssey* 19.185–189; see [[crete]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod's Hera, the last wife of Zeus, bears him Hebe, Ares and Eileithyia (*Theogony* 921–923; see [[hebe]] and [[ares]]). In the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* Leto is in labor on Delos for nine days and nights, because Hera in her jealousy keeps Eileithyia on Olympus, unaware; when Iris brings her, Apollo is born as soon as she sets foot on the island (89–119; see [[leto]], [[apollo]] and [[iris]]). [[pindar|Pindar]] addresses her as the one who sits beside the deep-minded Fates, “child of mighty Hera”, who brings children to birth; without her no one sees light or dark night, or wins her sister Hebe (*Nemean* 7.1–4).

Her cult at Amnisus lasted. [[strabo|Strabo]] says that Minos used Amnisus as his port, “where the sanctuary of Eileithyia is” (*Geography* 10.4.8), and Pausanias reports that the Cretans believed she was born at Amnisus and that Hera was her mother (1.18.5). A cave near Amnisus, east of Heraklion, has been identified with the one the Odyssey names. The Romans called her Lucina: [[horace|Horace]] asks Ilithyia, “whether you would be called Lucina or Genitalis”, to bring births gently to term and to protect mothers (*Carmen Saeculare* 13–16).
