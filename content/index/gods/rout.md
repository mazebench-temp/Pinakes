## Etymology

φόβος belongs with φέβομαι, “flee in fear”. In Homer the noun regularly means panic flight, the rout of an army, and the verb φοβέω means “put to flight”; the ordinary later sense, “fear” (as in English *phobia*), is hardly found. The mares of Eumelus are said to carry “the panic of Ares” (φόβον Ἄρηος, 2.767). The translation renders the god “Rout” to keep the Homeric sense.

## In Homer

{{quote:c99a1945-6921-5957-8d4d-c000d5479a6a}}

At the first clash of the armies Rout is one of the powers that drive the men on, with [[terror|Terror]] and [[strife|Strife]] (4.439–440). On Athena's aegis he runs in a circle all around it, and [[strife|Strife]], [[courage|Courage]] and chilling [[onslaught|Onslaught]] are set within, with the head of the [[gorgon|Gorgon]] (5.739–742). On the shield of Agamemnon he and Terror surround the Gorgon (11.36–37). In a simile of Book 13 he is Ares' “dear son, strong and fearless”, “who routs even a warrior of enduring heart” (13.299–300), and in Book 15 Ares tells Terror and Rout to yoke his horses (15.119–120).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes Phobos and Deimos the sons of Ares and Aphrodite (*Theogony* 933–936). In Aeschylus' *Seven against Thebes* the seven champions swear by Ares, Enyo and “blood-loving Phobos”, touching the blood of a bull (*Seven against Thebes* 42–46). When Asaph Hall discovered the two moons of Mars in 1877, they were named Phobos and Deimos from the lines of Iliad 15 in which Ares calls on the pair to yoke his horses.
