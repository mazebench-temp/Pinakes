## Etymology

Ἐρινύς (Erinys), plural Ἐρινύες. Homer uses both: a father calls on “the hateful Erinyes” (9.454), and “the Erinys who walks in darkness” hears a mother's curse (9.571). The name has no agreed etymology. A Linear B tablet from Knossos lists an offering of oil to *e-ri-nu*, probably the same name (KN Fp 1).

## Descriptions

ἠεροφοῖτις, “who walks in darkness” (9.571; 19.87; see [[who-walks-in-darkness]]), = ἀήρ (“mist, darkness”) + φοιτάω (“go to and fro”): she goes about unseen. ἀμείλιχον ἦτορ ἔχουσα, “she of the implacable heart” (9.572; see [[implacable]]), is said of the Erinys as Agamemnon says it of Hades (9.158). στυγεραί, “hateful” (9.454; Od. 2.135, 20.78; see [[hateful]]), is their standing epithet. In the Odyssey one Erinys is θεὰ δασπλῆτις, “the dread goddess” (Od. 15.234), a word of uncertain meaning.

## In Homer

The two parental curses that Homer tells in full are both in Phoenix's speech in Book 9 (see [[curses]]). His father Amyntor “called on the hateful Erinyes” when he cursed him to have no son, and Hades and Persephone fulfilled the curse (9.453–457; see [[the-flight-of-phoenix]]). She prays to the rulers of the dead, kneeling and beating the earth, and the Erinys hears her “out of Erebus” (see [[the-death-of-meleager]], [[hades]], [[persephone]] and [[erebus]]).

Elsewhere they uphold claims that rest on birth or on helplessness. When Poseidon will not yield to his elder brother Zeus, Iris reminds him that the Erinyes always follow the elder (15.204). Athena, having struck Ares down, tells him that he is paying off his mother's Erinyes, for Hera is angry that he has left the Achaeans (21.410–414). In the Odyssey Telemachus will not drive his mother from the house, because she would call down the hateful Erinyes (Od. 2.134–136), and Oedipus is left the sorrows that a mother's Erinyes bring to pass (Od. 11.279–280). The beggar has his too: struck by Antinous, Odysseus curses him “if there are gods and Erinyes for beggars” (Od. 17.475). They punish perjury. Agamemnon swears by Zeus, Earth, Sun and the Erinyes, who under the earth punish whoever swears falsely (19.258–260); compare the two unnamed powers below the earth in the oath of Book 3 (3.278–279; see [[oath]]). Agamemnon names the Erinys with Zeus and Moira as the powers that cast blind folly into his mind (19.86–88; see [[ate]]), and in the Odyssey the Erinys put blind folly into the mind of Melampus (Od. 15.233–234). When Achilles' horse Xanthus speaks and foretells his master's death, the Erinyes stop his voice (19.418). The orphaned daughters of Pandareus were snatched away by the storm winds and given to the hateful Erinyes to serve (Od. 20.77–78).

Homer does not describe them. They are not yet the snake-haired pursuers of tragedy, but the powers that make a curse or an oath come true.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes them children of Earth, born from the drops of blood that fell when Cronus cut off his father's genitals (*Theogony* 183–185), and says that on the fifth day of the month they attended the birth of Oath, whom Strife bore as a bane to perjurers (*Works and Days* 803–804; see [[hesiod]]). In Aeschylus' *Oresteia* they hunt Orestes for his mother's murder, and in the *Eumenides* they are the chorus. They call Night their mother (321–322), plead against Apollo before the court of the Areopagus, and are persuaded by Athena to accept a home and honors beneath Athens, where they are led away as the August ones, σεμναί (1041; see [[aeschylus]]). Pausanias saw by the Areopagus a sanctuary of the goddesses whom the Athenians call the August and Hesiod the Erinyes, and says that Aeschylus was the first to give them snakes in their hair (Pausanias 1.28.6). The play's title, Eumenides, “the Kindly Ones”, is a name of good omen for them; the Romans called them Furies.
