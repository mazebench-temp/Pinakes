## Etymology

ὄσσα (ossa), “a voice, a rumor”, from the same root as ὄψ (“voice”) and Latin *vox*. In Homer it is the talk that runs through a crowd, a report whose speaker no one knows; Hesiod uses the word of the Muses' voice (*Theogony* 10). Here it is a person, “the messenger of Zeus”.

## In the Iliad

{{quote:5bad5cca-13a1-53f0-b4e4-a77cbd254402}}

δεδήει (“blazed”) is the pluperfect of δαίω (“kindle”): the news spreads through the army like fire. Rumor is Zeus' messenger because a report that comes from no one seems to come from the god, and here it drives on an assembly that Zeus' own Dream has set up. See [[dream]] and [[agamemnons-dream]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

When the suitors have been killed, “Rumor went swiftly as a messenger through all the city”, telling of their death (Odyssey 24.413–414). Athena tells Telemachus to listen for news of his father, “a rumor from Zeus, which most of all brings report (κλέος) to men” (Odyssey 1.282–283). See [[kleos]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod warns against the talk of men, Φήμη (“Report”): no report ever wholly dies that many people voice, “she too is a god” (*Works and Days* 760–764). Virgil's Fama is a monster with as many eyes, tongues, mouths and ears as she has feathers (*Aeneid* 4.173–197); she is modeled on Homer's Strife (see [[virgil-rumor]]). Ovid gives Rumor a house of bronze at the center of the world, where every sound arrives (*Metamorphoses* 12.39–63).
