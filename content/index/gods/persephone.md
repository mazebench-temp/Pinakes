## Etymology

Περσεφόνεια (Persephoneia) is the epic form of the name; Hesiod and the Homeric Hymn to Demeter also have Περσεφόνη (*Theogony* 913; *Hymn to Demeter* 56), and Attic speech said Φερσεφόνη and Φερρέφαττα. The name has no Greek etymology and may be older than Greek. In Plato's *Cratylus* Socrates says that many people fear the name in the form Φερσεφόνη, which sounds terrible to them, and offers a playful derivation of Φερρέφαττα from wisdom (404c–d; see [[plato]]).

## Family

Homer calls her “daughter of Zeus” (Διὸς θυγάτηρ, Od. 11.217) and does not name her mother. She is always named with Hades, and the house of the dead is theirs together, “the house of Hades and dread Persephone” (Od. 10.491, 10.564; see [[hades]]). Hesiod makes her the daughter of Zeus and Demeter, carried off by Aidoneus from her mother and given to him by Zeus (*Theogony* 912–914; see [[demeter]]).

## Descriptions

ἐπαινή, “dread” (9.457, 9.569; Od. 10.491, 10.534, 10.564, 11.47; see [[dread]]), is hers alone, always in the formula ἐπαινὴ Περσεφόνεια. In the Odyssey she is also ἀγαυή, “illustrious” (Od. 11.213, 11.226, 11.635), and ἁγνή, “holy” (Od. 11.386).

## In Homer

In the Iliad she is named twice, both times in Phoenix's speech and both times in a curse. Amyntor's curse on his son is fulfilled by “Zeus of the underworld and dread Persephone” (9.457), that is, by Hades and his queen:

In the story of Meleager his mother beats the earth with her hands, “calling on Hades and dread Persephone” to give death to her son, and the Erinys hears her (9.568–572; see [[the-flight-of-phoenix]], [[the-death-of-meleager]], [[erinyes]] and [[curses]]). Prayers for harm go down to the powers below.

The Odyssey shows her realm. Circe sends Odysseus there to consult the seer Teiresias, to whom Persephone alone of the dead has left his mind (Od. 10.490–495). Her groves of poplars and willows stand on the shore of Oceanus, where Odysseus beaches his ship (Od. 10.508–511), and he prays to Hades and Persephone over the slaughtered sheep (Od. 10.533–534, 11.46–47). She sends up the souls of the women of the heroic age and scatters them again (Od. 11.225–227, 11.385–386). When Odysseus tries three times to embrace his mother's shade, he asks whether Persephone has sent him a mere image to make him grieve the more, and his mother answers that “Persephone, daughter of Zeus” is not deceiving him; this is the way of mortals when they die (Od. 11.210–222). At last he fears that she will send up the Gorgon's head from Hades, and he flees (Od. 11.632–636).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod places before the echoing house of “mighty Hades and dread Persephone” a pitiless hound that lets no one out (*Theogony* 767–773). The Homeric *Hymn to Demeter* tells her story: Hades, with Zeus' consent, carried her off as she gathered flowers (2.1–20); Demeter searched for her and withheld the grain until Zeus sent Hermes to bring her back; but Hades had given her a pomegranate seed to eat (2.371–374, 2.411–413), and so she spends a third of each year below the earth and the rest with her mother and the gods (2.398–403, 2.445–447). Demeter taught her rites to the kings of Eleusis, and the hymn ends with a prayer to Demeter and to her daughter (2.473–495). The hymn often calls Persephone simply κούρη, “the girl”, and in cult she was Κόρη, the Maiden; the Romans called her Proserpina.
