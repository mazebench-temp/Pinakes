## Etymology

Κίρκη (Kirkē). The origin of the name is unknown. The Suda explains it as “she who mixes the drugs” (ἡ κιρνῶσα τὰ φάρμακα, from κίρνημι, “mix”), or derives it from κερκίς, the weaver's shuttle, and adds that subtle women are called Circes (Suda κ 1662). W. W. Merry reports the derivation from κερκίς from the Suda and the *Etymologicum Magnum*, because she is shown as a weaver at her loom (*Odyssey* 10.222), and the other from κεράννυμι, “mix”, because she mixes the magic potion (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.135). The name has the letters of κίρκος, the hawk or falcon of the similes (*Iliad* 17.757, 22.139; see [[falcon]]), the word the Suda glosses next (κ 1663).

## Family

Daughter of the Sun, [[helios|Helios]], and of [[perse|Perse]], whom [[oceanus|Oceanus]] begot, and own sister, αὐτοκασιγνήτη, of [[aeetes|Aeetes]] (*Odyssey* 10.137–139). αὐτοκασιγνήτη is the feminine of the Iliad's αὐτοκασίγνητος, “own brother” (*Iliad* 2.706; see the Odyssey translation's key-word row). Homer names no husband or child of hers.

## Descriptions

ἐυπλόκαμος, “of the lovely tresses” (*Odyssey* 10.136, 11.8, 12.150), and θεὰ καλλιπλόκαμος, “the goddess of the lovely tresses” (*Odyssey* 10.220, 10.310), are said of her hair, as of Calypso's and of Dawn's (see [[with-lovely-tresses]] and [[of-the-lovely-tresses]]). δεινὴ θεὸς αὐδήεσσα, “a dread goddess with human voice” (*Odyssey* 10.136, 11.8, 12.150), she shares with Calypso (*Odyssey* 12.449; see [[dread-goddess]] and [[with-human-voice]]). πολυφάρμακος, “skilled in many drugs” (*Odyssey* 10.276), = πολύς (“many”) + φάρμακον (“drug”), is hers alone in the Odyssey (see [[with-many-drugs]]). She is πότνια, “lady Circe” (*Odyssey* 8.448, 10.394, 10.549, 12.36; see [[queenly]]); δῖα θεάων, “brilliant among goddesses” (*Odyssey* 10.400, 10.455, 10.487, 10.503, 12.20, 12.115, 12.143, 12.155; see [[brilliant-among-goddesses]]); νύμφη, “the nymph” (*Odyssey* 10.543; see [[nymphs]]); and Αἰαίη δολόεσσα, “the wily one of Aeaea” (*Odyssey* 9.32; Κίρκης τʼ Αἰαίης, 12.268, 12.273; see [[aeaea]] and [[wily]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Odysseus names her first at the beginning of his tale, beside Calypso, as a goddess who kept him in her halls, “longing for me to be her husband” (*Odyssey* 9.31–32; see [[calypso]]), and he tells her story in *Odyssey* 10–12 (see the Book 10 note). When he sails from Aeaea to the dead, she sends behind his ship “a following wind that filled the sail, a good companion” (*Odyssey* 11.7, a plain rendering; again 12.149). The soul of Elpenor, whose body was left unburied in her hall, meets him first among the dead (*Odyssey* 11.51–54). On his return she comes to the shore with her handmaids, bringing bread, meat and wine, and greets the men who have gone down alive into the house of Hades as δισθανέες, “twice-dying”, since other men die once (*Odyssey* 12.16–22, a plain rendering; see [[hades]]); then she tells Odysseus the way past the Sirens, the Wandering Rocks, Scylla and Charybdis, and warns him to spare the cattle of the Sun on Thrinacia (*Odyssey* 12.37–141; see [[scylla]], [[odysseus-passes-scylla]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). At Scylla's rock Odysseus forgets her hard command not to arm himself (*Odyssey* 12.226–227); off Thrinacia he remembers her warning and Teiresias' (*Odyssey* 12.266–273); and in Ithaca he tells Penelope of “the guile and the many devices of Circe” (*Odyssey* 23.321, a plain rendering).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod gives the same parents: Perseis, “the famous daughter of Oceanus”, bore to the tireless Sun Circe and king Aeetes, and Aeetes became the father of Medea (*Theogony* 956–962; see [[medea]] and [[hesiod]]). At the end of the *Theogony* Circe bears to Odysseus Agrius and Latinus, who ruled over all the famous Tyrrhenians far away in a recess of the sacred islands; a verse that editors bracket adds Telegonus (1011–1016). Merry thinks the passage spurious, but useful as pointing to an early transference of Circe to Italy and the cities of Magna Graecia (on 10.135). The *Telegony* made her the mother of Telegonus (see [[telegonus-kills-odysseus]] and [[telegonus]]). In Euripides' *Trojan Women* Cassandra names among the sufferings in store for Odysseus “the Ligurian Circe, who shapes men into swine” (*Trojan Women* 437–438; see [[euripides]]).

Strabo held that Homer, knowing of Jason's voyage to Aea and of the tales of the sorcery of Medea, made Aeaea after Aea and Circe a sorceress after Medea, “own sister of baleful Aeetes”, and moved both out into Oceanus (*Geography* 1.2.10, 1.2.40). He describes the Circaeum on the coast of Latium, a mountain made an island by the sea and the marshes, said to be full of roots, perhaps to fit it to the myth of Circe, with a small town, a sanctuary of Circe and an altar of Athena, where a bowl of Odysseus was said to be shown (5.3.6), and he reports that her tomb was shown on the larger of the Pharmacussae, two islets off the coast of Attica (9.1.13; see [[strabo]]). Merry reports the explanation that the Odyssey as we have it joins two forms of her story, one that sets her island in the east, near the house of Dawn and the risings of the Sun (*Odyssey* 12.3–4), and one that sets it in the west, the eastern one probably belonging to the legend of the Argonauts; in her mother Perse, a daughter of Oceanus, he sees perhaps the first hint of a connection between Circe and the West (on 10.135; see [[perse]]). Cicero's Cotta, testing where the Stoics would draw the line between gods and mortals, asks why Circe, Pasiphae and Aeetes, children of the Sun by Perseis, daughter of Oceanus, should not be counted among them, “though Circe too is devoutly worshipped by our colonists at Circeii” (*On the Nature of the Gods* 3.48; see [[cicero]]).
