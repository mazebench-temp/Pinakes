## Etymology

ὄνειρος (“dream”) is the common Homeric word for a dream; [[onar|ὄναρ]] is its shorter twin. The Greek text prints it with a small letter, and Homer draws no sharp line between a dream and a dream-god. In *Iliad* 2 the Dream is a person. Zeus speaks to it, and it listens, goes, takes a shape, stands and talks, and flies away (2.71).

## Descriptions

{{quote:de36e79b-2678-5b3a-9b3c-c57b9c8f5849}}

οὖλος (“baneful”) is usually taken as a form of οὐλόμενος (“ruinous”), from ὄλλυμι (“destroy”), the word the proem uses of the wrath (1.2). The Dream is baneful for what it leads to. See [[baneful]].

θεῖος ὄνειρος, “the divine Dream” (2.22, 2.56): it comes from a god, and Agamemnon feels its “divine voice” still around him when he wakes (θείη ὀμφή, 2.41).

## Dreams in Homer

Dreams come from outside the sleeper, and they can come from Zeus (1.63; see [[a-dream-comes-from-zeus]]). A god who sends one gives it the form of someone the sleeper knows. Athena sends Penelope a phantom in the likeness of her sister Iphthime (*Odyssey* 4.795–841) and visits Nausicaa as a friend of hers (*Odyssey* 6.20–23). The ghost of Patroclus stands above Achilles' head as he sleeps (Iliad 23.65–107; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]]). Penelope knows that some dreams deceive: the true ones come through the gate of horn, the false through the gate of ivory (Odyssey 19.562–567). Hermes leads the suitors' souls past “the people of dreams” on the road to Hades (Odyssey 24.12).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod lists the “tribe of Dreams” (φῦλον Ὀνείρων) among the children of Night (*Theogony* 211–212).

Ancient readers were troubled that Zeus should lie. Plato's Socrates says that, much as he praises Homer, he will not praise the sending of the dream by Zeus to Agamemnon (*Republic* 2, 383a). See [[plato-lying-dream]]. Aristotle reports how Hippias of Thasos defended the passage by a change of accent in a line of the Dream's message that our text of 2.15 does not contain (*Poetics* 25, 1461a21–23). See [[aristotle-and-hippias]].
