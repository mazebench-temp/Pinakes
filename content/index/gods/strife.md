## Etymology

Ἔρις is the ordinary word ἔρις, “strife, quarrel, rivalry” (see [[eris]]), made a person. The Greek text prints a capital when she acts, but Homer draws no sharp line between the word and the goddess, as with the [[dream|Dream]] of Book 2.

## Family

Ἄρεος ἀνδροφόνοιο κασιγνήτη ἑτάρη τε, “the sister and companion of man-slaying Ares” (4.441; see [[ares]] and [[man-slaying]]). The Iliad names no parents for her.

## Descriptions

ἄμοτον μεμαυῖα, “raging without end” (4.440): ἄμοτον is “insatiably, unceasingly”, and μεμαυῖα is from μέμαα, “be eager, rage”. The half-line returns in Book 5, where Apollo, Ares and Strife stir up the toil of battle (5.517–518). Later books call her “grievous” (ἀργαλέη, 11.4), “of many groans” (πολύστονος, 11.73), and “mighty, rouser of hosts” (κρατερὴ λαοσσόος, 20.48).

## Her growth

ὀλίγη μὲν πρῶτα κορύσσεται, αὐτὰρ ἔπειτα / οὐρανῷ ἐστήριξε κάρη καὶ ἐπὶ χθονὶ βαίνει: “small when first she rears her head, but afterwards she plants her head against the sky and walks upon the earth” (4.442–443). κορύσσεται is literally “arms herself, puts on her helmet” (κόρυς, “helmet”), and so “rises up”. Twenty lines earlier the same words describe the wave to which the Achaean advance is compared: it “rears its head first on the open sea, but then it breaks upon the land” (πρῶτα κορύσσεται, αὐτὰρ ἔπειτα, 4.424–425; see [[waves-on-the-shore]]). The verbs are in the tense of general truth, and the next line turns to the occasion: “she then too” (καὶ τότε) cast the quarrel into their midst (4.444). The growth is what Strife always does, and this battle is one instance of it. It is also the shape of the poem's own story, which begins on the day two men “stood apart in strife” (1.6) and is set in a war that Menelaus calls “my quarrel” (ἐμῆς ἔριδος, 3.100). What she casts is νεῖκος, “the quarrel, common to all”, and she goes through the throng “increasing the groaning of men” (4.444–445). The translation keeps ἔρις “strife” and νεῖκος “quarrel” apart (see [[neikos]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In Book 5 she is worked into Athena's [[aegis]] with [[courage|Courage]] and [[onslaught|Onslaught]] (5.740). In Book 11 Zeus sends her to the Achaean ships “holding a portent of war in her hands”; she shouts from the ship of Odysseus, and war becomes sweeter to the Achaeans than going home (11.3–14). She rejoices as she watches the battle that follows, for she alone of the gods is present at it (11.73–74). On the shield of Achilles she moves through the fighting with Tumult and Doom (18.535), and when the gods go down to battle in Book 20, mighty Strife, rouser of hosts, rises among them (20.48).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes Eris a daughter of Night (*Theogony* 225) and gives her children of her own, among them Toil, Famine, Pains, Battles, Slaughters, Quarrels, Lies, Lawlessness and Ruin, and last Oath, who does most harm to men on earth “when anyone knowingly swears a false oath” (*Theogony* 226–232). In the *Works and Days* there are two Strifes: a cruel one who “fosters evil war and battle”, with the verb Homer uses of Strife “increasing” the groaning of men (ὀφέλλει; ὀφέλλουσα, 4.445), and a good one who stirs even an idle man to work in rivalry with his neighbor (*Works and Days* 11–26; see [[hesiods-two-strifes]]). In the *Cypria*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, Eris comes while the gods are feasting at the wedding of Peleus and Thetis and starts the quarrel about beauty among Athena, Hera and Aphrodite that leads to the Judgment of Paris; [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] has her throw an apple among them (*Epitome* 3.2).

“Longinus” quotes her growth as an example of grandeur: the distance from earth to heaven is the measure of Homer as much as of Strife (*On the Sublime* 9.4; see [[longinus-strife]]). Virgil gave the same growth to Rumor, who is small at first for fear, then lifts herself into the air, walks on the ground and hides her head among the clouds (*Aeneid* 4.173–177; see [[virgil-rumor]]).
