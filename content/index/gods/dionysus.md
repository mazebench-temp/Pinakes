## Etymology

Διώνυσος (Diōnysos), with a long ō, is the usual form in epic (6.132, 6.135; Od. 24.74). Διόνυσος, the form of later Greek, occurs in the Odyssey once (Od. 11.325). The name is older than Homer. Linear B tablets from Pylos write it *di-wo-nu-so* (PY Xa 102, Xa 1419), and a tablet from Chania in Crete names him beside Zeus in a list of offerings (KH Gq 5). The first part of the name is usually connected with that of Zeus (Δι-, as in Διός). The second has been connected with Nysa, where he was nursed, but it is unexplained.

## Family

Son of [[Zeus]] and Semele. Zeus names her in the list of women he has loved: ἣ δὲ Διώνυσον Σεμέλη τέκε χάρμα βροτοῖσιν, “and Semele bore Dionysus, a joy to mortals” (14.323–325). χάρμα (“joy, delight”) may point to wine, though Homer never names wine as his gift. In Book 6 he has nurses (τιθῆναι, 6.132), so the Iliad's one story of him belongs to his childhood. Hesiod makes Semele a daughter of Cadmus, “a mortal who bore an immortal; now both are gods” (*Theogony* 940–942, 975–976), and gives Dionysus a wife, Ariadne, daughter of Minos, whom Zeus made immortal and ageless (*Theogony* 947–949).

## Descriptions

μαινόμενος, “raving” (6.132), is his one epithet in the Iliad. It is the participle of μαίνομαι, “rage, be mad”, the verb of warriors in their fury: Helenus says that Diomedes “rages beyond measure” (λίην μαίνεται, 6.100–101). The epithet gives the god the frenzy of his worshippers, the μαινάδες or “raving women” who follow him in later poetry; Homer has the word once, in a simile (22.460). Hesiod calls him πολυγηθής, “bringing much joy” (*Theogony* 941; *Works and Days* 614), and χρυσοκόμης, “golden-haired” (*Theogony* 947). In the *Homeric Hymns* he is ἐρίβρομος, “loud-roaring” (7.56, 26.1), from βρόμος, “roar”, like his later name Bromios, and κισσοκόμης, “ivy-haired”, crowned with ivy (26.1).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Besides the story of Lycurgus (6.130–140) and the line on his birth (14.325), the Odyssey names him twice. Artemis killed Ariadne on the island of Dia “on the testimony of Dionysus” (Διονύσου μαρτυρίῃσιν, Od. 11.321–325); what he testified, and why, is not said. And the golden jar in which the bones of Achilles and Patroclus lie together was Thetis' gift, “a gift of Dionysus and the work of famous Hephaestus”, as she said (Od. 24.73–76). She had once taken him in (6.136), but Homer does not say why the jar passed to her (see [[thetis]]). Wine is everywhere in the poems, yet Homer never calls it his gift, and he never shows him among the gods on Olympus. Why he has so small a place in epic is uncertain. The old view that he was a late arrival in Greece, from Thrace or Phrygia, is hard to hold now that his name has been found on the Mycenaean tablets.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod gives him the character he keeps: the vintage is “the gifts of much-cheering Dionysus” (*Works and Days* 614). In the later story of his birth, told by Euripides and by [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]], Semele, deceived by Hera, asked Zeus to come to her as he came to Hera; she died by his lightning, and Zeus sewed the unborn child into his thigh, from which he was born (*Bacchae* 1–9, 88–98; Apollodorus 3.4.3). Herodotus heard the Greeks say that Zeus then carried him to Nysa, above Egypt in Ethiopia (2.146; see [[nysa]]), and a *Homeric Hymn* has the nymphs nurse him in the glens of Nysa and follow him through the woods (*Hymn* 26.3–10; see [[nymphs]]).

Many of his myths are stories of resistance. Lycurgus, king of the Edonians in Thrace, drove him into the sea and was driven mad and killed by his will (Apollodorus 3.5.1; see [[lycurgus]] and [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]]). Pentheus, king of Thebes and his cousin, was torn apart by the women of Thebes, his mother among them (Euripides, *Bacchae*). The daughters of Proetus went mad, Hesiod said, because they would not accept his rites (Apollodorus 2.2.2; see [[proetus]]). The Tyrrhenian pirates who seized him at sea became dolphins (*Homeric Hymn* 7).

He is the god of wine, which Teiresias in the *Bacchae* sets beside Demeter's grain as the second great gift to mortals (*Bacchae* 274–285), and of the ecstatic worship of women, the maenads with their fawnskins and thyrsi. At Athens tragedy and comedy were performed at his festivals, in the theater of Dionysus below the Acropolis. Herodotus reports that the Greeks counted him among the youngest of the gods (2.145). Walter F. Otto called him “the god who comes” (*Dionysos*, 1933): on this view the stories of his arrival express his nature rather than his history. The Romans called him Bacchus and identified him with their Liber.
