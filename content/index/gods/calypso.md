## Etymology

Καλυψώ (Kalypsō), genitive Καλυψοῦς (*Odyssey* 4.557, 5.14). The name belongs with καλύπτω, “cover, hide”, and W. W. Merry glosses it “the concealer” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.52).

## Family

{{quote:45ff2123-13af-5967-9bb4-69dd1ed75c14}}

Daughter of [[Atlas]], who knows the depths of the sea and holds the pillars that keep earth and heaven apart. Odysseus too calls her “the daughter of Atlas” when he tells Arete of Ogygia (*Odyssey* 7.245). Homer names no mother for her.

## Descriptions

νύμφη πότνια, “the lady nymph” (*Odyssey* 1.14, and πότνια νύμφη at *Odyssey* 5.149): πότνια, “lady, mistress”, is a title of goddesses. She is a νύμφη, one of the goddesses of springs, caves and wild places (see [[nymphs]]), and Athena, pleading for Odysseus, calls her simply θεά, “a goddess” (*Odyssey* 1.51).

δῖα θεάων, “brilliant among goddesses” (*Odyssey* 1.14), is her standing epithet in the story of her parting from Odysseus (*Odyssey* 5.78, 5.85, 5.116, 5.180, 5.202, 5.242, 5.246, 5.258, 5.276, 9.29; see [[brilliant-among-goddesses]]).

ἐυπλόκαμος, “of the lovely tresses” (*Odyssey* 1.86, 5.30, 5.58, 7.246, 7.255, 12.449), = εὖ (“well”) + πλόκαμος (“lock, braid”); also ἠύκομος, “lovely-haired” (*Odyssey* 8.452, 12.389). Merry sets the two apart: ἐυπλόκαμος points to the beauty of the way her hair is dressed, since πλόκαμοι are ringlets or plaits, as when Hera “plaited the shining tresses” for her meeting with Zeus (on 1.86, comparing *Iliad* 14.176; see [[with-lovely-tresses]]).

δολόεσσα, “crafty” (*Odyssey* 7.245), is said also of Circe (*Odyssey* 9.32), and both goddesses are δεινὴ θεὸς αὐδήεσσα, “a dread goddess with human speech” (*Odyssey* 10.136, 12.449; see [[circe]]).

## Mythology

Odysseus came to her island alone. Zeus had shattered his ship with a thunderbolt in the open sea, and he drifted nine days on the keel until on the tenth night the gods brought him to Ogygia (*Odyssey* 7.249–254, 12.415–419, 12.447–449). She loved him and cared for him, and promised to make him immortal and ageless all his days, but she never persuaded his heart (*Odyssey* 5.135–136, 7.255–258, 23.334–337). He stayed seven years (*Odyssey* 7.259), sitting on the shore in tears by day and lying with her at night in her hollow caves, unwilling beside a willing goddess (*Odyssey* 5.151–158).

Proteus tells Menelaus that he is held there by force, with no ships or companions to take him home (*Odyssey* 4.555–560), and Athena says the same at the second council of the gods (*Odyssey* 5.13–15). Then Zeus sends Hermes to her with the order that Athena proposed in Book 1, in nearly the same words (*Odyssey* 5.28–31; compare *Odyssey* 1.84–87). She protests that the gods begrudge goddesses who openly take mortal men as husbands, as they begrudged Dawn her Orion and Demeter her Iasion (*Odyssey* 5.118–129). But she obeys, gives him the tools to build a raft, and on the fifth day sends him off, bathed and dressed in fragrant clothes, with wine, water and food, and a fair wind (*Odyssey* 5.137–170, 5.228–268). In the eighth year, Odysseus tells Arete, she bade him go, “at the message of Zeus, or her own mind changed” (*Odyssey* 7.261–263).

## Outside Homer

[[hesiod|Hesiod]] counts “lovely Calypso” (ἱμερόεσσα Καλυψώ) among the eldest daughters of Oceanus and Tethys (*Theogony* 359, 362–363; see [[oceanus]] and [[tethys]]), and at the end of the *Theogony* names Nausithous and Nausinous as the sons whom “Calypso, brilliant among goddesses” bore to Odysseus (*Theogony* 1017–1018). In the *Hymn to Demeter* she is one of the companions with whom Persephone was gathering flowers in a meadow when the earth opened and Hades carried her off (*Hymn to Demeter* 417–433).
