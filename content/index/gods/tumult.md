## Etymology

κυδοιμός is the noise and confusion of battle, the uproar of a rout (10.523, 11.52, 11.538, 18.218), and the verb κυδοιμέω is to make an uproar, to spread confusion: Diomedes and Odysseus go through the throng “spreading tumult” (11.324; also 15.136). Its origin is unknown. The capital marks the person; the common noun is not claimed by this entry.

## In Homer

When Hector charges with Ares at his side, “lady Enyo” leads the Trojans too, ἣ μὲν ἔχουσα Κυδοιμὸν ἀναιδέα δηϊοτῆτος, “she bringing with her Tumult, shameless in combat” (5.593; see [[enyo]]). ἔχουσα is literally “holding, having”; how she holds him is not said. ἀναιδής, “shameless”, is the lack of αἰδώς, the regard that restrains (see [[anaideia]]); the first battle had its “shameless stone” (4.521; see [[shameless-stone]]).

He appears once more, among the figures worked on the shield of Achilles, in the scene of the city at war: “and Strife and Tumult joined in the throng, and deadly Doom” (18.535; see [[strife]]). With Rout, Strife, Courage and Onslaught, who are set on Athena's aegis later in Book 5 (5.739–740; see [[rout]], [[courage]] and [[onslaught]]), he belongs to a small company of battle powers that Homer names but does not describe.

## Outside Homer

The shield of Heracles, in the poem ascribed to Hesiod, repeats the line of Iliad 18 almost word for word: “and Strife and Tumult rushed about” ([Hesiod], *Shield* 156; see [[hesiod]]). In Aristophanes' *Peace* (421 BC) Kydoimos is the slave of War, who sends him to Athens and to Sparta to fetch a pestle for pounding the Greek cities in a mortar; he comes back empty-handed from both, for Cleon and Brasidas, the “pestles” of the two cities, are dead.
