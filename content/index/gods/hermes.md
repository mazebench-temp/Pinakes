## Etymology

Ἑρμείας (Hermeias) is the longer epic form of the name; Homer also uses Ἑρμῆς (Hermes), as at 20.72. The name is often connected with ἕρμα (“a heap of stones”), the cairns that marked roads and boundaries and later gave their name to the stone pillars called herms; the connection is uncertain. The god is already named on Linear B tablets from Pylos, where the name is read as *e-ma-a₂*.

## Descriptions

{{quote:778cced3-e225-5437-8786-4ee584223e25}}

διάκτορος is traditionally “guide” or “messenger”; its formation is disputed. ἀργεϊφόντης is traditionally “slayer of Argus”, the many-eyed watchman Hermes killed in the story of Io; others have explained it as “shining” or as “dog-slayer”. The sense was already uncertain in antiquity. See [[guide]] and [[argeiphontes]].

His gear is described in the same verses in both poems: the “ambrosial, golden” sandals that carry him over the sea and the boundless earth as fast as the wind, and the wand with which he bewitches the eyes of men and wakes the sleeping (24.340–345 = *Odyssey* 5.44–49; *Odyssey* 24.2–4; see [[sandals]] and [[wand]]). In the Odyssey he is χρυσόρραπις, “of the golden wand” (*Odyssey* 5.87, 10.277, 10.331). When he meets Priam, and Odysseus in the Odyssey, he takes the likeness of a young man “with the first down on his lip, when youth is at its most graceful” (24.348 = *Odyssey* 10.279).

In *Iliad* 2 Hermes is the second holder of the scepter that [[Hephaestus]] made. Zeus gave it to him, and he gave it to [[Pelops]]. The god who carries messages between Zeus and men is the link through which a king's authority comes down from Zeus. See [[the-scepter-of-agamemnon]] and [[scepter]].

## Mythology

Later books of the Iliad show him as a guide and a thief. Zeus sends him to lead Priam through the Achaean camp to Achilles and back to the ford of the Xanthus (24.331–469, 24.677–694), his only errand for Zeus in the Iliad, and the gods had urged him to steal Hector's body (24.23–24). He once stole Ares out of the bronze jar in which the sons of Aloeus had shut him (5.385–391). He declines to fight Leto in the battle of the gods (21.497–501; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]), and by Polymele he is the father of the Myrmidon leader Eudorus (16.179–186).

In the Odyssey he carries Zeus' order to Calypso (*Odyssey* 5.28–148), gives Odysseus the herb moly against Circe (*Odyssey* 10.277–306), and leads the souls of the suitors down to Hades (*Odyssey* 24.1–14). There he is also a god of the herdsmen: the swineherd Eumaeus sets aside a portion of his sacrifice “for the nymphs and for Hermes, son of Maia” (*Odyssey* 14.435; see [[maia]] and [[nymphs]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes him the son of Zeus and [[maia|Maia]], daughter of Atlas (*Theogony* 938–939), and gives him the herds: Hecate is good “in the steadings, with Hermes, at increasing the stock”, herds of cattle and wide herds of goats and flocks of sheep (ἐσθλὴ δʼ ἐν σταθμοῖσι σὺν Ἑρμῇ ληίδʼ ἀέξειν, *Theogony* 444–447). The *Homeric Hymn to Hermes* tells how, on the day he was born, he made the first lyre from a tortoise shell and stole the cattle of Apollo; he roasted the meat of two of them on spits, with “the chines of honor”, and divided it into twelve portions by lot, adding a full share of honor to each (*Hymn to Hermes* 120–129). At the end of the Hymn he is made lord of the flocks, of “white-tusked boars”, dogs and sheep, all that the wide earth nourishes (*Hymn to Hermes* 567–571), and in the *Hymn to Pan*, “god though he was”, he tends sheep for a mortal man in Arcadia for love of a nymph, the daughter of Dryops, who bears him Pan (*Hymn* 19.30–37; see [[pan]]). Apollodorus tells how he killed Argus with a stone, “and so he was called Argeiphontes” (*Library* 2.1.3). In the later story of Pelops' chariot race, the charioteer Myrtilus, whom [[Pelops]] used and then killed, was a son of Hermes (Apollodorus, *Epitome* 2.4–9). Horace's hymn to Mercury recalls the night of the ransom: with the god as his guide, rich Priam left Ilium and slipped past the proud sons of Atreus, the Thessalian fires and the camp hostile to Troy (*quin et Atridas duce te superbos / Ilio dives Priamus relicto / Thessalosque ignis et iniqua Troiae / castra fefellit*, *Odes* 1.10.13–16; see [[horace-mercury-and-priam]]).

Virgil's Mercury, sent by Jupiter to Aeneas at Carthage, binds on the golden sandals, takes the wand and swoops down to the sea like a bird, as Hermes does on his way to Calypso (*Aeneid* 4; see [[virgils-mercury]]).
