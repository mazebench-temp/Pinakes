## Etymology

Ἑρμείας (Hermeias) is the longer epic form of the name; Homer also uses Ἑρμῆς (Hermes), as at 20.72. The name is often connected with ἕρμα (“a heap of stones”), the cairns that marked roads and boundaries and later gave their name to the stone pillars called herms; the connection is uncertain. The god is already named on Linear B tablets from Pylos, where the name is read as *e-ma-a₂*.

## Descriptions

{{quote:778cced3-e225-5437-8786-4ee584223e25}}

διάκτορος is traditionally “guide” or “messenger”; its formation is disputed. ἀργεϊφόντης is traditionally “slayer of Argus”, the many-eyed watchman Hermes killed in the story of Io; others have explained it as “shining” or as “dog-slayer”. The sense was already uncertain in antiquity. See [[guide]] and [[argeiphontes]].

In Book 2 Hermes is the second holder of the scepter that [[Hephaestus]] made. Zeus gave it to him, and he gave it to [[Pelops]]. The god who carries messages between Zeus and men is the link through which a king's authority comes down from Zeus. See [[the-scepter-of-agamemnon]] and [[scepter]].

## Mythology

Later books of the Iliad show him as a guide and a thief. Zeus sends him to lead Priam through the Achaean camp to Achilles (24.333–469), and the gods had urged him to steal Hector's body (24.23–24). He once stole Ares out of the bronze jar in which the sons of Aloeus had shut him (5.385–391). He declines to fight Leto in the battle of the gods (21.497–501), and by Polymele he is the father of the Myrmidon leader Eudorus (16.179–186).

In the Odyssey he carries Zeus' order to Calypso (5.28–148), gives Odysseus the herb moly against Circe (10.277–306), and leads the souls of the suitors down to Hades (24.1–14). There he is called the son of Maia (14.435).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes him the son of Zeus and Maia, daughter of Atlas (*Theogony* 938–939). The *Homeric Hymn to Hermes* tells how, on the day he was born, he made the first lyre from a tortoise shell and stole the cattle of Apollo. Apollodorus tells how he killed Argus with a stone, “and so he was called Argeiphontes” (*Library* 2.1.3). In the later story of Pelops' chariot race, the charioteer Myrtilus, whom [[Pelops]] used and then killed, was a son of Hermes (Apollodorus, *Epitome* 2.4–9).
