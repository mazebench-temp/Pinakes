## Etymology

ἶρις is also the ordinary word for the rainbow. The serpents on Agamemnon's corselet are "like rainbows, which the son of Cronus fixes in the clouds as a sign to mortal men" (11.26–28), and Athena comes down wrapped in a cloud like the "purple rainbow" that Zeus stretches out as a portent of war or of storm (17.547–552). Homer never shows the goddess as a rainbow, though. She runs and flies, and her epithets are about her feet. The origin of the word is unknown. Plato's Socrates derives the name from εἴρειν, "to speak", because she is a messenger (*Cratylus* 408b).

## Descriptions

{{quote:06383a26-5ced-57db-a633-cd4a8bf59986}}

ποδήνεμος = πούς ("foot") + ἄνεμος ("wind"): [[wind-footed]], with feet like the wind. ὠκέα, "swift", is the feminine of ὠκύς. ἄγγελος, "messenger", is her office, and what she carries is an ἀγγελίη, a "message" or "news".

Her commonest formula, πόδας ὠκέα Ἶρις, "swift-footed Iris" (2.790, 2.795), is the feminine of Achilles' πόδας ὠκὺς Ἀχιλλεύς ([[swift-footed]]). Later books add χρυσόπτερος, "golden-winged" (8.398, 11.185), and ἀελλόπος, "storm-footed" (8.409, 24.77).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Iris is the messenger of the Iliad. Zeus sends her to the Trojans in Book 2, to Hera and Athena to turn them back from the battle (8.397–425), to Hector (11.185–210), to Poseidon (15.157–219), and in the last book to Thetis and to Priam (24.77–188). Hera sends her to Achilles without Zeus' knowledge (18.165–202). She also acts on her own: she leads the wounded Aphrodite out of the battle (5.353–369) and carries Achilles' prayer to the Winds (23.198–212). Twice she takes a mortal's likeness: that of Polites in Book 2, and in Book 3 that of Laodice, Priam's daughter, when she calls Helen to the wall (3.121–124).

In the Odyssey the gods' messenger is Hermes, and Iris does not appear. Her name survives there only in a joke: the beggar Arnaeus is nicknamed Irus because he runs errands for anyone who asks (Odyssey 18.5–7).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes Iris the daughter of Thaumas and of Electra, a daughter of Oceanus, and the sister of the Harpies (*Theogony* 265–269). In the *Theogony* she also fetches the water of the Styx in a golden jug when a god must swear the great oath of the gods (780–787). Plato found the genealogy apt. Wonder (θαυμάζειν) is where philosophy begins, and whoever made Iris the child of Thaumas, "Wonder", did well (*Theaetetus* 155d). On the stage she brings Madness to Heracles in Euripides' *Heracles* (822–873), and Aristophanes makes her a comic intruder in the sky city of the *Birds*.
