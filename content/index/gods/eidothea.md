## Name

Εἰδοθέη (Eidotheē). Merry takes it for a name coined with reference to her father, either to his many transformations or to his vast knowledge (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.366): the first would join εἶδος, “form”, and the second εἰδέναι, “know”, to θεά, “goddess” (see [[eidos]]). Zenodotus read Εὐρυνόμη in her verse, and the scholia add that Aeschylus named her Εἰδοθέα in his *Proteus*; Dindorf observed that Aeschylus called her Εἰδώ (Merry's critical note on 4.366; see the translation's note on 4.366). Nauck prints Εἰδώ, from the same scholium, as a fragment of the play (fr. 212 Nauck). Merry adds that she was known in later times as Theonoe (on 4.366): in Euripides' *Helen* the daughter of Proteus is called Eido as a child and Theonoe when she is grown, because she knows all things divine, those that are and those that are to be (*Helen* 10–15).

## Descriptions

She is “one of the gods” to Menelaus, who addresses her as “whoever you are of the goddesses” (*Odyssey* 4.364, 4.376), and the narrator gives her δῖα θεάων, “brilliant among goddesses” (*Odyssey* 4.382, 4.398), the title of Calypso and Circe in the Odyssey (see [[brilliant-among-goddesses]]).

## Family

Daughter of [[proteus|Proteus]], “the daughter of mighty Proteus, the Old Man of the Sea” (*Odyssey* 4.365); “they say that he is my father, who begot me”, she says herself (*Odyssey* 4.387). Homer names no mother.

## Outside Homer

Aeschylus' satyr play *Proteus* named her Eido (fr. 212 Nauck; see [[aeschylus]] and [[proteus]]). In Euripides' *Helen* the daughter of Proteus, Eido as a child and Theonoe when grown, is a prophetess, θεσπιῳδός (*Helen* 145, 859), and like Eidothea she takes Menelaus' side against her own house: she knows that the gods are to debate his homecoming, and she will keep silent and not tell her brother that he has come (*Helen* 10–15, 876–893, 998–1023; see [[euripides-helen]]). Merry reports that Sextus Empiricus read father and daughter as an allegory, Proteus as the first cause and Eidothea as the substance that turns into forms (on 4.459; see [[platos-proteus]] for Proclus' reading of the pair).
