## Etymology

Διώνη (Diōnē) is built on the stem of Zeus' own name, Δι- (as in Διός, Διί), and is usually understood as a female counterpart of the sky god. Tablets from Mycenaean Pylos name a goddess *di-u-ja* (Diwia), whose name is formed on the same stem (PY Tn 316). Whether she is Dione is unknown.

## Family

{{quote:1edee823-43d4-5b5c-acc9-c8260d718a5d}}

Mother of [[Aphrodite]]: μητρὸς ἑῆς, “her mother”, and θυγατέρα ἥν, “her daughter” (5.371). Dione calls her τέκνον ἐμόν, “my child” (5.382). Homer gives Aphrodite one father, Zeus. She is Διὸς θυγάτηρ, “daughter of Zeus” (3.374, 5.131, 5.312), and Zeus too calls her “my child” (5.428). So Dione bore her to Zeus, but Homer never calls Dione his wife and names no other child of hers. She lives among the gods: “many of us who have our homes on Olympus have endured”, she says to her daughter (5.383).

## Descriptions

δῖα θεάων, “brilliant among goddesses” (5.381). The title is not hers alone. The Iliad gives it to Athena (6.305), Hera (14.184) and Thetis (19.6, 24.93), and the Odyssey above all to Calypso and Circe. See [[brilliant]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod names Dione among the gods whom the Muses sing (*Theogony* 17) and among the daughters of Oceanus and Tethys (*Theogony* 353). His Aphrodite has no mother. She grows from the foam that gathers around the severed genitals of Uranus and comes ashore at Cythera and Cyprus (*Theogony* 188–206). Later writers kept both births. [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] counts Dione among the Titanesses, the daughters of Uranus and Earth (*Library* 1.1.3), and makes Aphrodite her daughter by Zeus (1.3.1). In Plato's *Symposium*, Pausanias distinguishes two Aphrodites: the elder, Heavenly Aphrodite, daughter of Uranus without a mother, and the younger, Common Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus and Dione (180d–e; see [[plato-two-aphrodites]]). The *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* counts Dione among the goddesses who stood by Leto when she bore Apollo on Delos (93).

At [[dodona]] Dione was worshipped beside Zeus, and the questions to the oracle scratched on lead tablets there are often addressed to the two together. Roman poets call Venus *Dionaea*, “Dione's daughter” (Virgil, *Aeneid* 3.19).
