## Etymology

Μοῦσα (Mousa), plural Μοῦσαι. The etymology is uncertain. It is often connected with the root *men-* "mind, remember", which would make the Muses goddesses of memory, fitting their mother Mnemosyne ("Memory") in Hesiod.

## In Book 1

{{quote:efb6b93b-3d25-5a78-bafa-a8d83e96c016}}

θεά, "goddess". The poem opens by asking her to sing. The singer presents himself as the mouthpiece of a goddess who knows, not as the poem's author. Compare the Odyssey's first line, where the Muse is named: ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, Μοῦσα.

{{quote:1447f80d-5464-551b-845c-ed9a80e9b022}}

At the book's end the Muses sing "answering one another" (ἀμειβόμεναι) with lovely voices, while Apollo plays the lyre. Their song is the gods' own entertainment, the heavenly counterpart of the poem.

## Outside Homer

The half-line of 1.604, ἀμειβόμεναι ὀπὶ καλῇ, returns once more in Homer. At Achilles' funeral "all nine Muses, answering one another with lovely voices, sang the lament" (Odyssey 24.60–61). The voices that sing at the gods' feast in Book 1 will sing for Achilles when he dies.

In Iliad 2.484–492 the poet asks the Muses for the Catalogue of Ships: "for you are goddesses, and are present, and know all things." Hesiod names the nine Muses, daughters of Zeus and Mnemosyne (*Theogony* 53–79). He says they sing "the things that are, the things to come, and the things that were before" (*Theogony* 38), the same line Homer uses of the seer [[Calchas]] (1.70). See [[calchas-and-the-muses]].
