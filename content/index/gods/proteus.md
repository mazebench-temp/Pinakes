## Name and descriptions

Πρωτεύς (Prōteus), genitive Πρωτέος. Homer names him twice, both times in his daughter's account of him: she is “the daughter of mighty Proteus” (Πρωτέος ἰφθίμου, *Odyssey* 4.365), and he is ἀθάνατος Πρωτεὺς Αἰγύπτιος, “immortal Proteus of Egypt” (*Odyssey* 4.385). Everywhere else he is γέρων ἅλιος νημερτής, “the unerring Old Man of the Sea” (*Odyssey* 4.349, 4.384, 4.401, 4.542, and *Odyssey* 17.140, where Telemachus repeats Menelaus' words to his mother; see [[unerring-old-man-of-the-sea]]), or simply ὁ γέρων, “the old man”. ἅλιος is “of the sea”, from ἅλς, “salt, sea”; the Iliad gives the title, in the genitive ἁλίοιο γέροντος, to the father of Thetis, and the Odyssey to Phorcys as well (see [[old-man-of-the-sea]] and [[phorcys-the-sea-god]]). νημερτής, “unerring”, is νη-, “not”, with the root of ἁμαρτάνω, “miss the mark”. Elsewhere in Homer it is said of words and counsels that do not miss the truth, as in νημερτὲς ἐνίσπες, “tell me truly”, and of no other person. Hesiod gives the word to Nereus, whom men call the Old Man “because he is unerring and gentle” (*Theogony* 233–236; see [[hesiod]]).

He “knows the depths / of the whole sea” and is Ποσειδάωνος ὑποδμώς, “the servant of Poseidon” (*Odyssey* 4.385–386; see [[poseidon]]). ὑποδμώς is found only here in Homer, and Eustathius took the ὑπό to mark service in general, not a servant of lower rank (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.389). He is θεῖος γέρων, “the divine old man” (*Odyssey* 4.395), and ὀλοφώια εἰδώς, “skilled in wiles” (*Odyssey* 4.460). His daughter promises to tell Menelaus “all the wiles of that old man” (ὀλοφώια τοῖο γέροντος, *Odyssey* 4.410), in the verse with which Hermes promises to tell Odysseus all the ὀλοφώια δήνεα, the baleful devices, of Circe (*Odyssey* 10.289; see [[circe]]). Merry calls Proteus the type of a wizard, and compares ὀλοόφρων, “baleful”, the epithet of Atlas (on 4.389; *Odyssey* 1.52; see [[atlas]]).

## Family

His daughter is the sea goddess [[eidothea|Eidothea]], who says of him: “they say that he is my father, who begot me” (*Odyssey* 4.387). Homer names no wife for him.

## Outside Homer

**Aeschylus.** Aeschylus wrote a satyr play called *Proteus*. A few words of it survive, among them Εἰδώ, the name it gave Proteus' daughter, from a scholium on *Odyssey* 4.366 (fr. 212 Nauck; see [[eidothea]] and [[aeschylus]]).

**The king of Egypt.** In Herodotus Proteus is a man of Memphis who became king of Egypt, in whose precinct stood a shrine of “the foreign Aphrodite”, whom Herodotus took to be Helen. The priests told him that Alexander, driven to Egypt by winds on his way home from Sparta, was brought before Proteus, who kept Helen and the stolen treasure for her husband and sent Alexander away; after the fall of Troy Menelaus came to Memphis and received her back. Herodotus quotes this book to show that Homer knew of Alexander's wanderings, the drugs of Polydamna and the gods who held Menelaus in Egypt (Herodotus 2.112–119, quoting *Odyssey* 4.227–230 and 4.351–352 at 2.116.4–5; see [[helen-in-egypt]] and [[herodotus]]). In Euripides' *Helen* he is a mortal king who lived on Pharos, the husband of the sea nymph Psamathe and the father of Theoclymenus and of Eido, called Theonoe when she grew up; Hermes brought Helen to his house, and she waits out the war there and takes refuge at his tomb (*Helen* 4–15, 44–48, 60–65; see [[euripides-helen]]). Psamathe is one of Hesiod's Nereids (*Theogony* 260).

**Plato.** Socrates makes Proteus the image of a speaker who slips away from the question: the sophists of the *Euthydemus* “imitate Proteus, the Egyptian sophist”, and must be held as Menelaus held him until they show what they are in earnest about (*Euthydemus* 288b–c); the rhapsode Ion, like Proteus, takes every shape until he escapes as a general (*Ion* 541e). In the *Republic* no poet may tell lies about Proteus and Thetis, since a god, being the best, remains always in his own form (*Republic* 2, 381b–d). See [[platos-proteus]] and [[plato]].

**Later readers.** Merry reports that later philosophical writers saw in the transformations the doctrines of the Ionian philosophers on the origin of the universe: Sextus Empiricus says that the poet speaks in an allegory, calling the first and most original cause Proteus, and the substance that turns into forms Eidothea (on 4.459). He also reports that Virgil retold the story, setting Proteus in the Carpathian sea (*Georgics* 4.387 and following; on 4.389), and quotes Ovid's list of his shapes (*Metamorphoses* 8.732 and following; on 4.458). See [[virgils-proteus]].
