## Etymology

Ποσειδάων (Poseidaōn), Attic Ποσειδῶν. The name is old: it appears on the Linear B tablets from Pylos as *po-se-da-o*. The first part is often read as πόσις, "lord, husband", and the second is disputed. One common guess is Δα-, "earth", which would make him "lord of the earth".

## Elsewhere in Homer

Poseidon is a son of Cronus and Rhea (see [[rhea]]). When the sons of Cronus drew lots, he won the sea, Zeus the sky and Hades the dead (15.187–193; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]). He favors the Achaeans throughout the Iliad and pursues Odysseus through the Odyssey. In the likeness of the river Enipeus he lay with Tyro, who bore him Pelias and Neleus, and Neleus lived in sandy Pylos (*Odyssey* 11.235–257; see [[tyro-and-her-sons]] and [[neleus]]): Nestor is his grandson.

## Outside Homer

The short Homeric *Hymn to Poseidon* says that the gods gave him a double honor, to be a tamer of horses and a savior of ships, and greets him with the words of the Odyssey's prayers to him, χαῖρε, Ποσείδαον γαιήοχε, κυανοχαῖτα, “Hail, Poseidon, earth-holder, dark-haired” (*Hymn to Poseidon* 4–6; compare *Odyssey* 3.55, 9.528). In the *Shield* ascribed to Hesiod he is ταύρεος Ἐννοσίγαιος, “the Earth-shaker of the bull” (104), a title that W. W. Merry connects with the bulls sacrificed to him (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 3.6). Strabo, quoting *Odyssey* 3.177–178, records at Geraestus in Euboea “a temple of Poseidon, the most notable of those in that region” (*Geography* 10.1.7; see [[geraestus]]), and he placed the Pylians' sacrifice on the shore at the shrine of Samian Poseidon in Triphylia (*Geography* 8.3.16; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]).
