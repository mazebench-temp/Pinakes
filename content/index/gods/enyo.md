## Etymology

Ἐνυώ has the same root as Ἐνυάλιος, Enyalius, a war god whose name Homer uses for Ares (17.211, 20.69) and in the formula “Meriones, the equal of Enyalius, slayer of men” (2.651; see [[enyalius]] and [[equal-of-ares]]). Enyalius is already a god's name on a Linear B tablet from Knossos (KN V 52), long before Homer. The root itself is unexplained.

## In Homer

Enyo is named first where Diomedes chases Aphrodite: he knew that Cypris “was a feeble god, and not one of those goddesses who rule over the war of men, neither Athena nor Enyo, sacker of cities” (5.331–333; see [[aphrodite]]). The line sets her beside Athena as a goddess who belongs in battle, and gives her an epithet otherwise used of heroes, πτολίπορθος, “sacker of cities” (see [[sacker-of-cities]]). Achilles and Odysseus bear it, and among the gods only Ares shares it with her (20.152).

The second time she is in action. When Hector charges, “Ares led them, and lady Enyo” (πότνια, “lady, queen”; see [[queenly]]), and she comes “bringing with her Tumult, shameless in combat” (5.592–593; see [[tumult]] and [[hector]]). The participle is ἔχουσα, literally “holding” or “having”: the poem does not say how she holds him. She does not act again. Ares stays at Hector's side and is wounded by Diomedes later in the book (5.855–863); Enyo is not mentioned again in Homer.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod's Enyo is someone else, one of the Graeae, the two gray-haired daughters of Phorcys and Ceto, “Pemphredo of the lovely robe and saffron-robed Enyo” (*Theogony* 270–273). In Aeschylus' *Seven against Thebes* the seven champions swear by “Ares, Enyo and blood-loving Phobos” (45; see [[rout]] and [[aeschylus-seven]]). Strabo calls the great goddess of Comana in Cappadocia, whom the natives named Ma, Enyo (12.2.3). The Romans identified Enyo with their war goddess Bellona.
