## Etymology

Ἥφαιστος (Hēphaistos). The name has no accepted Greek etymology and may be pre-Greek. It could stand for fire itself: at Agamemnon's sacrifice the entrails are held “over the flame of Hephaestus” (2.426), that is, over the fire.

Son of [[Zeus]]. Here he calls Zeus πατήρ, "father". In Hesiod, by contrast, Hera bears him without a father (*Theogony* 927–929).

## Descriptions

{{quote:fff14d70-614d-52ce-8914-a0fc7622d3c6}}

κλυτοτέχνης = κλυτός (famous) + τέχνη (craft): famed for his craft

{{quote:8f9bb6f1-e051-520e-a2bf-b2793eca4dde}}

περικλυτὸς ἀμφιγυήεις. ἀμφιγυήεις is from ἀμφί (on both sides) + γυῖον (limb). Traditionally it means "lame in both legs"; others take it as "strong in both arms". See [[lame-in-both-legs]].

κυλλοποδίων, “the crook-footed god”, names him by his lameness (18.371, 20.270, 21.331; see [[crook-footed]]). He limps (χωλεύων, 18.411, 18.417, 20.37).

## His works

He built a house for each of the gods on Olympus (1.606–608), Hera's chamber with its secret bolt (14.166–168), and his own house of bronze (18.369–371; see [[house]]). He made the scepter that came down from Zeus to Agamemnon (2.101–108; see [[the-scepter-of-agamemnon]]), the aegis that he gave Zeus to carry (15.308–310; see [[aegis]]), the corselet of Diomedes (8.194–195), tripods that go of themselves to the assembly of the gods and golden handmaids that have mind and voice (18.373–377, 18.417–420; see [[tripod]] and [[the-golden-handmaids]]), and the armor of Achilles (18.468–617; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]] and [[the-making-of-the-arms]]). The phrase ἰδυίῃσι πραπίδεσσι, “with his cunning skill”, is used in Homer only of his work (1.608, 18.380, 18.482, 20.12; *Odyssey* 7.92; see [[with-cunning-skill]]). In the Odyssey he made the gold and silver dogs that guard the palace of Alcinous (*Odyssey* 7.91–94), the silver mixing bowl that the king of the Sidonians gave Menelaus (*Odyssey* 4.615–619 = 15.115–119), and the golden jar, which Thetis said was a gift of Dionysus, in which the bones of Achilles were laid (*Odyssey* 24.73–76).

## Mythology

Homer tells two falls of Hephaestus from heaven. In *Iliad* 1 Zeus seized him by the foot and threw him from the threshold of heaven when he tried to defend his mother, and he fell all day and came down on Lemnos at sunset, where the Sintians cared for him (1.590–594; see [[lemnos]] and [[sintians]]). In Book 18 he says that his mother wished to hide him away because he was lame, and that Thetis and Eurynome, daughter of Oceanus, took him in and kept him hidden for nine years in a cave by the stream of Oceanus, where he made jewelry for them (18.394–405; see [[thetis]] and [[eurynome]]). See [[fall-of-hephaestus]]. Lemnos is “dearest to him of all lands” in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 8.283–284).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes Aglaea, the youngest of the Graces, his wife (*Theogony* 945–946; see [[charis-wife-of-hephaestus]] and [[graces]]). At Zeus' bidding he molded the first woman out of earth, mixing it with water and putting in it a human voice and strength, and Hermes named her Pandora (*Works and Days* 60–82; *Theogony* 570–584; see [[hesiod]]).

Plato's Socrates would ban the story of Hephaestus hurled by his father from the ideal city (*Republic* 2.378d). See [[plato-hephaestus-hurled]].
