## Etymology

Ἄρτεμις (Artemis). The origin of the name is unknown, and it may not be Greek. Forms that may be her name, *a-te-mi-to* and *a-ti-mi-te*, appear on Linear B tablets from Pylos, but the reading is not certain. Plato's Socrates derives it, among other guesses, from ἀρτεμής, “sound, whole”, for her love of virginity (*Cratylus* 406b). The adjective is Homeric, and Aeneas comes back “alive and unhurt” (ζωόν τε καὶ ἀρτεμέα, 5.515) from the temple where she helped to heal him, though the phrase is a formula (7.308).

## Family

Daughter of [[Zeus]] and [[Leto]], and “the sister of the Far-Shooter” (κασιγνήτη ἑκάτοιο, 20.71; see [[apollo]]). When Hera has beaten her in the battle of the gods, she flies weeping to Olympus and sits on her father's knees, while Leto gathers up the bow and arrows she dropped and goes after “her daughter” (21.502–510). Niobe, who boasted that Leto had borne only two children, lost her twelve to the two of them (24.602–609).

## Descriptions

ἰοχέαιρα, her standing epithet (5.53, 5.447, and six times more in the Iliad), is ἰός (“arrow”) + χέω (“pour”): “of the showering arrows” (see [[of-the-showering-arrows]]). She is also χρυσηλάκατος, “of the golden distaff” (some prefer “of the golden arrow”), and κελαδεινή, “clamorous”, for the noise of the hunt (16.183, 20.70); χρυσήνιος, “of the golden reins” (6.205); χρυσόθρονος, “of the golden throne” (9.533; see [[golden-throned]]); and πότνια θηρῶν, “mistress of wild beasts”, and ἀγροτέρη, “of the wild” (21.470–471; see [[queenly]]).

## In Homer

**The archer.** Her arrows, like Apollo's, are Homer's explanation of sudden death: hers for women, his for men. She killed Andromache's mother in her father's halls (6.428) and, in anger, Laodameia, the mother of Sarpedon (6.205). Achilles wishes she had shot Briseis (19.59–60), and Hera tells her that Zeus has made her “a lion to women” (21.483). In the Odyssey she kills Orion and Ariadne, and Penelope prays to her for death (*Odyssey* 5.121–124, 11.324–325, 18.202–204, 20.60–81).

**The huntress.** The poem first names her as the teacher of a hunter:

Her gift does not save him from Menelaus' spear (see [[scamandrius]]). Hera tells her to kill wild beasts in the mountains rather than fight her betters (21.485–486), and the Odyssey pictures her on Taygetus or Erymanthus, delighting in boars and swift deer (*Odyssey* 6.102–108). She sent the boar on Calydon when Oeneus forgot her at the harvest offerings (9.533–540; see [[the-death-of-meleager]]).

**On the Trojan side.** With Leto she heals Aeneas in Apollo's temple on Pergamus (5.445–448; see [[pergamus]]). In the battle of the gods she comes to the Trojans with Apollo and Leto (20.38–40) and faces Hera, who seizes her by the wrists and beats her about the ears with her own bow (21.470–496). Her flight repeats Aphrodite's in Book 5: hurt in battle, she goes up to Olympus to a parent's knees, and Zeus asks her Dione's question, “Who of the heavenly gods has done such things to you, dear child?” (21.509–510 = 5.373–374; see [[aphrodite]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod names Apollo and Artemis as the children of Leto and Zeus (*Theogony* 918–920), and the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* has Leto bear her on Ortygia and Apollo on Delos (14–16). Homer never calls her a virgin. The *Hymn to Aphrodite* names her with Athena and Hestia as the goddesses whom Aphrodite cannot subdue (16–20), and in Callimachus' hymn the child Artemis asks Zeus for lasting virginity (*Hymn* 3.6). In the *Cypria*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, she held the fleet at Aulis until Agamemnon offered his daughter Iphigenia, and snatched the girl from the altar (see [[aulis]] and [[aeschylus-aulis]]).
