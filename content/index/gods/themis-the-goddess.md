## The name

θέμις is the common word for what has been laid down as right, from the root of τίθημι, “set, lay down”: the order that men keep in custom and in judgment (see [[themis]]). The goddess is that order made a person, and only the editor's capital tells the two apart, as with Sleep and Night (see [[sleep]] and [[night]]). Homer has four cases of her name, each once: Θέμιστι (15.87), the vocative Θέμι (15.93), Θέμιστα (20.4) and Θέμιστος (*Odyssey* 2.68).

## Descriptions

καλλιπάρῃος, “fair-cheeked” (15.87), = καλός (“beautiful”) + παρειά (“cheek”). It is an epithet of women and goddesses: Chryseis and Briseis (1.143, 1.184), Theano (6.298) and, of the goddesses, Themis and Leto (24.607; see [[fair-cheeked]]). Hera calls her θεά, “goddess” (15.93).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Themis belongs to the places where gods and men gather. She is the first to greet Hera at the gods' feast in the house of Zeus (15.87–95), and at Zeus' bidding she calls the gods to assembly in his house, from the rivers to the nymphs, on the day he lets them go down to the war (20.4–9; see [[olympians]] and [[assembly]]). In the *Odyssey* Telemachus, at the first assembly held in Ithaca since Odysseus sailed (*Odyssey* 2.26–27), begs the people “by Olympian Zeus and by Themis, who dissolves and seats the assemblies of men” (*Odyssey* 2.68–69). The common word keeps the same company: the Achaeans' place of assembly by the ships of Odysseus is where “the assembly and θέμις” were (11.806–808; see [[themis]]).

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod, Themis is one of the Titans, the children of Earth and Heaven (*Theogony* 135; see [[titans]]), and the Muses sing of “revered Themis” among the gods (*Theogony* 16). She was the second wife of Zeus, after Metis, and bore him the Hours, Eunomia, Dike and Eirene (Good Order, Justice and Peace), who watch over the works of mortal men, and the Fates, Clotho, Lachesis and Atropos (*Theogony* 901–906; see [[hours]], [[moira]] and [[fates]]); in an earlier passage the Fates are daughters of Night (*Theogony* 217–219; see [[hesiod]]).

The *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* counts “Ichnaean Themis” among the greatest goddesses who wait on Leto in her labor on Delos (*Hymn to Apollo* 92–95), and when Apollo is born his mother does not nurse him: Themis gives him nectar and ambrosia “with her immortal hands” (123–125; see [[apollo]] and [[leto]]). Strabo names Ichnae, in Thessaly, as the place where Themis Ichnaea is honored (*Geography* 9.5.14). In the *Hymn to Aphrodite* Anchises wonders whether the goddess before him is Artemis, Leto, Aphrodite, “well-born Themis” or Athena (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 92–94), and the short hymn to Zeus praises him as the god who converses in close counsel with Themis as she sits leaning beside him (*Homeric Hymn* 23.2–3).

In Pindar, when Zeus and Poseidon strove to marry Thetis, Themis, “of good counsel”, spoke among the gods: it was fated that the sea goddess should bear a son mightier than his father, who would wield a weapon stronger than the thunderbolt, and they should give her instead to Peleus, the most pious of men (*Isthmian* 8.27–47; see [[pindar]], [[thetis]] and [[peleus]]).

In Aeschylus' *Eumenides* the Pythia begins her prayer at Delphi with Earth, “the first prophet”, and after her Themis, “who sat second at her mother's oracle” (*Eumenides* 1–4). In *Prometheus Bound* Themis is the mother of Prometheus (18), whom Hesiod makes the son of Iapetus and Clymene (*Theogony* 507–510), and “Themis, and Earth, one form of many names” foretold to him how the future would be fulfilled (211–215), and told him the oracle of the archer who would free him (871–874; see [[aeschylus]] and [[prometheus]]). Plato would not let the young hear that the strife and judgment of the gods came about “through Themis and Zeus” (*Republic* 2.379e–380a; see [[plato]]).
