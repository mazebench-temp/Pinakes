## Etymology

Ἀΐδης (Aïdēs), dative Ἄϊδι. Ancient and modern readers have taken it as "the unseen one" (ἀ- "not" + ἰδεῖν "see"). The derivation is attractive but uncertain. Plato's Socrates says that most people take the name from τὸ ἀιδές, “the unseen”, and call the god Pluto because they fear it, and derives it himself from his “knowing” (εἰδέναι) all fine things (*Cratylus* 403a, 404b).

## Family

{{quote:bacffc5f-a805-5f8a-818e-1919b31d69f4}}

Son of [[Cronus]] and [[Rhea]], and brother of [[Zeus]] and [[Poseidon]]. When the three sons of Cronus divided the world by lot, Hades got ζόφον ἠερόεντα, “the misty darkness”, and he is ἐνέροισιν ἀνάσσων, “lord of those below” (see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Odyssey's eleventh book takes Odysseus to the edge of his realm.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod names Hades among the children whom Rhea bore to Cronus, “mighty Hades, who dwells in halls beneath the earth, with a pitiless heart” (*Theogony* 453–456). Before his echoing halls and those of Persephone a dreadful dog keeps watch, fawning on those who come in and devouring any who try to go out again (*Theogony* 767–773), and Hades carried off Persephone from her mother, and Zeus gave her to him (*Theogony* 913–914; see [[persephone]] and [[hesiod]]). The Homeric *Hymn to Demeter* begins with that rape, and in it the Sun consoles Demeter that Aidoneus is her own brother, who got his share of honor “when first the division into three was made” and dwells among those whose lord the lot made him (*Hymn to Demeter* 1–3, 84–87): the division of which Poseidon speaks in Iliad 15. Plato's Socrates begins his myth of the judgment of the dead from it: “as Homer says”, Zeus, Poseidon and Pluto divided the rule among them when they took it over from their father (*Gorgias* 523a; see [[plato]]).
