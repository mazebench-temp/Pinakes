## Etymology

νύξ, νυκτός is the common word for night, the same word as Latin *nox, noctis* and English *night*. The goddess is the word with a capital, which the editor prints only where night acts as a person (14.259, 14.261).

## Descriptions

{{quote:482d4510-28b0-5de5-bd01-af8beba663de}}

δμήτειρα θεῶν … καὶ ἀνδρῶν: “the tamer of gods and men”. δμήτειρα, from δάμνημι, “tame, overpower”, occurs only here in Homer. The same verb runs through the book: Aphrodite's love and longing “overcome” (δαμνᾷ) all the immortals and mortal men (14.199), desire for Hera has “overcome” the heart of Zeus as never before (ἐδάμασσεν, 14.316), and he sleeps “overcome by sleep and love” (δαμείς, 14.353).

θοή: “swift”, an epithet of night (see [[swift-night]]).

## In Homer

Night is a goddess in one passage, in Sleep's story of the day he first lulled Zeus for Hera. Zeus woke in fury and would have thrown Sleep from the upper air into the sea, but Sleep fled to Night, and she saved him: “he stopped, angry though he was; / for he shrank from doing what would displease swift Night” (14.258–261; see [[sleep]] and [[zeus]]). ἅζομαι, “shrink from, revere”, is the verb of men's awe before a god: Chryses asks the Achaeans to free his daughter “in reverence for the son of Zeus, far-shooting Apollo” (ἁζόμενοι, 1.21). Here the king of the gods feels it before Night. Homer does not say why, or that she is the mother of Sleep.

The common word carries some of the same weight. Night ends the fighting, and heralds and warriors tell each other that “it is good to yield to night too” (7.282, 7.293; see [[yield-to-night]] and [[war-and-night]]); it is “immortal” (14.78) and “ambrosial” (2.57).

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod, Night is one of the first powers. Out of Chaos came Erebus and black Night, and from Night, lying in love with Erebus, came Aether and Day (*Theogony* 123–125). Without lying with anyone she bore Doom, black Fate and Death, Sleep and the tribe of Dreams (211–213), and then Blame and Misery, the Hesperides, the Fates, Nemesis, Deceit and Love (Ἀπάτη, Φιλότης), Old Age and Strife (214–225). Her dreadful house stands in the murk at the sources and limits of all things, wrapped in dark clouds; she and Day greet each other at its bronze threshold and take turns going out over the earth, Day with light for men, Night with Sleep in her arms (744–757; see [[hesiod]]). Aristotle speaks of “the theologians who generate from Night” (*Metaphysics* 12.6, 1071b27), and of the old poets for whom not the first powers, “Night and Heaven, or Chaos, or Ocean”, but Zeus is king (*Metaphysics* 14.4, 1091b4–6; see [[aristotle]]). On the chest of Cypselus at Olympia she was shown holding two children, Death and Sleep, “the nurse of both” (Pausanias 5.18.1).
