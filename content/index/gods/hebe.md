## Etymology

ἥβη is the ordinary Greek word for youth, the bloom and strength of a young man. To reach “the measure of youth” (ἥβης μέτρον) is to come of age (11.225), and Nestor's wish to be young again is εἴθʼ ὣς ἡβώοιμι, “if only I were young” (7.157, 11.670, 23.629). The goddess is that bloom made a person, a fitting servant for gods who never grow old.

## In Homer

{{quote:2490a58c-3f84-5ecf-b764-42420587d9c5}}

πότνια Ἥβη, “lady Hebe”: πότνια (“lady, queen”) is a title of honor for goddesses, as in “ox-eyed queen Hera” (4.50; see [[ox-eyed]]). The Iliad gives her three errands, all among the gods. She pours the nectar at their council (ἐοινοχόει, “poured wine”, the verb used of Hephaestus at the end of Book 1, 1.597–598; see [[cupbearing]] and [[nectar]]); she fits the wheels to Hera's chariot (5.722); and she bathes Ares and dresses him after he has been healed (5.905). The Iliad never names her parents, but these are errands for a daughter of the house, and Hesiod makes Hera her mother and Ares her brother. The poem's other cupbearers of the gods are Hephaestus and Ganymede, whom the gods carried off to pour wine for Zeus “for the sake of his beauty” (20.232–235).

In the Odyssey, Heracles feasts among the immortals and has “Hebe of the lovely ankles, the daughter of great Zeus and of Hera of the golden sandals” (Od. 11.601–604). These lines have often been thought a later addition.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod names Hebe first among the children of Zeus and Hera, with Ares and Eileithyia (*Theogony* 921–923), and tells how Heracles, his labors done, took her as his wife on snowy Olympus (*Theogony* 950–955). Pindar ends the first *Nemean* with the same marriage (*Nemean* 1.69–72). Pausanias saw a gold-and-ivory statue of Hebe by Naucydes beside Hera's in the Heraion near Argos (Pausanias 2.17.5), and at Phlius a sanctuary and place of asylum of a goddess whom the Phliasians first called Ganymeda and later Hebe (Pausanias 2.13.3–4). The Romans identified her with Iuventas, the goddess of youth.
