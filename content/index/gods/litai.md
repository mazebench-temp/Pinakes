## Etymology

λιτή (litē), “prayer, entreaty”, belongs with λίσσομαι, “beg, entreat”, the verb of suppliants, and with λιτανεύω, “entreat” (9.581). Homer has the noun only here and once in the Odyssey, where Odysseus entreats the dead “with vows and prayers” (εὐχωλῇσι λιτῇσί τε, *Odyssey* 11.34). The text prints λιταί lowercase, though they are persons; the translation capitalizes “the Prayers”, and beside them ἄτη is “Blind Folly”.

## Descriptions

The Prayers are χωλαί, “lame”, ῥυσαί, “wrinkled”, and παραβλῶπες ὀφθαλμώ, “with eyes that look askance” (9.503). ῥυσός and παραβλώψ occur nowhere else in Homer. Blind Folly is σθεναρή, “strong”, and ἀρτίπος, “sound of foot” (9.505), a word Homer uses otherwise only of Ares, set against the lame Hephaestus in Demodocus' song of Ares and Aphrodite, where “the slow overtakes the swift” (κιχάνει τοι βραδὺς ὠκύν, *Odyssey* 8.308–311, 8.329). The Prayers' looks are usually explained from the bearing of suppliants and of men who come late to make amends, halting, careworn, and ashamed to look the injured man in the face. Homer does not explain them. They are “daughters of great Zeus” (Διὸς κοῦραι μεγάλοιο, 9.502), the plural of a formula used of Athena (6.304, 6.312, 10.296) and of Artemis (*Odyssey* 6.151). Phoenix gives the singular to Artemis a few lines later, when Oeneus forgets her offering (9.536; see [[artemis]]).

## In Homer

{{quote:c2831218-dcb1-500c-9bd7-7a2efa3feca8}}

Phoenix tells of them at the turn of his plea, after the story of his own youth. Achilles should tame his great spirit, since “even the gods themselves can be turned” by offerings and prayer when a man has done wrong (9.496–501; see [[even-the-gods-can-be-turned]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]). Blind Folly runs ahead “doing harm to men”, and the Prayers come after her and heal the harm (9.505–507). The same half-verse, βλάπτουσʼ ἀνθρώπους, describes Agamemnon's Ate in Book 19 (19.94). Whoever respects the Prayers is helped and heard. Whoever refuses them is followed by Blind Folly, at their request to Zeus, “so that he may be harmed and pay the price” (9.512).

Then Phoenix turns the story on Achilles: “you too grant that the daughters of Zeus be attended / by the honor that bends the minds of other men” (9.513–514). The harm is Agamemnon's blind folly, which he has owned (ἀασάμην, “I was blinded”, 9.116; see [[ate]] and [[agamemnon]]). His gifts and the envoys, the men dearest to Achilles, sent “to entreat” him (λίσσεσθαι, 9.520), are the Prayers that follow it. Achilles, the injured man, must give them honor or be harmed himself (see [[time]] and [[honor-and-dishonor]]).

The verb of the Prayers runs through the speech. Phoenix's mother and his kinsmen begged him (9.451, 9.465), men entreat the gods (9.501), and the Prayers entreat Zeus (9.511). In the story that follows, Meleager refuses the entreaties of the elders and priests, his father, his sisters and mother and his companions, gives way to his wife only when the city is burning, and fights without the gifts (9.574–599; see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]] and [[meleager]]). When the envoys come back, Diomedes' first words are “I wish you had never entreated the noble son of Peleus” (μὴ ὄφελες λίσσεσθαι, 9.698).

Achilles refuses, and the poem never says that Blind Folly followed him. Readers have often found the warning fulfilled when he sends Patroclus out in his place and loses him; ἄτη seizes Patroclus himself when Apollo strikes him (16.805). In Agamemnon's story Ate is Zeus' eldest daughter, flung out of heaven to the works of men (19.91–94, 19.126–131); Phoenix does not name her parent. The Prayers appear nowhere else in Homer.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod gives the same office to Dike, Justice, a maiden born of Zeus. When anyone harms her, she sits beside her father, the son of Cronus, and tells him of men's unjust mind, so that the people pay for the recklessness of their kings (*Works and Days* 256–262). The words are those of 9.511–512: the son of Cronus (Κρονίωνι, Κρονίωνα), harm (βλάπτῃ, βλαφθείς), and payment (ἀποτίσῃ in both).

In Plato's *Republic* Adeimantus complains that the sellers of rites who promise to cure a man's wrongdoing call Homer as witness that men can sway the gods, and he quotes the end of 9.497 and 9.499–501 (2.364b–e). His text reads λιστοί, “open to entreaty”, an adjective from the root of λιταί, where ours has στρεπτοί, “can be turned” (see [[plato-phoenix-and-the-gifts]]).

In tragedy λιταί is the ordinary word for a suppliant's entreaties. The king of Argos, faced with the daughters of Danaus, says that it is not wise “to dishonor these prayers” (τάσδʼ ἀτιμάσαι λιτάς, Aeschylus, *Suppliants* 378), and Medea asks Creon whether he will “respect no prayers” (κοὐδὲν αἰδέσῃ λιτάς, Euripides, *Medea* 326), with Phoenix's verb αἰδέομαι (9.508).
