## Etymology

Νηρηΐδες, “daughters of Nereus”, is formed from the father's name as Βρισηΐς, Briseis, is formed from Briseus (κούρην Βρισῆος, “the daughter of Briseus”, 1.392). Homer never names Nereus. In the Iliad the father of the Nereids is the Old Man of the Sea (γέρων ἅλιος, 18.141; see [[old-man-of-the-sea]]), as he is the father of Thetis (1.358, 1.538), and in the Odyssey they are κοῦραι ἁλίοιο γέροντος, the daughters of the old man of the sea (*Odyssey* 24.58). The word Νηρηΐδες itself occurs in Homer only in Iliad 18 (18.38, 18.49, 18.52). Elsewhere they are the immortal goddesses of the sea, ἀθάναται ἅλιαι (18.86; *Odyssey* 24.47, 24.55), or simply ἅλιαι θεαί, the sea goddesses (24.84).

## Family

Thetis calls them her sisters, κασίγνηται (18.52; see [[kasignetos]]). They live with their father in the depths of the sea, where they gather around Thetis as she sits “beside her aged father” (18.36–38; the verse 18.36 is 1.358), and when she sends them home, it is “to see the Old Man of the Sea and our father's house” (18.141). Hesiod and Apollodorus name their mother Doris, a daughter of Oceanus (see Outside Homer).

## Elsewhere in Homer

**Iliad 24.** When Zeus asks for Thetis to be summoned, Iris plunges into the sea between Samos and Imbros and finds Thetis in her hollow cave, the other sea goddesses sitting gathered around her, while she weeps for the fate of her son, who was to die at Troy far from his homeland (24.77–86; see [[iris]]).

**Odyssey 24.** In the underworld the shade of Agamemnon tells the shade of Achilles of his funeral. When the news came, his mother rose from the sea with the immortal goddesses of the sea, and a cry went up over the water that would have sent the Achaeans running to the ships if Nestor had not held them back. The daughters of the old man of the sea stood around the dead man lamenting piteously and clothed him in immortal garments, and the nine Muses sang the dirge (*Odyssey* 24.47–62; see [[muses]] and [[achilles]]).

## Outside Homer

**Hesiod.** Pontus, the Sea, fathered Nereus, the eldest of his children, ἀψευδέα καὶ ἀληθέα, without falsehood and true; men call him the old man because he is unerring and gentle and does not forget what is right (*Theogony* 233–236). With Doris, a daughter of Oceanus, Nereus had fifty daughters, skilled in blameless works, and Hesiod lists their names (*Theogony* 240–264). Seventeen of the Iliad's thirty-three names stand in his list ([[glauce]], [[cymothoe]], [[speio]], [[thoe]], [[halia]], [[melite]], [[agave-the-nereid|Agave]], [[doto]], [[proto]], [[pherusa]], [[dynamene]], [[nesaea]], [[actaea]], [[doris-the-nereid|Doris]], [[galateia]], [[cymodoce]] and [[nemertes]]), together with Thetis herself (*Theogony* 244), and one verse is the same in both poems: Δωτώ τε Πρωτώ τε Φέρουσά τε Δυναμένη τε (18.43 = *Theogony* 248). Near the end of the poem Hesiod gives Nereus Homer's title, “the old man of the sea” (ἁλίοιο γέροντος), when he tells how two of his daughters bore sons to mortal men: Psamathe bore Phocus to Aeacus, and Thetis bore Achilles to Peleus (*Theogony* 1003–1007; see [[hesiod]]).

**The Hymns.** In the *Hymn to Apollo* Hera tells the gods how she threw her lame son Hephaestus into the broad sea, and how Thetis, the silver-footed daughter of Nereus, took him in and cared for him among her sisters (*Hymn to Apollo* 316–320). In the Iliad Hephaestus himself tells of his fall and of the nine years he spent with Thetis and Eurynome in a hollow cave by the stream of Oceanus (18.394–405; see [[hephaestus]] and [[eurynome]]).

**Apollodorus.** Apollodorus makes the Nereids the daughters of Nereus and Doris and lists forty-five of them; fifteen of the Iliad's names are among them, three of which Hesiod lacks: Panope (Hesiod has a Panopeia), Ianeira and Limnoreia (*Library* 1.2.7; see [[apollodorus]], [[panope]], [[ianeira]] and [[limnoreia]]).

**Aeschylus.** Aeschylus named a tragedy the *Nereids*. Among the few words that survive is a phrase in the feminine plural, δελφινοφόρον πεδίον πόντου διαμειψάμεναι, “having crossed the dolphin-bearing plain of the sea” (fragment 150 Nauck; see [[aeschylus]]).

**Virgil and Ovid.** Virgil puts four of the Iliad's names into a single verse, *Nisaee Spioque Thaliaque Cymodoceque*. In the *Aeneid* they ride on the left of Neptune's chariot with Thetis, Melite and the maiden Panopea (*Aeneid* 5.825–826; see [[virgil-neptunes-chariot]]); in the *Georgics* the same verse, with *Nesaee*, names four of the nymphs in the chamber of Cyrene (*Georgics* 4.338; see [[virgil]]). In Ovid the Nereid Galatea, “whose father is Nereus, whom sea-blue Doris bore”, tells Scylla how the Cyclops loved her (*Metamorphoses* 13.738–897; see [[galateia]] and [[ovid]]).
