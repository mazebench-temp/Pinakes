## Etymology

θάνατος is the common word for death, from the root of θνῄσκω, “die” (see [[thanatos]]). As with Sleep, only the editor's capital marks the god: Monro–Allen print Θανάτοιο at 14.231 and θανάτῳ in Book 16.

## Family

{{quote:3106b6a1-680c-5619-8587-ec64a347a498}}

Death is the brother of [[sleep|Sleep]] (κασιγνήτῳ Θανάτοιο), and in Book 16 the two are twins, διδυμάοσιν (16.672, 16.682). Homer gives them no parents; in Hesiod both are children of [[night|Night]].

## In Homer

Death is a person in two scenes only. In Book 14 he is named, and nothing more, when Hera finds his brother on Lemnos (14.231). In Book 16 the twins Sleep and Death carry the body of Zeus' son Sarpedon from the battlefield to Lycia, where his kin will bury him (16.453–457, 16.666–683; see [[sarpedon]]). The two gods who come for Sarpedon do not kill: the spear has done that, and Death carries the body home.

Everywhere else θάνατος is the thing itself, “black death” and death “that lays men low” (see [[black-death]], [[that-lays-men-low]] and [[thanatos]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes Death, Sleep and the Dreams children of Night, born without a father (*Theogony* 211–213). The brothers live in the dark by Night's house, where the Sun never looks on them; Sleep is gentle to men, but Death has a heart of iron and a pitiless spirit of bronze, keeps whomever he first catches, and is hateful even to the immortal gods (758–766; see [[hesiod]]). In a fragment of Aeschylus' *Niobe*, Death alone of the gods loves no gifts: sacrifice and libation do nothing, he has no altar and no paean, and from him alone Persuasion stands apart (fr. 161 Nauck, quoted by Stobaeus; see [[aeschylus]]). In Euripides' *Alcestis* he is a character: he argues with Apollo over Alcestis' life (24–76), and Heracles lies in wait at her tomb for “the black-robed lord of the dead” and wrestles her back from him (843–849, 1140–1142; see [[euripides]]). On the chest of Cypselus at Olympia a woman held two children, a white one asleep and a black one “like one who is asleep”, and the inscription named them Death and Sleep, with Night the nurse of both (Pausanias 5.18.1).
