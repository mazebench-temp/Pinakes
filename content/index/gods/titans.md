## The name

{{quote:c0e79d8d-d657-54e0-b64e-d6d5667db2d1}}

Τιτῆνες καλέονται, “who are called Titans”, is the only place where Homer uses the name. Hesiod derives it from τιταίνω, “strain, stretch”: their father Heaven called them Titans in reproach, saying that “in straining” they had done a great and reckless deed for which vengeance would come (*Theogony* 207–210). ὑποταρταρίους, “beneath Tartarus”, occurs only here in Homer (see [[tartarus]]).

## In Homer

The Titans belong to the world below. Sleep asks Hera to swear by the Styx, with one hand on the earth and one on the sea, so that “the gods below who are around Cronus” may be witnesses (14.271–274; see [[styx]] and [[oath]]), and she names them all, “who are called Titans” (14.278–279; see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]). Zeus says in Book 8 that Iapetus and Cronus sit at the lowest limits of earth and sea, with no share in the sun or the winds, “and deep Tartarus is all around them” (8.478–481; see [[iapetus]] and [[cronus]]). In Book 15 he tells Apollo that if Poseidon had not yielded, “the gods below who are around Cronus” would have heard of their fight (15.224–225). Homer tells nothing of the war in which they were overthrown, but he knows that Zeus put Cronus “beneath the earth and the barren sea” (14.203–204; see [[fall-of-cronus]]).

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod the Titans are the children of Earth and Heaven: Oceanus, Coeus, Crius, Hyperion, Iapetus, Theia, Rhea, Themis, Mnemosyne, Phoebe, Tethys and, youngest, Cronus (*Theogony* 132–138; see [[oceanus]], [[rhea]] and [[tethys]]). They fought the gods born of Cronus for ten years, until the Hundred-Handers, freed by Zeus, overwhelmed them with rocks, bound them and sent them beneath the earth, as far below it as heaven is above it, to Tartarus (617–735; see [[hundred-handed]] and [[hesiod]]). In the Homeric *Hymn to Apollo*, Hera, angry with Zeus, strikes the earth with her hand and prays to Earth, Heaven and “the Titan gods who dwell beneath the earth / about great Tartarus” for a son of her own (*Hymn to Apollo* 332–336), as in Book 14 she swears with her hand on the earth and names the gods beneath Tartarus.
