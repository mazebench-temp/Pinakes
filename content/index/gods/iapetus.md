## Etymology

Ἰαπετός (Iapetos) has no Greek etymology. Its likeness to Japheth, a son of Noah (Genesis 5.32), has often been remarked, but no connection has been shown.

## In Homer

{{quote:0eec6ccb-bbef-5d71-886b-fff2591c49d5}}

Zeus names him once, in his last words to Hera on the evening of the second day of battle. She may go in her anger to the lowest limits of earth and sea, where the two old gods sit, and he will not care (8.477–483; see [[zeus]] and [[hera]]). The place is Tartarus, below the house of Hades (8.13–16; see [[tartarus]]). They sit (ἥμενοι) in a dark where neither the rays of "Hyperion the Sun" nor the winds reach them (see [[hyperion]]). Iapetus is the only one besides Cronus of the gods imprisoned below whom Homer names; elsewhere they are "the gods below, who are around Cronus" (14.274; 15.225) and "the gods beneath Tartarus, who are called Titans" (14.279; see [[cronus]] and [[fall-of-cronus]]).

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod's *Theogony* Iapetus is one of the twelve children of Uranus and Gaia, whom their father called Titans; Cronus was the youngest (133–138, 207). He married Clymene, a daughter of Oceanus, and she bore Atlas, Menoetius, Prometheus and Epimetheus (507–511). Zeus struck Menoetius down to Erebus with a thunderbolt for his recklessness and overbearing manhood (514–516; see [[erebus]] and [[exultant]]), and Atlas holds up the wide sky at the limits of the earth (517–520, 746–747). Prometheus is "the son of Iapetus" (Ἰαπετιονίδης, 528, 543, 559, 614). Hesiod's Titans are bound below the earth after their war with Zeus (717–735), and among the gods who tremble when Zeus fights Typhoeus are "the Titans beneath Tartarus, who are around Cronus" (851), in words close to those of the Iliad.
