## Etymology

Ἀφροδίτη (Aphroditē). Hesiod explains the name from ἀφρός, "foam", out of which she was born (*Theogony* 195–198). That is a folk etymology, and the real origin of the name is unknown. Many scholars look to the Near East, where the Semitic goddess Astarte shares much of her character and cult. Others have argued for an inherited Indo-European goddess. Neither view has won general agreement.

Mother of [[Aeneas]] by [[Anchises]]. θεὰ βροτῷ εὐνηθεῖσα, "a goddess who lay with a mortal": the Iliad states it without comment, and the *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* tells the story (see [[aphrodite-and-anchises]]). δῖα, "brilliant" ([[brilliant]]), is a general honorific of goddesses.

In the Iliad she is the daughter of Zeus and Dione. When Diomedes wounds her, she falls into her mother's lap on Olympus (5.370–371). In Hesiod she is older than Zeus, born from the sea foam after Cronus castrated Uranus (see Outside Homer).

## Descriptions

Her own epithets appear in later books: φιλομμειδής, "laughter-loving" (3.424, 5.375, 14.211), and χρυσέη, "golden" (3.64). In Book 5, where Diomedes wounds her, she is also Κύπρις, "the Cyprian" (5.330, 5.422, 5.458).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the Iliad Aphrodite takes the Trojan side. Paris will not have her "lovely gifts" thrown in his face (3.64–66). She snatches him from his duel with Menelaus and forces Helen to go to his bed (3.373–420). When Diomedes wounds Aeneas, she wraps her son in her robe. Diomedes wounds her hand, and she flees to Olympus, where Zeus tells her that the works of war are not for her; she should see to the works of marriage (5.311–430). Hera borrows her embroidered strap, which holds love and desire, to seduce Zeus (14.187–223). In the battle of the gods Athena knocks her down (21.416–433). After Hector's death she keeps the dogs from his body and anoints it with oil of roses (23.184–187). The last book recalls the Judgment of Paris, in which he preferred the goddess who offered him "grievous lust" (24.29–30).

In the Iliad the wife of Hephaestus is Charis (18.382–383). In the Odyssey it is Aphrodite, and Demodocus sings of her adultery with Ares (8.266–366).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells of her birth from the foam around the severed genitals of Uranus. She came ashore at Cyprus, and Hesiod explains her names Cythereia and Cyprogeneia from Cythera and Cyprus (*Theogony* 188–200). Her great cult centers were Paphos in Cyprus, where the Odyssey already places her precinct and altar (8.362–363), and the island of Cythera.
