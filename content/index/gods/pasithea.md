## The name

Πασιθέη seems to be made of πᾶς, “all”, and θεός, θεά, “god, goddess”. Hesiod gives the same name to a Nereid, one of the fifty daughters of Nereus (*Theogony* 246; see [[nereids]]), and does not count her among the Graces, whom he calls Aglaea, Euphrosyne and Thalia (907–909; see [[graces]]).

## In Homer

{{quote:e077f683-6720-55c0-bd49-84b5347e97a7}}

When Sleep refuses to lull Zeus a second time, Hera raises her offer from a golden throne to a bride: “one of the younger Graces / to marry and to be called your wife” (14.267–268; see [[sleep]] and [[hera]]). Sleep makes her swear by the Styx that she will give him “one of the younger Graces, / Pasithea, whom I myself have longed for all my days” (14.275–276; see [[oath]] and [[styx]]). Only then does he agree. The poem does not say whether Hera kept her word.

Verse 14.269, in which Hera herself names Pasithea, “for whom you are always longing, all your days”, repeats 14.276 with the person changed. It is missing from the Perseus text of Monro–Allen and is printed here in brackets (see the translation's note on 14.269). Without it, Hera offers only “one of the younger Graces”, and Sleep is the first to name the one he wants. In the Iliad the wife of Hephaestus is another Grace, Charis herself (18.382–383; see [[charis-wife-of-hephaestus]] and [[hephaestus]]).

## Outside Homer

Pausanias, writing of the Graces of Orchomenus, says that Homer made one of them the wife of Hephaestus, naming her Grace, and that he made Sleep a lover of Pasithea; and because Sleep asks for one of “the younger Graces”, some suspected that Homer knew of older Graces too (Pausanias 9.35.4).
