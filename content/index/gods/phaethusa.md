## Etymology

Φαέθουσα (Phaethousa) is the feminine participle of φαέθω, “shine”: “Shining”. The Sun himself is φαέθων, “shining” (Ἠέλιος φαέθων, *Odyssey* 11.16, 19.441; *Iliad* 11.735; see [[helios]]), and the two colts that draw Dawn are named Lampus and Phaethon (*Odyssey* 23.246; see [[eos]]), as the Sun's two daughters are Lampetie and Phaethusa. W. W. Merry calls the nymphs “Bright” and “Shining”, and their mother “Younger”, “fit wife and daughters for the Sun” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 12.132; see [[lampetie]] and [[neaera]]).

## Family

{{quote:6a6315fa-2e18-5d9e-9d8c-c9f2bdc04613}}

Daughter of the Sun, [[helios|Helios]], called here by both his names, “Hyperion the Sun” (see [[hyperion]]), and of [[neaera|Neaera]]; sister of [[lampetie|Lampetie]], with whom she is named. The two are θεαί, “goddesses”, and νύμφαι ἐυπλόκαμοι, “nymphs of the lovely tresses” (see [[nymphs]] and [[with-lovely-tresses]]). Their mother reared them and settled them on Thrinacia, “to dwell far off”, as herders of their father's flocks and herds (see [[thrinacia]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). The Sun's other children in the Odyssey are Circe and Aeetes, whom Perse bore him (*Odyssey* 10.138–139; see [[circe]], [[aeetes]] and [[perse]]).

## Outside Homer

Ovid makes Phaethusa and Lampetie two of the Heliades, the daughters of the Sun who mourned their brother Phaethon at his tomb until they were turned into trees: Phaethusa, *sororum / maxima*, “the eldest of the sisters”, found her feet grown stiff when she tried to throw herself on the ground, and *candida Lampetie*, “fair Lampetie”, trying to come to her, was held fast by a sudden root; and their tears, dripping from the new branches, hardened in the sun into amber (*Metamorphoses* 2.340–366; see [[ovid]]).
