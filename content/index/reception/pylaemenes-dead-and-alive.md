The Catalogue gives the Paphlagonians a single leader: “The Paphlagonians were led by Pylaemenes of the shaggy heart” (2.851). In Book 5 Menelaus kills him with a spear at the collarbone, and Antilochus kills his charioteer Mydon as he turns the horses (5.576–589; see [[pylaemenes]] and [[mydon]]).

In Book 13 he is alive. Harpalion, “son of king Pylaemenes”, who had followed his father to Troy to fight and never came home again, strikes the middle of Menelaus' shield and draws back among his companions, and Meriones shoots him as he goes (13.643–652). The Paphlagonians lift him into a chariot and take him to sacred Ilios, grieving, “and his father went with them, letting tears fall, / and no penalty was paid for his dead son” (13.656–659). The father is not named in that line, but he was named when his son came on, and the mourners are his own Paphlagonians.

The passage in Book 13 also repeats the words of Book 5. Meriones' arrow strikes Harpalion in the right buttock and passes straight through, under the bone and into the bladder (13.651–652). In Book 5 Meriones overtook Phereclus and struck him, and the wound is described in almost the same words: he “struck him in the right buttock; and straight through, under the bone and into the bladder, went the point” (5.66–67; see [[phereclus]]). The lines that bring Pylaemenes back to life use the language of the book in which he died.

Readers have answered the contradiction in three ways.

- **Two men.** The father who walks behind the chariot is another Pylaemenes. But the poem gives no sign of a second man of the name, and it calls Harpalion's father a king. Where the Iliad does repeat a name, it keeps the men apart: the two Phocian leaders called Schedius, both killed by Hector, are sons of different fathers (15.515–516, 17.306–307).
- **A slip.** In a poem of this length, made for performance, a minor figure killed in one battle can come back in another. Horace, who was vexed whenever good Homer nodded, allowed that in a long work sleep may creep in (*Ars Poetica* 359–360; see [[horace]]).
- **Different poets.** The analysts, who divided the Iliad among poets of different times, took the contradiction as evidence that Books 5 and 13 were composed separately.

None of these can be proved. The contradiction stands in the text as we have it.

The name outlived the man. When Pompey had defeated Mithridates and was dividing his lands, Strabo says, he gave some of the inland Paphlagonians to be ruled as kings by “those descended from Pylaemenes” (*Geography* 12.3.1; see [[strabo]]).
