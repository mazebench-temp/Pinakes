**The prologue.** Helen stands at a tomb before a palace on the Nile. “Proteus, while he lived, was lord of this land, / dwelling on the island of Pharos, king of Egypt” (*Helen* 4–5). He married the sea nymph Psamathe and had two children, a son, Theoclymenus, and a daughter whom they called Eido when she was a baby, and Theonoe when she grew up, “for she knew all things divine, / the things that are and the things to come”, an honor she had from her forefather Nereus (6–15). Hera, angry at losing the judgment of beauty, gave Paris a breathing image of Helen made of sky, while Hermes carried Helen herself to the house of Proteus, judging him the most self-controlled of mortals, so that she might keep her bed untouched for Menelaus (31–48). Proteus is now dead, his son wants to marry her, and she has taken refuge at his tomb (60–67; plain renderings throughout; see [[helen-in-egypt]] and [[stesichorus-palinode]]).

**Homer's Egypt on the stage.** Euripides keeps the persons and places of *Odyssey* 4 and changes what they are. Homer's Proteus is “immortal Proteus of Egypt”, the unerring Old Man of the Sea, whom Menelaus seizes on Pharos among his seals (*Odyssey* 4.354–355, 4.384–386, 4.400–406); Euripides' is a mortal king who lived on Pharos and lies in his tomb (*Helen* 4–5, 61–64, 466; see [[proteus]]). Homer's Eidothea is the daughter of Proteus who pities Menelaus and teaches him how to take her father (*Odyssey* 4.363–424); Euripides' daughter of Proteus is named Eido as a child, the short form of Εἰδοθέα (Suda κ 1391), and the name that Aeschylus' satyr play *Proteus* gave her (fr. 212 Nauck; see [[eidothea]]). As Theonoe she knows what is and what is to come, as the Iliad's Calchas knew “the things that are, the things to come, and the things that were before” (*Iliad* 1.70; see [[calchas]]). When she first appears she tells Menelaus that the gods are to debate his homecoming that day, Hera to save him and Aphrodite to ruin him (*Helen* 876–886), as Homer's Menelaus asks the Old Man “who of the immortals fetters me” and how he will go home (*Odyssey* 4.469–470). Like Eidothea she takes Menelaus' side against her own house: she has “a great shrine of justice” in her nature, from Nereus, and she will keep silent and not tell her brother that he has come (*Helen* 998–1023). Hesiod's Nereus is the Old Man who is “unerring and gentle” and knows just counsels (*Theogony* 233–236; see [[hesiod]]), the title that the Odyssey gives Proteus.

**Menelaus.** He comes in shipwrecked, in rags from the wreck, having hidden the phantom Helen in a cave and left his few surviving men to guard her. Since Troy fell he has wandered the sea, sailed along all the desert coasts of Libya, and whenever he came near home a wind drove him back (*Helen* 400–429). Asked how long he wandered, he says: seven years, besides the ten at Troy (*Helen* 773–776). In the Odyssey Menelaus came home “in the eighth year”, after wandering to Cyprus and Phoenicia, Egypt, the Ethiopians, the Sidonians, the Erembi and Libya (*Odyssey* 4.81–85), and the gods held him in Egypt, “eager as I was to come home” (*Odyssey* 4.351–352). Homer's Menelaus brought home the wealth of those lands (*Odyssey* 4.81–82, 4.90); Euripides' has lost everything to the sea. An old woman at the palace gate drives him away, for the master is the enemy of all Greeks, and if he finds him, “death will be your gift of hospitality” (*Helen* 437–482). In Lacedaemon Menelaus rebukes his own attendant for asking whether two strangers at the door should be received (*Odyssey* 4.26–36; see [[xenia]] and [[eteoneus]]).

**The escape.** Helen tells Theoclymenus that Menelaus has drowned, and asks for a ship to give him an empty tomb at sea, since the Greeks, she says, do not bury on land those who die at sea; Menelaus, in his rags, will report his own death, and his men will take the ship (*Helen* 1049–1080). In Homer it is Menelaus who heaps up a mound in Egypt for a man who died far away, his brother Agamemnon (*Odyssey* 4.584).

**The island of the blessed.** At the end the Dioscuri stop Theoclymenus from killing his sister. Helen will be called a goddess after her death and share the libations given to her brothers, and the island off Attica where Hermes first set her down will bear her name; “and for the wanderer Menelaus it is fated by the gods / to dwell in the island of the blessed; / for the gods do not hate the well-born” (*Helen* 1642–1678). Proteus promises Menelaus the same end in the Odyssey, the Elysian plain at the limits of the earth, “because you have Helen as your wife, and in their eyes you are the son-in-law of Zeus” (*Odyssey* 4.561–569). Euripides keeps the promise and gives another reason (see [[elysium-and-the-isles-of-the-blessed]]).
