Strabo comes to these verses in his account of the peoples by the Danube, and stays with them for nine sections (*Geography* 7.3.2–10). Three questions run through them: who the Mysians are, what ἄβιοι means, and whether Homer knew the peoples he names or made them up. The peoples themselves are described under [[abii]].

**The Mysians.** The Getae and the Mysians, Strabo says, were Thracians living on both sides of the Danube, and the Mysians of Asia, between the Lydians, Phrygians and Trojans, came from them. Posidonius was right to take Homer's Mysians for the Mysians of Europe. Zeus turns his eyes “away” (πάλιν) from Troy; to look from the Trojans to the land of the Thracians and name with it the Mysians of Asia, who are not “far off” but border on the Troad and lie behind it and on either side, would confuse the continents (*Geography* 7.3.2). Posidonius explained “who fight hand to hand” as “unconquered”, as good warriors are, and proposed to write Μοισῶν, “Moesians”, in the thirteenth book. Strabo thought it needless to change a reading accepted for so many years, since the people were more likely called Mysians from the first and renamed later (7.3.3–4). In his own day, he adds, Aelius Catus moved fifty thousand Getae from beyond the Danube into Thrace, where they live and are called Moesians (7.3.10; see [[mysians]] and [[thracians]]). The ancient commentary meets the difficulty another way: the poet says not “Thrace” but “the land of the Thracians”, and the Bithynians and Thynians of Asia were Thracian colonists (Porphyry, in the scholia on 13.3).

**The Abii.** Posidonius reported that the Mysians abstain from living things out of piety, and so from their flocks, and live quietly on honey, milk and cheese, and that some Thracians live without women and are held sacred. The poet, he thought, meant all of these together by “proud mare-milkers, milk-drinkers and Abii, the most just of men”, and called them ἄβιοι because they live without wives, a life only half complete, as Protesilaus' house was left ἡμιτελής, “half-finished”, without him (2.701; *Geography* 7.3.3). Strabo prefers men without hearths, who live in wagons. Wrongdoing arises above all over contracts and property, so men who live simply on little may well be called the most just; but to live without women suggests nothing of the kind, least of all among the Thracians and the Getae, whom Menander makes marry ten or eleven wives apiece (7.3.4). The ancient commentary on 13.6 adds other explanations, among them men without bows (βιοί), men who live on wagons and, with the α taken as intensive, the long-lived, and it reports that when all the Scythians bowed to Alexander the Abii alone did not (scholia on 13.6). Aristarchus read the line without τε, so that “the most just of men” went with all the peoples named (scholium A on 13.6).

**Invented, or known?** Apollodorus of Athens, in the preface to the second book of his work on the Catalogue of Ships, praised Eratosthenes' view that Homer and the other early poets knew Greek places well and distant ones hardly at all (see [[strabo-and-the-catalogue]]). Of the forty or so rivers that flow into the Pontus, Apollodorus said, Homer names not even the most famous, and he does not mention the Scythians, but invents “some proud mare-milkers and milk-drinkers and Abii”; the sea was not sailed then, and was called Axenos, “inhospitable”, for its storms and for the savagery of the Scythians, who sacrificed strangers, ate flesh and drank from skulls (*Geography* 7.3.6). Strabo turns the argument round. If the poets did not know the Scythians' savagery, why did they call the sea inhospitable? The peoples beyond the Mysians, Thracians and Getae are still called wagon-dwellers and nomads, live on their herds, on milk and cheese and above all mares' milk, and know nothing of storing wealth or of trade except barter. That the men of old called them mare-milkers, Hesiod is witness, in verses that Eratosthenes himself quoted: “Ethiopians and Ligurians and mare-milking Scythians”. It is no wonder that Homer called “most just” men who live least by contracts and money and hold everything in common except sword and cup, wives and children too, in Plato's way; and Aeschylus agrees with the poet when he calls the Scythians “well-governed, eaters of mare's-milk cheese” (*Geography* 7.3.7; see [[hesiod]] and [[aeschylus]]). The Greeks, he adds, still think them the simplest and least malicious of men (7.3.7).

**Ephorus.** His last witness is Ephorus, who wrote in the fourth book of his history, the *Europe*, that some Scythians and Sauromatae are so savage that they eat men, while others abstain even from other animals, and that he would tell of the most just. Some of the nomad Scythians live on mares' milk and excel all men in justice, and the poets mention them: Homer, when he says that Zeus looks down on the land “of the milk-drinkers and Abii, the most just of men”, and Hesiod, in the so-called *Circuit of the Earth*, where the Harpies carry Phineus “to the land of the milk-drinkers, who have wagons for their homes”. They live cheaply, do not seek wealth, and share all things, wives, children and kin; and toward outsiders they are unconquerable, since they have nothing for which they could be enslaved (*Geography* 7.3.9). Ephorus is not always truthful, Strabo grants, but his point stands: by a common report, believed by the ancients and by later men alike, the nomads farthest from other men were milk-drinkers, ἄβιοι and most just, and Homer did not invent them (7.3.9).

**Elsewhere.** In his first book Strabo cites the verses to show that Homer knew the northernmost peoples: having no common name for them all, he described them by their way of life (*Geography* 1.1.6). In his account of the Paphlagonians he answers Apollodorus again (*Geography* 12.3.26–27).

Herodotus describes how the Scythians milk their mares and live in wagons (*Histories* 4.2, 4.46; see [[herodotus]]). See also [[strabo]] and [[hesiod]].
