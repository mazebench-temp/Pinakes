**Plato.** At the start of the third book of the *Republic* Socrates turns from what the young guardians of his city may hear about the gods to what will make them brave. No one who believes that the things in Hades are real and dreadful will be fearless of death, or choose death in battle before defeat and slavery; so the poets must be asked not to revile Hades but rather to praise it, since what they say is neither true nor useful to men who are to be fighters (386a–c). Then he lists the verses to be struck out. Achilles in the underworld would rather be a hired laborer on the land of a poor man than be king over all the dead (*Odyssey* 11.489–491). Hades fears that the earth will split and that his dreadful, moldering halls, which even the gods loathe, will be shown to mortals and immortals (20.61–65; Plato quotes the last two verses). Achilles cries out that there is in the house of Hades a soul and an image, but no wits in it at all (23.103–104). Teiresias alone among the dead has his wits, while the others flit about as shadows (*Odyssey* 10.495). The soul flies from the limbs to Hades, “lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth” (16.856–857). A soul goes beneath the earth like smoke, gibbering (23.100–101), and the souls of the suitors follow Hermes gibbering, like bats in the depth of a cave (*Odyssey* 24.6–9; *Republic* 386c–387a). “These and all such things,” Socrates says, “we shall beg Homer and the other poets not to be angry if we strike out, not because they are unpoetic or unpleasant for the many to hear, but because the more poetic they are, the less they should be heard by boys and by men who must be free, and must fear slavery more than death” (387b). The dreadful names go with them, Cocytus and Styx, “those below” and “the sapless”, which make all who hear them shudder (387b–c; see [[styx]]).

**Whose soul.** Plato does not say whose soul it is. In the Iliad the couplet is said twice, when Patroclus dies and when Hector dies (16.856–857, 22.362–363), each time after the dying man has foretold his killer's death, and nowhere else in Homer (see [[leaving-manhood-and-youth]]). Two more of Plato's quotations are about the soul of Patroclus: its vanishing like smoke when Achilles reaches out to it in his sleep, and Achilles' cry that follows (23.99–104; see [[patroclus]] and [[psyche]]). The verse before the couplet, “As he spoke, the end of death covered him” (16.855), also closes Sarpedon's last words (16.502; see [[the-end-of-death]]), but there the soul is drawn out with the spear point (16.505), and no flight to Hades follows. Plato's text writes the end of the first verse as one word, Ἄϊδόσδε, “to Hades”; the text printed here divides it, Ἄϊδος δέ. The Suda quotes the first verse in Plato's form under the word ῥέθος (ρ 92).

**Proclus.** In his essays on the *Republic* Proclus asks what the poet means by all that he says of Hades: service on earth preferred to all the riches below, the dwellings that even the gods loathe, the soul and the image bereft of mind, the laments of the souls carried there from here, the bats, the smoke and the gibbering. Socrates, he says, did not reject these things as false, for Plato himself uses such names and riddles, the rivers and the wanderings of souls in the *Phaedo*, and in the myth at the end of the *Republic* the bellowing mouth, Tartarus and the fiery figures who flay the tyrant Ardiaeus (*Republic* 10.614d–616a); he rejected them for the guardians' sake, lest they think death fearful (I 118 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). Proclus' own answer is that the souls that leave the body differ, and so do the places that receive them. Souls purified by philosophy carry vehicles that are pure and full of light; souls drawn by affection to the body and to its life have shadowy and heavy ones, which is why Socrates in the *Phaedo* says that such souls haunt the tombs as shadowy phantoms, and the poet that they flit about as shadows. Among the souls that still cling to this life there are further differences: those that lived a life of action and have not yet given it up hold to the instrument that served their actions and are vexed at being parted from it, “like the soul of Patroclus, leaving manhood and youth”, and in Hades they long for its company, like the soul of Achilles, who would rather live on earth (I 119–120 Kroll). For Proclus, then, the verse describes a kind of soul truly. He closes by restating Socrates' reason for striking such verses out: through them the soul's love of the body grows, and its parting from here is imagined as more fearful than anything (I 122 Kroll).
