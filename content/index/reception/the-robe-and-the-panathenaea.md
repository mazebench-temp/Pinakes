**The Trojan robe.** Helenus tells Hector to have their mother gather the old women at Athena's temple on the height of the city, open its doors with the key, lay on the goddess's knees the loveliest and largest robe in her hall, and promise twelve yearling cows if Athena will pity the city and keep Diomedes away (6.86–98). Hector passes the instructions on (6.269–278), and they are carried out. Hecuba chooses from her chamber the finest of the embroidered robes, the work of the Sidonian women whom Paris brought from Sidon, one that “shone like a star”. The priestess Theano opens the temple, the women raise the ritual cry with their hands lifted, and Theano lays the robe on the knees of the goddess and prays that Diomedes may fall before the Scaean gates. Athena refuses (6.286–311; see [[the-robe-for-athena]], [[hecuba]] and [[theano]]).

**The Athenian robe.** At Athens a robe (πέπλος) was woven for Athena Polias and carried in procession to the Acropolis at her festival, the Panathenaea, held every year, and every fourth year as the Great Panathenaea (Suda π 152). Plato's Socrates speaks of “the robe full of such embroideries”, the wars among the gods, “that is carried up to the Acropolis at the Great Panathenaea” (*Euthyphro* 6b–c). In Euripides' *Hecuba* the captive women of Troy wonder where they will be taken as slaves: perhaps to the city of Pallas, where they will embroider on her saffron robe the horses of her chariot, or the Titans whom Zeus lays to rest with his fire (*Hecuba* 466–474). The women who offered Athena a robe at Troy imagine weaving one for her at Athens, as Hector foresaw Andromache weaving at another woman's bidding in Argos (6.456).

The Iliad was recited at the same festival. A dialogue handed down among Plato's works says that Hipparchus brought Homer's poems to Athens and required the rhapsodes at the Panathenaea to go through them in order, one taking up where another left off ([Plato], *Hipparchus* 228b). The orator Lycurgus says that the Athenians' fathers made a law that Homer alone of the poets should be recited at each Great Panathenaea (*Against Leocrates* 102). An Athenian audience could hear the Trojan women offer their robe at the festival at which their own city offered one.

**The comparison.** Many modern readers have compared the two, and some have wondered whether the Athenian festival colored the Trojan scene. The likeness is in the outline: the women of the city bring a robe to Athena in her temple on the citadel, with a priestess, a procession and a sacrifice. The differences are as plain. The Trojan robe is not woven for the goddess but taken from Hecuba's store, a foreign work brought home on the voyage that brought Helen (6.289–292). It is not a festival gift but a supplication in a crisis, with a sacrifice promised if the goddess will help. It is laid on her knees, not put on her. And robes for images were no Athenian peculiarity. At Olympia sixteen women wove a robe for Hera every fourth year (Pausanias 5.16.2), and at Amyclae the women wove a tunic for Apollo every year (Pausanias 3.16.2). The Trojan scene needs no Athenian model, and cannot be shown to have one.

**The seated goddess.** The Trojan image sits, since the robe is laid on its knees (6.92, 6.273, 6.303). Strabo used the point against the people of Ilium in his day, who claimed that their city had never been wiped out and showed a standing image of Athena as the old one: Homer's was seated. He rejects the reading of those who took ἐπὶ γούνασιν as “beside the knees”, and notes that many ancient wooden images of Athena are seated, as at Phocaea, Massalia, Rome and Chios (Strabo 13.1.41; see [[temple]]).

Virgil painted the refusal. On the walls of Juno's temple at Carthage, Aeneas sees the Trojan women going with loosened hair to the temple of unfriendly Pallas, carrying the robe and beating their breasts, while the goddess keeps her eyes turned away, fixed on the ground (*Aeneid* 1.479–482; see [[virgil]]).
