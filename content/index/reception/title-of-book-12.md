The traditional title of Book 12 is Τειχομαχία, the Teichomachia, “the fight at the wall”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter, this one is Μ, mu (see [[title-of-book-5]]). The book's story is told under [[the-fight-at-the-wall]], and the wall itself under [[the-achaean-wall]].

**The word.** τειχομαχία is not Homer's. It is a compound of τεῖχος, “wall”, and μάχη, “battle”, built like Τειχοσκοπία, “the view from the wall”, the old name for Helen's scene on the wall of Troy in Book 3 (see [[the-view-from-the-wall]]). There the wall is Troy's and the Trojans look out from it; here it is the Achaeans', and the Trojans storm it. In classical prose τειχομαχία is the ordinary word for fighting at a fortification. Herodotus uses it of the end of the battle of Plataea, when the Persians had fled into their wooden fort and climbed its towers. The Lacedaemonians, who did not know how to fight at walls, made no headway; when the Athenians came up, the fight at the wall became fierce and long, until the Athenians climbed the wall and threw it down, and the Greeks poured in (Herodotus 9.70.1–2; see [[plataea]] and [[herodotus]]). His closing words have two verbs of Homer's assault, the one with which the Trojans throw down the battlements (ἔρειπον ἐπάλξεις, 12.258) and the one with which they pour in through the gates (ἐσέχυντο, 12.470). Homer sets the two nouns of the title side by side when the narrator turns from the wall's future to its present: “but now battle and cries blazed around / the well-built wall” (12.35–36). Strabo uses the verb of the Trojan assault: once the wall was built, he says, they fought at it (ἐτειχομάχουν), broke into the naval station and fought at the ships (*Geography* 13.1.36; see [[aristotle-and-the-wall]]).

**Plato.** The name is old. In the *Ion* Socrates gives the seer, not the rhapsode, the passages of Homer that concern divination, “in many places in the Odyssey” and “in many places in the Iliad too, as at the Teichomachia” (ἐπὶ τειχομαχίᾳ), and recites the eight verses of the eagle and the serpent (539b–d, quoting 12.200–207; see [[plato-ion-the-seers-passages]]). It is the oldest surviving use of the name for this part of the poem. It shows that Plato's Teichomachia took in the omen at the ditch, which comes before the Trojans reach the wall. Herodotus' “Aristeia of Diomedes” and Plato's “Prayers” are classical names of the same kind (see [[title-of-book-5]] and [[title-of-book-9]]). Aelian's list of the parts once sung on their own has a “battle at the ships” but no Teichomachia (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14, as reported under [[title-of-book-10]]).

**What the title covers.** The book is the wall's from first to last. After two verses on Patroclus in the hut of Eurypylus, it opens with the wall's end: after Troy has fallen and the Achaeans have gone home, Poseidon and Apollo turn the rivers of Ida against it, Apollo sends their stream at it for nine days while Zeus rains, and Poseidon sends its foundations out to sea and covers the beach again with sand (12.3–33). Then the narrator returns to the battle. The horses balk at the ditch, and the Trojans leave their chariots and go forward on foot in five companies (12.50–107). Asius drives at the gate on the left, which men are holding open for fugitives, and two Lapiths hold it against him (12.110–194). An eagle drops a serpent among the Trojans at the ditch, and Hector overrides Polydamas' reading of the sign (12.195–250). The Trojans tear at the battlements while the two Ajaxes rally the defense and the stones fly like snow (12.251–289). Sarpedon pulls a battlement down and opens a path, but the battle hangs level (12.290–436). Then Hector breaks the gate with a stone, and the Trojans pour over the wall and through the gates (12.437–471).

**What it leaves out.** The name tells of the fighting, not of the book's two great speeches: Hector's answer to Polydamas, “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (12.243; see [[one-omen-is-best]]), and Sarpedon's words to Glaucus on why the kings of Lycia must stand among the foremost (12.310–328; see [[sarpedons-speech]]). It passes over the gods, who decide the fight. Zeus' heart wishes to give Hector glory (12.173–174). He sends the storm of dust from Ida (12.252–255), rouses his son Sarpedon (12.292–293), wards off death from him at the battlement (12.402–403), gives Hector “the higher glory” (12.437) and makes his stone light (12.450). Like “the curtailed battle” of Book 8, and unlike the titles of Books 5, 10 and 11, it names no man (see [[title-of-book-8]]). The book itself divides the honor of the breach: Sarpedon tears away the battlement and “made a path for many” (12.397–399), but it is Hector “who was the first to leap inside the wall of the Achaeans” (12.438). In Book 16 Patroclus says the same words of Sarpedon, dead: “there lies the man who first leapt inside the wall of the Achaeans” (16.558–559).

Nor does the fight at the wall end with the book. In Book 13 Hector holds on where he first leapt through the gates and the wall, by the ships of Ajax and Protesilaus, where the wall was built lowest (13.679–684). In Book 14 Nestor comes out of his hut and sees that the wall of the Achaeans has been thrown down (14.15), and Agamemnon says that neither wall nor ditch has helped them (14.65–68). In Book 15 Apollo kicks the banks of the ditch into it, making a causeway for the Trojans, and throws down the wall as easily as a child by the sea scatters with hands and feet the sand toys he has built (15.355–366). The title names the action of one book; the wall is fought over from Book 12 to Book 15, and its final ruin, told at the start of Book 12, falls outside the poem.
