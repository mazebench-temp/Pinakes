**The ancient note.** Among the marginal notes on the first verse of Book 10, in the T scholia and again in Eustathius' commentary, is a report of what “they say”: that this book was composed by Homer separately and was not part of the Iliad, but was placed in the poem by Peisistratus. The note does not deny that Homer made the book. It says that he made it on its own, and that the Athenian tyrant of the sixth century BCE put it where it stands. It gives no grounds.

The report belongs to a wider ancient story of how the poems were put together. Aelian says that the ancients sang Homer in separate episodes, “a Doloneia” among them, until Peisistratus collected the poetry and produced the Iliad and the Odyssey (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[title-of-book-10]]), and Cicero calls Peisistratus the first, by report, to arrange the books of Homer as we now have them (*On the Orator* 3.137). On that account every episode was once sung on its own, not this one only. By the fourth century the book was read as Homer's: Plato quotes Diomedes' “when two go together” as Homer's words (*Protagoras* 348c–d; see [[when-two-go-together]]), and the chapter of Aristotle's *Poetics* on problems draws on this book more than on any other (see [[aristotle]]).

**The case for an addition.** Modern critics have pressed three observations.

- *The later books are silent.* Nothing after Book 10 mentions the raid, Dolon, Rhesus or the Thracian horses; the name Rhesus returns only as a river of the Troad (12.20). The next morning Odysseus and Diomedes fight side by side without a word about the night (11.310–400), and in the chariot race of Book 23 Diomedes drives the horses of Tros that he took from Aeneas (23.290–292; 5.319–327), not the team that Nestor found “terribly like the rays of the sun” (10.547; see [[the-horses-of-rhesus]]).
- *The book can be lifted out.* Book 9 ends with the chiefs asleep in their huts, where “they lay down to rest and took the gift of sleep” (9.712–713), and Book 11 begins with the dawn (11.1–2). Without Book 10 the story runs on without a gap. Walter Leaf called the opening verses, in which “the others” sleep, “a merely formal tag”, since none of the chiefs can have slept much that night (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 10.1).
- *Its language and manner are its own.* Many of its words occur nowhere else in Homer, among them καταῖτυξ, “skullcap” (10.258), and ληΐτις, “goddess of the spoil” (10.460). It shares whole verses with the Odyssey (10.292–294 = *Odyssey* 3.382–384; 10.534 = *Odyssey* 4.140; 10.576 = *Odyssey* 4.48) and ends with the bath and meal of the Odyssey's welcomes (10.572–579). Its Odysseus carries a bow, as nowhere else in the Iliad (10.260), and the two ride the captured horses, as no one does in the Iliad outside a simile (10.513, 10.529; 15.679–684). It lingers over skins and gear, it kills a suppliant and sleeping men, and it has small inconsistencies: the whip that was not taken is used (10.500–501, 10.530), and the thirteenth man is counted twice (10.495, 10.561; see [[the-evil-dream]]).

**The case for its place.** Others answer that the book cannot have been made apart from the poem around it.

- *It continues the night of Book 9.* The fires Agamemnon sees are the Trojan fires of the end of Book 8 (10.11–13; 8.553–565). The watch he fears for is the one posted in Book 9, with Thrasymedes and Meriones among its captains (9.80–88; 10.57–59). The chiefs meet beyond the ditch where Hector turned back when night fell (10.199–201; 8.485–488), and Diomedes, who ended the embassy's council with a call to fight at dawn (9.707–709), is the first to volunteer (10.219–226).
- *It looks ahead.* Odysseus tells Dolon that Achilles' horses are hard for any mortal but Achilles to master (10.402–404), and in Book 17 Apollo says the same three verses to Hector, who is chasing those horses (17.75–78; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Hector holds his night council by the tomb of Ilus (10.415), and the next day Paris shoots Diomedes leaning against the pillar on that tomb (11.369–377; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]]).
- *Its strangeness suits its subject.* A night raid calls for spies, disguise, stealth and skins, and the Iliad knows the ambush (λόχος) as a test of courage. Achilles taunts Agamemnon for never daring to go out on ambush with the best of the Achaeans (1.226–228), and Idomeneus says that in an ambush the coward changes color and his teeth chatter (13.276–287), as Dolon's do when he is caught (10.375). The Odyssey gives Odysseus other exploits of stealth at Troy: he spied in the city disguised in a beggar's rags (*Odyssey* 4.242–264), and in a tale he tells in disguise he lies in ambush under the walls on a frozen night (*Odyssey* 14.468–503; see [[ambush]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]).

**Modern views.** For the nineteenth-century analysts Book 10 was the clearest case of a later addition, and many scholars who defend the unity of the rest of the poem have allowed that this book may have been composed apart. Georg Danek read it as the work of a poet who knew the Iliad and wrote for its place (*Studien zur Dolonie*, 1988), and M. L. West held it to be by a different poet (*The Making of the Iliad*, 2011). Alexander Shewan defended it as Homer's (*The Lay of Dolon*, 1911), and Casey Dué and Mary Ebbott argued that its differences belong to the traditional poetry of ambush and do not show a later hand (*Iliad 10 and the Poetics of Ambush*, 2010). No consensus has formed. The book stands in every manuscript of the Iliad, and the translation renders it like the others (see the translation's note on 10.1–579).
