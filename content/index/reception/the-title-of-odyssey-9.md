The first name, which Plato and Aristotle already used and which became a proverb for a long story, belongs to the whole of Odysseus' tale in Books 9 to 12 of the Odyssey; the second names the adventure that fills more than four fifths of the book, after the Cicones and the Lotus-eaters.

The title of *Odyssey* 9 in Merry's edition is Ἀλκίνου ἀπόλογοι, “the tales to Alcinous”, with Κυκλώπεια, “the Cyclopeia”, beneath it (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, heading of Book 9); this library calls the book “The Cyclops”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter of the alphabet, this one is ι, iota (see [[title-of-book-5]]). The tale as a whole is told under [[the-tale-to-alcinous]], its teller under [[odysseus]] and its hearer under [[alcinous]]; the Cyclopes and their land are under [[cyclopes]], and the one Cyclops of the story under [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]].

**The tales to Alcinous.** The narrative of *Odyssey* 9–12, Merry notes, was called Ἀλκίνου ἀπόλογος or ἀπόλογοι, “the story told to Alcinous”, as early as the time of Plato (Merry on *Odyssey* 9.1). Plato's Socrates begins the myth of Er with a play on the name: “But it is not a tale to Alcinous that I will tell you, but the tale of a valiant man (ἀλκίμου), Er the son of Armenius, a Pamphylian by birth” (*Republic* 10.614b, plain renderings throughout; see [[plato]]). Proclus explains the jest. The tale to Alcinous, he says, means Odysseus' Nekyia told in the house of Alcinous, the sights of Hades, and it prevailed as a proverb to call every such story “a tale to Alcinous”; Plato's differs only in that Alcinous, who heard the one, was no man of war but looked to peace and luxury, while Er, who told the other, was a valiant man who had died in war. Colotes the Epicurean mocked the opening as childish for its jingle of names, and Porphyry rebuked him for it (*On the Republic* II 111–112 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). Aristotle uses the name twice: “the tale to Alcinous, which is retold to Penelope in sixty lines” (*Rhetoric* 1417a13–15), and the recognition through memory “in the tale told to Alcinous”, though, as Mayor remarked, his reference there is rather to the tears of Book 8 (*Poetics* 1455a2–3; Merry on *Odyssey* 9.1; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-8]] and [[aristotle]]). The Suda keeps the proverb: ἀπόλογος Ἀλκίνου, “of those who talk nonsense and draw out a long speech” (Suda α 3402). The noun is not Homer's. In the book Odysseus asks what he shall recount first, καταλέξω (*Odyssey* 9.14), and offers to tell his homecoming, ἐνίσπω (*Odyssey* 9.37); and the heading gives the king's name in its later, contracted genitive, Ἀλκίνου, where Homer's is Ἀλκινόου (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-7]]).

**The Cyclopeia.** The second name is formed from the people, Κύκλωπες, and names the book by the adventure it tells. The poem names the adventure the same way when it retells in a few lines the story Odysseus told Penelope: he began with how he first overcame the Cicones, and then came to the rich plowland of the Lotus-eating men, “and all that the Cyclops did, and how he took vengeance for his mighty companions, whom the Cyclops ate and did not pity” (*Odyssey* 23.310–313, a plain rendering). The adjective it comes from, Κυκλώπειος, “of the Cyclopes”, is Strabo's word for the built labyrinths in the caves next to Nauplia, “which they call Cyclopean” (*Geography* 8.6.2), and for the life of a people who reap without plowing (*Geography* 11.4.3; see [[plato-the-cyclopes]]).

**What the title covers.** Odysseus' answer to Alcinous begins at the second line of the book and runs on through *Odyssey* 12 (see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). Of this book the Cyclopeia covers more than four fifths. Before it come Odysseus' praise of the feast and the singer, his name and his home (*Odyssey* 9.2–38; see [[the-praise-of-the-feast-in-later-writers]]), the raid on Ismarus and the losses to the Cicones (*Odyssey* 9.39–66; see [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]], [[ismarus]] and [[cicones]]), the storm off Malea (*Odyssey* 9.67–81; see [[malea]]) and the Lotus-eaters (*Odyssey* 9.82–104; see [[the-lotus-eaters]]). The Cyclopeia begins at the next landfall, “and we came to the land of the Cyclopes, overbearing and lawless” (*Odyssey* 9.106), and runs to the last line of the book, when the ships sail on from the island, “glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions” (*Odyssey* 9.566; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]).

**Merry's headings.** Merry's titles for the books of the tale name its stages. This one names the whole tale and its first great adventure; the next, Τὰ περὶ Αἰόλου καὶ Λαιστρυγόνων καὶ Κίρκης, “the events with Aeolus and the Laestrygonians and Circe”, three more; and the twelfth, Σειρῆνες, Σκύλλα, Χάρυβδις, βόες Ἡλίου, “the Sirens, Scylla, Charybdis, the cattle of the Sun” (Merry, headings of Books 9, 10 and 12; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-8]], [[the-title-of-odyssey-10]], [[the-title-of-odyssey-12]], [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[odyssey]]).
