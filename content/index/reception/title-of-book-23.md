The traditional title of Book 23 is Ἆθλα ἐπὶ Πατρόκλῳ, “the Games for Patroclus”, or more literally “prizes in honor of Patroclus”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter, this one is Ψ, psi (see [[title-of-book-5]]). The games are told under [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]], the funeral that comes before them under [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]], and the contests as a custom under [[contests]].

**The words.** Both words are the poem's. ἆθλα, in Homer's form ἄεθλα, are the prizes: when the mound has been heaped, Achilles keeps the army in a wide gathering and brings ἄεθλα out of his ships, “cauldrons and tripods / and horses and mules and the strong heads of oxen, / and fair-belted women, and gray iron” (23.257–261), and he sets out ἄεθλα for each contest in turn (23.262, 23.653, 23.740). ἐπί with the dead man's name in the dative says in whose honor the prizes are given. Achilles will not race his own horses, since “If now we Achaeans were holding games in honor of another, / truly I would take the first prize and carry it to my hut” (εἰ μὲν νῦν ἐπὶ ἄλλῳ ἀεθλεύοιμεν Ἀχαιοί, 23.274–275); and the title's own words are said of the oxen in whose dung Ajax slips in the footrace, “which swift-footed Achilles killed in honor of Patroclus” (οὓς ἐπὶ Πατρόκλῳ πέφνεν, 23.776). In the Odyssey the shade of Agamemnon tells Achilles of the “very beautiful prizes” that Thetis set out in his honor, ἐπὶ σοί, at his own funeral (*Odyssey* 24.85–92).

**Games for the dead.** The poem knows such games outside this book. Nestor, when he receives his prize, remembers how the Epeians buried lord Amarynceus at Buprasium, “and his sons set out prizes in honor of the king”, and he won every contest but the chariot race; and he bids Achilles go, “and likewise honor your companion with funeral games” (ἀέθλοισι κτερέϊζε, 23.629–646; see [[the-funeral-games-of-amarynceus]] and [[nestor]]). Euryalus, the boxer, once went to Thebes for the funeral of Oedipus and beat all the Cadmeians (23.679–680; see [[euryalus]] and [[oedipus]]). In the simile of the chase in Book 22 the horses run for “a great prize … / a tripod or a woman, in honor of a man who has died” (22.163–164; see [[racehorses-at-the-turning-post]]). Hesiod tells how he crossed to Chalcis for the games of wise Amphidamas, where “the sons of the great-hearted man set out many prizes announced beforehand”, and won a tripod with handles for his song (*Works and Days* 654–657; see [[hesiod]]); the words are close to Nestor's of Amarynceus' sons (23.631), and Achilles' first prize is “a tripod with handles” too (23.264).

**The older names.** Aelian, writing that the ancients sang the poems of Homer in separate parts, gives for the Iliad “the Battle at the Ships, and a Doloneia, and the Aristeia of Agamemnon, and the Catalogue of Ships, and the Patrocleia, and the Ransom, and the Games for Patroclus, and the Obliteration of the Oaths” (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[aelian]] and [[title-of-book-16]]). His words are the title's, in the other order: Ἐπὶ Πατρόκλῳ ἆθλα. Much earlier, Plato's Socrates asks the rhapsode Ion to recite what Nestor says to his son Antilochus, advising him to be careful at the turn “in the horse race in honor of Patroclus” (ἐν τῇ ἱπποδρομίᾳ τῇ ἐπὶ Πατρόκλῳ, *Ion* 537a; see [[nestors-lesson-in-driving]]). Plato names the chariot race only, the contest that fills the greater part of the games. Where the name was first given to the book is not known.

**What the title covers.** The games run from the gathering after the mound is heaped to the end of the book (23.257–897). The chariot race is told at most length: Achilles sets out five prizes and keeps his own immortal horses out of the race; Eumelus, Diomedes, Menelaus, Antilochus and Meriones enter, and Nestor tells his son how to take the turn; Apollo strikes the whip from Diomedes' hands and Athena breaks Eumelus' yoke; Antilochus forces his way past Menelaus where the road narrows, and Idomeneus and the son of Oileus quarrel over who is leading; then the prizes are disputed, Menelaus charges Antilochus and the young man gives way, and the fifth prize goes unasked to Nestor (23.262–652; see [[turning-post]], [[antilochus]], [[menelaus]] and [[eumelus]]). Seven shorter contests follow. Epeius beats Euryalus at boxing (23.653–699); Ajax and Odysseus wrestle to a draw (23.700–739); in the footrace Ajax son of Oileus slips in the dung and Odysseus wins with Athena's help (23.740–797); the armed duel of Ajax and Diomedes is stopped when the Achaeans fear for Ajax (23.798–825); Polypoetes throws the lump of iron farthest (23.826–849); Teucer cuts the cord and Meriones hits the dove (23.850–883); and in the javelin Achilles gives Agamemnon the prize without a contest (23.884–897; see [[epeius]], [[odysseus]], [[ajax-son-of-oileus]], [[polypoetes]], [[meriones]], [[teucer]] and [[agamemnon]]).

**What the title leaves out.** The first half of the book belongs to the funeral. The Myrmidons drive three times around the body and mourn; Achilles refuses to wash until Patroclus is burned (23.1–61). The ghost of Patroclus comes to him in his sleep and asks for burial (23.62–107; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]]). The wood is cut on Ida, the Myrmidons cover the body with their hair, and Achilles gives the dead man the lock he had grown for the Spercheius (23.108–153); the pyre takes sheep and cattle, four horses, two dogs and twelve Trojans; Iris brings the North Wind and the West Wind to kindle it; and the bones are gathered into a golden bowl and the mound heaped (23.154–257; see [[cremation]], [[human-sacrifice]] and [[tomb]]).

**The older readers.** The ancient readers went to both halves, and they remembered the funeral at least as well as the games. Plato would strike out the soul of Patroclus going beneath the earth like smoke and Achilles' cry that follows (*Republic* 3.386d–387a; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]), and names the hair given to the dead and the slaughter of the captives at the pyre among the things that must not be believed of Achilles (*Republic* 3.391b); Proclus answered him, and read the winds, the night's libation and the twelve Trojans as a rite for the soul (*On the Republic* I 149–153 Kroll; see [[plato-achilles-and-the-river]]). The orator Aeschines had the ghost's speech read to a jury (*Against Timarchus* 149; see [[aeschines-on-achilles-and-patroclus]]), and Aristotle quotes the line that follows it for the pleasure in mourning (*Rhetoric* 1.11, 1370b25–29; see [[the-longing-for-lament]]). Pausanias found in the lock for the Spercheius proof of an old custom of cutting the hair for rivers (*Description of Greece* 1.37.3; see [[the-lock-for-the-spercheius]]). Virgil's Sibyl tells Aeneas that the unburied may not cross the river, as the ghost tells Achilles (*Aeneid* 6.325–330; see [[virgil-the-unburied]]), and Propertius' dead Cynthia comes to his bed as Patroclus came to Achilles (Propertius 4.7; see [[propertius-the-ghost-of-cynthia]]). Of the games, the chariot race had most readers. Plato and Xenophon quote Nestor's lesson on the turn (*Ion* 537a–c; Xenophon, *Symposium* 4.6; see [[nestors-lesson-in-driving]]), and Aristotle the stump at the turning post (*Poetics* 25, 1461a21–23; see [[the-stump-that-rots]]); Sophocles' *Electra* tells a chariot race at Delphi lost at the turning post (*Electra* 680–763; see [[the-false-report-of-orestes-death]]); and Pausanias quotes the verses on Arion, the horse of Adrastus (*Description of Greece* 8.25.8; see [[arion-the-horse]]). Virgil gave Anchises funeral games in the fifth book of the *Aeneid*, with a ship race in place of the chariot race, a runner who slips in the blood of the sacrifice, an old boxer, and archers shooting at a dove tied to a mast (*Aeneid* 5.104–544; see [[virgil-the-games-for-anchises]]).
