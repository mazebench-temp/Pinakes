In the sixth century BCE Athens and its neighbor Megara fought over Salamis, the island that lies between them. According to Plutarch the quarrel went to arbitration before five Spartan judges, and "most writers" say that Homer's authority helped Solon win it. He inserted a line into the Catalogue of Ships and read out the passage at the hearing, so that Ajax stationed his twelve ships "where the battalions of the Athenians stood" (*Solon* 10).

Plutarch adds that the Athenians themselves called this nonsense. On their account Solon won by other arguments. Ajax's sons Philaeus and Eurysaces became Athenian citizens and handed the island over. The Salaminians buried their dead in the Athenian manner, not the Megarian. And the Delphic oracle had called Salamis "Ionian". In the *Rhetoric* Aristotle gives the case as a standard example of an "ancient witness": "the Athenians used Homer as a witness about Salamis" (1.15, 1375b30).

Strabo reports the charge in full. Some say Peisistratus, others Solon, added 2.558 after 2.557, and the critics reject the line because other passages of the poem contradict it. Why, they ask, are Ajax's ships drawn up at the end of the line, not with the Athenians but with Protesilaus' Thessalians, “where the ships of Ajax and of Protesilaus were” (13.681)? And when Agamemnon reviews the army, Menestheus and the Athenians stand next to Odysseus, while Agamemnon comes to the two Ajaxes elsewhere (4.273, 4.327–329). Strabo adds that the Megarians answered with a version of their own, which gave Ajax ships from Megarian places (9.1.10). The rest of Homer's evidence agrees with the critics: Ajax's ships are drawn up at one end of the camp and Achilles' at the other (11.5–9). Megara itself is not named in the Iliad at all.

Our text has the line, and whether it is an Athenian addition cannot be proved. The story shows how early the Catalogue was treated as a title deed to land, and how early readers suspected that it had been tampered with. Compare the Athenian envoy who claimed a naval command on the strength of Homer's praise of Menestheus, [[herodotus-menestheus]], and the line on Theseus in Book 1, [[theseus-line]].
