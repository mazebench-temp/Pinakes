summary: Gorgythion's head, drooping like a poppy heavy with seed and spring rain (8.306–308), became a pattern for the deaths of the young and the beautiful. Stesichorus gave the image to the dying Geryon, Virgil joined it to a flower cut by the plough for the death of Euryalus, and Ovid gave it to Hyacinthus.

**Homer.** Teucer's arrow, meant for Hector, strikes Gorgythion, a son of Priam and of lovely Castianeira, and his head falls to one side, weighed down by his helmet, as a poppy in a garden droops its head, “heavy with its seed and with the showers of spring” (8.307; see [[the-poppy]], [[gorgythion]] and [[castianeira]]). The flower bows under its own ripeness and the rain, the young man under his helmet.

**Stesichorus.** In the *Geryoneis*, known from scraps of papyrus, Heracles shoots the three-bodied Geryon, and Geryon's neck droops to one side like a poppy that spoils its tender beauty and suddenly sheds its petals (fr. S15 Campbell; see [[stesichorus]]). Already in the sixth century BCE the image has changed: the poppy is not weighed down but stripped, and the likeness is of beauty lost.

**Virgil.** In the ninth book of the *Aeneid*, the young Trojan Euryalus is caught by Latin horsemen after the night raid he made with Nisus, and Volcens drives a sword through his ribs. “Euryalus rolls over in death; over his lovely limbs the blood runs, and his neck sinks and rests on his shoulders: as when a purple flower, cut by the plough, languishes as it dies, or poppies with weary necks bow their heads, when the rain happens to weigh them down” (*Aeneid* 9.433–437). The second flower is Homer's poppy, with the rain kept and the seed head gone. The first is from Catullus, whose love has fallen “like a flower at the meadow's edge, touched by the passing plough” (11.21–24). Virgil leaves out Homer's helmet, but a helmet has already done its work in the episode. Euryalus had put on the plumed helmet of Messapus as spoil, and in the half-light of night it caught the gleam and betrayed him (9.365–366, 9.373–374; see [[virgil]]).

**Ovid.** In the *Metamorphoses* Apollo's discus strikes Hyacinthus, and the boy's dying face is compared to violets, a poppy or lilies broken in a garden, which droop their heads, cannot hold themselves up, and look at the ground; his neck, too weak to bear itself, sinks on his shoulder (*Metamorphoses* 10.190–195; see [[ovid]]). The garden is Homer's, and the neck that rests on the shoulder is Virgil's.

The likeness that Homer gives to one young man, killed in his brother's place, became a likeness for the death of youth itself.
