Strabo's *Geography* opens by naming Homer the founder of geography (1.1.2). Eratosthenes had held that a poet aims to entertain, not to instruct, and Strabo spends much of his first book arguing against him that Homer's knowledge of places was wide and accurate.

In the books on Greece (8–10) the Catalogue is his framework. He takes its regions and towns in turn, quotes the lines and their epithets, and tries to identify each place: which towns still stood, which had moved or changed their names, which could no longer be found. When the poet calls a town "grassy" or "of the many doves", Strabo treats the epithet as a fact to be checked on the ground.

He built on two Hellenistic commentaries. Apollodorus of Athens, a pupil of Aristarchus in the second century BCE, wrote a long work *On the Catalogue of Ships*, which Strabo cites often, sometimes to disagree. (He is not the author of the mythological handbook later transmitted under his name: see [[apollodorus]].) For the Trojan Catalogue (2.816–877), Strabo relied on Demetrius of Scepsis, a native of the Troad, whose *Trojan Battle-Order* ran, Strabo says, to thirty books on some sixty lines of Homer (13.1).

The method produced long debates. The best known concerns Nestor's Pylos. Three places claimed the name, and Strabo argued at length that Homer meant the Pylos in Triphylia, between Elis and Messenia, not the Messenian Pylos of his own day (8.3). Most scholars now identify Nestor's home with the Mycenaean palace excavated by Carl Blegen from 1939 at Ano Englianos in Messenia, whose Linear B tablets name the place Pylos. Strabo also reports the charge that Athens had added a line to the Catalogue to claim Salamis (9.1.10): see [[solon-and-salamis]].

For modern debate on the Catalogue's geography and date, see [[catalogue-and-mycenae]]. See also [[strabo]].
