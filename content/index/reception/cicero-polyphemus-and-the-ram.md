**Homer.** At dawn the blinded Cyclops sits in the doorway, feeling along the backs of his flocks as they go out to pasture, and does not notice the men bound beneath them. The ram that carries Odysseus comes last, “weighed down by his wool and by me, thinking shrewd thoughts” (*Odyssey* 9.437–445; see [[ram]] and [[sheep-following-the-ram]]). The Cyclops speaks to it. It was always first, first to the grass, first to the rivers and first to long for the farmstead in the evening, and now it is the very last; surely it misses its master's eye; if only it could speak and tell him where Nobody is skulking, his heart would find relief. Then he sends it out of the door (*Odyssey* 9.447–461; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). Eustathius, as Merry reports, was reminded of the heroes of the Iliad who talk to their horses, Hector, Achilles and Antilochus (*Iliad* 8.185, 19.400, 23.402; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 9.447).

**Cicero.** The fifth book of the *Tusculan Disputations* argues that virtue is enough for a happy life, and near its end Cicero takes up the evils that seem to threaten it, blindness and deafness (*Tusculan Disputations* 5.110–117). Democritus, when he had lost his sight, could no longer tell white from black, but could still tell good from bad and just from unjust, and he thought that the sight of the eyes even hindered the sight of the mind; Homer is said to have been blind, “but it is his painting, not his poetry, that we see” (*Tusculan Disputations* 5.114; see [[demodocus-and-the-blind-homer]]). Then the poets: “And so the poets never bring on the augur Tiresias, whom they represent as wise, lamenting his blindness; but Homer, when he had made Polyphemus monstrous and savage, makes him even talk with a ram and praise its fortune, because it could go where it wished and touch what it wished. And rightly; for the Cyclops himself was no wiser than that ram” (*at vero Polyphemum Homerus cum inmanem ferumque finxisset, cum ariete etiam conloquentem facit eiusque laudare fortunas, quod, qua vellet, ingredi posset et, quae vellet, attingere*, *Tusculan Disputations* 5.115, plain renderings throughout; see [[cicero]]). Deafness follows (*Tusculan Disputations* 5.116).

**Merry's objection.** Merry thought that Cicero failed to understand the Cyclops' natural craving for sympathy in his distress, even from an animal, and that his whole description was so unlike the Homeric picture that he must either have forgotten the original or confused it with a later account, perhaps some tragedian's (Merry on *Odyssey* 9.447). In the poem the Cyclops does not praise the ram's fortune: he asks why it comes last, supposes that it grieves for its master, and wishes it had a voice. What Cicero's Cyclops envies, to go where one likes and to touch what one likes, is what the blind giant of the poem has lost. He gropes with his hands to take the stone from the doorway, sits in it with both hands stretched out to catch anyone going out among the sheep, and feels along the backs of his flock (*Odyssey* 9.415–418, 9.440–442). Homer's Tiresias, whom Cicero sets against him, is the blind seer to whom alone among the dead Persephone gave understanding (*Odyssey* 10.492–495).

**Another Cyclops.** In the speeches against Verres the Cyclops is a byword for savagery. Verres in Sicily, Cicero tells the judges, was more dangerous to sailors than Charybdis or Scylla, surrounded by more and fiercer dogs, “a second Cyclops, far more ruthless”, for he besieged the whole island, while the other is said to have held only Etna and that part of Sicily (*Against Verres* 2.5.146; see [[cyclopes]] and [[sicily]]).
