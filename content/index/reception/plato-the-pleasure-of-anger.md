**Homer.** Achilles has just chosen to die after Hector. He wishes that strife might perish from among gods and men, and with it anger, χόλος, which even the wise cannot resist and which is sweeter than honey as it swells in the breast; “so has Agamemnon, lord of men, angered me now” (18.107–111). Then he lets the past be past, and goes to find Hector (18.112–115; see [[cholos]] and [[achilles]]).

**Plato.** In the *Philebus* Socrates sorts the mixtures of pleasure and pain. He has dealt with those of the body, and with those in which the soul sets pleasure against the body's pain, as when a man who is emptied suffers from the emptiness but rejoices in the hope of being filled (47c–d). One mixture remains, which the soul takes in by itself. Anger, fear, longing, lament, love, jealousy, envy and the like, Socrates says, are pains of the soul itself; and yet we shall find them full of extraordinary pleasures. “Or do we need to be reminded of ‘which sets even a man of much wisdom to rage, which, far sweeter than dripping honey’, and of the pleasures in laments and longings, mixed in with pains?” (*Philebus* 47d–48a). He quotes the verses without naming the poet, and stops before the smoke. Protarchus agrees, and Socrates goes on to the spectators of tragedy, who weep and rejoice at once, and to comedy, where laughing at a friend's foibles mixes pleasure with envy, a pain of the soul; until the argument shows the same mixture in laments, in tragedy and comedy, “not only in plays but in the whole tragedy and comedy of life” (48a–50b; see [[plato]]). His word for the passion is ὀργή, “anger”; Homer's is χόλος.

**Aristotle.** Aristotle quotes the verse twice in the *Rhetoric*. Things that delight when present delight also when hoped for, and so even being angry is pleasant, for no one is angry with someone on whom revenge seems impossible (*Rhetoric* 1.11, 1370b10–14). In the definition of anger he adds the next line: every anger is followed by a pleasure, the pleasure of the hope of revenge, and so “it has been well said of temper, ‘which, far sweeter than dripping honey, swells in the breasts of men’” (*Rhetoric* 2.2, 1378b4–7; see [[aristotle-anger]] and [[sweeter-than-dripping-honey]]). For Plato the pleasure is mixed into the pain of the passion itself; for Aristotle it lies in the hope of revenge, and in the thoughts of taking it on which the angry man dwells (*Rhetoric* 1378b8–9). Achilles, in the poem, wishes the sweetness gone.
