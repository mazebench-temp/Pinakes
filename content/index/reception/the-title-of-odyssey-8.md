The title of *Odyssey* 8 in Merry's edition is Ὀδυσσέως σύστασις πρὸς Φαίακας, “the introduction of Odysseus to the Phaeacians” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, heading of Book 8); this library calls the book “Odysseus and the Phaeacians”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter of the alphabet, this one is θ, theta (see [[title-of-book-5]]). Its stories are told under [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]], [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]], [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]] and [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]], the people under [[phaeacians]] and the singer under [[demodocus]].

**The words of the title.** The noun σύστασις is not Homer's, and its verb comes once in the poems, of war “joined” (πολέμοιο συνεσταότος, 14.96). Later Greek gave the word the sense of the heading. The Suda glosses σύστασις as “affection, concord”, a metaphor, it says, from horses stabled together, and the passive of its verb as “to become a table-companion and a friend” (Suda σ 1683–1684); the active verb is “to commend”, as Abel, consecrating the firstborn to God, “commended himself” as a lover of God rather than of himself (Suda σ 1580; plain renderings). In Aristotle the same noun is “the structure of the incidents”, the most important of the parts of tragedy, and the Odyssey is his example of a “double structure” (*Poetics* 1450a15, 1453a30–33; see [[systasis]]). The heading means the stranger presented to a people who make him their guest and friend, and the book states that purpose in its own words. Athena, in the likeness of the king's herald, summons the leaders “so that you may learn about the stranger” (*Odyssey* 8.12), and she pours grace on him “so that he might be dear to all the Phaeacians, / and feared and respected” (*Odyssey* 8.21–22; see [[gold-poured-around-silver]]). The heading gives the people's name in its later form, Φαίακας, as Merry's heading of Book 6 does; Homer's accusative is Φαίηκας (*Odyssey* 8.86).

**Aelian's list.** Aelian, writing that the ancients sang the poems of Homer divided into parts, passes in his names for the Odyssey from “the events about the raft” to “the tales to Alcinous” (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[aelian]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-3]]). The raft is the fifth book's (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-5]]), and the tales are Odysseus' story in *Odyssey* 9–12 (see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]); between them he names neither this book nor the two before it (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-6]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-7]]). Aristotle, however, uses the name of the tales for a scene of this book. His example of recognition through memory is “the one in the tale told to Alcinous, where, hearing the lyre player, he remembered and shed tears, and so they were recognized” (*Poetics* 1455a2–3). The tears are shed here, at Demodocus' first song and his third, and Merry reports Mayor's remark that Aristotle's reference “is rather to b. 8. vv. 83–95, and 521–534” (Merry on *Odyssey* 9.1; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]] and [[aristotle]]). The recognition follows the tears. Alcinous, who alone noticed them, asks the stranger his name (*Odyssey* 8.532–550), and the tales begin with it: εἴμʼ Ὀδυσεὺς Λαερτιάδης, “I am Odysseus, son of Laertes” (*Odyssey* 9.19, a plain rendering).

**Merry's headings.** Merry's titles for the books of the Phaeacians follow Odysseus inward, a stage to a book: the arrival in their land (Book 6), the entry to the king (Book 7) and the introduction to the people (Book 8), with Ἀλκίνου ἀπόλογοι, “the tales to Alcinous”, for Book 9 (Merry, headings of Books 6–9; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-6]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-7]]). The introduction begins where the entry ended. The king and his guest rise from the house in which they slept, and Alcinous leads the way “to the place of assembly of the Phaeacians, which was built for them beside the ships” (*Odyssey* 8.2–5; see [[assembly]]).

**What the title covers.** The book is one day and its evening, from dawn (*Odyssey* 8.1) to the sunset at which the gifts are brought in (*Odyssey* 8.417), and the supper after it; the introduction is made in stages. In the place of assembly Alcinous presents the stranger without a name: “This stranger, I do not know who he is, has come to my house in his wanderings, / whether from the men of the east or from the men of the west” (*Odyssey* 8.28–29), and he asks the people for a ship and an escort (*Odyssey* 8.31–45; see [[pompe]]). At the feast the singer's first song draws tears that only the king sees (*Odyssey* 8.62–95). At the games the young men try him: Laodamas invites him, Euryalus taunts him as a merchant captain, and he answers with a speech and with a discus thrown beyond every mark (*Odyssey* 8.132–233; see [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]]). Alcinous answers for the people with what they are good at and what they love (*Odyssey* 8.236–255; see [[different-gifts]]); the dancers and the song of Ares and Aphrodite follow. Then the Phaeacians make him their guest. The twelve kings and Alcinous give him a mantle, a tunic and a talent of gold each, and Euryalus a sword with an apology (*Odyssey* 8.387–417; see [[guest-gifts]]). By nightfall the stranger they gathered to see has been feared and respected and made welcome, as Athena intended, and still no one knows his name. Nausicaa, who first received him, bids him farewell (*Odyssey* 8.457–468; see [[nausicaa]]). At supper he honors the singer, asks for the song of the wooden horse and weeps again, and Alcinous asks at last: “Tell me the name by which they called you there, your mother and your father” (*Odyssey* 8.550), and his land, his people and his city, and why he weeps (*Odyssey* 8.550–586). The book ends with the question; the answer opens the next.

**Introduced to each other.** The introduction runs both ways. Twice Alcinous wants the stranger to carry the Phaeacians' name home, at the games and at the dance, “so that the stranger may tell his friends, / when he has returned home, how far we surpass others” (*Odyssey* 8.101–102, 8.251–252), and he asks him to tell it to another of the heroes as he feasts in his own halls, “remembering our excellence” (*Odyssey* 8.241–245). He gives his own golden goblet “so that remembering me all his days” the stranger may pour libations to Zeus at home (*Odyssey* 8.430–432; see [[cup]]), and Nausicaa bids him farewell so that “you may remember me” (*Odyssey* 8.462). The people who learn about the stranger present themselves, in turn, to be remembered.

**The summary to Penelope.** When the poem retells what Odysseus told his wife of his wanderings, his whole stay among the Phaeacians takes four lines: he came to the Phaeacians, who honored him in their hearts like a god and sent him in a ship to his dear native land, giving him bronze and gold in plenty and clothing (*Odyssey* 23.338–341, a plain rendering; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-6]]). Of the games, the songs and the tears it says nothing; the honor and the gifts are this book's.
