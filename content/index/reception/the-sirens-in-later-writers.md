summary: Homer's two Sirens, who sit in their meadow among the bones of men and promise Odysseus the knowledge of all things, were given names, parents, wings and a place on the map by later writers. Strabo found their islets off Surrentum and Parthenope's tomb at Naples; Plato set a Siren on each circle of the spindle of Necessity and made them a figure for words that hold the hearer, and Proclus counted three kinds of them; Cicero read their song as a promise of knowledge, Horace called idleness a Siren, Ovid told how they got their wings, and Virgil's fleet passed their rocks, white with bones, on the night Palinurus was lost.

**Homer's Sirens.** The Sirens come only in Odysseus' tale, in Circe's warning and in the passage of their island, and once more in the summary of his wanderings that he gives Penelope (*Odyssey* 12.39–54, 12.158–200; 23.326). Circe says what they do, not what they are. They sit in a meadow with a heap of bones and dwindling skins about them, and whoever comes near in his ignorance and hears their voice is bewitched and never comes home. The poem speaks of them in the dual, “the two Sirens” (Σειρήνοιιν, *Odyssey* 12.52, 12.167), and gives them no shape, no parents and no names (see [[sirens]]). The ship comes to their island in a sudden calm, “a windless calm, and a god lulled the waves to sleep” (*Odyssey* 12.168–169); Odysseus stops his companions' ears with wax and is bound to the mast, so that he alone hears their song, which promises the knowledge of all that was suffered at Troy and of all that comes to pass on the earth (*Odyssey* 12.173–200; see [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]] and [[wax]]). The oldest allusion to the scene, as W. W. Merry reports from Nitzsch, is a line of Alcman in which Circe herself anoints the ears of Odysseus' companions (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 12.47); in Homer she tells Odysseus to do it (*Odyssey* 12.47–49, 12.177). Their number, names, parents and shape, and the place of their island, were left to later writers.

**Names, parents and home.** A fragment of the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* names their island Anthemoessa, “Flowery”, which the son of Cronus gave them; the writer who quotes it gives their names as Thelxiope or Thelxinoe, Molpe and Aglaophonus, and says that Hesiod made them bewitch even the winds (*Catalogue of Women* fr. 47 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]). Homer's meadow is “the flowering meadow”, λειμῶνʼ ἀνθεμόεντα (*Odyssey* 12.159; see [[flowering]]). The Suda gives three names, Thelxiepeia, Peisinoe and Ligeia, and calls their island Anthemusa (σ 280). Merry reports that Eustathius named Homer's two Aglaopheme and Thelxiepeia, and that when the Sirens were moved with other Homeric marvels to the coast of Italy they became Parthenope, the Siren of Naples, Ligeia and Leucosia, with Molpe and Thelxinoe added (Merry on *Odyssey* 12.39). Euripides calls them daughters of Earth, Χθονὸς κόραι (*Helen* 168). Ovid calls them daughters of the river Achelous, *Acheloides*, “learned Sirens” who were among the companions of Proserpina when she was gathering the flowers of spring (*doctae Sirenes*, *Metamorphoses* 5.552–555; see [[achelous]] and [[persephone]]), and Merry adds that in the legend Ovid follows their mother was one of the Muses (on *Odyssey* 12.39).

**Wings.** Homer says nothing of their shape. Euripides' Helen calls on them as “winged maidens, virgin daughters of Earth, Sirens”, πτεροφόροι νεάνιδες, / παρθένοι Χθονὸς κόραι / Σειρῆνες, to come to her laments with the Libyan pipe, the panpipes or the lyre and answer her tears, and prays that Persephone may send music in tune with her dirges (*Helen* 164–178, plain renderings; see [[euripides]]); in a fragment from an unknown play of his a speaker, with golden wings on his back and “the winged sandals of the Sirens”, will rise to the sky to join Zeus (fr. 911 Nauck, a plain rendering). Ovid tells how they got their wings. When Proserpina was carried off, the Sirens searched for her over the whole earth, and then, so that the seas too might feel their care, they wished to be able to rest on the waves with the oars of wings; the gods were kind, and their limbs grew yellow with sudden feathers, but their maidens' faces and human voices remained, so that the song born to soothe the ears should not lose the use of its tongue (*Metamorphoses* 5.552–563, plain renderings; see [[ovid]]). The Suda describes them the other way round, with the form of sparrows from the breast up and of women below, and reports that the interpreters of myth make them little birds with women's faces (σ 280). The Muses, Proclus says, are said to have overcome the Sirens and to be crowned with their feathers (*On the Republic* II 239 Kroll), and the Suda too says that the Muses were crowned with a Siren's feather (μ 1293).

**The Sirens' islands.** Eratosthenes held that the places of Odysseus' wanderings were invented, and offered as proof that those who thought them real could not agree: some set the Sirens on the Pelorias, the cape of Sicily at the strait, and others on the Sirenussae, more than two thousand stades away, which he took for a three-peaked rock dividing the gulf of Cumae from the gulf of Poseidonia. Strabo answers that the rock neither has three peaks nor rises to a peak at all: it is a long, narrow headland running out from the country of Surrentum toward the strait of Capreae, with the sanctuary of the Sirens on one side of its ridge, and on the other, toward the gulf of Poseidonia, three small, deserted, rocky islets “which they call the Sirens”; on the strait itself stands the Athenaeum, whose name the headland shares (*Geography* 1.2.12, plain renderings; see [[strabo]]). The disagreement, he argues, only confirms that the Sirens were placed somewhere in Italy or Sicily, and the tomb of Parthenope, one of the Sirens, shown at Naples on the same gulf, confirms it further (*Geography* 1.2.13). Describing the coast of Campania, he adds that at Naples a gymnastic contest is held at the bidding of an oracle; that the Athenaeum, which some call the headland of the Sirenussae, has on its tip a temple of Athena founded by Odysseus; and that on the side toward Surrentum a sanctuary is shown, with old offerings from the people round about, who honor the place (*Geography* 5.4.7–8). Further south, off the gulf of Poseidonia, lies the island of Leucosia, named after one of the Sirens, who was cast ashore there after the casting of the Sirens into the deep that the myth tells of (*Geography* 6.1.1). “What poet or historian”, he asks, “persuaded the Neapolitans to speak of a tomb of Parthenope the Siren?” (*Geography* 1.2.18, a plain rendering; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-12]]). The Suda knows Naples as the city where a statue of the Siren Parthenope stands (ν 115).

**Words that hold the hearer.** Hermione, in Euripides' *Andromache*, blames the visits of bad women, who asked her whether she would endure a captive slave sharing her husband's bed: “and I, hearing these words of Sirens, / [of clever, knavish, wily chatterers,] / was puffed up with folly” (*Andromache* 930–938, plain renderings; the bracketed verse is suspected). Plato's Alcibiades says that Socrates makes him feel his life not worth living as it is, and that if he lent him his ears he could not hold out: “so I stop my ears by force, as if against the Sirens, and go off in flight, so as not to sit here beside him and grow old” (*Symposium* 216a, plain renderings; see [[plato]]). In the *Phaedrus* the cicadas sing over the two speakers in the noonday heat. If they see the two dozing, charmed by them like most men at midday, they will laugh at them; but if they see them talking and “sailing past them, as past the Sirens, unbewitched” (ἀκηλήτους), they may give them the gift they have from the gods for men; for the cicadas were once men so struck by the pleasure of song when the Muses were born that they sang without food or drink and died without noticing it (*Phaedrus* 258e–259c, plain renderings). In the *Cratylus* the Sirens are themselves bewitched. No one who has gone to Hades is willing to come back, “not even the Sirens themselves”, but they and all the rest are held under a spell, such fine words does Hades know how to speak (*Cratylus* 403d–e, plain renderings).

**The Sirens of the spheres.** In the myth of Er the spindle of Necessity turns on her knees, and on each of its eight circles stands a Siren, carried round with it, “sending forth one voice, one note”, and from all eight comes a single concord; around sit the three Fates, daughters of Necessity, dressed in white with garlands on their heads, and sing to the Sirens' harmony, Lachesis the things that have been, Clotho the things that are, and Atropos the things that will be (*Republic* 10.617b–c, plain renderings; see [[fates]]). Homer's Sirens sing that they know all that was toiled through at Troy and “all that comes to pass upon the bountiful earth” (*Odyssey* 12.189–191); Plato gives the Sirens the music and the Fates the knowledge of past, present and future. Proclus asks who these Sirens are. Not the Muses, he answers, but divine souls set over the circles; Plato called them Sirens to show that the harmony they give the circles is bodily, and they are heavenly Sirens, distinct from the Sirens of generation, which Plato elsewhere advises us to sail past like Homer's Odysseus. Those begin from a dyad, as the poet shows by speaking of them in the dual (Proclus quotes the words φθογγὴν Σειρήνοιιν, which are not quite Homer's; the dual is at *Odyssey* 12.52 and 12.167), while the heavenly ones begin from a monad, the Siren of the one outermost circle, which leads the other seven (*On the Republic* II 237–238 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). There are Sirens in Hades as well, those of the *Cratylus*, bewitched by the wisdom of Pluto, so that Plato knows three kinds: “heavenly, of Zeus; generative, of Poseidon; underground, of Pluto” (II 239 Kroll, a plain rendering). Elsewhere Proclus gives the Sirens the harmony that binds souls to matter, as the Muses give the harmony that saves them, and the Sirens are those “whom the man who is led upward and saves himself will sail past, pursuing the better harmony and the truly musical, while the many will embrace it, bound by the Sirens to remain in nature and in the sweetness of nature, bewitched by them” (II 68 Kroll, plain renderings). The Suda keeps the allegory in a line: “Sirens: the harmonious and musical powers of the soul” (σ 281, a plain rendering).

**Knowledge, pleasure and idleness.** The Sirens' song is a promise:

{{quote:942650c5-b2f0-5e90-b429-e58644022ae1}}

Cicero, arguing that the love of knowledge is inborn in us, translates it into Latin hexameters:

> O decus Argolicum, quin puppim flectis, Ulixes,
> auribus ut nostros possis agnoscere cantus!
> Nam nemo haec umquam est transvectus caerula cursu,
> quin prius adstiterit vocum dulcedine captus,
> post variis avido satiatus pectore musis
> doctior ad patrias lapsus pervenerit oras.
> Nos grave certamen belli clademque tenemus,
> Graecia quam Troiae divino numine vexit,
> omniaque e latis rerum vestigia terris.

Homer, he says, saw something of this kind in his invention of the Sirens' songs. They did not call back those who sailed past by the sweetness of their voices, or by any novelty and variety of singing, but because they professed to know many things, so that men clung to their rocks from desire of learning; Homer saw that the story could not be approved if so great a man were held entangled by little songs, and so they promise knowledge, which it was no wonder a man eager for wisdom held dearer than his country (*De Finibus* 5.48–49, plain renderings; see [[cicero]]). Cicero's sixth line, “more learned, he reaches the shores of his fathers”, renders the Sirens' promise that the hearer “goes home delighted, and knowing more” (*Odyssey* 12.188), and Merry draws the same distinction from the Greek: τερψάμενος, “he has had his pleasure”, is past, while the knowledge, εἰδώς, abides with him (on *Odyssey* 12.188). Horace, who has been rereading Homer at Praeneste, holds up Ulysses as the example of what virtue and wisdom can do, and reminds his friend, “you know the Sirens' voices and Circe's cups” (*Sirenum voces et Circae pocula nosti*, *Epistles* 1.2.1–2, 1.2.17–23, a plain rendering; see [[horace-on-the-opening-of-the-odyssey]]). In the *Satires* Damasippus, scolding him for writing almost nothing even after retreating to his villa for the Saturnalia, tells him that he will be despised: “you must shun that wicked Siren, Sloth”, *vitanda est improba Siren / desidia*, or else resign with a calm mind whatever he won in a better time of life (*Satires* 2.3.1–16, plain renderings; see [[horace]]); Merry quotes the phrase as the common view of the Sirens (on *Odyssey* 12.39). Ovid, teaching girls the arts that charm, recalls that the Sirens were monsters of the sea, who held back ships with their tuneful voice however fast they came; hearing them, the son of Sisyphus, as Ovid calls Ulysses, all but broke loose, “for his companions had their ears smeared with wax”; song is a winning thing, so let girls learn to sing (*Monstra maris Sirenes erant, quae voce canora / quamlibet admissas detinuere rates* … *nam sociis inlita cera fuit*, *Art of Love* 3.311–316, plain renderings). Merry quotes the half line on the wax for Odysseus' signal with his brows, since speaking would have done no good (on *Odyssey* 12.194). From exile Ovid counts among Ulysses' pleasant delays that “it is no hardship to hear girls singing well” (*Letters from Pontus* 4.10.17, a plain rendering), and Propertius gives Ulysses the labor of having “approached the Sirens' waters with a deaf crew” (*Sirenum surdo remige adisse lacus*, *Elegies* 3.12.34, a plain rendering; see [[propertius]]). The Suda turns the story into morals and into nature. A Greek story, it says, made them women with fine voices who sat on an islet and so delighted those sailing past that they held them there till death; the interpreters of myth make them little birds with women's faces, who deceive the passing sailors and charm their ears with harlots' songs, “and the song of pleasure has no good end, but death alone”; and the true account is that there are places in the sea, narrowed by mountains, where the squeezed current gives out a shrill sound, and those who hear it trust themselves to the current and perish, men and ships together (σ 280, plain renderings).

**The Sirens' rocks.** In the fifth book of the *Aeneid* the god Sleep comes to the helmsman Palinurus on a calm night. Palinurus will not be persuaded: “Do you bid me not to know the face of the calm sea and the quiet waves?” But the god throws him overboard with part of the stern and the rudder, and the fleet runs on safe, trusting in Neptune's promise, “and now, borne on, it was nearing the rocks of the Sirens, once dangerous and white with the bones of many (then the hoarse rocks were sounding far with the ceaseless brine)”, when Aeneas felt the ship drifting without its helmsman, steered it himself through the night, and mourned his friend: “O you who trusted too much in a calm sky and sea, naked, Palinurus, you will lie on an unknown shore” (*iamque adeo scopulos Sirenum advecta subibat, / difficilis quondam multorumque ossibus albos*, *Aeneid* 5.848–871, plain renderings; see [[virgil]] and [[virgil-the-unburied]]). The whitened bones recall Circe's “great heap of bones / of rotting men” (*Odyssey* 12.45–46), but the singers are gone: the rocks were dangerous “once”. Merry takes the Sirens to personify the hidden dangers of the calm sea, which he calls, in Lucretius' words, *placidi pellacia ponti*, “the lure of the calm sea”, and their song to be the sound of water splashing in caves and over broken rocks, like the stories of the Lorelei (on *Odyssey* 12.39; Lucretius 5.1004).
