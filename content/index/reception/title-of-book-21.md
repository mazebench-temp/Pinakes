The noun is Homer's and the adjective later Greek, and the title fits the first half of the book, in which Achilles fills the Scamander with the dead, the river rises against him, and Hephaestus' fire overcomes the river. The traditional title of *Iliad* 21 is Μάχη παραποτάμιος, “the Battle by the River”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter, this one is Φ, phi (see [[title-of-book-5]]). Achilles' fight with the river is told under [[achilles-and-the-river]], and the river himself under [[scamander]].

**The words.** μάχη, “battle”, is Homer's word, and the river has a battle of his own in the book. When the Scamander has all but drowned Achilles, Hera rouses Hephaestus, since the gods “judged eddying Xanthus to be matched in battle” against him (μάχῃ, 21.331–332), as the gods were set in pairs in *Iliad* 20 (20.73–74; see [[hephaestus]]). The adjective παραποτάμιος, “by the river”, is not Homeric. In later Greek it is said of what lies along a river: the Egyptians sailing to Bubastis put in at every town on the river (πόλιν παραποταμίην, Herodotus 2.60.2), the chorus of Euripides' *Bacchae* sing of a fawn leaping over the riverside plain (πεδίον παραποτάμιον, *Bacchae* 873–874), and Aristotle calls the elephant an animal of the riverside, not of the river (*History of Animals* 9.46). Homer says the same thing with a preposition. The Paeonians flee “along the eddying river” (πὰρ ποταμόν, 21.206), and at the end of the book Apollo, in Agenor's likeness, leads Achilles away from the city, “turning him along the deep-eddying river Scamander” (21.603). The nearest the poem comes to the title's words is on the shield that Hephaestus made in *Iliad* 18, where the men of the besieged city and their enemies “set their battle in array and fought beside the river's banks” (μάχην ποταμοῖο παρʼ ὄχθας, 18.533; see [[the-two-cities]]).

Where the name was first given to the book is not known. Aelian's list of the parts of Homer that the ancients sang separately names, for the Iliad, the Battle at the Ships, a Doloneia, the Aristeia of Agamemnon, the Catalogue of Ships, the Patrocleia, the Ransom, the Games for Patroclus and the Breaking of the Oaths, and nothing that answers to this book (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[aelian]] and [[title-of-book-16]]).

**What the title covers.** The river runs through the first half of the book. At the ford of the Xanthus Achilles splits the routed Trojans, drives some across the plain toward the city and pens the rest in the river, where he kills them with the sword until “the water grew red with blood”; then he takes twelve young men alive, “as the penalty for Patroclus, son of Menoetius, who had died” (21.1–33; see [[human-sacrifice]] and [[patroclus]]). Walter Leaf reports that the ford is named only in the formal couplet with which the book begins (21.1–2 = 14.433–434 = 24.692–693), and takes it for a poetical invention for occasional use (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.4). Achilles kills Lycaon, son of Priam, who comes up out of the river and clasps his knees, throws the body into the stream for the fishes, and tells the Trojans that the river to which they sacrifice bulls and horses will not save them (21.34–135; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[horses-for-the-rivers]]). He kills Asteropaeus, grandson of the river Axius, into whose heart the Scamander had put might, and boasts that “the lineage of Zeus is mightier than a river's” (21.136–204; see [[asteropaeus]] and [[axius]]). Then the river speaks. In the likeness of a man he calls out from a deep eddy that his lovely streams are full of corpses and he can find no way to pour his stream into the sea; Achilles leaps into the midst of him, and the river rises against him and chases him over the plain, until Poseidon and Athena give him heart and the Scamander calls on the Simoeis to help him bury Achilles in sand and gravel (21.205–327; see [[simoeis]]). Hera sends Hephaestus, whose fire burns the dead, the trees on the banks and the river himself, until the water boils and the river swears “never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans” (21.328–384; see [[fire]]).

**What the title leaves out.** The second half of the book belongs to the gods and to the city. When the fire is quenched, strife falls on the other gods: “the great sky trumpeted around”, and Zeus laughs on Olympus as he sees the gods coming together in strife (21.385–390). Athena fells Ares with a boundary stone and strikes down Aphrodite as she leads him away; Apollo will not fight Poseidon “for the sake of mortals, / wretched creatures, who, like leaves, at one time / blaze with life”; Hera takes Artemis' bow from her shoulders and beats her about the ears with it; and Hermes will not fight Leto (21.391–513; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]] and [[gods-and-men-not-alike]]). The battle that gave *Iliad* 20 its title is fought here (see [[title-of-book-20]]). Of the pairs set against each other in that book, Hephaestus and the river come to blows first, in the part of this book that the title names; the others fight after it. Then Apollo goes into Ilios, Priam has the gates opened for the fleeing army, and Apollo rouses Agenor, son of Antenor, to stand against Achilles, snatches him away in mist and, in his likeness, draws Achilles off while the Trojans pour into the city (21.514–611; see [[priam]], [[agenor]] and [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]). The river is still there at the end: it is along the Scamander that Achilles chases the god.

**The older readers.** The ancient readers went to three parts of the book: the fight with the river, Achilles' words to Lycaon, and the gods. Plato's Socrates would not let the young believe that Achilles was disobedient to the river, though it was a god, and ready to fight it; Proclus answered that Achilles fought the visible water that stood in his way, or a local power, with Athena and Poseidon at his side (*Republic* 3.391a–b; *On the Republic* I 148–149 Kroll; see [[plato-achilles-and-the-river]]). The *Cratylus* names the Scamander as the river that fought in single combat with Hephaestus (391e; see [[plato-chalcis-and-cymindis]]), and Proclus read that fight as heat and dryness against cold and wet, the opposites from which all becoming is made (*On the Republic* I 95 Kroll; see [[proclus-on-the-battle-of-the-gods]]); Walter Leaf calls the allusion to the enmity of fire and water obvious (on 21.332). The author of *On the Sublime* sets the trumpet of the great sky among the images of the Theomachy (9.6; see [[longinus-wounded-gods]]).

Achilles' words to Lycaon became a saying. Plutarch tells how Callisthenes, out of Alexander's favor, said to himself as he went away, “Patroclus died too, who was far better than you” (*Alexander* 54.1; see [[callisthenes-quotes-achilles]]), and Lucretius gives his reader the same thought to say to himself, with Epicurus among the dead (*On the Nature of Things* 3.1024–1045; see [[lucretius-on-the-great-dead]] and [[patroclus-died-too]]). Virgil gave the scene to Aeneas, who refuses the ransom of the suppliant Magus and leaves Tarquitus to the fishes (*Aeneid* 10.510–560; see [[virgils-lycaon]]), and his Neptune remembers the rivers that groaned, filled with Achilles' dead, when the Xanthus could find no way to roll out to the sea (*Aeneid* 5.803–810; see [[virgil-the-house-of-aeneas]]).

Others took up single details. Sophocles' Neoptolemus tells Philoctetes that Achilles died by no man's hand but a god's, shot by Phoebus, as his mother had told him in this book that he would (*Philoctetes* 331–336; see [[sophocles]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). Strabo used the book for Altes' Leleges and their Pedasus on the Satnioeis, for the bowl that Euneus gave for Lycaon, and for the Paeonians, once called Pelagonians, so that their leader Asteropaeus was not without reason called the son of Pelegon (*Geography* 13.1.7, 13.1.50, 1.2.33, 7 fr. 38–39; see [[strabo]]). Horace's Juno dates the doom of Troy from the day Laomedon cheated the gods of their agreed wage (*Odes* 3.3.21–22; see [[horace]] and [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]]). The grammarian Ptolemy, called Pindarion, a pupil of Aristarchus, wrote a book *On Asteropaeus Mentioned in Homer* (Suda π 3034; see [[asteropaeus]]).
