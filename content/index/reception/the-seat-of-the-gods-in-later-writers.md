summary: The Odyssey's picture of Olympus, the seat of the gods that stands firm forever, unshaken by winds, unwet by rain, untouched by snow, under a cloudless sky, became the later writers' picture of the calm above the world. The treatise On the Cosmos quotes it for the pure upper region where god dwells; Lucretius gives it to the quiet seats of Epicurus' gods; Lucan sets Olympus above the clouds as a figure of the peace the great keep; Seneca makes the calm of the upper sky a likeness of the mind above anger; and Proclus finds in its last verse the poet's own proof that the gods know no strife.

**Homer.** When Athena has spoken to Nausicaa in her dream, she goes back to Olympus, and the poet pauses on the place: the seat of the gods, “where, they say”, stands firm forever; it is not shaken by winds nor wet with rain, snow does not come near it, “but the clear sky / is spread wholly cloudless, and a white gleam runs over it; / in it the blessed gods take their delight all their days” (*Odyssey* 6.42–46; see [[olympus]] and [[blessed-gods]]). The Iliad's Olympus is a snowy mountain, and Merry discusses how the two pictures agree (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 6.42; see the translation's note on 6.42–46). In the same note he gathers the passages of later writers set out here.

**Hesiod and Pindar.** The words ἕδος ἀσφαλὲς αἰεί, “a seat secure forever”, come again in Hesiod and in Pindar. Hesiod gives them to Earth, “broad-breasted Earth, the ever-secure seat of all / the immortals who hold the peak of snowy Olympus” (Γαῖʼ εὐρύστερνος, πάντων ἕδος ἀσφαλὲς αἰεὶ / ἀθανάτων, οἳ ἔχουσι κάρη νιφόεντος Ὀλύμπου, *Theogony* 117–118, a plain rendering; the second verse is bracketed in the text), so that the seat is the earth and the gods' mountain is snowy (see [[hesiod]]). Pindar gives them to heaven: men and gods are one race, but all their power divides them, “the one is nothing, while the bronze heaven stays a secure seat forever” (*Nemean* 6.1–4; Merry on *Odyssey* 6.42, who cites the verse as *Nemean* 6.5; see [[pindar-one-race]]).

**On the Cosmos.** The treatise *On the Cosmos*, which has come down among Aristotle's works but is generally thought not to be his, holds that god is not in the middle of the world, where the earth and this turbid region are, but above, pure in a pure place, which men rightly call οὐρανός, “heaven”, because it is the upper boundary, and Ὄλυμπος because it is “all-shining” and set apart from every darkness and disorderly motion, such as come among us through storms and the force of the winds, “as the poet too said”; and it quotes the four verses from “to Olympus, where, they say” to “a white gleam runs over it” (*On the Cosmos* 6, 400a, quoting *Odyssey* 6.42–45; see [[aristotle]]). The whole of life bears witness, it goes on, by giving the upper region to god, for all men stretch out their hands to the sky when they pray (400a; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]).

**Lucretius.** At the opening of his third book Lucretius praises Epicurus, at whose reasoning the terrors of the mind flee and the walls of the world part: “the majesty of the gods appears, and their quiet seats, which no winds shake, nor clouds sprinkle with rain, nor snow, congealed with sharp frost, falling white, violates; but an ever cloudless sky covers them and laughs with light spread wide. Nature supplies them with everything, and nothing at any time takes away their peace of mind” (*apparet divum numen sedesque quietae, / quas neque concutiunt venti nec nubila nimbis / aspergunt neque nix acri concreta pruina / cana cadens violat semperque innubilus aether / integit et large diffuso lumine ridet: / omnia suppeditat porro natura neque ulla / res animi pacem delibat tempore in ullo*, *On the Nature of Things* 3.18–24, plain renderings throughout; Merry on *Odyssey* 6.42). The wind, the rain, the snow and the cloudless sky are Homer's, in Homer's order; the last verse of the Odyssey's passage, the blessed gods' delight, becomes the Epicurean gods' freedom from care. The next verse turns to what Epicurus has abolished: “but nowhere appear the regions of Acheron” (*On the Nature of Things* 3.25; see [[lucretius]] and [[lucretius-on-the-great-dead]]).

**Lucan.** In Lucan's *Pharsalia* Brutus comes to Cato by night, at the outbreak of the civil war, and urges him to stay out of it: let him live at peace alone, as the heavenly stars roll on unshaken in their course; the air nearer the earth is kindled by thunderbolts, and the depths of the earth take in the winds and the flashing tracts of flame, but “Olympus rises above the clouds. By the law of the gods discord troubles the smallest things; the great keep peace” (*nubes excedit Olympus. / lege deum minimas rerum discordia turbat, / pacem magna tenent*, *Pharsalia* 2.267–273; Merry on *Odyssey* 6.42, who quotes the last words as *pacem summa tenent*).

**Seneca.** Seneca, in *On Anger*, makes the calm of the upper world a likeness of the mind: “The higher part of the world, more ordered and near the stars, is neither gathered into cloud nor driven into storm nor whirled into a tornado; it is free of all tumult; the lower parts are struck by lightning. In the same way the lofty mind, always calm and set in a tranquil station, pressing down beneath it everything from which anger is gathered, is modest, venerable and composed” (*On Anger* 3.6.1; Merry on *Odyssey* 6.42).

**Proclus.** Defending Homer against Plato's charge that he tells of strife among the gods, Proclus answers that there is neither faction nor any mortal division among them, but peace and a life without harm, as the poet himself shows when he speaks of Olympus, that it is spread beneath the gods and that they have every gladness and sights of unspeakable beauty: “in it the blessed gods take their delight all their days” (*On the Republic* I 87 Kroll, quoting *Odyssey* 6.46; see [[proclus-on-the-battle-of-the-gods]]). The battle of the gods in *Iliad* 20 and 21 must then be read as a figure (see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]).

**Later poets.** Merry adds that Tennyson imitates the passage in his *Morte d'Arthur*, in the “island valley of Avilion”, and compares Coleridge's *Hymn in the Vale of Chamouni* (on *Odyssey* 6.42; see [[tennyson]]).
