The title of *Odyssey* 6 in Merry's edition is Ὀδυσσέως ἄφιξις εἰς Φαίακας, “the arrival of Odysseus among the Phaeacians” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, heading of Book 6); this library calls the book “Odysseus Comes to the Phaeacians”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter of the alphabet, this one is ζ, zeta (see [[title-of-book-5]]). Its story is told under [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]], the people under [[phaeacians]] and their land under [[scheria]].

**The words of the title.** The noun ἄφιξις, “arrival”, is not Homer's, but its verb is, and the summary in which the poem retells what Odysseus told Penelope of his wanderings puts this part of the story in the title's words: “and how he came to the Phaeacians after much toil” (ἠδʼ ὡς ἐς Φαίηκας ἀφίκετο πολλὰ μογήσας, *Odyssey* 23.338, a plain rendering), who honored him in their hearts like a god and sent him home in a ship with bronze and gold and clothing (*Odyssey* 23.339–341). The verb comes once in this book, when Nausicaa tells Odysseus how long to wait outside the city: “But when you think that we have reached the house” (ἀφῖχθαι, *Odyssey* 6.297). The heading gives the people's name in its later form, Φαίακας; Homer's is Φαίηκας, the word of the prayer with which the book ends (*Odyssey* 6.327).

**Aelian's list.** Aelian, writing that the ancients sang the poems of Homer divided into parts, gives the names they used for the Odyssey: “the events at Pylos, and the events at Lacedaemon, and the cave of Calypso, and the events about the raft, and the tales to Alcinous”, and so on to “the events at Laertes' farm” (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[aelian]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-3]]). The raft is the fourth name, and it belongs to Book 5 (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-5]]). The fifth, the tales to Alcinous, is Odysseus' own story of his wanderings in *Odyssey* 9–12, a name already current in Plato, whose Socrates introduces the story of Er as “not a tale to Alcinous” (*Republic* 10.614b; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). Between the two, Aelian has no name: neither for this book nor for the two that follow, in which Odysseus comes into the house of Alcinous and is entertained at the feast and the games. Whether the part about the raft ran on to the Phaeacians' shore, or the part named for the tales to Alcinous began with his coming to their land, he does not say.

**Merry's headings.** In Merry's edition this heading is one of a series. Book 5 is Ὀδυσσέως σχεδία, “the raft of Odysseus”; Book 7 is Ὀδυσσέως εἴσοδος πρὸς Ἀλκίνουν, “the entry of Odysseus to Alcinous” (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-7]]); Book 8 is Ὀδυσσέως σύστασις πρὸς Φαίακας, “the introduction of Odysseus to the Phaeacians” (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-8]]); and Book 9 is Ἀλκίνου ἀπόλογοι, “the tales to Alcinous”, with Κυκλώπεια, “the Cyclopeia”, beneath it (Merry, headings of Books 5, 7, 8 and 9). Three titles divide among three books the stay that Aelian passes over: the arrival in the land, the entry into the house, and the introduction to the people.

**What the title covers.** The arrival is gradual, and the book is about its first stage. It begins with the goddess, not the man: while Odysseus sleeps under the olives where *Odyssey* 5 left him, Athena goes “to the land and the city of the Phaeacian men” (*Odyssey* 6.3) and to the chamber of the king's daughter (see [[athena]]). Odysseus wakes not knowing where he is: “Ah me, to the land of what mortals have I come this time?” (*Odyssey* 6.119). He tells the girl that he knows no one “of the people who hold this city and land” (*Odyssey* 6.177), and learns the people's name from her: “The Phaeacians hold this city and land” (*Odyssey* 6.195; see [[nausicaa]]). He does not enter the city in this book. Nausicaa describes it to him, its rampart, its two harbors and the place of assembly around the precinct of Poseidon (*Odyssey* 6.262–267; see [[scheria]]), and sends him to wait in the grove of Athena “as far from the city as a man can make himself heard with a shout” (*Odyssey* 6.294; see [[the-grove-of-athena]]) until she has reached her father's house. There, at sunset, the book ends, with his prayer: “Grant that I come to the Phaeacians as one welcomed and pitied” (*Odyssey* 6.327; see [[welcomed-and-pitied]]). The prayer asks for what the title names. The entry into the city belongs to the next book: Odysseus rises to go to the city, Athena pours a mist around him, and he goes through the house and throws his arms around the knees of Arete (*Odyssey* 7.14–17, 7.139–142; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-7]]).

**Washerwomen.** The title names the man and the people, not the girl who receives him. Later writers took titles of their own from her part of the story. Eustathius records that Sophocles wrote a satyr play, the *Washerwomen* (Πλύντριαι), and himself played Nausicaa in it (as Merry reports him on *Odyssey* 6.115; see [[sophocles]]), and the Suda lists among the comedies of Philyllius one called *Washerwoman, or Nausicaa* (Πλύντρια ἢ Ναυσικάα, Suda φ 457). Both name the washing at the river (*Odyssey* 6.85–95; see [[washing-troughs]]).

**Odysseus' own summary.** In the next book, when Arete asks him who gave him the clothes he wears, Odysseus ends the account of his voyage with the events of this book. He slept all night long among the leaves, and into the dawn and to midday, until the sun was declining and sweet sleep let him go; he noticed her daughter's handmaids playing on the shore, and her among them, like the goddesses; he came to her as a suppliant, and she did not fall short of good sense, as you would not expect one younger to do, for the young are always thoughtless; she gave him bread in plenty and sparkling wine, bathed him in the river and gave him these clothes (*Odyssey* 7.287–297). He says nothing of the ball, the branch or his nakedness. When Alcinous blames his daughter for not bringing the stranger home with her handmaids, Odysseus answers that she told him to follow with them, but that he would not, out of fear and shame, lest the king's heart too should be indignant when he saw it (*Odyssey* 7.298–307). In this book she had him follow the wagon with the handmaids only as far as the fields reached, and then wait in the grove while she drove into the city, for fear of what the Phaeacians would say (*Odyssey* 6.255–299; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]]). In the summary he gives Penelope she has no place: he came to the Phaeacians, and they sent him home (*Odyssey* 23.338–341).
