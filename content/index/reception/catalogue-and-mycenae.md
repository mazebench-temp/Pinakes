The Catalogue describes a Greece that did not exist in Homer's day. Mycenae is the seat of the greatest king (2.569–580). There are no Dorians. The Peloponnese, Boeotia and Thessaly are divided among heroes and peoples whom later Greeks knew only from legend. Twenty-nine contingents bring 1,186 ships. Since the decipherment of the Linear B tablets as Greek in 1952, the question has been sharper: how much of this map goes back to the Mycenaean age, which ended around 1100 BCE, and how could it have reached a poem composed some four or five centuries later?

## The case for a Mycenaean core

T. W. Allen, *The Homeric Catalogue of Ships* (1921), defended the Catalogue as a genuine record of the heroic age. Denys Page, *History and the Homeric Iliad* (1959), argued that it was composed in the Mycenaean period and handed down in verse, pointing to places that had been deserted or forgotten by historical times. R. Hope Simpson and J. F. Lazenby, *The Catalogue of the Ships in Homer's Iliad* (1970), surveyed the sites. Of the places that can be located, most were inhabited in the late Mycenaean period, and several were abandoned soon afterward.

Some details fit that age well. Thebes, the seat of a major Mycenaean palace, appears only as Hypothebae, "Lower Thebes" (2.505). This is often explained by the sack of Thebes by the Epigoni, the sons of the Seven, in the generation before Troy; Sthenelus boasts of it at 4.406.

## The difficulties

Other features point the other way. The Catalogue does not agree with the rest of the Iliad. When the scepter is described, Agamemnon rules "many islands and all Argos" (2.108), but the Catalogue gives Argos and Tiryns to Diomedes and no islands to Agamemnon. Boeotia heads the list, with more leaders and towns than any other region, though the Boeotians do little in the poem. Tlepolemus' Rhodians were settled "in three divisions, by tribes" (2.668), which has been read as a reflection of the three Dorian cities of historical Rhodes. And the tablets, which name many places in the kingdoms of Pylos and Knossos, match the Catalogue only in part.

## Where the question stands

Many scholars now take a middle view: the Catalogue contains old material, including names that go back to the Bronze Age, but it was reshaped in the course of oral transmission and fitted into the Iliad's tenth year, perhaps from a list of the fleet at Aulis (see [[the-boeotia]]). G. S. Kirk, *The Iliad: A Commentary*, vol. 1 (1985), discusses the entries one by one. Edzard Visser, *Homers Katalog der Schiffe* (1997), studies the Catalogue as a product of the technique of oral verse-making. How old its core is, and how much was added later, remains disputed.
