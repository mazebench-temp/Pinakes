**Homer.** Achilles has flung Lycaon into the Scamander, and he turns on the Trojans: “Not even the river, fair-flowing and silver-eddying though it is, / will protect you, to whom you have long sacrificed many bulls, / and thrown single-hoofed horses alive into its eddies” (21.130–132; see [[scamander]] and [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). The verbs ἱερεύετε and καθίετε are presents, and with δηθά, “long”, Walter Leaf takes them for a custom still kept: “you have long been sacrificing bulls” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.131). He reports from Didymus that Aristophanes athetized the six lines 130–135, as added by readers who did not see why the river is angry, though the poem says why at 146, that δηθά was objected to as un-Homeric, and that Aristarchus perhaps agreed; Leaf finds the arguments weak (on 21.130).

Bulls are a river's victims elsewhere in Homer. In the war of Nestor's youth against the Epeians the Pylians, coming to the sacred stream of the Alpheus, offered fine victims to Zeus, a bull to the Alpheus and a bull to Poseidon (11.726–728; see [[alpheus]]). Leaf connects the bull with the common figure of a river in the form of a bull or a bull-headed man (on 21.131), and he thinks that the river's bellowing in this book, when the Scamander casts the dead out of his streams “bellowing like a bull”, probably explains that figure (21.237; Leaf on 21.237; see [[rivers]]). The live horses have no parallel in Homer (Leaf on 21.131).

**Barbarian sacrifice.** Leaf suggests that Achilles names the horses with contempt, as a barbarian custom, and refers to Herodotus (on 21.131). Herodotus says that the Scythians sacrifice other cattle too, and horses most of all (*Histories* 4.61.2). When Xerxes' army, marching west past the Paeonians who live north of Mount Pangaeum, reached the river Strymon, the Magi sought good omens from the river by slaughtering white horses (ἐς τὸν οἱ Μάγοι ἐκαλλιερέοντο σφάζοντες ἵππους λευκούς, 7.113.1–2). After working these charms on the river, and many others besides, they crossed by the bridges at the Nine Ways of the Edonians, and there buried alive as many boys and girls of the country (7.114.1; see [[herodotus]] and [[xerxes]]). Herodotus' horses are slaughtered, not thrown into the water alive, and they are offered at a crossing, by an army that has to cross.

Herodotus also tells of a river that took a white horse unbidden. As Cyrus was about to cross the Gyndes on his march against Babylon, one of his sacred white horses went into the river in high spirits to swim across, and the river swept it under and carried it away. Cyrus, angry with the river for its insolence, threatened to make it so weak that women could cross it without wetting their knees, and his army spent a whole summer dividing it into three hundred and sixty channels (*Histories* 1.189–190.1).

**A Greek instance.** Leaf points also to Pausanias for Greek instances (on 21.131). Crossing into the territory of Mantinea, Pausanias comes to the plain called Argon, “Idle”, whose water would make it a lake if it did not disappear into a chasm in the earth. It rises again at Dine, by the place called Genethlium in the Argolid, as sweet water coming up out of the sea, and in the old days the Argives used to let down into Dine horses for Poseidon, adorned with bridles (καθίεσαν ἐς τὴν Δίνην τῷ Ποσειδῶνι ἵππους, *Description of Greece* 8.7.1–2; see [[pausanias]] and [[poseidon]]). The verb is Achilles' καθίετε, and the spring is named Δίνη, “Eddy”, the word of 21.132 (ἐν δίνῃσι); but the god is Poseidon, and the water is a spring in the sea, not a river. Leaf adds that the sacrifice is always made to the gods of the waters (on 21.131).
