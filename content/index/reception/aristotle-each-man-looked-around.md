When Patroclus and the Myrmidons fall on the Trojans at the ships, the Trojans see “the valiant son of Menoetius, / himself and his attendant, gleaming in their armor” (16.278–279), and think that the swift-footed son of Peleus has thrown away his wrath and chosen friendship (16.281–282). The ruse works as Nestor devised it and Patroclus put it to Achilles, “in the hope that, taking me for you, they may hold off from the war” (16.41; 11.796–801; see [[patroclus]] and [[nestor]]). The battalions waver, and “each man looked around for a way to escape sheer destruction” (16.283). Only then does Patroclus throw his first spear (16.284).

**Aristotle.** Aristotle's lost *Homeric Problems* collected difficulties in the poems with their solutions (see [[problems-and-solutions]]). A note in the scholia on this line, which Gigon prints among its fragments, reports a judgment of his on the verse: it is one of Homer's most terrible expressions, for in it everyone is trying to flee; and it is fitting for barbarians (fr. 386 Gigon; compare [[aristotle-gorgon]]). The verb παπταίνω is to look about, as a man does who is searching for someone or on his guard (4.200, 13.649); here each man looks only for a way out.

**The same verse.** The verse is said three times in Homer. At the end of Book 14 Peneleos holds up the head of Ilioneus on his spear and boasts over it, “and trembling seized the limbs of them all, / and each man looked around for a way to escape sheer destruction” (14.506–507; see [[peneleos]] and [[ilioneus]]). In the Odyssey it follows Odysseus' revelation of himself to the suitors: pale fear seizes them all, and each man looks around for a way to escape (*Odyssey* 22.42–43; see [[odysseus]]). In the Iliad it is said only of Trojans.

**Barbarians.** Homer does not call the Trojans barbarians. The nearest word in the Iliad is the epithet of the Carians, “of barbarous speech” (2.867; see [[barbarophonos]]), and Thucydides observed that Homer never speaks of barbarians at all, because, as he thought, the Hellenes had not yet been set apart under one name as their opposite (Thucydides 1.3.3; see [[thucydides-hellenes]]). The word is Aristotle's, not Homer's; and in the Odyssey the same verse is said of Greeks, in Odysseus' own house.
