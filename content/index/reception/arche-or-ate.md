Menelaus' ἕνεκ᾽ ἀρχῆς means "for the sake of the beginning": Alexander started it. Twice later in the poem the same half-line ends with a different word. Helen tells Hector that the toil of war weighs on him most of all "for the sake of me, a dog, and of Alexander's ἄτη" (εἵνεκ᾽ ἐμεῖο κυνὸς καὶ Ἀλεξάνδρου ἕνεκ᾽ ἄτης, 6.355–356), and the narrator says that Troy became hateful to Hera, Poseidon and Athena "because of Alexander's ἄτη", when he insulted the goddesses who came to him (24.25–30, lines that Aristarchus rejected, holding that Homer did not know the judgment of Paris). The textual tradition also has ἄτης at 3.100. Monro and Allen print ἀρχῆς, and the Pinakes translation follows them: "because Alexander began it".

The choice matters for Book 3's question of blame (see [[who-is-to-blame]]). Ἄτη is a blindness of mind, a delusion, often sent by a god. A man can plead it: Agamemnon will say of his quarrel with Achilles that he was not to blame (αἴτιος), but Zeus and Fate and the Fury, who put savage ἄτη in his mind (19.86–88). Ἀρχή makes Alexander the one who started the wrong, with no god to share it. It is the word the injured husband would use. Helen and the narrator, speaking of Paris' folly, use the other. And in the same book Priam lays the blame on the gods (3.164).

Menelaus pairs the word with "my quarrel" (ἐμῆς ἔριδος). The strife is his as much as Paris', and he asks that it end with the death of one of them, whichever it is fated to be (3.101–102). See [[ate]], [[eris]] and [[menelaus]].
