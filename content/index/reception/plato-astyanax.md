The *Cratylus* asks whether names are correct by nature or by convention. Socrates proposes to learn from Homer, who sometimes gives both the name that the gods use and the name that men use: the river of Troy that the gods call Xanthus and men Scamander (20.74), the bird that the gods call χαλκίς and men κύμινδις (14.291), the mound that men call Batieia and the gods the tomb of Myrina (2.813–814). The gods, surely, call things by their right names (391d–392b; see [[scamander]]).

Then he turns to a question “more human, and easier”: the two names of Hector's son, Scamandrius and Astyanax. Which did Homer think the more correct? Hermogenes cannot say, and Socrates reasons it out. The wiser give names more correctly than the less wise, and in cities, taken as a whole, men are wiser than women. Homer says that the Trojans called the child Astyanax; so the women, plainly, called him Scamandrius, and Homer thought Astyanax the more correct name. Homer even gives the reason: “for he alone guarded their city and long walls”. It is right to call the son of the savior the lord of what his father saved (392b–e).

Socrates goes one step further. Did Homer name Hector too? ἄναξ, “lord”, and Ἕκτωρ mean nearly the same, and both are royal names: “of whatever a man is lord, he is surely its holder too; for plainly he rules it and possesses it and holds (ἔχει) it” (393a–b). He takes Ἕκτωρ as “the holder”, from ἔχω, “hold”, and modern etymologists mostly agree (see [[hector]]).

Socrates' Homer is not exactly ours. His quotation is in the third person, like the line of Book 6, “for Hector alone was guarding Ilios” (6.403). But its “long walls” come from Book 22, where Andromache, mourning, speaks to Hector himself: the Trojans call the boy Astyanax, “for you alone guarded their gates and their long walls” (22.506–507). And his proof overlooks Book 6, where the name Scamandrius is Hector's own: “whom Hector called Scamandrius, but the others Astyanax” (6.402–403). The father named the boy for the river, and the city named him for what his father did. Much of the etymologizing in the *Cratylus* is playful, and Socrates may not mean the proof to be taken seriously.
