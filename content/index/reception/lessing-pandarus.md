Lessing's *Laocoön* argues that painting and poetry work with different means. Painting sets figures and colors side by side in space, and so is suited to bodies; poetry sets words one after another in time, and so is suited to actions (see [[lessing-helen]]). Homer, Lessing says, paints almost nothing but actions in progress.

His example is the shot of Pandarus. Every moment is given, one after another. Pandarus takes the lid from his quiver and chooses a new, feathered arrow; he fits it to the string; he draws the notch and the string together, the string to his breast and the iron to the bow; the great bow twangs, the string sings, and the arrow leaps away, eager to fly into the throng (4.116–126). The moments follow so closely, Lessing observes, and yet are so distinct, that someone who did not know how to use a bow could learn it from this one passage. A painter could show only one of them.

The description of the bow itself, a few lines earlier, works in the same way. Homer does not say what the bow looks like. He tells how it was made: Pandarus shot the wild goat from ambush, its horns grew sixteen palms long, and a horn-worker fitted them together, smoothed them and set a golden tip on the end (4.105–111).
