The traditional title of Book 16 is Πατρόκλεια, “the Patrocleia”, the lay of Patroclus. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter, this one is Π, pi (see [[title-of-book-5]]). The book's story is told under [[the-death-of-patroclus]], and its fighting belongs to [[the-battle-at-the-ships]] and [[the-third-day-of-battle]].

**The name.** The title is made from the hero's name, as Δολώνεια, the title of Book 10, is made from Dolon's, and Ὀδύσσεια from Odysseus' (see [[title-of-book-10]]). The word itself is not in the poem, which declines the hero's name in two ways, Πάτροκλος and Πατροκλέης (vocative Πατρόκλεες; see [[patroclus]]).

**Aelian.** The name is old. Aelian, writing that the ancients once sang the poems of Homer in separate parts, gives the names they used: “the Battle at the Ships, and a Doloneia, and the Aristeia of Agamemnon, and the Catalogue of Ships, and the Patrocleia, and the Ransom, and the Games for Patroclus, and the Obliteration of the Oaths” (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[aelian]] and [[title-of-book-13]]). Patroclus is the only hero of the Iliad who gives his name to two of them; the Games for Patroclus are the funeral games of Book 23. Where the Patrocleia began and ended in performance is not known. Patroclus' part begins in Book 11, when Achilles calls him out of the hut, “and this was the beginning of evil for him” (11.604; see [[title-of-book-11]]), and the fight over his body fills Book 17. The book that bears the name ends with his death and with Hector turning from the body to chase Automedon (16.855–867).

**The narrator and his hero.** The book is Patroclus' from beginning to end. It opens with him standing beside Achilles, shedding tears like a spring of dark water (16.2–4), and ends with his death and Hector's answer to the dead man (16.855–861). Between them he wins the armor (16.21–100), arms (16.130–144), drives the Trojans from the ships and puts out the fire (16.284–305), kills Sarpedon (16.462–505), three times sets foot on the angle of the wall of Troy (16.698–711) and is struck down by Apollo, Euphorbus and Hector (16.786–857). No other book of the Iliad has the narrator speak so often to a character. Eight times he turns to Patroclus as “you”, from “Groaning heavily, you said to him, horseman Patroclus” (16.20) to “Then you spoke to him feebly, horseman Patroclus” (16.843), and once his address is a question, the question that introduces a list of the slain (16.692–697; see [[apostrophe]] and [[whom-first-whom-last]]). In no other book does he speak so to Patroclus.

**The counterattack.** The title of Book 15 takes its words from Zeus' forecast of a counterattack from the ships, which comes only in this book (see [[title-of-book-15]]). In the same speech Zeus foretold this book: Achilles would rouse up his companion Patroclus, and Hector would kill him before Ilios after he had destroyed many young men, among them Sarpedon, Zeus' own son (15.64–67; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). The title names the man who carries out the counterattack.

**What the title leaves out.** It passes over the book's other great death. Sarpedon, the king of the Lycians and the son of Zeus, holds its middle: Zeus' grief, Hera's warning and the bloody raindrops (16.431–461), his death and his last charge to Glaucus (16.462–505), the fight over his body (16.506–665), and Sleep and Death carrying him home to Lycia (16.666–683; see [[sarpedon]] and [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]). It passes over Achilles' prayer to the Zeus of Dodona, granted in part and refused in part (16.220–252; see [[dodona]]), the catalogue of the Myrmidons (16.168–197; see [[myrmidons]]), and Hector, who flees before Patroclus twice and kills him in the end (16.367–383, 16.656–658, 16.818–861; see [[hector]]).

**The older readers.** Apart from Aelian's list, the ancient readers who use the book do not name it, and they remember Sarpedon at least as well as Patroclus. Plato quotes Zeus' lament for Sarpedon among the verses that show the greatest of the gods grieving unworthily, and the departure of Patroclus' soul without naming him (*Republic* 3.386d, 3.388c–d; see [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]] and [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]). Cicero recalls Jupiter complaining that he could not save Sarpedon from death against fate, and Virgil's Jupiter consoles Hercules with the death of his own son Sarpedon (*On Divination* 2.25; *Aeneid* 10.464–473; see [[jupiter-and-sarpedon]]). Proclus names the soul of Patroclus, “leaving manhood and youth”, as the soul of a man of action (*On the Republic* I 119 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). Strabo takes from the book the Pelasgian Zeus of Dodona and the Selli, Menesthius son of the Spercheius, the Pelian ash, Asius son of Dymas and Cebriones (see [[strabo]]), and a note collected among the fragments of Aristotle's *Homeric Problems* singles out the verse on the Trojans' panic at the sight of Patroclus (see [[aristotle-each-man-looked-around]]).
