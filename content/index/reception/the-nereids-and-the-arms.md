summary: In the Iliad Thetis brings the arms of Hephaestus to Achilles alone, at dawn, having sent her sister Nereids home the day before. Later art gave her their company. Pausanias reports that a scene on the chest of Cypselus at Olympia was taken for the Nereids in winged chariots and Thetis receiving the arms from Hephaestus, and Walter Leaf calls the bringing of the arms, with the Nereids, a favorite subject of Greek art. Aeschylus named a tragedy the Nereids; its few fragments speak of women who have crossed the sea, of linen for a body, and of a spear.

**Homer.** In Book 18 the Nereids rise from the sea with Thetis, come ashore at Troy and stand around Achilles as she laments; then she sends them home to the Old Man of the Sea, their father, and goes up alone to Hephaestus (18.65–69, 18.138–145; see [[nereids]]). When the armor is made, Hephaestus “lifted it and set it before the mother of Achilles. / And she like a hawk leapt down from snowy Olympus, / carrying the gleaming armor from Hephaestus” (18.615–617). At dawn she comes to the ships, “bringing the gifts from the god” (19.3), sets the armor down before Achilles, and all its intricate designs ring; the Myrmidons cannot bear to look at it, and Achilles delights in it (19.12–19; see [[thetis]] and [[the-making-of-the-arms]]).

**On the chest of Cypselus.** Pausanias describes at Olympia a chest of cedar, with figures of ivory, of gold and of the cedar itself, in which the mother of Cypselus, later tyrant of Corinth, hid him as a baby from the Bacchiads; his descendants dedicated it in thanks for his escape (*Description of Greece* 5.17.5). Most of its figures have inscriptions in old letters, but the topmost of its five bands has none, and its scenes have to be guessed (5.17.6, 5.19.7). Among them are pairs of horses with golden wings, women standing in the chariots, and a man giving armor to one of the women. “These, they infer, have to do with the death of Patroclus: the women in the chariots are Nereids, and Thetis is receiving the arms from Hephaestus”; and indeed the man who gives the arms is not strong on his feet, and a servant follows him holding a pair of tongs (5.19.8). The Centaur nearby is said to be Chiron, already taken from among men to live with the gods, come to give Achilles some relief from his grief (5.19.9; see [[chiron]], [[hephaestus]] and [[pausanias]]). The scene, if it is rightly read, shows the moment at the end of Book 18 when Hephaestus sets the armor before Thetis, and gives her the sisters whom Homer had sent home. Hephaestus is “lame in both legs” in Homer too (18.614; see [[lame-in-both-legs]]).

**Walter Leaf.** Leaf notes that the bringing of the arms to Achilles was a favorite subject of Greek art from the chest of Cypselus onward, especially in its later stages; the Nereids are always with it, and it became a favorite excuse for showing a number of female figures in graceful movement (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.1).

**Aeschylus' Nereids.** Aeschylus named a tragedy after the daughters of Nereus. It is lost, and Nauck's collection of his fragments has five pieces of it, quoted by scholiasts, lexicographers and grammarians (frr. 150–154 Nauck): a phrase in the feminine plural, “having crossed the dolphin-bearing plain of the sea” (fr. 150); a corrupt couplet that speaks of a slayer, of wrath on high and of the immortals (fr. 151); “the double tongue of the spear-shaft” (fr. 152); “Let fine linen be wrapped around the body” (fr. 153); and the single word ἀθήρ, “spear-point” (fr. 154). The fragments name no one, and what the play showed they do not say. Women who have crossed the sea, linen for a body and a spear would suit the Nereids' journeys of Books 18 and 19, the body of Patroclus, which Achilles' companions covered “with a soft linen cloth / from head to foot” (18.352–353), and the arms of Achilles; that is as far as they go (see [[aeschylus]] and [[aeschylus-myrmidons]]).
