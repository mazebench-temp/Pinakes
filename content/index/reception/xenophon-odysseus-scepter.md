In the *Memorabilia* Xenophon defends the memory of Socrates against the charges that brought him to trial and death in 399 BCE. The accuser, he reports, claimed that Socrates picked out the worst passages of the famous poets and used them to teach his companions to be wrongdoers and tyrants. From Homer, the accuser said, Socrates often quoted these lines, in which Odysseus restrains "a king or a man of note" with gentle words, but strikes "a man of the people" with the scepter and tells him to sit still and listen to his betters. He explained them, so the charge ran, as meaning that the poet approved of beating common men and the poor (1.2.58).

Xenophon answers that Socrates meant nothing of the kind; he would then have thought that he himself deserved a beating. His point was rather that people who are useful neither in word nor in deed, and cannot help the army, the city or the people when help is needed, should be restrained by every means, above all if they are insolent as well, and even if they are very rich (1.2.59).

Both readings start from a real feature of the scene. Odysseus sorts the fleeing army by rank. To the kings he gives reasons and warnings (2.190–197), to the commoners orders and blows (2.199–206). Thersites, the one commoner who speaks, is then beaten into silence. In democratic Athens a fondness for these lines could be presented as contempt for the people, and Xenophon turns it into contempt for the useless.
