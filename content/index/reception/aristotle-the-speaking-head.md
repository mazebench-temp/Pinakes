Dolon has told his captors all they asked and more, and begs to be taken to the ships or left bound. Diomedes refuses, and as Dolon is about to take him by the chin, the suppliant's gesture, the sword cuts through his neck: “and while he was still speaking his head was mingled with the dust” (10.457; see [[dolon]] and [[supplication]]). The verse occurs once more in Homer, in the Odyssey's version of the same scene. The seer Leodes clasps Odysseus' knees and pleads for his life; Odysseus, looking at him darkly, refuses, strikes him in the middle of the neck with a sword, and the same verse follows (*Odyssey* 22.310–329). In both places φθεγγομένου goes with τοῦ γε, “he”: the man was still uttering sound when his head fell, whether words or a cry. Walter Leaf preferred the cry, since Leodes has finished his plea before he is struck (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 10.457).

**Aristotle.** In the *Parts of Animals*, discussing the diaphragm, Aristotle reports that men wounded in battle near the diaphragm are said to laugh, from the heat of the wound. That, he says, is heard from more trustworthy witnesses than the story that a man's head speaks after it has been cut off. “For some say so, citing Homer too, as if that was why he composed ‘and the head, speaking, was mingled with the dust’, and not ‘while he was speaking’”: they read φθεγγομένη, agreeing with κάρη, “head”, for φθεγγομένου (*Parts of Animals* 3.10, 673a10–16). In Caria, he goes on, people believed the thing so firmly that they held a trial. The priest of Zeus Hoplosmios had been killed, no one knew by whom, and some said they had heard the severed head say several times, “Cercidas killed a man upon a man”; so they looked for someone of that name in the district, and tried him. Aristotle answers that speech is impossible once the windpipe is cut and the movement from the lung is gone, that nothing of the kind has ever happened among the barbarians who cut off heads quickly, and he asks why, if it were so, it never happens with other animals (673a16–27).

The reading he reports needs a feminine κάρη, but Homer's word is neuter. Leaf notes that later poets sometimes made it feminine, as Quintus of Smyrna does where a severed head rolls away while its owner still strains to speak (*Posthomerica* 11.58; Leaf on 10.457). Our manuscripts have φθεγγομένου in both poems.
