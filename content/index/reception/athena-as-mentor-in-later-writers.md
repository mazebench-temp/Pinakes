**Homer.** Mentor is the companion of Odysseus to whom he entrusted his house when he sailed for Troy (*Odyssey* 2.225–227; see [[mentor]]). Athena first takes his likeness on the shore of Ithaca, when Telemachus prays to the god who came to him the day before (*Odyssey* 2.262–268). She goes through the city in the likeness of Telemachus himself to gather a crew, and then, as Mentor again, calls him to the ship and sails with him to Pylos (*Odyssey* 2.383, 2.399–401, 2.416–418). There she leaves in the likeness of a bird, and old Nestor knows her for the daughter of Zeus (*Odyssey* 3.371–379). She takes Mentor's form again in the hall on the day of the slaughter, and at the end, in the poem's last verse, as she makes the peace (*Odyssey* 22.205–206, 24.502–503, 24.548; see [[athena]]).

**Plato and Proclus.** In the *Republic* Socrates lays down that a god, being in every way the best, could change only for the worse, and so “remains always simply in his own shape” (*Republic* 2.381c). Let no poet tell us, then, that “gods, in the likeness of strangers from abroad, taking on all sorts of forms, go up and down the cities” (381d, quoting *Odyssey* 17.485–486), nor lie about Proteus and Thetis, nor let mothers frighten their children with stories of gods going about at night in the likeness of strangers (381d–e; see [[plato]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). Proclus, defending Homer, finds three ways in which the myths give changes to gods who do not change (*On the Republic* I 112–114 Kroll). In the first, the change is in those who receive the god, not in the god; in the second, a god of many powers shows a different form to each who looks at him, as Proteus does; in the third, the same god proceeds down through the orders below it to the last of them, and the myths say that it changes into the form of the order it has reached, “for they say that Athena is likened to Mentor, Hermes to the gull and Apollo to the hawk” (καὶ τὴν Ἀθηνᾶν τῷ Μέντορι καὶ τὸν Ἑρμῆν τῷ λάρῳ τῷ ὄρνιθι καὶ τὸν Ἀπόλλω τῷ ἱέρακί φασιν ἐξομοιοῦσθαι, I 113; *Odyssey* 5.51, *Iliad* 15.237). And so, he concludes, the poets keep a divine epiphany without form or shape, as when Athena appears to Achilles alone and nothing is said of how she looked (*Iliad* 1.197–198); an angelic one they clothe in a general form, as when Poseidon and Athena come to Achilles “in form like men” (*Iliad* 21.285); and only the visitations of daemons do they let change into particular persons, or into the shapes of animals (I 114; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). Athena in the likeness of one man, Mentor, belongs to the third.

**Plato's Laws.** Plato quotes the goddess's own words in Mentor's form. Telemachus has asked her how he is to approach Nestor, and she answers: “Telemachus, some things you will think of yourself in your own mind, and others a god will suggest; for I do not think that you were born and raised against the will of the gods” (a plain rendering of *Odyssey* 3.26–28). The Athenian of the *Laws*, having laid down the types of song and dance for his citizens, says that the poet spoke well, and that the young must likewise think that what has been said is enough, and that the god and the daemon will suggest the rest (*Laws* 7.803e–804a).

**Cicero.** When Caesar was expected at Formiae, Cicero wrote to Atticus: “Here I could wish I had that Minerva of Homer's, disguised as Mentor, to whom I might say, ‘Mentor, how shall I go, how shall I …’” (*hic ego vellem habere Homeri illam Minervam simulatam Mentori cui dicerem, Μέντορ, πῶς τʼ ἄρʼ ἴω, πῶς τʼ ἂρ*, *Letters to Atticus* 9.8.2; see [[cicero]]). The Greek is the beginning of Telemachus' question to the goddess at Pylos, “Mentor, how shall I go? How shall I greet him?” (*Odyssey* 3.22), the question of a young man who is not yet practiced in close-knit words. “I have never thought of anything harder,” Cicero goes on, “but I am thinking of it all the same, and I shall not be, as far as one can in misfortune, unprepared.”

**Fénelon.** François de Salignac de La Mothe-Fénelon wrote *Les Aventures de Télémaque* for his pupil, the Duke of Burgundy, whose preceptor he was; the book was printed without his consent from a copy that a servant made in secret and sold to a bookseller (the notice of the Hachette edition of 1893 gives the year as 1700). It continues the journey of Telemachus beyond Homer. “Telemachus, led by Minerva in the form of Mentor, lands after a shipwreck on the island of Calypso” (*Télémaque*, summary of Book 1); Calypso sees two men from afar, “of whom one seemed old; the other, though young, resembled Ulysses”, and “Minerva, who was accompanying Telemachus in the form of Mentor, did not wish to be known by Calypso” (*Télémaque* 1). Mentor guides him through his adventures and teaches him the principles of wise government. In the last book, “the goddess herself, hidden in the form of Mentor, takes back her divine form and makes herself known”: Telemachus sees his friend's face take on a new form, “by these marks Telemachus knows Minerva”, and she says, “Son of Ulysses, listen to me for the last time”, gives him her last instructions, and rises into the air, wrapped in a cloud of gold and azure; Telemachus sails for Ithaca and finds his father with the faithful Eumaeus (*Télémaque* 18, summary and text). Among the judgments printed in that edition, Désiré Nisard's says that as Idomeneus is modeled on Louis XIV and Telemachus on the Duke of Burgundy, so “Mentor is none other than Fénelon himself” (*Histoire de la littérature française*, vol. 3). In Homer the goddess leaves Mentor's form at Pylos, and father and son meet in Eumaeus' hut (*Odyssey* 16; see [[telemachus]] and [[eumaeus]]).
