**Homer.** Of all the souls that come to Odysseus' pit, the soul of Ajax, son of Telamon, alone stands apart, still angry over the victory in the contest for the arms of Achilles (*Odyssey* 11.541–551; see [[ajax]] and [[the-judgment-of-arms]]). Odysseus speaks to it “with soothing words” (*Odyssey* 11.552; see [[soothing-words]]): he wishes he had never won such a prize, calls the arms ruinous, tells Ajax that the Achaeans grieve for him as for Achilles, and lays the blame on Zeus, who hated the army of the Danaans; “But come here, my lord, so that you may hear my word / and speech; and tame your might and your proud spirit” (*Odyssey* 11.548–562). Ajax answers nothing and goes off into Erebus among the other souls. Odysseus says that even so, angry as he was, Ajax would have spoken to him, or he to Ajax, had he not wished to see the others (*Odyssey* 11.565–567), verses that W. W. Merry suspected (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.565; see the Odyssey translation's note on 11.565–567).

**Virgil.** After the Sibyl has brought Aeneas across the Styx, they come to the Fields of Mourning, *Lugentes campi*, where those whom hard love has consumed with its cruel wasting are hidden by secret paths in a myrtle wood, “and their cares do not leave them even in death”; there Aeneas sees Phaedra and Procris and sad Eriphyle, showing the wounds her son dealt her, and Evadne and Pasiphae, Laodamia, and Caeneus, once a youth and now a woman (*Aeneid* 6.440–449, plain renderings throughout). Among them Phoenician Dido, her wound still fresh, is wandering in the great wood. Aeneas knows her dimly through the shadows, as one sees, or thinks one has seen, the new moon rising through the clouds, and he weeps and speaks to her with sweet love: so the news was true that she was dead and had sought her end with the sword; was he, alas, the cause of her death? He swears by the stars and the gods above, and by whatever faith there is beneath the earth, that he left her shore unwillingly, driven by the commands of the gods, which now drive him through the shades; he could not believe that his going would bring her such grief. *siste gradum teque aspectu ne subtrahe nostro. / quem fugis? extremum fato quod te adloquor hoc est*, “Stay your step, and do not withdraw from my sight. Whom do you flee? This is the last time fate allows me to speak to you” (*Aeneid* 6.450–466). So Aeneas tries with his words to soothe her burning spirit and her fierce looks, and his tears flow (*Aeneid* 6.467–468). Then:

*illa solo fixos oculos aversa tenebat / nec magis incepto vultum sermone movetur / quam si dura silex aut stet Marpesia cautes. / tandem corripuit sese atque inimica refugit / in nemus umbriferum, coniunx ubi pristinus illi / respondet curis aequatque Sychaeus amorem*, “She, turned away, kept her eyes fixed on the ground, and her face was no more moved by the speech he had begun than if she stood there hard flint or Marpesian rock. At last she tore herself away and fled, his enemy, into the shadowy grove, where her first husband, Sychaeus, answers her cares and returns her love” (*Aeneid* 6.469–474). Aeneas, struck by her unjust fate, follows her far with his tears and pities her as she goes (*Aeneid* 6.475–476; see [[aeneas]] and [[virgil]]).

**The two meetings.** The two scenes go step by step together. In each the hero speaks first, and gently: Odysseus with soothing words, Aeneas trying to soothe her spirit, *lenibat dictis animum*. Each was the cause of the grief, by a victory or by a departure, and says so with regret: “Would that I had never been the victor for such a prize!” (*Odyssey* 11.548), *funeris heu tibi causa fui?*, “was I, alas, the cause of your death?” (*Aeneid* 6.458). Each puts the blame on the gods: “no one else / is to blame, but Zeus” (*Odyssey* 11.558–559), *sed me iussa deum … imperiis egere suis*, “but the commands of the gods drove me with their authority” (*Aeneid* 6.461–463). Each asks the shade to stay and listen: “But come here, my lord, so that you may hear my word” (*Odyssey* 11.561), *siste gradum* (*Aeneid* 6.465). And each shade answers nothing and turns away into the dark, Ajax into Erebus, Dido into the shadowy grove. The heroines among whom Dido walks are Homer's too: Phaedra, Procris and Eriphyle are three of the women whom Persephone sent to Odysseus' pit (*Odyssey* 11.321, 11.326; see [[eriphyle]] and [[ariadne]]).

**Other readers.** Plato's myth of Er keeps the anger of Ajax's soul past death: remembering the judgment of the arms, it will not be born a man again, and chooses the life of a lion; in the *Apology* Socrates hopes to meet Ajax among those who died by an unjust judgment (*Republic* 10.620b; *Apology* 41b; see [[plato-the-choice-of-lives]]).
