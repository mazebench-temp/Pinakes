At Memphis, Herodotus says, the precinct of King Proteus held a temple of "the foreign Aphrodite", whom he took to be Helen (2.112). The priests told him how she came there. Alexander, sailing home from Sparta with Helen, was driven by winds to the Canopic mouth of the Nile. His servants took refuge in a temple of Heracles and denounced him, and the warden of the mouth reported to Proteus. Proteus would not kill a stranger, but he charged Alexander with wronging his host (see [[xenia]]), kept Helen and the stolen treasure for her husband, and sent him away (2.113–115).

Herodotus thought Homer knew this story but set it aside as less suited to epic. As proof he quotes the lines on the robes made by the Sidonian women whom Paris brought from Sidon, "on that voyage on which he brought back Helen" (Iliad 6.289–292): Homer knew that Paris wandered on his way home (2.116). He concludes that the *Cypria*, in which Paris sailed from Sparta to Troy in three days, was not Homer's (2.117). The priests went on: the Greeks sent envoys to Troy to demand Helen, with Menelaus among them; the Trojans swore that she was in Egypt; the Greeks took the city, did not find her, and believed. Menelaus went to Egypt and got her back (2.118–119).

Then Herodotus gives his own reasons (2.120). If Helen had been in Troy, she would have been given back, with or without Alexander's consent. Priam was not so mad as to risk his own life, his children and his city so that his son could live with her, and in the war not a battle passed without two or three or more of Priam's sons being killed. Nor was Alexander the heir. Hector was older and the better man, and would not have let his brother's wrong ruin them all. The Trojans could not give back a woman they did not have, and the gods arranged their utter destruction to show that great wrongs bring great punishments.

Book 3 shows Troy in the position Herodotus found incredible. The old men on the wall admire Helen and still want her sent home in the ships (3.159–160). Hector wishes his brother had never been born (3.40). After the duel no Trojan will hide Paris, "for he was hateful to them all, like black doom" (3.454), and Agamemnon demands Helen and the treasure (3.456–460). The Iliad does not give her back. In the next book the truce is broken, and in Book 7 Paris refuses outright to give her up (7.357–364).

Euripides' *Helen* (412 BCE) combines the Egyptian story with Stesichorus' phantom. In the prologue Helen explains that Hera, angry at losing the judgment of beauty, gave Paris a living image of her fashioned from the sky, while Hermes carried the real Helen to the house of Proteus. Menelaus, shipwrecked in Egypt on his way home with the phantom, finds his true wife, faithful to him, and they escape together. Euripides had already used the phantom at the end of his *Electra*, where the Dioscuri announce that Helen never went to Troy and that Zeus sent an image of her there "so that there might be strife and slaughter of mortals" (1280–1283).
