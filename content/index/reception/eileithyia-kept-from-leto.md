summary: In the Homeric Hymn to Apollo Hera, jealous of the strong and blameless son that Leto is about to bear, keeps Eileithyia on Olympus unaware of the birth, and Leto labors on Delos for nine days and nights until the goddesses send Iris to fetch her. In Agamemnon's story Hera holds back the Eileithyiai from Alcmene. The Hymn shares the Iliad's words for the goddess who brings hard birth and for the child's coming out into the light, and feeds the newborn god the nectar and lovely ambrosia that Athena drips into Achilles.

**The Iliad.** In Agamemnon's story Hera, having made Zeus swear that the child of his blood born that day would rule all who dwell around him, hastens one birth and delays another: she brings the wife of Sthenelus to an early birth in Argos, “and she stopped Alcmene's childbirth, and held back the Eileithyiai” (19.119; see [[the-birth-of-heracles]] and [[eileithyia]]). Zeus had boasted that “Eileithyia, who brings hard birth, will bring into the light” a man of his blood (19.103–104; see [[who-brings-hard-birth]]). The Iliad's birth goddesses are plural here and in a simile of Book 11, where the Eileithyiai are “the daughters of Hera” (11.270–271), and single at the birth of Eudorus (16.187–188; see [[the-birth-of-eudorus]]).

**The Hymn.** The *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* tells how Apollo was born on Delos. When the island has agreed to receive him, Leto swears the great oath of the gods, by Earth, broad Heaven and the water of Styx, that his altar and precinct will stand there forever (*Hymn to Apollo* 79–88). Then for nine days and nine nights she is pierced by hopeless birth pangs. All the greatest goddesses are with her, Dione and Rhea, Themis and Amphitrite, but not white-armed Hera, who sits in the halls of Zeus; and Eileithyia alone has not heard, for she sits on the peak of Olympus under golden clouds, by the contrivance of white-armed Hera, who keeps her back out of jealousy, because Leto of the lovely hair is about to bear a blameless and mighty son (91–101). The goddesses send Iris, promising Eileithyia a great necklace strung with golden threads, nine cubits long, and tell her to call Eileithyia apart from Hera, so that Hera may not turn her back with words (102–106). As soon as Eileithyia sets foot on Delos the birth takes hold of Leto. She throws her arms around the palm tree and presses her knees into the soft meadow, the earth beneath her smiles, and the child leaps out into the light, ἐκ δʼ ἔθορε πρὸ φόωσδε, and all the goddesses cry aloud (115–119). His mother does not nurse him; Themis gives him nectar and lovely ambrosia with her immortal hands, and once he has tasted the food of the gods the golden bands cannot hold him (123–129; see [[leto]], [[apollo]] and [[iris]]).

**Shared words.** Walter Leaf compares the Hymn for Hera's interference with the Eileithyiai (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.119). The two poems share more than the plot. μογοστόκος Εἰλείθυια, “Eileithyia, who brings hard birth”, is the Iliad's formula (19.103; 16.187; plural 11.270) and the Hymn's (97, 115). The child comes out “into the light”, πρὸ φόωσδε in the Hymn and πρὸ φόως δέ in the Iliad (19.118; 16.188), where Aristarchus took πρό to mean “before his time” and Leaf rejects it (on 19.118; see the translation's note on 19.115–123). The newborn god is fed νέκταρ τε καὶ ἀμβροσίην ἐρατεινήν, “nectar and lovely ambrosia” (124), the words of Zeus' command to Athena and of the drops she puts into the breast of the fasting Achilles (19.347, 19.353; see [[nectar]] and [[fasting]]). And Leto's oath begins with the words of Hera's oath to Zeus in Book 15, “Let Earth now be witness to this, and the wide Heaven above, / and the down-flowing water of Styx” (15.36–37; *Hymn to Apollo* 84–85; see [[oath]]).

**Hera's two births.** In both poems Hera works through the goddess of childbirth against a rival's son. In the Hymn she delays one birth; in the Iliad she delays one and hastens another (19.117–119). The Hymn gives her motive, jealousy (ζηλοσύνη, 100); Agamemnon gives none, and his story turns on Zeus' oath rather than on Hera's feelings (see [[hera]] and [[aristotle-the-oath-of-zeus]]). Later writers told how Alcmene's delayed labor was at last set free (see [[the-birth-of-heracles]]).
