In the third book of the *Republic* Socrates asks what poetry the guardians of his city may hear. They will need self-control (σωφροσύνη), which for most people means obeying their rulers and ruling their own appetites for drink, sex and food. Homer supplies good examples: Diomedes telling his companion to sit in silence and obey (4.412), and the lines that go with it, "the Achaeans went breathing fury, in silence, fearing their commanders" (389e).

The quotation joins two passages. The first half is 3.8, where the Achaeans advance against the clamoring Trojans; the second is 4.431, where they march into battle "in silence, fearing their commanders", while the Trojans bleat like ewes. Plato may have quoted from memory, or used a text that ran so. Either way the two passages make the same contrast. In Book 3 the Trojans come on "with clamor and with cries, like birds", like cranes flying to war with the Pygmies (3.2–7), and the Achaeans come on without a word, "eager in their hearts to stand by one another" (3.9).

Socrates turns at once to what the young should not hear: Achilles' insults to Agamemnon (see [[plato-achilles-insults]]).
