When Diomedes' spear pierces Aphrodite's hand, the poet stops to say what flows from it. It is ἰχώρ, “ichor, such as flows in the blessed gods; for they do not eat bread, they do not drink sparkling wine, and so they are bloodless, and are called immortals” (5.340–342). The body marks the line between gods and men: what the gods eat and drink, and what they bleed (see [[ichor]]).

Alexander used the line against himself. Plutarch, describing how he bore his reputed descent from Zeus, says that with the barbarians he behaved like a man fully convinced of his divine birth, but with the Greeks he made himself a god only sparingly and with restraint. Later, when he was hit by an arrow and in great pain, he said: “This, my friends, that flows here is blood, and not ichor, such as flows in the blessed gods” (*Alexander* 28.3). The second half of the sentence is Homer's line, word for word. The gods do not bleed blood. Alexander does, and so he is no god.

The saying was told of more than one man. Diogenes Laertius gives it to the philosopher Anaxarchus, who went with Alexander on his campaigns: seeing the king bleeding from a wound, he pointed at it and quoted the line, to turn him from thinking himself a god. Diogenes adds that Plutarch gives the words to Alexander himself (9.60).

In Homer the line is spoken of a goddess wounded like a mortal, not of a mortal who would be a god. Aphrodite's wound is real. She goes away “distraught, in terrible pain”, and her beautiful skin darkens (5.352–354). What she cannot do is die of it. The king who quoted the line knew the other half of the difference: his blood could kill him.
