**At Carthage.** Aeneas, cast ashore in Africa, finds the war of Troy painted on the walls of Juno's temple and weeps at scene after scene: the Trojan women carrying a robe to Athena, who keeps her eyes on the ground (*Aeneid* 1.479–482; see [[the-robe-and-the-panathenaea]]), and then this: “Three times around the walls of Ilium Achilles had dragged Hector, and was selling the lifeless body for gold. Then indeed he groans deeply from the bottom of his heart, as he sees the spoils, the chariot, the very body of his friend, and Priam stretching out unarmed hands” (*Ter circum Iliacos raptaverat Hectora muros, / exanimumque auro corpus vendebat Achilles*, 1.483–487).

The picture joins three scenes of the Iliad. In Homer the three circuits of the walls belong to the chase, when the two men ran three times around the city of Priam (22.165). The dragging goes from the walls to the ships (22.395–404, 22.464–465), and later, day after day, three times around the tomb of Patroclus (24.14–18). The gold is the ransom of Book 24, which in Book 22 Achilles says he will never take, “not even if he should bid them weigh out your own body in gold, / Priam, descendant of Dardanus” (22.351–352). In Euripides, too, Hector is dragged around the walls (*Andromache* 107–108; see [[euripides-andromache]]).

**The dream.** On the night the Greeks come out of the horse, Aeneas tells Dido, “in sleep, behold, before my eyes most sorrowful Hector seemed to stand, shedding floods of tears, dragged by the chariot as once he was, black with bloody dust, and pierced through his swollen feet with thongs” (*raptatus bigis ut quondam, aterque cruento / pulvere perque pedes traiectus lora tumentis*, *Aeneid* 2.270–273). The marks of the dragging are Homer's. Achilles pierced the tendons of both his feet from heel to ankle, fastened oxhide straps to them, and let the head drag in the dust (22.396–402); and Virgil's *bigae*, the two-horse chariot, answers the pair of horses in Homer's dual, “and the pair flew on not unwilling” (22.400; see [[horses]]).

“Ah me, how he looked, how changed from that Hector who returns clad in the spoils of Achilles, or who hurled Phrygian fires on the ships of the Danaans! His beard was foul and his hair matted with blood, and he bore those wounds, so many, that he received around his native walls” (*Aeneid* 2.274–279). Hector puts on Achilles' armor, stripped from Patroclus, in *Iliad* 17, and dies in it (17.192–197, 22.322–323). Aeneas' contrast is the one the Achaeans make as they stand over the body and stab it: “Ah, truly much softer to handle now / is Hector than when he burned the ships with blazing fire” (22.373–374). In Homer Hector dies of a single wound, at the throat (22.326–327); the many wounds are the Achaeans', for “not one stood beside him without stabbing him” (22.371).

Aeneas asks him what has kept him and why his face is defiled, and Hector does not answer the vain questions. Groaning, he tells him to flee: the enemy holds the walls, Troy is falling from its height, enough has been given to his country and to Priam; “if Pergamum could be defended by a right hand, it would have been defended by this one too”. Troy entrusts its holy things and its household gods to Aeneas, to carry over the sea to the great walls he will found (*Aeneid* 2.281–297). In the Iliad Hector was the city's one defender; his son was called Astyanax “for you alone guarded their gates and long walls” (22.507; see [[astyanax]]). In the *Aeneid* his last charge passes the city's gods to the man whom Poseidon saves in *Iliad* 20 to rule the Trojans (20.302–308; see [[the-rule-of-aeneas]]).
