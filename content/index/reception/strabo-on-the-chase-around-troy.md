**Two Iliums.** In the thirteenth book of the *Geography* Strabo describes the Trojan plain after Demetrius of Scepsis, a native of the region, who held that the Ilium of Strabo's day did not stand on the site of Homer's city. The old Ilium, it was believed, had stood at the Village of the Ilians, thirty stades from the present city (*Geography* 13.1.35; see [[callicolone]] and [[thymbre]]). Strabo sets out the evidence. The places that Homer names are shown in the plain of the Scamander, the wild fig tree, the tomb of Aesyetes, Batieia and the tomb of Ilus (13.1.34; see [[the-lookout-at-troy]]); the fig tree, a rough place overgrown with wild figs, lies just below the old site and far from the present city (13.1.35; see [[the-fig-tree]]); the naval station is so close to the present city that the Achaeans would have been mad to leave it unwalled so long, or the Trojans cowards not to attack it (13.1.36); and Polites, who sat as a lookout for the Trojans on the tomb of Aesyetes, would have watched to no purpose, since from the height of the citadel he could have seen as far (13.1.37, on 2.791–794; see [[polites]]). Then the chase: “Nor does Hector's running around the city make any sense; for the present city cannot be run around, because of the continuous ridge, but the old one can be run around” (*Geography* 13.1.37). Earlier Strabo describes a great neck of high ground that divides the plain of the Scamander from the plain of the Simoeis, starting from the present Ilium and joined to it (13.1.34). Of the old city, Strabo adds, no trace survives, for its stones were carried off to rebuild the cities around it (13.1.38).

**The chase in the poem.** Homer does not measure the course. The two men run past the lookout and the windy wild fig tree, always out from under the wall, along the wagon road, to the springs of the Scamander (22.145–148), and so “three times around the city of Priam” (22.165); each time Hector makes for the Dardanian gates, Achilles gets in front of him and turns him back toward the plain (22.194–198; see [[dardanian-gates]]), and on the fourth time they come to the springs (22.208). The argument assumes that the poet knew the site and that the chase could be run on it.

**The two springs.** Homer's springs are two, one flowing with warm water, with smoke rising from it as from a blazing fire, the other in summer as cold as hail, or snow, or ice (22.147–152; see [[the-springs-of-the-scamander]]). Demetrius, Strabo says, knew the places as a native: the Scamander, the Granicus and the Aesepus all rise on Cotylus, a hill of Ida about a hundred and twenty stades above Scepsis, the Scamander flowing west from a single spring. So the verses raise a question, “for neither are hot waters now found in the place, nor is the source of the Scamander there, but in the mountain, and it is one, not two.” The hot spring has probably failed, and the cold one rises in that place from water seeping underground from the Scamander; or else, because it is near the river, this water too is called a source of the Scamander, as several springs are called the sources of one river (13.1.43). The text glosses Homer's λιαρῷ, “warm”, as θερμῷ, “hot”.

Demetrius' answer comes again in the first book of the *Geography*, among Strabo's examples of the changes the earth undergoes. Recalling the verses on the springs, Demetrius would not have anyone wonder that the spring of cold water remains while the hot one is no longer seen: the failure of the hot water must be blamed. He cites what Democles reported of great earthquakes long ago around Lydia and Ionia as far as the Troad, by which villages were swallowed up and Sipylus overturned in the reign of Tantalus, lakes formed from marshes, and a wave flooded Troy (1.3.17).
