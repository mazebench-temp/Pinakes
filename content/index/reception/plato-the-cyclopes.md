**Homer.** Odysseus begins his account of the Cyclopes with their land. They are “overbearing and lawless”, and trusting in the immortal gods they neither plant nor plow, but wheat, barley and vines grow for them unsown, “and the rain of Zeus makes it grow for them” (*Odyssey* 9.106–111; see [[cyclopes]] and [[overbearing]]). Their trust, Merry notes, is no devoutness, as the Cyclops' boast that they care nothing for Zeus shows, but a careless confidence in the bounty of nature (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 9.107; *Odyssey* 9.275–276). Then come the four verses on their way of life (*Odyssey* 9.112–115). Merry renders θεμιστεύει “is lord and master over”, and finds a kind of irony in a people that knows no θέμιστες, “ordinances”, while each of them gives the law: “Laws imply Society: the Cyclopes are a law to themselves” (Merry on *Odyssey* 9.114; see [[themis]] and the translation's key words). The one Cyclops of the story keeps even from these: “he did not go among the others, / but lived apart, and knew lawless ways” (*Odyssey* 9.188–189; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]).

**Plato.** In the third book of the *Laws* the Athenian stranger asks how constitutions began. The few who survived the great flood had no need of lawgivers; they had no letters yet and lived by custom and what are called ancestral laws, and that too is a kind of constitution (*Laws* 680a). “Everyone, I think, calls the constitution of that time a lordship (δυναστεία), which still exists in many places among Greeks and barbarians; and Homer says somewhere that it existed in the dwelling of the Cyclopes”, and he quotes the four verses as our text has them (680b–c). The Cretan Clinias answers that the poet seems a graceful one, though the Cretans make little use of foreign poems; the Spartan Megillus that they use him, and that he seems the best of such poets, though the life he describes is Ionian, not Laconian, and that here he bears witness to the argument, “having brought their antiquity back to savagery by a tale” (680c–d). The Athenian takes him as an informant that such constitutions come about: out of the households scattered by the destructions, in which the eldest rule, since the rule came to them from father and mother, men follow their elders like birds in a single flock, “governed by their fathers and ruled by the most just kingship of all” (680d–e; plain renderings throughout; see [[plato]]). Larger settlements on the foothills follow, and then the city of Dardania, for which Homer is a witness again (681a–e; see [[plato-the-slopes-of-ida]]). The poet said those verses and the verses on the Cyclopes “in accordance with god and with nature”, the Athenian concludes, for the race of poets, divine and inspired in its singing, lays hold, with certain Graces and Muses, of many things that truly happened (682a).

**Aristotle.** The first book of the *Politics* builds the city from the same beginning. The village is a colony of the household, and so cities were at first ruled by kings, and nations still are, “for every household is ruled as a kingdom by its eldest, and so are the colonies, because of their kinship; and this is what Homer says, ‘each one gives the law / to his children and his wives’; for they were scattered, and so they lived in ancient times”; and men say that the gods too are ruled by a king for that reason, making the lives of the gods like their own (*Politics* 1.2, 1252b19–27; see [[aristotle-man-without-a-city]], [[basileus]] and [[aristotle]]). At the end of the *Nicomachean Ethics* the verse is turned against the cities of Aristotle's own day: only at Sparta and in a few other cities has the lawgiver taken care of upbringing and pursuits, while in most each man lives as he wishes, “laying down the law Cyclops-fashion (κυκλωπικῶς) for his children and his wife” (*Nicomachean Ethics* 10.9, 1180a24–29). There Aristotle writes the singular ἀλόχου, “wife”, where the *Politics* keeps Homer's ἀλόχων, “wives”; Merry, who notes the difference, thinks that the plural does not mean polygamy but is assimilated in number and sound to παίδων (Merry on *Odyssey* 9.114, and his critical note on *Odyssey* 9.115).

**Strabo.** Strabo retells Plato's three forms of constitution in his account of the Troad, giving the first, on the mountain tops, to the Cyclopes, “who lived on fruits that grow of themselves and held the mountain tops in certain caves”, and quoting, besides the four verses, the line on the harvest that comes unsown, “but all these things grow unsown and unplowed” (*Odyssey* 9.109); in his copy the first verse begins οὐκ ἀγοραί for οὔτʼ ἀγοραί (*Geography* 13.1.25; see [[plato-the-slopes-of-ida]] and [[strabo]]). The same line serves him in his account of the Albanians of the Caucasus, whose land bears every fruit and every plant without care, “all these things grow unsown and unplowed”, as those who campaigned there say, “describing a sort of Cyclopean life” (Κυκλώπειόν τινα βίον): sown once, it bears two or three harvests, the first fiftyfold, without lying fallow, though it is plowed with a plow of wood (*Geography* 11.4.3, plain renderings).
