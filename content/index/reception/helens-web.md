{{quote:5cdf6064-d775-5899-ae3c-f49a4ef09c27}}

Helen is weaving a great web, a double-folded purple cloth, and "working into it" (ἐνέπασσεν) the many ἄεθλοι ("trials, struggles") of the horse-taming Trojans and the bronze-armored Achaeans, which they suffer for her sake at the hands of Ares. The verb, "sprinkle in", is used of figured weaving. The same words describe Andromache at her loom in Book 22, "a double-folded purple cloth", into which she works flowers, when she hears the cry from the wall for Hector (22.440–441). Homer never says what becomes of Helen's cloth.

The scholia on these lines remark that the poet has fashioned here a worthy archetype of his own poetry. Helen does in thread what the poem does in words: she makes a record of the war while it is being fought, and she is its subject as well as its maker. In Book 6 she tells Hector that Zeus has given her and Paris an evil fate "so that hereafter we may be a theme of song for men to come" (6.357–358). Modern critics have read the web in the same way, as the poem's image of itself.

Book 3 takes up the image again. When Antenor remembers the Achaean envoys addressing the Trojans, he says that they "wove" (ὕφαινον) their words and counsels before all (3.212). In the Odyssey Helen gives Telemachus a robe she has made, "a memorial of Helen's hands" (Odyssey 15.126). What she weaves outlasts the moment, as the poem does.
