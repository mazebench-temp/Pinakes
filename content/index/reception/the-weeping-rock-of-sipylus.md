summary: Homer's Niobe, turned to stone on Sipylus, broods over the sorrows the gods gave her. Later writers made the stone weep. Sophocles' Antigone and Electra sing of her wasting under rain and snow and weeping forever in her rocky tomb, Ovid's marble still drips tears, and in Apollodorus tears flow from the stone night and day. Pausanias, who went up Sipylus to see her, found near at hand a crag with no likeness of a woman, and from farther off a woman in tears with her head bowed; and he counted the story that she weeps in summer among the embellishments of the credulous.

**Homer.** Achilles ends his story of Niobe with the place where she is now. She remembered food “when she was worn out with shedding tears” (24.613), and now, a stone among the crags of Sipylus, “she broods over the sorrows the gods gave her” (κήδεα πέσσει, 24.617; see [[sipylus]] and [[the-children-of-niobe]]). The verb is used of nursing anger, as Achilles “broods over his heart-grieving anger” by the ships (4.513), and Priam answers Achilles in the same words: since Hector died, “always I groan and brood over countless sorrows”, and only now has he tasted food (24.639–642; see [[priam]] and [[fasting]]). In Homer the stone does not weep. Its tears are the later poets'. Walter Leaf says that “the legend told that the water trickling down the rock-image actually was a perpetuation of Niobe's grief in stone” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.614); the ancient critics who athetized the four lines, and his discussion of the rock that later travelers took for her, are given under [[sipylus]].

**Sophocles.** Antigone, led away to her tomb in the rock, likens herself to Niobe: she has heard that the Phrygian stranger, Tantalus' daughter, died most miserably on the heights of Sipylus, “whom the growth of rock, like clinging ivy, subdued; and the rains, as men tell, never leave her as she wastes away, nor the snow, and she wets the ridges with her ever-weeping brows”, and like her a god lays Antigone to rest (*Antigone* 823–833). In the *Electra* the chorus tells Electra that she will never raise her father from the lake of Hades by laments or by prayers, and that her grief is past all remedy; Electra answers that he is a fool who forgets parents who died piteously, and that the mourning bird who cries “Itys, ever Itys” suits her heart: “Ah, all-enduring Niobe, you I count a goddess, you who in your rocky tomb weep forever” (*Electra* 137–152). The chorus' argument is Achilles' to Priam, who must not mourn relentlessly, “nor will you raise him up” (24.549–551). Achilles makes Niobe the mother who ate when she had wept enough; Electra makes her the mother who never stops weeping.

**Ovid and Apollodorus.** Ovid's Niobe grows rigid among the bodies of her children and her husband, and a whirlwind carries her off to her homeland, where, fixed on a mountain's top, she melts, “and even now the marble drips tears” (*Metamorphoses* 6.301–312; see [[ovid-niobe]]). In Apollodorus' handbook she leaves Thebes and goes to her father Tantalus at Sipylus, and there, praying to Zeus, she is turned to stone, “and tears flow night and day from the stone” (*Library* 3.5.6; see [[apollodorus]]). This is the form of the story by which Leaf reconciles Homer's two lines, the mother who ate and the mother who is stone (on 24.613).

**Pausanias.** In Athens, at the top of the theater, Pausanias describes a cave in the rocks under the Acropolis with a tripod above it, on which Apollo and Artemis are killing the children of Niobe. Then he adds what he has seen: “This Niobe I myself saw, when I had gone up to Mount Sipylus. Near at hand it is a rock and a cliff, which gives one standing by it no shape of a woman, mourning or otherwise; but if you go farther away, you will think you see a woman in tears, with her head bowed” (δεδακρυμένην δόξεις ὁρᾶν καὶ κατηφῆ γυναῖκα, *Description of Greece* 1.21.3). He speaks of the region as his own. Signs remain to this day, he writes, that Pelops and Tantalus once lived “among us” (τῆς παρʼ ἡμῖν ἐνοικήσεως): a lake called after Tantalus and a famous grave, and on a peak of Sipylus the throne of Pelops, beyond the sanctuary of Plastene the Mother (5.13.7). Elsewhere he is less trusting. Lies built on a foundation of truth, he says, have made men disbelieve even what is true, as with the story that ever since Lycaon a man has turned into a wolf at the sacrifice to Lycaean Zeus; “likewise they say that Niobe on Mount Sipylus weeps in the season of summer” (ὡσαύτως δὲ καὶ Νιόβην λέγουσιν ἐν Σιπύλῳ τῷ ὄρει θέρους ὥρᾳ κλαίειν), and those who like to hear marvels add marvels of their own (8.2.6–7). He had seen the rock, and he does not say that he saw it weep. Leaf quotes his description of the rock against the identification of the carved figure near Smyrna, which an inscription names as Plastene, Mother of the Gods, with the ancient Niobe (on 24.614; see [[sipylus]] and [[pausanias]]).
