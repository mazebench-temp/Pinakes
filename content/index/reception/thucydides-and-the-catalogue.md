The opening chapters of Thucydides' history, often called the "Archaeology", argue that no earlier war matched the Peloponnesian War in scale. The Trojan War is his main test case, and Homer, "if he is sufficient evidence for anyone", is his main witness (1.9.4).

He first asks why the Greeks followed Agamemnon. Not, he thinks, because Helen's suitors were bound by their oath to Tyndareus, but because Agamemnon was the most powerful man of his time, heir to the wealth that Pelops had brought from Asia (1.9.1–2). Homer bears this out. Agamemnon brought the most ships, and lent ships to the Arcadians besides (2.576, 2.610–614). And where the poet tells how the scepter came down to him, he says that Agamemnon ruled "many islands and all Argos" (2.108). A king on the mainland could rule many islands only with a fleet (1.9.4).

Then he weighs the numbers. Mycenae was a small place, but that proves nothing: if Sparta were deserted, later ages would doubt its power (1.10.1–2). The expedition was the largest of its time, but small by later standards, "if we are to trust Homer's poetry here too, which, being a poet, he is likely to have embellished" (1.10.3). By Thucydides' count Homer's fleet has 1,200 ships. The Boeotian ships carried 120 men each (2.510) and Philoctetes' ships 50 (2.719), "showing, as I think, the largest and the smallest". The rowers fought as well, as the poet shows by making all of Philoctetes' rowers archers (2.719–720). Averaged over the fleet, that is not a large army for a force drawn from all Greece (1.10.4–5). The cause, Thucydides concludes, was not a shortage of men but of money. The Greeks could not feed a larger army, and had to scatter to farm and to raid, which is why the war took ten years (1.11).

The method was new. Thucydides treats the Catalogue as a document, discounts the poet's exaggeration, and computes. His figure is rounded: the ships of the Catalogue add up to 1,186.
