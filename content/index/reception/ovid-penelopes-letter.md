**The letter.** The *Heroides* are letters in elegiac verse from women of myth to the men who have left them (see [[ovid-heroides]]). The first is Penelope's, and it begins with its point: “Your Penelope sends this to you, slow Ulysses; there is no use in your writing back: come yourself!” (*Haec tua Penelope lento tibi mittit, Ulixe; / nil mihi rescribas attinet: ipse veni!*, *Heroides* 1.1–2). Troy has fallen, and she wishes that Paris had drowned when he sailed for Lacedaemon: she would not have lain cold in a deserted bed, nor would the hanging web have wearied her widowed hands through the long nights (*Heroides* 1.3–10; see [[penelopes-web-in-later-writers]]). “The Argive chiefs have come home, the altars smoke” (*Argolici rediere duces, altaria fumant*, *Heroides* 1.25), and only Ulysses is missing. Telemachus, sent to look for his father, has brought back from Nestor at Pylos the stories of the war, among them the night of Rhesus and Dolon, which she tells over in fear (*Heroides* 1.37–46; see [[ovid-dolon-and-rhesus]]). “We sent to Pylos, the Nelean fields of old Nestor; uncertain is the report sent back from Pylos. We sent to Sparta too: Sparta too does not know the truth. What lands do you live in, or where do you linger?” (*Heroides* 1.63–66).

**The house.** Her father Icarius urges her to leave her widowed bed and keeps chiding her endless delays; let him chide: “yours I am, yours I must be called; Penelope will always be the wife of Ulysses” (*Heroides* 1.81–84). The suitors from Dulichium, Same and lofty Zacynthus rush upon her, a luxurious crowd, and reign in his hall with no one to stop them, “our vitals, your wealth, are torn apart” (*Heroides* 1.87–90). She names Pisander, Polybus, cruel Medon, and the greedy hands of Eurymachus and Antinous, whom he feeds in his shameful absence with the wealth he won by his blood, and, as the last disgrace, the beggar Irus and Melanthius, who drives in the flock to be eaten (*Heroides* 1.91–96). “We are three, unwarlike: a wife without strength, old Laertes and Telemachus, a boy. He was almost taken from me lately by an ambush, while he made ready to go to Pylos against the will of all” (*Tres sumus inbelles numero, sine viribus uxor / Laertesque senex Telemachusque puer. / ille per insidias paene est mihi nuper ademptus, / dum parat invitis omnibus ire Pylon*, *Heroides* 1.97–100). On her side are the keeper of the cattle, the aged nurse and the faithful keeper of the sty; but Laertes, useless in arms, cannot hold the kingdom among enemies, and a stronger age will come for Telemachus only if he lives. “Nor have I the strength to drive the enemies from the house. Come quickly, you, the harbor and altar of your people!” (*nec mihi sunt vires inimicos pellere tectis. / tu citius venias, portus et ara tuis!*, *Heroides* 1.103–110).

**The letter and the Odyssey.** Most of what she writes is in Homer. The suitors from Dulichium, Same and Zacynthus are those of Telemachus' count (*Odyssey* 1.245–247, 16.247–251; see [[the-suitors]]); her father and brothers urge her to marry Eurymachus (*Odyssey* 15.16–17; see [[icarius]]); the cowherd, the nurse and the swineherd are Philoetius, Eurycleia and Eumaeus (see [[eurycleia]] and [[eumaeus]]). The ambush is Antinous' plan to lie in wait for Telemachus in the strait between Ithaca and Same, which he escapes on his way home (*Odyssey* 4.669–672, 4.842–847, 16.364–370). But Homer's Penelope did not send her son. He left in secret, after binding the nurse by an oath not to tell her (*Odyssey* 2.372–377); she learned of it from the herald Medon only when the suitors were already plotting his death, and said that had she known he was planning the voyage he would have stayed, or left her dead in the hall (*Odyssey* 4.696–702, 4.729–734; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). Ovid's letter speaks of the journey once as a mission, “we sent”, and once as a departure “against the will of all”.
