**The dialogue.** Ion of Ephesus is a rhapsode, a professional reciter of Homer. He has just won first prize at the festival of Asclepius at Epidaurus, hopes to win at the Panathenaea, and claims to speak about Homer better than anyone, but only about Homer (530a–531a). Socrates asks what his knowledge is knowledge of. Each art has its own field, and what is said in its field only that art can judge (537c–538a), and Socrates tests the rule on Homer. Nestor's advice to Antilochus on taking the turn in the chariot race is for a charioteer to judge (537a–c, quoting 23.335–340). Hecamede's drink for the wounded Machaon is for a doctor (538b–c; see [[plato-asclepiads]]). The lead weight that drops through the water to the fish is for a fisherman (538c–d, quoting 24.80–82). Divination had been Socrates' first example: when Homer and Hesiod speak of the seer's art, where they agree and where they differ, a good seer would explain them better than Ion, and Ion agrees (531a–b).

**The seer's passages.** Then Socrates turns the questioning around and answers for himself. Since he finds in Homer what belongs to each art, let Ion ask him for the passages of the seer and of divination, and see how readily he answers (538d–e). There are many in the Odyssey, such as the words of Theoclymenus, “the seer of the line of Melampus”, to the suitors: their heads and faces are wrapped in night, the forecourt and the hall are full of ghosts hastening to the darkness, the sun has perished from the sky (539a, quoting *Odyssey* 20.351–357). And there are many in the Iliad too, “as at the Teichomachia” (οἷον καὶ ἐπὶ τειχομαχίᾳ), where Socrates recites the eagle and the serpent (539b–d, quoting 12.200–207). “These and the like I will say belong to the seer, both to examine and to judge” (539d). Ion agrees. Asked in turn for the passages that belong to the rhapsode, he says “all of them”, then falls back on what it befits a man or a woman, a slave or a free man, a ruler or a subject to say (539e–540b), and ends by claiming to be the best general in Greece, having learned it from Homer (540d–541c). Socrates leaves him the choice of being thought unjust or divine, a praiser of Homer by divine dispensation and not by art (542a–b). Earlier in the dialogue he had said that the god takes away the poets' minds and uses them as his servants, as he uses the oracle-singers and the divine seers, so that it is the god himself who speaks (534c–d).

**The verses.** Plato's text of the Iliad passage is ours but for small matters of spelling, among them the Attic ζῷον for ζωόν, “alive” (12.203). He stops at 12.207, as the eagle flies off screaming, before the Trojans shudder at the snake lying among them, “the portent of aegis-bearing Zeus” (12.208–209): Socrates gives the seer the sign without its reading. His quotation of the Odyssey is freer. It begins δαιμόνιοι where our text has ἆ δειλοί, has γυῖα for γοῦνα, and leaves out one verse, the walls and the fair crossbeams “spattered with blood” (*Odyssey* 20.354). That perfect, ἐρράδαται, “are spattered”, occurs elsewhere in Homer only in Book 12, of the towers and battlements of the Achaean wall, spattered with the blood of men on both sides (12.430–431).

**In the Iliad.** No seer is present at the ditch. The bird comes on the left as the Trojans are eager to cross, the serpent strikes it, and it drops its prey among them (12.200–209; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]], [[eagle]] and [[serpent]]). Polydamas gives the reading: as the eagle let its prey go before it reached its nest, so the Trojans, even if they break the gates and the wall, will not come back from the ships in good order. “So would a prophet interpret it, one who clearly in his heart / knew of portents, and whom the people would obey” (12.228–229; see [[polydamas]] and [[divination]]). Socrates' rule, that such verses are for a seer to judge, is the authority Polydamas claims for his own reading. Hector rejects it: he trusts the counsels of Zeus and does not care whether birds fly to the right or the left, and “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (12.230–243; see [[one-omen-is-best]]). Polydamas' reading comes true in Book 16, when Patroclus drives the Trojans from the ships and they cross back over the ditch “not in due order” (οὐδὲ κατὰ μοῖραν), leaving their chariots broken in it (16.364–371).

Both of Socrates' passages are warnings that their hearers reject. The suitors laugh at Theoclymenus, and Eurymachus says that the stranger is out of his mind (*Odyssey* 20.358–362); Hector tells Polydamas that the gods themselves have destroyed his wits (12.234). Plato does not draw the parallel; he chose the passages for the seer's art. In both of them the warning comes true.
