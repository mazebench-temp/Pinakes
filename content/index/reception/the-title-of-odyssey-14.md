summary: Book 14 of the Odyssey is headed Ὀδυσσέως πρὸς Εὔμαιον ὁμιλία, “the talk of Odysseus with Eumaeus”, and the book is talk. Sixteen of its seventeen speeches pass between the beggar and the swineherd, and more than three quarters of its lines are speech. The swineherd is first named in the poem in this book, by the poet, who turns to address him; Strabo calls the story of the cloak “the narrative to Eumaeus”, in the heading's construction, and Monro heads the next book with the same two words.

The title of *Odyssey* 14 in Monro's edition is Ὀδυσσέως πρὸς Εὔμαιον ὁμιλία, “the talk of Odysseus with Eumaeus” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, heading of Book 14); this library calls the book “Odysseus' Talk with Eumaeus”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter of the alphabet, this one is ξ, xi (see [[title-of-book-5]]). The swineherd is told of under [[eumaeus]], his farmstead under [[fold]], [[sties]] and [[hut]], and the beggar's long false tale under [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]], the second of the series that begins with [[the-first-cretan-tale]], told to Athena in the book before.

**The words of the title.** ὁμιλία is not Homer's word. Homer has the verb, ὁμιλέω, “to be among, keep company with”, and never of talk. In the Iliad it is mostly the throng of battle, “Hector was in the throng with them” (*Iliad* 11.502). In the Odyssey it is the company of the suitors: one of Aegyptius' sons “kept company with the suitors” (*Odyssey* 2.21), Telemachus is told to go home and “keep company with the suitors” (*Odyssey* 2.288), and Penelope would tell him “not to keep company always with the overbearing suitors” (*Odyssey* 18.167, a plain rendering). The sense of the heading, “converse”, is the later one, and the Suda uses it to explain Homer's own word for intimate talk: “ὀαρίζω: I speak with women; for ὄαρος is ὁμιλία” (Suda ο 2, a plain rendering). That is the verb of Hector, who “had talked intimately with his wife”, and of the maiden and the young man who “talk intimately with each other” (*Iliad* 6.516, 22.128). The poem has its own formula for such an exchange, and it closes the talk of this book before supper: “So they spoke such words to one another” (τοιαῦτα πρὸς ἀλλήλους ἀγόρευον, *Odyssey* 14.409). The genitive Ὀδυσσέως is the later form; the book has Homer's Ὀδυσσῆος and Ὀδυσῆος (*Odyssey* 14.144, 14.159, 14.321, 14.515), as the heading of the book before has the later Φαιάκων for Homer's Φαιήκων (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-13]]).

**The swineherd's name.** The heading puts the name in the accusative, Εὔμαιον, a form that does not occur in this book. The narrator never names him here in the third person. He is “the swineherd”, “the brilliant swineherd” (δῖος ὑφορβός, *Odyssey* 14.3, 14.48, 14.401, 14.413) and “the swineherd, leader of men” (*Odyssey* 14.22, 14.121; see [[brilliant]] and [[leader-of-men]]). His name is heard only in the vocative, seven times: twice from the beggar (*Odyssey* 14.440, 14.462), and five times from the poet himself, who turns from his story to speak to him, “Answering him, you said, swineherd Eumaeus” (*Odyssey* 14.55, 14.165, 14.360, 14.442, 14.507; see [[apostrophe]] and [[answering-him]]). The first of these (*Odyssey* 14.55) is the first time the name is spoken in the poem. Athena had sent Odysseus to him without naming him: “But you yourself go first of all to the swineherd” (*Odyssey* 13.404). The accusative of the heading comes first in the next book but one, when Odysseus, hearing footsteps at dawn, “addressed Eumaeus with winged words” (*Odyssey* 16.7, a plain rendering).

**Talk of an absent man.** The heading gives the guest the name that no one in the book gives him. To the swineherd he is “stranger” and “old man” (*Odyssey* 14.37, 14.56, 14.122), and the Odysseus of their talk is the absent master. Eumaeus grieves for him, counts his wealth and says that he must be dead (*Odyssey* 14.40–44, 14.96–108, 14.133–137). He speaks the name in his longing and then shrinks from it: “Him, stranger, even though he is not here, I feel shame / to name; for he loved me greatly and cared for me in his heart; / but I call him dear master, even though he is far away” (*Odyssey* 14.144–147; see [[dear-brother]]). The beggar swears to him “that Odysseus is coming home”, and that “In this very year Odysseus will come here” (*Odyssey* 14.152, 14.161; see [[oath]]), and says that he heard of Odysseus in [[thesprotians|Thesprotia]] and saw his treasure there (*Odyssey* 14.321–333). In his last story he puts Odysseus beside him in the ambush at Troy, “who was near me” (*Odyssey* 14.484; see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]). Eumaeus believes all the rest of the tale and refuses only this: “But in this, I think, you have not spoken aright, nor will you persuade me / with what you say of Odysseus” (*Odyssey* 14.363–364; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]] and [[truth-and-deception]]).

**What the title covers.** The talk fills the book from the first greeting to the bed by the fire. Of 533 lines, 409 are speech, 249 of them the beggar's and 160 the swineherd's, and all the seventeen speeches but one pass between the two of them; the exception is Eumaeus' order to his men to bring the best of the swine (*Odyssey* 14.414–417). The swineherd sets the order of the evening at the gate: “so that you too, / when you have had your heart's fill of bread and wine, / may tell me where you are from and how many sorrows you have endured” (*Odyssey* 14.45–47). The talk runs from the welcome and the meal of piglets, with Eumaeus' account of the suitors and of his master's wealth (*Odyssey* 14.37–108), through the question about the master, the oath offered and refused, and the swineherd's grief for Telemachus (*Odyssey* 14.115–190), to the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|Cretan tale]], which takes 168 lines, the longest speech of the book (*Odyssey* 14.192–359). Eumaeus' answer and the wager follow (*Odyssey* 14.361–408; see [[rhetre]]), then the sacrifice of the boar, with the beggar's blessing and the host's reply (*Odyssey* 14.409–456), and last, on a stormy night, the tale of the cloak, told “testing the swineherd”, and Eumaeus' answer, “Old man, the tale you have told is flawless” (*Odyssey* 14.459–517; see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]], [[ainos]] and [[peira]]). The heading leaves out the narrative around the talk: the climb from the harbor and the description of the fold, the sties and the dogs (*Odyssey* 14.1–36; see [[dogs]]), the slaughter and the portions (*Odyssey* 14.418–438; see [[sacrifice]] and [[plato-chine-of-ajax]]), and the close, when Odysseus rejoices “that he cared so much for his substance while he was far away”, and the swineherd arms himself against the cold and goes out to sleep beside the boars (*Odyssey* 14.518–533). The book began with the swineherd “who cared most for his substance” (*Odyssey* 14.3). It is one day and its night, the day of Odysseus' waking on Ithaca, and it begins in the middle of the sentence with which *Odyssey* 13 ends, “she went to Sparta, while he &c.” (Monro on *Odyssey* 13.440).

**The talk of two nights.** The exchange is of sorrows. Eumaeus asks for the stranger's at the gate and again before the tale, “But come, old sir, tell me of your own sorrows” (*Odyssey* 14.185; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]), and the beggar answers that a year of food and wine in the hut would not be long enough to tell them (*Odyssey* 14.193–198). Eumaeus tells his own in the next book, on another night that the two spend talking in the hut, and that night's talk ends in the formula that closes the talk of this book before supper (*Odyssey* 14.409 = 15.493; see the Book 15 note, [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]] and [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]]). Monro heads the next book Τηλεμάχου πρὸς Εὔμαιον ἄφιξις, “the arrival of Telemachus at Eumaeus' hut” (Monro, heading of Book 15; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-15]]), with this heading's preposition and name, and the arrival is the one that Athena orders in the very verse with which she sent the father: “But you yourself go first of all to the swineherd” (*Odyssey* 13.404 = 15.38). The heading of the book after that names the recognition that follows in the swineherd's hut, Τηλεμάχου ἀναγνωρισμὸς Ὀδυσσέως, “the recognition of Odysseus by Telemachus” (Monro, heading of Book 16).

**False stories set at Troy.** The book holds two of the four passages that Monro cites for his observation that “the various false stories told by Ulysses all turn upon events and characters in the Trojan war”: the Cretan's war at Troy with Idomeneus and the ambush beneath Troy on a winter night (Monro, Appendix I §3, with the note “See Od. 13.256 ff., 14.235 ff., 14.468 ff., 19.172 ff.”; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]], [[idomeneus]], [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]] and [[the-doloneia-question]]). Both are stories of the war that the Iliad does not tell.

**Heroic words for a swineherd.** Monro hears parody in the book's descriptions and a colloquial note in its talk. The twelve sties, “close to one another”, each with fifty swine, “are almost a parody of the description of Priam's palace”, with its fifty chambers and its twelve (Monro on *Odyssey* 14.13–16; *Iliad* 6.244–249; see [[sties]]). “ὑλακόμωρος is a kind of parody of the heroic epithets ἐγχεσίμωρος, ἰόμωρος” (Monro on *Odyssey* 14.29; see [[obscure-iomoroi]]). In his Appendix on the passages of the Iliad borrowed or imitated in the Odyssey he gives the sties and the dogs as cases in which “the spirit of parody is shown by the use of a lofty epic formula where the subject is unworthy of it” (Monro, Appendix II §2). The swineherd's five-year-old boar is an “imitation (or parody?)” of Agamemnon's five-year-old ox, and, as Pierron observed, “the epithet ‘five years old’ is proper for beef, but not for pork” (Monro on *Odyssey* 14.419; *Iliad* 2.402–403; see [[five-years-old]]). ἀμαιμάκετον, the epithet of the Chimaera in the Iliad (*Iliad* 6.179, 16.329), means here, Monro thinks, that the mast was “of vast length”; the senses “invincible” and “raging” hardly suit the place, he says, though “such an epithet might however be applied to a mast in a spirit of burlesque, such as we occasionally trace in the Odyssey” (Monro on *Odyssey* 14.311; see [[irresistible]] and the translation's note on 14.311). The beggar's ἀνέκραγον, “I have once spoken out”, has “a colloquial stamp” (Monro on *Odyssey* 14.467), and the swineherd's δνοπαλίξεις, “you will knock about” in your own [[rags|rags]], is “doubtless colloquial, like so much of the vocabulary of the Odyssey” (Monro on *Odyssey* 14.512). In a note to his first Appendix Monro counts “the examples of parody, &c. quoted in the notes on 14.13 ff.” among the signs that the poet of the Odyssey drew on the Iliad, signs “the more convincing when we observe that the poet of the Odyssey never repeats what has been told in the Iliad. He consciously aims at novelty in the substance as well as the form of his narrative” (Monro, Appendix I §3, note).

**Later readers.** Strabo names a part of the book in the heading's construction. Arguing, after Demetrius of Scepsis, that Homer puts a great distance between the Achaeans' naval station and the city, he says that “the narrative to Eumaeus, contrived by Odysseus” shows it, “as when we led an ambush beneath Troy”, for further on Odysseus says, “For we have come very far from the ships” (ἡ διήγησις ἡ πρὸς τὸν Εὔμαιον ὑπὸ τοῦ Ὀδυσσέως διασκευασθεῖσα, *Geography* 13.1.36, plain renderings; *Odyssey* 14.469, 14.496; see [[strabo]] and [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]). Cicero counts among the things that Ulysses endured in his long wandering that “in every conversation he wished to be affable to all”, and at home he bore even the insults of slaves and maidservants, “so as to come at last to what he desired” (*in omni sermone omnibus affabilem esse se vellet*, *On Duties* 1.113, plain renderings; see [[cicero]]). The first slave he meets at home is the swineherd of this book, who honors him with the chine (*Odyssey* 14.437–438).
