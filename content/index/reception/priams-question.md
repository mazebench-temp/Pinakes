The war has lasted nine years: "nine years of great Zeus have gone by", Agamemnon said in Book 2 (2.134), and Calchas' sign promised the city in the tenth (2.329). Yet on the wall Priam asks Helen who the tall and kingly man is (3.166–170), who the broad-shouldered one (3.192–198), who the giant (3.226–227), and she names Agamemnon, Odysseus, Ajax and Idomeneus. It seems strange that the king of Troy should not know by sight the men who have besieged his city so long. The difficulty is not only Priam's. The whole book, with its duel between the two husbands to decide the war, its oaths, and Priam's invitation to Helen to look at her former husband and her kinsmen (3.163), seems to belong to the war's first days.

There are three kinds of answer.

**It is natural enough.** Priam is old, and the elders are "done with war now because of their age" (3.150). From the wall a king who never goes out to battle might not know one armed man from another, and the armies have now laid their armor on the ground (3.114–115, 3.195). His questions are also a kindness: he calls Helen "dear child", seats her in front of him, and tells her that the gods, not she, are to blame (3.162–165).

**It is left over.** Critics who took the Iliad for a compilation of older poems saw in Book 3 a piece of a poem about the beginning of the war, set down in the tenth year without much adjustment. Other passages show the same loose chronology: in Book 24 Helen says it is the twentieth year since she left home (24.765–766).

**It is deliberate.** Many modern readers think the poet chose to open his battle books by recalling how the war began. The Catalogue of Ships reviews the army as if it were mustering at Aulis (see [[the-boeotia]]); the duel stages the quarrel of the two men who caused the war; the view from the wall introduces the enemy leaders, to the audience as much as to Priam; and the oath and its breaking in Book 4 make the Trojans guilty again. The Iliad tells a few days of the tenth year, but it contains the whole war (see [[the-war-in-small]]). G. S. Kirk discusses the question in his commentary (*The Iliad: A Commentary*, vol. 1, 1985).

The answers are not exclusive. The scene may be old in its type and still placed with purpose.
