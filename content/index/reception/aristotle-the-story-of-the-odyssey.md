summary: Aristotle twice uses the Odyssey to show what a plot is. Homer did not put into it everything that happened to Odysseus, but built it around one action; and its story is short. A man is away from home for many years, watched by Poseidon and alone, while at home suitors waste his property and plot against his son; he comes home storm-tossed, makes himself known, attacks, and is saved, and his enemies perish. All the rest is episodes.

**One action.** In the eighth chapter of the *Poetics* Aristotle argues that a plot is not one because it is about one man, for many things happen to one man that make no single action. Homer saw this:

{{quote:5263fefa-dead-5ad8-9bff-13b2462469f6}}

The wound on Parnassus is the scar by which Odysseus is later recognized, and the feigned madness is a story the Odyssey does not tell (see [[the-boar-hunt-on-parnassus]], [[odysseus-feigns-madness]] and [[unity-of-action]]).

**The story.** In the seventeenth chapter Aristotle tells the poet to set out a story first in general terms, then to supply the names and add the episodes, taking care that the episodes belong to the story; his example is the *Iphigenia* (1455a34–b15; see [[katholou]]). In drama the episodes are short, but epic is lengthened by them, and the Odyssey shows how little of an epic is its story:

{{quote:94abfee7-8b8c-5d3b-8d16-ca2ddc8e943b}}

The outline names only Poseidon. The man, his son, the suitors and the people to whom he makes himself known are left general, as the names are left out of the outline of the *Iphigenia*. The wanderings, the Phaeacians and the son's journey to Pylos and Sparta are not in it; by Aristotle's account they belong to the episodes (see [[epeisodion]] and [[logos]]).

**The rest of the Poetics.** Elsewhere Aristotle calls the Odyssey complex, “for there is recognition throughout”, and a poem of character (1459b15–16), and makes it the type of the double plot, which ends in opposite ways for the better and the worse (1453a30–33; see [[the-double-plot]] and [[odyssey]]).
