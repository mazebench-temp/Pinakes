summary: Book 7 of the Odyssey is headed Ὀδυσσέως εἴσοδος πρὸς Ἀλκίνουν, “the entry of Odysseus to Alcinous”, and Aelian's list of the parts of the Odyssey once sung on their own has no name for it. The title of *Odyssey* 7 in Merry's edition is Ὀδυσσέως εἴσοδος πρὸς Ἀλκίνουν, “the entry of Odysseus to Alcinous” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, heading of Book 7); this library calls the book “Odysseus at the House of Alcinous”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter of the alphabet, this one is η, eta (see [[title-of-book-5]]). Its story is told under [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]], the king under [[alcinous]] and the queen under [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]].

**The words of the title.** The noun εἴσοδος, “entry”, is Homer's only once, for the narrow mouth of the Laestrygonians' harbor, ἀραιὴ δʼ εἴσοδός ἐστιν, “and the entrance is narrow” (*Odyssey* 10.90, a plain rendering). This book tells the entry with the adverb: Odysseus “quickly stepped over the threshold into the house”, ὑπὲρ οὐδὸν ἐβήσετο δώματος εἴσω (*Odyssey* 7.135). The rest of the title is close to the book's own words, Ἀλκινόου πρὸς δώματʼ ἴε κλυτά, “went to the glorious house of Alcinous” (*Odyssey* 7.82), with the king in place of his house. The heading gives the king's name in its later, contracted form, Ἀλκίνουν, where Homer's accusative is Ἀλκίνοον (*Odyssey* 7.55, 7.141). The same contraction is in Merry's heading of Book 9, Ἀλκίνου ἀπόλογοι, “the tales to Alcinous”, and in Aristotle's name for those tales, ὁ Ἀλκίνου ἀπόλογος (*Rhetoric* 1417a14; ἐν Ἀλκίνου ἀπολόγῳ, *Poetics* 1455a2); Homer's genitive is Ἀλκινόου (*Odyssey* 7.82).

**Aelian's list.** Aelian, writing that the ancients sang the poems of Homer divided into parts, passes in his names for the Odyssey from “the events about the raft” to “the tales to Alcinous” (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[aelian]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-3]]). The raft is the fifth book's (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-5]]) and the tales are Odysseus' story in *Odyssey* 9–12 (see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]), whose name became a proverb “for those who talk nonsense and draw out a long speech” (ἀπόλογος Ἀλκίνου, Suda α 3402, a plain rendering); between them Aelian names neither this book nor the books on either side of it (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-6]]). The tales begin where this book's questions stop. Arete asks the stranger, “Who are you among men, and from where? Who gave you these clothes?” (*Odyssey* 7.238; see [[who-are-you-among-men]]), and he answers only the second question, with the story of Ogygia, the raft, the storm and the river, which ends with her daughter on the shore (*Odyssey* 7.241–297; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). His name waits until Alcinous asks for it at the end of the next book (*Odyssey* 8.550), and the tales open with it: εἴμʼ Ὀδυσεὺς Λαερτιάδης, “I am Odysseus, son of Laertes” (*Odyssey* 9.19, a plain rendering).

**A name for the Phaeacian books.** Ovid knew one. Writing from exile to his friend Tuticanus, he remembers correcting his verses “when the Pierian goddesses taught you to compose a Phaeacid worthy of Maeonian pages” (*dignam Maeoniis Phaeacida condere chartis / cum te Pieriae perdocuere deae*, *Letters from Pontus* 4.12.27–28), and in his roll of the poets of his day he counts “the one who turned the Maeonian Phaeacid”, *et qui Maeoniam Phaeacida vertit* (*Letters from Pontus* 4.16.27; plain renderings). “Maeonian” is Homer's, whom Ovid calls Maeonides (*Amores* 1.15.9). The *Phaeacis* is lost; from Ovid's words it was a Latin version of Homer's story of the Phaeacians, with a title of the same form as the *Aeneid*'s (see [[ovid]] and [[phaeacians]]).

**Merry's headings.** Merry's titles for the books of the Phaeacians follow Odysseus inward, one stage to a book: Ὀδυσσέως ἄφιξις εἰς Φαίακας, “the arrival of Odysseus among the Phaeacians” (Book 6); Ὀδυσσέως εἴσοδος πρὸς Ἀλκίνουν, the entry to the king (Book 7); Ὀδυσσέως σύστασις πρὸς Φαίακας, “the introduction of Odysseus to the Phaeacians” (Book 8), where Alcinous leads him to the people's place of assembly by the ships (*Odyssey* 8.4–5; Merry, headings of Books 6, 7 and 8; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-6]]).

**What the title covers.** The entry takes most of the book, and the guest comes after the daughter. Nausicaa reaches “the famous house of her father” first; her brothers unyoke the mules, and her old chamber-woman lights her fire (*Odyssey* 7.1–13; see [[nausicaa]] and [[eurymedusa]]). Then Odysseus rises to go to the city, and Athena pours a thick mist around him (*Odyssey* 7.14–17; see [[achlys]]). He asks the girl with the water jar to lead him “to the house of the man / Alcinous” (*Odyssey* 7.22–23), walks unseen through the city, marveling at the harbors, the ships, the places of assembly and the long walls (*Odyssey* 7.39–45), and stands pondering before he reaches the bronze threshold (*Odyssey* 7.82–83). Nearly fifty lines on the house and the garden come between his halt before the threshold and his step over it (*Odyssey* 7.83–135; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]] and [[the-garden-of-alcinous]]). Inside, he goes through the house in the mist “until he came to Arete and King Alcinous” (*Odyssey* 7.141). The rest of the book is his reception: the hearth and the chair of Laodamas, the feast and the promise of an escort, Arete's question and his story, Alcinous' wish for him as a son-in-law, and the beds. It ends inside the house, with Odysseus asleep beneath the colonnade and Alcinous lying down “in the inner part of the high house”, the queen beside him (*Odyssey* 7.344–347).

**To the king by way of the queen.** The title names Alcinous, and Odysseus asks for his house; but both his guides send him to the queen. Nausicaa told him to pass her father by and throw his arms around her mother's knees (*Odyssey* 6.310–312), and Athena tells him at the door, “First you will find the lady in the halls; / Arete is her name” (*Odyssey* 7.53–54), with the promise that Nausicaa had given: “If she thinks of you in friendship in her heart, / then there is hope that you will see your friends” (*Odyssey* 7.75–76 = 6.313–314). Merry takes Athena's words to mean that his eye will rest first on the queen of all who are in the hall. He adds that a stranger was held to be above all the suppliant of the one to whom he first turned, and cites Alcinous' complaint that his daughter did not bring the stranger home, “and it was to her first that you came as a suppliant” (*Odyssey* 7.299–301), and Arete's words to the Phaeacians in a later book, ξεῖνος δʼ αὖτʼ ἐμός ἐστιν, “and he is my guest” (*Odyssey* 11.338, a plain rendering; Merry on *Odyssey* 7.53). Odysseus' first words in the house are to the queen, “Arete, daughter of godlike Rhexenor” (*Odyssey* 7.146; see [[supplication]]). The answer comes from the men: the old Echeneus speaks to Alcinous, and Alcinous raises the stranger from the hearth (*Odyssey* 7.155–171). The queen speaks only after the feast, when she knows the clothes he wears (*Odyssey* 7.233–239).

**The way out.** The words of the entry come back once, when he leaves. When the sun has set on the day of his departure, Odysseus puts a cup in Arete's hand and bids her farewell, wishing her joy in her house, her children, her people and King Alcinous; then he “stepped over the threshold”, ὑπὲρ οὐδὸν ἐβήσετο, and went down to the ship (*Odyssey* 13.56–70, plain renderings). The phrase is in Homer only there and at his entry (*Odyssey* 7.135). His last words among the Phaeacians, like his first words in the house, are to the queen.
