At the start of the *Symposium*, Aristodemus meets Socrates, freshly bathed and wearing sandals, on his way to dinner at the house of the poet Agathon. Socrates urges him to come along uninvited, and plays on a proverb. "The good go uninvited to the feasts of the good" becomes, with a pun on the host's name, "the good go uninvited to Agathon's feasts" (174b).

Homer, Socrates goes on, did worse than alter the proverb: he outraged it. He made Agamemnon an exceptionally good warrior and Menelaus "a soft spearman". Then, when Agamemnon was sacrificing and giving a feast, he had Menelaus come uninvited, the worse man to the feast of the better (174b–c).

The scene is 2.402–409. Before the first battle, Agamemnon sacrifices an ox to Zeus and invites the six chief elders. Menelaus comes without being called, "for he knew in his heart how his brother was laboring". Homer gives a reason of feeling. Socrates, for his joke, reads it as a breach of manners. The phrase "soft spearman" comes from a later book, where Apollo taunts Hector for having fled before Menelaus (17.587–588).
