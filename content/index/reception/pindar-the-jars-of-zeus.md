**Pindar.** The third *Pythian* ode is for Hieron, tyrant of Syracuse, and it begins with a wish that the centaur Chiron were still alive, who reared Asclepius; if he were, Pindar would persuade him to send a healer for the fevers of good men, and would come to Hieron bringing golden health with his song (*Pythian* 3.1–7, 3.63–76). Then he turns to a saying: “If you understand the true summit of words, Hieron, you know, having learned it from the men of old: for one good the immortals deal out two woes to mortals” (ἓν παρʼ ἐσλὸν πήματα σύνδυο δαίονται βροτοῖς ἀθάνατοι). Fools cannot bear these with grace, but good men can, turning the fair side outward (3.80–83). A secure life was granted neither to Peleus nor to godlike Cadmus, though they are said to have had the highest happiness of mortals. They heard the Muses sing at their weddings, Cadmus' to Harmonia and Peleus' to Thetis; “and the gods feasted with both”, and they saw the sons of Cronus on golden thrones and received their gifts. But in time Cadmus' daughters robbed him of his share of joy, and Peleus' son, “the only one whom immortal Thetis bore in Phthia, leaving his life in war by the bow, as he burned on the pyre roused the lament of the Danaans” (*Pythian* 3.86–103).

**Achilles.** Pindar's sequence is Achilles' in the hut. Achilles tells Priam that two jars stand on the floor of Zeus, and that the man to whom Zeus gives a mixture meets now with evil and now with good, while the man to whom he gives only from the grievous is driven over the earth (24.527–533; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]). Then he gives Peleus as the example: “So to Peleus too the gods gave splendid gifts / from his birth; for he excelled all men / in prosperity and wealth, and he ruled over the Myrmidons, / and to him, a mortal, they gave a goddess as his wife. / But on him too the god laid evil, that no / offspring of princely sons was born to him in his halls, / but he fathered one son, doomed to an untimely death” (24.534–540; see [[peleus]]). Pindar's ὄλβον ὑπέρτατον, the highest happiness, is the ὄλβος in which Homer's Peleus excelled all men, “in prosperity and wealth” (ὄλβῳ τε πλούτῳ τε, 24.536), and the only son whom Thetis bore is Homer's “one son” (ἕνα παῖδα, 24.540). The gods' feast at the wedding is in the same book, in Hera's words to Apollo: “And all you gods came to the wedding; and you among them / feasted with your lyre” (24.62–63; see [[thetis]] and [[hera]]). The death by the bow is foretold in the poem by the dying Hector, who names Paris and Phoebus Apollo (22.359–360; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).

**Two jars or three.** Leaf reports two ancient readings of the jars. On one, both of the “two jars” hold evils, and the jar of blessings is a third; on the other there are two jars only, one of evils and one of blessings. The first agrees well with what follows, since a man can at best expect a mixture and may have no good at all, “This is in fact the sentiment of Pindar”, he writes, quoting this ode. Plato took the passage the other way, and Leaf, on Plato's authority and the construction of the sentence, prefers the two jars (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.527; see [[plato-broken-oaths]] and the translation's note on 24.527–528). Pindar does not name Homer. He gives the saying as something learned “from the men of old” (προτέρων), and it keeps Homer's arithmetic of the mixed life, with the evils doubled.
