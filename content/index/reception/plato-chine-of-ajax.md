**Homer.** After the duel the Achaeans lead Ajax, “exulting in his victory”, to Agamemnon (7.311–312). The fight was stopped before either man won, but the army treats it as his. Agamemnon sacrifices a five-year-old bull to the son of Cronus, and when the meat has been roasted and shared, “no one's heart lacked an equal share of the feast”; but Ajax receives the νῶτα διηνεκέα, the long chine, the whole length of the back (7.313–322; see [[feast]] and [[sacrifice]]). The verb is γεραίρω, “give a mark of honor”, from γέρας (see [[geras]]). The same verse, with another name, tells how the swineherd Eumaeus honors his disguised master with the chine of a boar (*Odyssey* 14.437). Menelaus gives his young guests the chine that had been set before him as his own portion of honor (*Odyssey* 4.65–66), and Odysseus cuts a piece of chine for the singer Demodocus (*Odyssey* 8.474–477). The chine is the portion for the guest the feast most wishes to honor.

**Plato.** In the fifth book of the *Republic* Socrates asks how the guardians who distinguish themselves in war are to be rewarded. They will be crowned by their comrades, greeted with the right hand and kissed; and “according to Homer too” it is right to honor the brave among the young. “For Homer said that Ajax, when he had distinguished himself in war, was honored with the long chine, as this is the fitting honor for a man in his prime and brave, from which, together with the honor, he will also increase his strength” (468c–d). Socrates goes on: at sacrifices and on all such occasions the city will honor the good with hymns, and “with seats of honor and meats and fuller cups” (468d–e). The phrase is Homer's line on the honors of the Lycian kings, spoken by Sarpedon to Glaucus (12.311), and of Hector's taunt to Diomedes (8.162).

Plato gives the Homeric honor a reason of his own: the meat nourishes as well as honors. Earlier in the *Republic* Socrates had struck from the guardians' education Homer's laughing gods and quarreling heroes (see [[plato-homeric-laughter]] and [[plato-achilles-insults]]). Here he follows the poet: “In this, at least, we will obey Homer” (468d).
