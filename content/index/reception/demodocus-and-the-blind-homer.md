summary: Demodocus, the singer whom the Muse loved and robbed of his eyes but gave sweet song, was read from antiquity as Homer's portrait of himself. The singer of the Hymn to Apollo calls himself a blind man of Chios, and Merry takes that line for the root of both traditions, Homer's blindness and his likeness to Demodocus. Proclus says that the likeness was much talked of and makes Demodocus the pattern of inspired poetry; the Suda explains the blindness away, Cicero turns it to Homer's praise, and in the Brutus takes the singers of the Odyssey for poets before Homer.

**Homer.** Demodocus is the one blind singer in Homer. The Muse loved him above all, “and she gave him both good and evil: / she robbed him of his eyes, but gave him sweet song” (*Odyssey* 8.63–64). The herald seats him by a pillar, hangs the lyre above his head “and showed him where to take it in his hands”, and later takes him by the hand to lead him out to the games (*Odyssey* 8.65–70, 8.105–107; see [[demodocus]] and [[heralds]]). Blindness is rare in Homer. Zeus made Lycurgus blind for driving the nurses of Dionysus (6.130–139; see [[lycurgus]]); the seer Tiresias is “the blind seer”, μάντηος ἀλαοῦ, whose mind is still steady among the dead (*Odyssey* 10.493, 12.267, a plain rendering); and Athena, marking Odysseus' throw, says that “Even a blind man, stranger, could sort out your mark / by feeling for it” (*Odyssey* 8.195–196). Demodocus' case is the reverse of Thamyris', from whom the Muses in their anger took his song: “they maimed him, and they took away / his wondrous song and made him forget his lyre-playing” (2.599–600; see [[thamyris]]). From Demodocus the Muse took his eyes and gave the song. Odysseus praises him “far above all mortals”: the Muse or Apollo taught him, for he sings the fate of the Achaeans “as if perhaps you had been present yourself, or heard it from another”, and Odysseus promises to tell all men “how the god has readily granted you wondrous song” (*Odyssey* 8.487–498; see [[muses]] and [[singers]]).

**The blind man of Chios.** At the end of the Delian part of the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* the singer takes leave of the girls of Delos. Remember me hereafter, he asks, whenever some stranger worn by toil comes here and asks, “Girls, who is the sweetest of the singers who come here, and in whom do you delight most?” Then all of you answer well: τυφλὸς ἀνήρ, οἰκεῖ δὲ Χίῳ ἔνι παιπαλοέσσῃ, “a blind man, and he lives in rocky Chios; all his songs will be the best hereafter”. And we will carry your fame wherever we wander over the earth through the well-peopled cities of men, and they will believe it, since it is true (*Hymn to Apollo* 165–176, plain renderings; see [[the-man-of-chios]]). The scene has the elements of this book: a stranger who asks for the best singer, “sweet” song (ἥδιστος ἀοιδῶν, as the Muse gave Demodocus ἡδεῖαν ἀοιδήν), a singer's fame carried by a wanderer to “all men” (*Odyssey* 8.497–498), and a farewell that asks to be remembered, as Nausicaa asks Odysseus, “you may remember me” (*Odyssey* 8.462). Merry, on Demodocus' blindness, writes: “These words remind us of ‘blind Thamyris and blind Maeonides,’ and of our own poet who in these words parallels their lot with his own. The author of the Hymn to Apollo gives as the description of himself, τυφλὸς ἀνήρ, οἰκεῖ δὲ Χίῳ ἔνι παιπαλοέσσῃ, which line seems to be the foundation of the tradition of the blindness of Homer, in the first place; and, secondarily, of the tradition which takes the description of Demodocus in the text to be intended by the poet for himself” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 8.64). The poet he quotes without naming is Milton (see [[milton]]).

**Proclus.** Proclus meets the tradition twice in his essays on Plato's *Republic*. In the *Phaedrus* Socrates had said that Homer, unlike Stesichorus, never understood why he lost his sight (*Phaedrus* 243a; see [[stesichorus-palinode]]). Proclus answers that Stesichorus took the story of Helen for a tale of mere events and was justly punished, while Homer turned from the beauty of the senses to the true beauty, and so was said by the makers of such myths to have been deprived of his eyes, “and to have suffered something like what he himself says that Demodocus, the singer among the Phaeacians, suffered, whom the god who leads music robbed of his eyes and gave sweet song. For he set him forth directly as the pattern of his own inspired life” (*On the Republic* I 173–175 Kroll, plain renderings; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). Proclus' “god who leads music” stands where Homer has the Muse. In the essay on the kinds of poetry he finds all of them in Homer, the inspired kind above all, and he shows it in Demodocus, who began “stirred by the god”, whom the Muse or Apollo taught (*Odyssey* 8.488–499): “that he sets Demodocus forth in some way as himself, and makes him a pattern of what befell himself, is among the things much talked of, and ‘robbed of his eyes, but gave him sweet song’ seems to refer the story about himself directly to him” (*On the Republic* I 193–194 Kroll). Then he divides Homer's singers by the same kinds: Demodocus is the inspired singer; Phemius, who knows “deeds of men and gods”, the singer of knowledge (*Odyssey* 1.337–338; see [[phemius]]); the singer whom Agamemnon left with Clytemnestra, the imitative singer of right opinion, whose songs kept her temperate while he lived (*Odyssey* 3.267–268; see [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]]); and Thamyris, whom the Muses maimed, the singer of the lowest kind, who pursued a varied music that pleases the many (*On the Republic* I 194–195 Kroll).

**The Suda.** The Suda's life of Homer records the blindness and explains it away: he died old on the island of Ios and is buried there, “blind from childhood; but the truth is that he was not overcome by the desire that begins through the eyes, and for this reason he was recorded as blind” (Suda ο 251, a plain rendering). Later in the same entry another story begins with Homer “maimed in his sight” (πηρὸς … τὰς ὄψεις), wandering until he comes to the shepherd Glaucus: πηρός is the word of Thamyris' maiming.

**Cicero.** In the fifth book of the *Tusculan Disputations*, arguing that the wise man can be happy though blind, Cicero turns the tradition to Homer's praise: “It has come down to us that Homer was blind; but it is his painting, not his poetry, that we see. What region, what shore, what place in Greece, what form and shape of battle, what battle line, what rowing, what movement of men or of beasts has he not painted so that he made us see what he himself did not see?” (*Tusculan Disputations* 5.114, a plain rendering). In the *Brutus* he reads the singers of the Odyssey the other way, not as the poet's portrait but as his predecessors: there is no doubt that there were poets before Homer, “as can be understood from the songs which in his poems are sung at the feasts of the Phaeacians and of the suitors” (*Brutus* 71, a plain rendering; see [[cicero]]).
