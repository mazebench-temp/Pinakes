The traditional title of *Odyssey* 4 is Τὰ ἐν Λακεδαίμονι, “the events at Lacedaemon”; this library calls the book “At Lacedaemon”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter of the alphabet, this one is δ, delta (see [[title-of-book-5]]). Its story is told under [[the-journey-of-telemachus]], its hosts under [[menelaus]] and [[helen]], and the place under [[lacedaemon]] and [[sparta]].

**Aelian's list.** Aelian, writing that the ancients sang the poems of Homer divided into parts, names for the Odyssey first “the events at Pylos” and then “the events at Lacedaemon”, followed by the cave of Calypso, the raft, the tales to Alcinous, the Cyclopeia, the Nekyia, the events with Circe, the Washing, the killing of the suitors, the events in the country and the events at Laertes' farm (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[aelian]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-3]]). The first two names follow Telemachus from Nestor's house to Menelaus', the journey Athena laid down for him in the first book: “First go to Pylos and ask brilliant Nestor, / and from there to Sparta, to fair-haired Menelaus” (*Odyssey* 1.284–285). Herodotus, who knew a part of the Iliad by its old name, the Aristeia of Diomedes, quotes two passages of this book only as “in the Odyssey” (*Histories* 2.116.3–5; see [[title-of-book-5]] and [[helen-in-egypt]]).

**Lacedaemon.** The title takes the place-name with which the book opens: “And they came to hollow Lacedaemon with its many ravines, / and drove on to the house of glorious Menelaus” (*Odyssey* 4.1–2). Athena had spoken of Sparta, and the book names the city too, when Menelaus brings home for his son “the daughter of Alector of Sparta” (*Odyssey* 4.10). When Telemachus tells his mother of the journey, he says that Menelaus asked him what need had brought him “to shining Lacedaemon” (Λακεδαίμονα δῖαν, *Odyssey* 17.121).

**What the title covers.** The book is at Lacedaemon from its first line to line 624: the double wedding in Menelaus' house, the welcome of the strangers, the evening of tears, Helen's drug and the two tales of Troy, the night, and on the next morning Telemachus' errand, Menelaus' long tale of Egypt and Proteus, the gifts and the banqueters coming to the king's house (*Odyssey* 4.1–624; see [[menelaus]] and [[helen]]). Then the poem turns to Ithaca, “But the suitors, in front of the hall of Odysseus, / amused themselves throwing the discus and the javelin” (*Odyssey* 4.625–626), and the last 223 lines stay there: Noemon's question about his ship, the suitors' plot, the herald Medon's news, Penelope's lament and prayer, the ship launched for the ambush, and the phantom that Athena sends to Penelope in a dream (*Odyssey* 4.625–847; see [[the-suitors]] and [[penelope]]). The scenes in Ithaca fall on the day of the drive from Pylos to Pherae, before the arrival at Sparta, though the poem tells them after it (see the translation's note on 4.655–656). The title names the first and longer part of the book.

**Left at Lacedaemon.** The book does not end Telemachus' stay. The poem leaves him in Menelaus' house and comes back to him only in Book 15, when Athena goes to Lacedaemon to remind him of his homecoming and finds him and Peisistratus asleep in the forecourt of Menelaus' house (*Odyssey* 15.1–5); the leave-taking and the gifts follow there, and the two drive out through the city at *Odyssey* 15.182–183.

**Told at Lacedaemon.** Much of what the book tells happened elsewhere: Menelaus' eight years of wandering to Cyprus, Phoenicia, Egypt and Libya (*Odyssey* 4.81–92), Helen's tale of Odysseus in Troy and Menelaus' of the Wooden Horse (*Odyssey* 4.240–289; see [[odysseus-enters-troy-as-a-beggar]] and [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]]), and the days on Pharos, the capture of Proteus and the Old Man's news of Ajax, Agamemnon and Odysseus (*Odyssey* 4.351–586; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]] and [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]). As in the book of Pylos, the title gives the setting of the tales, not their matter.

**Telemachus' own summary.** At home again, Telemachus tells his mother that Nestor sent him on to Menelaus with horses and a chariot, that he saw there Argive Helen, “for whose sake Argives and Trojans toiled much by the will of the gods”, and that Menelaus asked his errand (*Odyssey* 17.116–122, plain renderings). The rest of the summary is Menelaus' answer in the words of this book: the hind who lays her fawns to sleep in a lion's lair, the wish that Odysseus might come among the suitors as he was when he wrestled Philomeleides on Lesbos, and what “the unerring Old Man of the Sea” told him of Odysseus held by Calypso (*Odyssey* 17.124–141 = 4.333–350; 17.142–146 = 4.556–560, with the first verse turned into reported speech). Of Helen's drug, her tale and the gifts he says nothing.
