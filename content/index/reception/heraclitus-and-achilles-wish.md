**Homer.** The wish comes in Achilles' answer to Thetis, after he has chosen to kill Hector and die. It is a wish against his own anger: “so has Agamemnon, lord of men, angered me now” (18.111), and he will let the past be past (18.112–113; see [[cholos]] and [[agamemnon]]). Walter Leaf takes ὡς with the optative as an exclamation, “how I wish that strife might perish”, like ὡς ὄφελον (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.107). The strife Achilles means is the quarrel that began the poem (1.6–7, 1.177; see [[eris]]). Later in the book Strife appears in person on the Shield, in the throng of battle with Tumult and Doom (18.535; see [[strife]]).

**Aristotle.** In the *Eudemian Ethics* Aristotle sets out the opinions on friendship. Some hold that like is a friend to like, and quote the proverbs; others that opposites are friends, since the dry earth desires the rain, while like is hostile to like, as Hesiod's potter is angry with potter (*Works and Days* 25). “And Heraclitus rebukes the poet who wrote ‘may strife perish from among gods and men’; for there would be no harmony without high and low, nor living things without female and male, which are opposites” (*Eudemian Ethics* 7.1, 1235a25–29; see [[heraclitus-of-ephesus]] and [[aristotle]]). Aristotle quotes the verse with καὶ ἀνθρώπων for Homer's ἔκ τʼ ἀνθρώπων. In Heraclitus' answer the verse is no longer Achilles' regret but the poet's own prayer, and a prayer against the order of the world.

**Plutarch.** In *On Isis and Osiris*, arguing that the philosophers agree with the old belief in two opposed powers, Plutarch reports the same judgment. Heraclitus, he says, calls war outright “the father and king and lord of all”, and says that Homer, praying that strife might perish from among gods and men, “did not notice that he was cursing the birth of all things”, since all things have their birth from battle and opposition (*On Isis and Osiris* 48).

**Hesiod.** The Iliad has one Strife, who stirs up war. Hesiod had already found two on earth, one who fosters war and battle and another, better for men, who rouses even the idle to work by rivalry with a neighbor (*Works and Days* 11–24; see [[hesiods-two-strifes]]). Heraclitus' strife is neither: it is the opposition from which, he held, all things are born.
