summary: In the fifth book of the Aeneid Aeneas holds funeral games on the anniversary of his father's death, and Virgil builds them on the games for Patroclus. A ship race rounds a turning post on a rock, the leader of the footrace slips in the blood of the sacrifice, an old boxer beats a young boaster and is stopped by the giver of the games, and of the archers shooting at a dove tied to a mast one cuts the cord and the next brings the bird down. Like Achilles, Aeneas gives every man a prize and settles the disputes with gifts.

**The anniversary.** A year after Anchises died, Aeneas is driven back to Sicily, calls the Trojans together beside his father's mound and announces games for the ninth day: a race of ships, then a footrace, a contest with the javelin and light arrows, and boxing with the rawhide glove, and “let all be present and look for the rewards of the palm they have earned” (*Aeneid* 5.42–71). Acestes, their host in Sicily, gives two head of cattle to each ship, *bina boum … capita* (5.61–62), which Walter Leaf sets beside Homer's “strong heads of oxen” among Achilles' prizes (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.260). At the tomb Aeneas pours wine, milk and blood, greets his father's ashes and shade, and a great serpent glides out, tastes the offerings and goes back beneath the mound (5.72–103). On the ninth day the prizes are set out in the middle of the ring, sacred tripods, green crowns and palms, arms, garments dyed purple, and talents of silver and gold (5.104–113). Achilles' prizes are cauldrons and tripods, horses and mules, oxen, women and gray iron, with two talents of gold for the fourth place in the chariot race (23.259–270; see [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]] and [[contests]]).

**The ship race.** Virgil's first contest takes the place of the chariot race and keeps its turning post. Far out at sea there is a rock, and on it Aeneas sets up a green branch of oak as a post, “for the sailors to know where to turn back and round their long course” (*Aeneid* 5.124–131). The comparison with chariots is his own: not so headlong do the chariots pour from the barrier, nor do the drivers so shake the rippling reins over their teams (5.144–147); Leaf sets the reins beside the straps with which Homer's drivers strike their horses at the start (on 23.363). At the rock Gyas bids his helmsman hug the shore and let the blade graze the rocks on the left, *litus ama et laeva stringat sine palmula cautes* (5.162–163), as Nestor bids Antilochus lean a little to the left and let the left-hand horse press close to the post (23.334–340; see [[turning-post]] and [[nestors-lesson-in-driving]]). The helmsman, fearing hidden rocks, steers for the open sea, and Cloanthus slips through on the inside, between Gyas and the reef, and takes the lead (5.159–171). Sergestus, cutting in too close, runs onto the rock and breaks his oars (5.201–209), the danger that Nestor warns of: “avoid touching the stone, / lest you wound the horses and smash the chariot; / that would be a joy to the others, and a disgrace to yourself” (23.340–342). The prizes have their own histories, as Homer's do. The second captain gets a corselet of gold mail that Aeneas himself stripped from Demoleos by the Simois (5.258–265), as Achilles gives Eumelus the corselet he took from Asteropaeus (23.560–562; see [[asteropaeus]]); the third gets two cauldrons of bronze, *lebetas*, Homer's word, as Achilles' third prize is a cauldron (5.266; 23.267; see [[cauldron]]); and Sergestus, coming in last with his broken ship, is given a Cretan slave woman skilled in Minerva's work, with her twin sons (5.282–285), where Achilles' first prize is a woman skilled in flawless handiwork (23.263).

**The Georgics.** The comparison repeats the opening of a chariot race that Virgil had already described in the *Georgics*, in verses that Leaf cites on Homer's race: the chariots pour from the barrier and seize the plain, the drivers lean forward over the lash and give the reins, the axle glows, and “now low, now raised high, they seem to be borne through the empty air and to rise into the breezes”; a cloud of sand goes up, and they are wet with the foam and breath of those behind (*Georgics* 3.103–112; on 23.368 and 23.381). In Homer the chariots “at one moment skimmed the bountiful earth, / and at another bounded high in the air”, the dust rose like a cloud, and the breath of Diomedes' horses warmed Eumelus' back (23.365–381). The fear that drains the young drivers' throbbing hearts in the *Georgics* (3.105–106) drains the rowers' at the start of the ship race (*Aeneid* 5.137–138), where Homer's drivers stood in their chariots, “and the heart of each was pounding / as they strove for victory” (23.370–371).

**The footrace.** Nisus is far ahead, but near the end he slips where the blood of slaughtered bullocks has soaked the grass, and falls “in the filthy dung and the sacred blood”, *immundoque fimo sacroque cruore* (*Aeneid* 5.327–333). Homer's Ajax, son of Oileus, slipped near the end “where the dung was spread from the loud-bellowing oxen that were slain, / which swift-footed Achilles killed in honor of Patroclus” (23.773–777; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). Nisus, rising, trips Salius so that his friend Euryalus wins (5.334–339). Virgil gives the name of Homer's beaten boxer, Euryalus, to the winner of his race (23.677; see [[euryalus]] and [[nisus-and-euryalus]]). Salius fills the ring with shouts that the honor was taken from him by a trick (5.340–342), as Menelaus claims that Antilochus took the prize from him by a trick (23.566–585); Aeneas keeps the order of the prizes but gives Salius the hide of a lion (5.348–352), as Achilles, keeping Antilochus' prize, gives Eumelus a corselet besides (23.555–565). Then Nisus shows his face and his limbs foul with the wet dung, and “the best of fathers laughed at him”, and gives him a shield (*Aeneid* 5.353–361), as the Achaeans laugh at Ajax standing with the dung in his mouth (23.779–784). Aeneas had promised that no one would go away without a gift (5.305), and Leaf quotes his words beside Achilles' custom of calling up only as many men as there are prizes (on 23.707).

**The boxing.** Dares, who used to stand up to Paris and had felled the giant Butes at Hector's tomb, steps forward, and when no one comes against him takes the bull by the horn and claims the prize (5.368–385), as Epeius lays hold of the mule and claims to be the best, and all are silent (23.664–676; see [[epeius]]). Old Entellus, shamed by Acestes, answers that he would come if he had the youth he once had (5.394–400), as Nestor wishes at the games for Patroclus (23.629; see [[old-age-and-memory]]), and throws down the gloves of Eryx, seven ox-hides stiff with lead and iron (5.400–405); Homer's boxers wear “the well-cut straps of an ox of the fields” (23.684). Leaf takes the cracking of the cheeks under hard blows (*duro crepitant sub vulnere malae*, 5.436) for Virgil's understanding of the “terrible grinding of jaws” of 23.688 (on 23.688). Entellus falls, rises in anger and drives Dares over the field until Aeneas stops the fight (5.443–467), as Achilles stops the wrestling and the Achaeans the armed duel (23.734–737, 23.822–823). Dares' companions lead him to the ships, dragging his knees, tossing his head from side to side and spitting out thick blood and teeth mixed with it (5.468–472), as Euryalus' friends lead him through the gathering “with trailing feet, / spitting thick blood, his head lolling to one side” (23.695–697); Leaf sees in Virgil's line “the truly Roman spirit which can take pleasure in outdoing this line in gory details” (on 23.697). In Homer the boaster wins; in Virgil the old man beats the boaster, and kills the prize bull with one blow (5.473–484).

**The archery.** Aeneas raises the mast of Serestus' ship and hangs from it a dove by a cord, and the lots are cast into a bronze helmet (5.485–491), as Achilles sets up the mast of a dark-prowed ship with a dove tied to it, and Teucer and Meriones shake their lots in a helmet (23.852–862). The first arrow strikes the mast. Mnestheus cuts the cord, and the dove flies off into the clouds (5.502–512), as Teucer's arrow cuts the cord, “for Apollo begrudged him that” (23.865–869; see [[teucer]]). Eurytion, praying to his brother, sees the dove high under a black cloud and shoots it, and it falls dead with the arrow in it (5.513–518), as Meriones, vowing a hecatomb of firstborn lambs to Apollo, strikes her “high up under the clouds” (23.870–881; see [[meriones]]). Eurytion is the brother of Pandarus, who broke the truce (5.495–497); the vow that Meriones makes and Teucer forgets is the vow that Athena bids Pandarus make before he shoots Menelaus (4.101–103, 4.119–121; see [[pandarus]]). Leaf finds Homer's contest “confused and obscure”, above all the prize set beforehand for cutting the string, “the extreme of absurdity”, and says that Virgil copied the scene while avoiding that blot (on 23.850); in Homer the arrow passes through and falls at Meriones' feet while the bird flies to the mast and dies there, and “Virgil allows the bird to die outright” (on 23.875). Last, old Acestes shoots into the empty sky and his arrow catches fire, an omen; Aeneas gives him the first prize, a mixing bowl that the Thracian Cisseus once gave to Anchises (5.519–540; see [[cisseus]]). Achilles too gives a king a prize without a victory, when he hands Agamemnon the cauldron of the javelin (23.884–894; see [[agamemnon]]), and his silver mixing bowl for the footrace has a history of its own, from the Sidonians who made it to Euneus, who paid it for Lycaon (23.741–747; see [[mixing-bowl]]).

**What Virgil leaves out.** There is no chariot race, no wrestling, no armed duel and no throwing of the lump of iron, and the javelin, announced with the arrows, is not held (5.68). The games end with a display that Homer does not have, the Game of Troy, in which the boys ride in troops under Ascanius; Virgil says that Ascanius taught it to the Latins when he walled Alba Longa, and that Rome kept it as an ancestral honor (5.545–603).
