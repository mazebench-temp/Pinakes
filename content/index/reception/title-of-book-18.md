The traditional title of Book 18 is Ὁπλοποιία, “the Making of the Arms”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter, this one is Σ, sigma (see [[title-of-book-5]]). Thetis' visit to Hephaestus and the forging are told under [[the-making-of-the-arms]], and the Shield under [[the-shield-of-achilles]].

**The name.** The title is a compound of ὅπλα, “arms”, and ποιέω, “make”. It is not Homeric, and in later Greek it is a common word for the manufacture of arms: Strabo uses it of the Carthaginians, who in their last war with Rome set up an arms-making and turned out shields, swords and spears every day (*Geography* 17.3.15). In Homer ὅπλα is gear of any kind. In the Odyssey it is a ship's tackle (*Odyssey* 2.430, 12.410); in the Iliad it is the armor that Diomedes and Odysseus put on for their night raid (10.254, 10.272). In this book the word is used first of Hephaestus' tools, which he puts away when Thetis comes, “while I put away my bellows and all my tools” (18.409, 18.412–413), and then of his work: “And when the famous god, lame in both legs, had made all the armor” (πάνθʼ ὅπλα, 18.614). Achilles calls the gift by the same word when his mother brings it (19.21). The verb of the title is the narrator's for the Shield: ποίει, “And first he made a shield” (18.478), and ποίησε for the two cities, the herd and the pasture (18.490, 18.573, 18.587). Its partner is τεύχω, “On it he made the earth” (ἔτευξʼ, 18.483), the verb of the four lines that finish the shield and make the corselet, the helmet and the greaves (18.609–613).

**Strabo and Athenaeus.** The name is old. Strabo, arguing that Homer knew the earth to be surrounded by Oceanus, cites Hera's journey to the limits of the earth and to Oceanus (14.200–201) and adds: “and in the Hoplopoiia (ἐν τῇ ὁπλοποιίᾳ) of the shield of Achilles he sets Oceanus in a circle around it, on the rim” (*Geography* 1.1.7, on 18.607–608; see [[oceanus]] and [[strabo]]). About two centuries later Athenaeus uses it as the name of a part of the poem. Homer, he says, knew dancing to song: young men danced while Demodocus sang (*Odyssey* 8.262–264), “and in the Hoplopoiia”, while a boy plays the lyre, the others leap before him in song and dance (*Deipnosophistae* 1.15d–e, on 18.569–572). In the fifth book he argues that the singer and the two tumblers at Menelaus' feast in the Odyssey were brought there “from the Hoplopoiia”, from the Cretan dance of which the poet speaks “in the Hoplopoiia” (*Deipnosophistae* 5.180c–f; see [[the-singer-on-the-shield]] and [[athenaeus]]). Aelian's list of the parts of Homer once sung on their own names a Patrocleia, but nothing that answers to this book (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[aelian]] and [[title-of-book-16]]).

**What the title covers.** The title names the last third of the book, but the first two thirds prepare it. Thetis promises Achilles that she will come back at dawn, “bringing fine armor from lord Hephaestus” (18.136–137), and Achilles tells Iris that he cannot arm until she does (18.188–191). The old armor is the cause. Hector stripped it from Patroclus, “the splendid gifts that the gods gave to Peleus” on his wedding day, and wears it on his own shoulders (18.82–85, 18.130–133; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]]). The making begins when Thetis reaches the bronze house of Hephaestus and finds him at work on twenty tripods (18.369–379). After their talk he turns his twenty bellows to the fire and casts in bronze, tin, gold and silver (18.468–477). Then come the shield, of five layers, with its designs (18.478–608), the corselet, the helmet with its golden crest, and the greaves of soft tin (18.609–613). Thetis leaps down from Olympus with the armor like a hawk (18.614–617). The gift is delivered only at the start of the next book, where Achilles delights in it as he looks at the intricate work (19.3–19).

**What the title leaves out.** Antilochus brings the news, and Achilles lies in the dust (18.1–34; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). Thetis and the Nereids lament in the sea and come up to him (18.35–69; see [[nereids]]), and he chooses to kill Hector though his own death must follow (18.70–126; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). Iris rouses him, and he shouts three times from the ditch with Athena's flame about his head (18.165–231). Hera sends the sun down unwilling (18.239–242). The Trojans meet in assembly, where Polydamas, born on the same night as Hector, counsels retreat and Hector refuses (18.243–313; see [[polydamas]]). The Achaeans mourn Patroclus all night, and Zeus and Hera exchange words over the rousing of Achilles (18.314–368).

**The older readers.** The philosophers and orators who quote the book go mostly to its first part; the poets went to the Shield. Plato's Socrates cites Achilles' choice in his own defense, and Phaedrus in the *Symposium* makes it a lover's choice (*Apology* 28b–d; *Symposium* 179e–180a; see [[plato-the-choice-of-achilles]]). The *Republic* would strike out the sooty dust on Achilles' head and Thetis' lament (*Republic* 3.388a–c; see [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]]), and the *Philebus* quotes the anger “far sweeter than dripping honey” (*Philebus* 47e; see [[plato-the-pleasure-of-anger]]). Aristotle quotes the verse twice in the *Rhetoric*, and Hector's “Enyalius is impartial” as a common maxim (*Rhetoric* 1370b10–14, 1378b4–7, 1395a15; see [[sweeter-than-dripping-honey]] and [[aristotle-one-omen]]). He also reports that Heraclitus blamed the poet for Achilles' wish that strife might perish (*Eudemian Ethics* 7.1, 1235a25–29; see [[heraclitus-and-achilles-wish]]). Aeschines recited Achilles' lament for Patroclus in court, and had his vow and his answer to Thetis read out (*Against Timarchus* 141–150; see [[aeschines-on-achilles-and-patroclus]]).

The forging and the Shield had readers of their own. Aristotle explains the Bear that “alone” has no share in the baths of Oceanus, and cites the tripods of Hephaestus that go of themselves to the gods' assembly (*Poetics* 1461a20–21; *Politics* 1.4, 1253b33–1254a1; see [[alone-without-a-share]] and [[aristotle-the-tripods-of-hephaestus]]). Strabo finds geography in its Bear and its Oceanus (*Geography* 1.1.6–7). Walter Leaf reports that Zenodotus rejected the whole description, that Agallis of Corcyra read it as the early history of Attica, with Attica and Eleusis for the two cities, and that Maximus of Tyre took the cities for Phaeacia and Ithaca, types of good government and of anarchy (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.483). Pausanias lists among the works of Daedalus the dance of Ariadne at Cnossus, worked on white stone (*Description of Greece* 9.40.3; see [[pausanias-the-dance-of-ariadne]]). Other poets made shields of their own: the Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles*, which shares four verses with this one, and Virgil's shield of Aeneas (see [[shield-of-heracles]] and [[virgil-shield-of-aeneas]]). Ovid's Ulysses tells the judges of the arms that Ajax does not understand its carvings (*Metamorphoses* 13.291–294; see [[ovid-the-carvings-of-the-shield]]).
