The poem survives on a papyrus from Oxyrhynchus. It opens with a list of rejected answers:

> Some say an army of horsemen, some of foot soldiers,
> some of ships is the most beautiful thing on the black earth,
> but I say it is whatever one loves.

The verb for "loves", ἔραται, is the verb of [[eros|ἔρος]], the desire that seizes Paris in Book 3 (3.442, 3.446).

The claim is easy to make clear to everyone, Sappho goes on. Helen, who far surpassed all mortals in beauty, left her husband, the best of men, and went sailing to Troy, "and gave no thought at all to her child or her dear parents", but someone led her astray. The name is lost in a gap; editors usually restore Cypris, Aphrodite. The poem then turns to Anactoria, who is far away. Sappho would rather see her lovely walk and the bright sparkle of her face than the chariots of the Lydians and their soldiers in armor.

Helen is Sappho's proof that what one loves outweighs everything, even what is valued most. The details are close to Book 3, and turned the other way. When Iris calls Helen to the wall, the goddess puts in her heart "sweet longing for her former husband, and her city, and her parents" (3.139–140). To Priam, Helen wishes she had died before she followed his son, leaving her bridal chamber, her kinsmen, and her "darling daughter" (3.173–175). Sappho's Helen does not remember them at all (πάμπαν). The Iliad's Helen remembers, and regrets. Sappho does not condemn her: Helen is the example of what love can do.

Several words of the stanza on Helen are editors' restorations, among them "best" for her husband.
