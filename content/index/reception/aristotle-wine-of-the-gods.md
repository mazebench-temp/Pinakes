Book 5 explains the gods' ichor by what they eat and drink: “they do not eat bread, they do not drink sparkling wine” (5.341). Their drink is nectar (see [[nectar]]). But the verb Homer uses for serving it is οἰνοχοέω, “pour wine”. Hephaestus goes round the gods “drawing sweet nectar from the mixing bowl” (1.598), and Hebe pours nectar for them on Olympus (4.3); in both places the Greek verb is “pour as wine” (οἰνοχόει, ἐοινοχόει), which the English renders “poured”. And the gods carried off Ganymede, the most beautiful of mortals, to pour wine for Zeus (Διὶ οἰνοχοεύειν, 20.232–235; see [[ganymede]]).

Critics of Homer made a problem of it, and Aristotle answers them in the chapter of the *Poetics* on “problems and their solutions”. Some difficulties, he says, are solved by the custom of the language. People call the mixed drink “wine”, and so the poet can speak of a greave “of newly wrought tin” (21.592); they call those who work iron “bronze-smiths” (χαλκέας), and so Ganymede is said to “pour wine for Zeus”, though the gods do not drink wine. This case, he adds, could also be explained as a metaphor (*Poetics* 25, 1461a27–31).

The argument is typical of the chapter. The poet is not convicted of contradiction when the fault lies in the critic's reading of a word. A verb made from “wine” came to mean “serve drink”, as the smith kept the name of bronze after he worked in iron. The same chapter solves the problem of Apollo's shooting the mules first by the meaning of a rare word (see [[aristotle-mules]]).
