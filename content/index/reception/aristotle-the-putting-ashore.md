**The passage.** In the twenty-fourth chapter of the *Poetics* Aristotle compares epic with tragedy. Epic has more room for the irrational, the ἄλογον, through which the marvelous most of all comes about, “because we do not see the person acting” (1460a12–14); and the poet “should prefer probable impossibilities to unconvincing possibilities” (1460a26–27; see [[alogon]] and [[thaumaston]]). A story should not be made up of irrational parts. It should have nothing irrational, or failing that the irrational should lie outside the plot, as Oedipus' not knowing how Laius died, not in the drama, like the report of the Pythian games in the *Electra* or the man in the *Mysians* who comes without a word from Tegea to Mysia; and to say that the plot would have been ruined otherwise is ridiculous, for such plots should not be built in the first place (1460a27–34; see [[oedipus-kills-laius]], [[electra-play]] and [[mysians-play]]). The next sentence is corrupt, and the text marks it with daggers (1460a34–35). Then comes the Odyssey: “Even the irrational things in the Odyssey about Odysseus' being put ashore would clearly be intolerable, if a bad poet had made them; as it is, the poet conceals the absurdity, sweetening it with his other virtues” (τὰ ἐν Ὀδυσσείᾳ ἄλογα τὰ περὶ τὴν ἔκθεσιν, 1460a35–b2). The example breaks the rule just laid down. The homecoming is not outside the plot: in Aristotle's own outline of the Odyssey it belongs to the story proper, not to the episodes, “he himself arrives, storm-tossed, and after making himself known to certain people he attacks” (1455b21–22; see [[aristotle-the-story-of-the-odyssey]]). The irrational here stands inside the plot, and it is borne because the poet is a good one.

**The landing.** Aristotle names the scene, not its absurdities. The poem tells it at the beginning of *Odyssey* 13. The Phaeacians spread a rug and a linen sheet on the deck “so that he might sleep unwaking”, and as they row, a sleep falls on him “unwaking, sweetest, most like death” (*Odyssey* 13.73–80; see [[sweet-sleep]]). The ship runs through the night faster than a falcon, “bearing a man whose counsels were like the gods'”, and “now he slept motionless, forgetful of all he had suffered” (*Odyssey* 13.86–92). At dawn they row into the harbor of Phorcys, lift him out of the ship “linen sheet and glossy rug and all, / and laid him down on the sand, overcome by sleep”, set his gifts by the trunk of the olive, away from the road, so that no wayfarer should harm them “before Odysseus woke”, and go home (*Odyssey* 13.113–125; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]] and [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]). Odysseus sleeps through his own homecoming, and the ship that brought him is gone before he wakes.

**How the poem prepares it.** The sleep is announced long before it comes. When Alcinous fixes the day of the escort, he tells his guest that “then you, overcome by sleep, / will lie down, and they will row over the calm, until you come / to your native land and your house” (*Odyssey* 7.318–320), and Arete, when she has packed his gifts in a chest, tells him to tie the lid “so that no one may do you harm on the way, when hereafter / you sleep sweet sleep as you go in the black ship”, and he ties it with a knot that Circe had taught him (*Odyssey* 8.443–448; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). Alcinous has also told him that the Phaeacian ships need no helmsmen or steering oars, for “the ships themselves know the thoughts and minds of men” (*Odyssey* 8.557–559; see [[phaeacians]]). The poem gives the sleep a precedent and a meaning. On the voyage from Aeolus, within sight of the fires of Ithaca, “sweet sleep came upon me, worn out as I was” (*Odyssey* 10.31), and the companions opened the bag of winds (see [[odysseus-and-aeolus]]); this time the sleep is safe, and the poet makes it the rest of a man “who before had suffered very many sorrows in his heart” (*Odyssey* 13.90). The gods take notice of it. Poseidon's complaint is that the Phaeacians “carried him asleep in a swift ship over the sea / and set him down in Ithaca” (*Odyssey* 13.134–135), and his anger falls on the ship (see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]).

**Odysseus' own version.** When Odysseus, not yet knowing his land, lies to the disguised Athena, he gives himself the same landing. Phoenician sailors, he says, brought him by night into this harbor; “Then sweet sleep came upon me, worn out as I was, / and they took my goods out of the hollow ship / and set them down where I myself lay on the sands”, and they sailed for Sidon (*Odyssey* 13.282–286; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]). The first of the two verses is the verse of the sleep off Ithaca (*Odyssey* 10.31). The lie keeps the sleep and gives it a plain cause: a hard night at the oars, and men who came ashore with no thought of supper and lay down where they were (*Odyssey* 13.278–281; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]).

**The problem in the schools.** The scene became a set question. The *Divisions* handed down under Aristotle's name divide the problems of philosophy into political, dialectical, natural, ethical and rhetorical, and give as their example of the rhetorical the kind that arises “when someone accuses or defends the poets”: “why did the companions put Odysseus, most prudent as he was, ashore on the island asleep?” (διατί τὸν Ὀδυσσέα οἱ ἑταῖροι φρονιμώτατον ὄντα ἐξέθηκαν εἰς τὴν νῆσον καθεύδοντα, *Divisions* 2.42, plain renderings). The verb is the one from which Aristotle's noun is made. The “companions” are the poem's Phaeacian crew, “the noble escorts” (*Odyssey* 13.71); the collection gives the question and no answer. Its form, “why did …?”, is that of the Homeric problems, whose rules Aristotle sets out in the next chapter of the *Poetics*: the irrational is to be referred to what people say, or shown not to be irrational, “for it is probable that things should happen even contrary to probability”, and criticism of it is right only when the poet uses it without necessity (1461b14–15, 1461b19–21; see [[problems-and-solutions]]). Strabo, for his part, counts the sailing in of Odysseus to Ithaca, with the killing of the suitors and the battle in the country, among the things that a sensible reader will not insist happened in just the poet's way (*Geography* 1.2.11; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-13]]).
