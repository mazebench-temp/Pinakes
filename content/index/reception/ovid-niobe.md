**Ovid's story.** The sixth book of the *Metamorphoses* passes from Arachne, the Lydian weaver whom Pallas turned into a spider, to Niobe, who had known Arachne as a girl, “when as a maiden she lived in Maeonia and on Sipylus”, and was not warned by her countrywoman's punishment (*Metamorphoses* 6.146–151). Niobe has much to be proud of, her husband's art, the birth of both of them and the power of their kingdom, but most of all her children, “and Niobe would have been called the most fortunate of mothers, if she had not seemed so to herself” (*Metamorphoses* 6.152–156). Manto, the daughter of Tiresias, bids the women of Thebes honor Latona and her two children with incense and laurel (6.157–164). Niobe comes, splendid in Phrygian dress woven with gold, and asks why they worship a goddess they have only heard of. Her father is Tantalus, who alone was allowed to touch the table of the gods; her mother a sister of the Pleiades; Atlas is one grandfather and Jupiter the other, and Jupiter is her father-in-law as well; the Phrygians fear her, the house of Cadmus is hers, and the walls that her husband's lyre raised are ruled by her and him. “Add to this seven daughters, and as many sons” (*huc natas adice septem / et totidem iuvenes*, 6.182–183). Latona, to whom the earth once refused a place to give birth until floating Delos took her in, has borne two, “the seventh part of my womb”; let Fortune take many, she will leave more (*Metamorphoses* 6.165–203).

Latona complains to her twins on Cynthus, and Phoebus and Phoebe come down to the citadel of Cadmus (6.204–217). On the plain outside the walls, where the young men ride, Apollo's arrows kill the seven sons one by one, Ismenus, Sipylus, Phaedimus and Tantalus, Alphenor, Damasichthon and Ilioneus, the last as he prays to all the gods to spare him (6.218–266). Amphion drives a sword through his own breast (6.271–272). Niobe throws herself on the bodies, and taunts Latona even now: “after so many deaths I win!” (*Metamorphoses* 6.273–285). The bowstring sounds again, and the daughters, standing in black before their brothers' biers, fall one after another, until only the youngest is left, and the mother covers her with her whole body and cries, “leave me one, the youngest!” (*Metamorphoses* 6.286–301). She dies as her mother begs for her. Niobe sits bereft among her dead sons and daughters and her husband, and grows rigid with grief. The breeze stirs no hair, her face is bloodless, her eyes stand fixed, her tongue freezes to her palate, her veins cease to move, and within, her body is stone. “Yet she weeps, and wrapped in a whirlwind of strong wind she is carried off to her homeland; there, fixed on a mountain's top, she melts, and even now the marble drips tears” (*flet tamen et validi circumdata turbine venti / in patriam rapta est: ibi fixa cacumine montis / liquitur, et lacrimas etiam nunc marmora manant*, 6.301–312).

**Homer's story.** Achilles tells it in sixteen lines, to make Priam eat (24.602–617). His Niobe has no city, no husband and no father; she has six sons and six daughters, and her offense is that “she used to liken herself to fair-cheeked Leto; / she said that the goddess had borne two, while she herself had borne many” (24.607–608), the boast that Ovid's Niobe makes at length. Apollo kills the sons “with arrows from his silver bow”, and Artemis the daughters, as in Ovid (24.605–606; see [[apollo]] and [[artemis]]). What follows is Homer's own. The children lie nine days in their blood, with no one to bury them, “for the son of Cronus had turned the people to stones”, and the gods bury them on the tenth; “And she remembered food, when she was worn out with shedding tears” (24.610–613). Only then is she stone, on Sipylus, brooding over the sorrows the gods gave her (24.614–617; see [[the-weeping-rock-of-sipylus]]). Ovid turns her to stone in her grief, among the bodies, and leaves out the meal for which Achilles tells the story. His Thebes, Tantalus and Amphion are not in the Iliad; Homer's ἐνὶ μεγάροισιν, “in her halls” (24.603), is all the place he gives the killing. Ovid's seven and seven are the number that Walter Leaf gives to the tragedians, and he adds that Hesiod, Pindar and Mimnermus are said to have spoken of ten of each (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.602). Apollodorus names seven sons, among them a Sipylus, and seven daughters, and notes that Hesiod gave ten of each and Homer six (*Library* 3.5.6; see [[apollodorus]]).

**Leto and Niobe.** In Homer Niobe “used to liken herself” to Leto, ἰσάσκετο, a verb found only here (24.607). Leaf notes the legend that the two were once close friends, Niobe, like her father Tantalus, having been admitted to the company of the immortals, and quotes a line of Sappho: “Leto and Niobe were very dear companions” (Λατὼ καὶ Νιόβα μάλα μὲν φίλαι ἦσαν ἔταιραι, fr. 31 Bergk; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.607; see [[sappho]] and [[leto]]). Ovid's Niobe calls Latona a goddess “heard of”, whom the Thebans set above the deity they can see (*Metamorphoses* 6.170–172), and the granddaughter of Atlas scorns the daughter of the Titan Coeus for having once been a wanderer with nowhere to give birth (*Metamorphoses* 6.185–191).
