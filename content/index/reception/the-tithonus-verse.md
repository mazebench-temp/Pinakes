**The verse.** Nestor ends his complaint against Achilles with his own weakness: “For my strength / is not such as it was before in my supple limbs” (οὐ γὰρ ἐμὴ ἲς / ἔσθʼ οἵη πάρος ἔσκεν ἐνὶ γναμπτοῖσι μέλεσσιν, 11.668–669). Then comes the wish with which he begins his story of the Epeian war, “If only I were young like that, and my strength were steadfast” (11.670). He spoke the same verse at the end of his story of Ereuthalion, and will speak it again to begin his story of the funeral games at Buprasium (7.157, 23.629; see [[nestor]], [[nestor-and-the-epeians]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]).

γναμπτός is “bent, bending”, said of limbs that flex at the joints; the translation has “supple” (see [[supple]]). The phrase ἐνὶ γναμπτοῖσι μέλεσσι, “in the supple limbs”, occurs six times in Homer, and always where age or death has touched the body. It is used of Nestor here, and of Priam, whose hair stands on end in his supple limbs when Hermes meets him on the plain (24.359). In the Odyssey the shade of Agamemnon has “no longer firm strength or vigor, such as there was before in his supple limbs” (Od. 11.393–394). Athena withers the fair skin on Odysseus' supple limbs to make him an old man (Od. 13.398, 13.430), and in that guise he asks the suitors to let him try the bow, to see “whether I still have strength, such as there was before in my supple limbs”, or whether his wanderings have destroyed it (Od. 21.282–284).

**Tithonus.** The Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite gives the whole verse to Tithonus. Aphrodite tells Anchises how Dawn carried Tithonus off and asked Zeus that he be immortal and live for all time, and Zeus granted it; but Dawn did not think to ask for youth. While youth held him, he lived with her by the streams of Oceanus at the ends of the earth. When the first gray hairs came, she kept away from his bed but still tended him in her halls; and when hateful old age pressed on him and he could not move or lift his limbs, she laid him in a chamber and shut the shining doors. “His voice flows on endlessly, but there is no vigor, / such as there was before in his supple limbs” (οὐδέ τι κῖκυς / ἔσθʼ, οἵη πάρος ἔσκεν ἐνὶ γναμπτοῖσι μέλεσσιν, *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* 218–238). The verse is Nestor's, with κῖκυς, “vigor”, where he has ἲς, “strength”; the line on Agamemnon's shade has both nouns. Aphrodite draws the lesson for Anchises: she would not choose to have him immortal on such terms (239–246; see [[aphrodite-and-anchises]], [[immortal-and-ageless]] and [[tithonus]]).

**In Book 11.** The book opens with the same couple. “Now Dawn rose from her bed beside noble Tithonus, / to bring light to the immortals and to mortals” (11.1–2), the verses that open the fifth book of the Odyssey (Od. 5.1–2; see [[eos]]). The Iliad tells nothing of Tithonus' fate; it names him only here and in Aeneas' account of his family, as a son of Laomedon and a brother of Priam (20.237; see [[laomedon]]). The day that rises from his bed is the longest in the poem, and in the middle of it the oldest man in the army, wishing he were young, says of his own body what the Hymn says of Tithonus (see [[the-third-day-of-battle]]). Whether the Hymn took the verse from the Iliad or both drew on a common stock of epic verse cannot be told; the Odyssey's two uses show that it was not Nestor's alone.
