In the *Ars Poetica*, Horace contrasts Homer with poets who begin at the very beginning. Homer does not start the return of Diomedes from the death of Meleager, nor the Trojan War "from the twin egg", the egg of Leda from which Helen was born. He "always hurries to the outcome, and snatches the listener into the middle of things (in medias res), as if they were known". He leaves out what he despairs of making shine in the telling, and so mixes the false with the true that the middle does not clash with the beginning, nor the end with the middle (*Ars Poetica* 146–152).

The praise follows directly on Horace's Latin version of the Odyssey's opening, *Dic mihi, Musa, virum*, which he sets against the cyclic poet's boast, “Of the fortune of Priam I will sing, and the noble war”: Homer means to bring not smoke from a flash but light from smoke, and keeps his wonders, Antiphates and Scylla, the Cyclops and Charybdis, for later (136–145; see [[horace-on-the-opening-of-the-odyssey]]). The rule fits both poems, and the notes below show how each begins.
