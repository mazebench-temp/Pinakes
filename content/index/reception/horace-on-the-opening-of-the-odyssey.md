**The Ars Poetica.** Horace is advising the Pisos how to handle a story that others have told before. Do not begin, he says, as the cyclic writer once did: “Of the fortune of Priam I will sing, and the noble war.” What will this promiser bring worthy of such a gaping mouth? “The mountains will labor, and a ridiculous mouse will be born” (*Ars Poetica* 136–139). How much better the poet who undertakes nothing foolishly:

> Dic mihi, Musa, virum, captae post tempora Troiae
> qui mores hominum multorum vidit et urbes.

“Tell me, Muse, of the man who, after the time of Troy's capture, saw the ways and the cities of many men” (140–142). He means to give not smoke from a flash, but light from smoke, so that afterwards he may bring out his striking wonders, “Antiphates and Scylla, and with the Cyclops Charybdis” (*non fumum ex fulgore, sed ex fumo dare lucem / cogitat, ut speciosa dehinc miracula promat*, 143–145). He does not begin the return of Diomedes from the death of Meleager, nor the Trojan War from the twin egg; he always hurries to the outcome and snatches the listener into the middle of things, as if they were known, and leaves out what he despairs of making shine in the telling; and so he invents, so he mixes false with true, that the middle does not clash with the beginning, nor the end with the middle (146–152; see [[in-medias-res]]).

Horace's two lines render the first three of the Greek. The request, the Muse and the man open the line, as in Homer; *captae post tempora Troiae* gives the sack of Troy (*Odyssey* 1.2), and *qui mores hominum multorum vidit et urbes* the cities and the mind of many men, with *mores*, “ways”, for νόον (*Odyssey* 1.3). There is no word for πολύτροπον, “of many turns”, nor for the wanderings, “who was driven / far astray” (*Odyssey* 1.1–2), nor for the sufferings at sea and the lost companions (*Odyssey* 1.4–9). The wonders that Horace says are to come, the Laestrygonians' king Antiphates, the Cyclops, Scylla and Charybdis, are all in the tale that Odysseus himself tells the Phaeacians in Books 9 to 12 (*Odyssey* 9.106, 10.106, 12.85, 12.104).

**The Epistles.** In the second letter of his first book of *Epistles* Horace tells Lollius Maximus that he has been rereading “the writer of the Trojan War” at Praeneste, who says what is beautiful, what is base, what is useful and what is not, more fully and better than the philosophers Chrysippus and Crantor (*Epistles* 1.2.1–4). The Iliad shows the folly of kings and peoples (1.2.6–16; see [[horace]]). The Odyssey shows the other side:

> Rursus, quid virtus et quid sapientia possit,
> utile proposuit nobis exemplar Ulixen,
> qui domitor Troiae multorum providus urbes
> et mores hominum inspexit, latumque per aequor,
> dum sibi, dum sociis reditum parat, aspera multa
> pertulit, adversis rerum immersabilis undis.

“Again, of what virtue and wisdom can do he has set before us a useful example in Ulysses, who, the tamer of Troy, looked with foresight on the cities and the ways of many men, and on the wide sea, while he was winning a return for himself and his companions, endured many hardships, never sunk by the waves of adversity” (1.2.17–22). This is the Odyssey's opening again, closer this time: the sack of Troy, the cities and the ways of men, the sea, and the return of himself and his companions (*Odyssey* 1.2–5). Horace goes on to the Sirens' voices and Circe's cups, which Ulysses resisted and his companions did not (1.2.23–26), and then turns on his own kind: “We are mere numbers, born to consume the fruits of the earth, the suitors of Penelope, good-for-nothings, and the young men of Alcinous, busier than was right in caring for their skin, who thought it fine to sleep till the middle of the day and to lull their cares to rest to the sound of the lyre” (*Nos numerus sumus et fruges consumere nati, / sponsi Penelopae nebulones Alcinoique …*, 1.2.27–31).

Livius Andronicus had put the same first line into Latin long before, as *Virum mihi, Camena, insece versutum* (see [[odusia]]).
