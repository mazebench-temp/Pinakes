To show that the Greeks did nothing in common before the Trojan War, Thucydides turns to their name. Hellen and his sons, he says, became powerful in Phthiotis, and their name spread from city to city, but slowly, and it took a long time to prevail over all (1.3.2).

"Homer is the best evidence," he goes on. "Though he lived long after the Trojan War, he nowhere uses the name for all of them, nor for anyone except the men who came with Achilles from Phthiotis, who were in fact the first Hellenes. In his poems he calls them Danaans, Argives and Achaeans. Nor does he speak of barbarians, because, as I think, the Hellenes had not yet been set apart under one name as their opposite" (1.3.3).

The Catalogue bears him out in the main. Hellas and the Hellenes belong to Achilles' kingdom (2.683–684), and the army as a whole is always the Achaeans, Argives or Danaans. But it also marks the edges of his claim. The Locrian Ajax "surpassed all the Panhellenes and Achaeans" with the spear (2.530), a phrase that seems to mean the Greeks at large. And the Carians are βαρβαρόφωνοι, "of barbarous speech" (2.867), the only place in Homer where the root of "barbarian" appears. Homer knows that some peoples speak strangely; what he lacks is the later opposition of Greek and barbarian.
