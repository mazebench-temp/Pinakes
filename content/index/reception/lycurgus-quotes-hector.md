**Homer.** Zeus has broken Teucer's bowstring as he drew it against Hector, and Hector, who has seen “the missiles of their best man foiled by Zeus” (15.489), calls to the Trojans, Lycians and Dardanians. The help that comes from Zeus is easy to see, he tells them, in those to whom he gives glory and in those whom he diminishes, “as now he diminishes the might of the Argives, and helps us” (15.493; see [[the-help-of-zeus-is-easy-to-see]]). So let them fight at the ships in a body. Whoever is struck and dies, let him die: “it is not unseemly for him, fighting in defense of his native land, / to die” (15.496–497), for his wife and children, his house and his allotment will be safe if the Achaeans sail home (see [[it-is-not-unseemly-to-die-for-ones-country]] and [[hector]]). On the other side Ajax answers with an exhortation of his own (15.501–513; see [[exhortation]]).

**Lycurgus.** The Athenian orator Lycurgus prosecuted Leocrates, who had fled the city after the defeat at Chaeronea, when the people had voted to bring the women and children within the walls (*Against Leocrates* 16–17; see [[lycurgus-of-athens]]). Later in the speech he calls the poets as witnesses of what a citizen owes his country. After Euripides' *Erechtheus*, in which the king's wife gives her daughter to be killed for the city (98–101), he turns to Homer: “I wish to bring Homer before you too, with praise. Your fathers held him so serious a poet that they made a law that at every Great Panathenaea his epics alone of all the poets should be recited by rhapsodes, showing the Greeks that they chose the finest of deeds. And with reason: the laws, because they are brief, do not teach but command what must be done, while the poets, imitating human life and choosing the finest deeds, persuade men with reasoning and demonstration” (102). “For Hector, exhorting the Trojans on behalf of their country, has said this”, and he recites the six verses (103). Hearing these verses, he goes on, the jurors' ancestors so emulated such deeds that they were ready to die not only for their own country but for all Greece as their common country, and at Marathon they defeated the host of all Asia (104). The Spartan poet comes next: Tyrtaeus, whose elegies, he says, the Spartans by law gathered to hear at their king's tent whenever they took the field, beginning “For it is a fine thing to die, fallen among the front-fighters, a good man fighting for his native land” (105–107).

**His text.** Lycurgus' verses differ from ours in three places. He has “fight at the ships without a break” (διαμπερές) for “in a body” (ἀολλέες, 15.494); “his little children” (νήπια τέκνα, the phrase of 2.136 and 4.238) for “his children after him” (παῖδες ὀπίσσω, 15.497); and “his allotment and his house” for “his house and his allotment” (15.498).

**The lesson.** Lycurgus makes a Trojan the model for Athenian citizens, without remark. What he takes from the verses is what Hector offers his men: a death that is not unseemly, and a household kept safe by it. Tyrtaeus' couplet, quoted next, says it in nearly the same words, τεθνάμεναι … περὶ ᾗ πατρίδι μαρνάμενον, “to die … fighting for his native land”, as Hector's ἀμυνομένῳ περὶ πάτρης τεθνάμεν. Hector had put the thought in a single verse in Book 12: “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (12.243; see [[one-omen-is-best]]).

The law of the Panathenaea that Lycurgus cites is reported under [[the-robe-and-the-panathenaea]] and [[rhapsody]].
