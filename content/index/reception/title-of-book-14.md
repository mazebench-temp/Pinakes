The traditional title of Book 14 is Διὸς ἀπάτη, “the Deception of Zeus”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter, this one is Ξ, xi (see [[title-of-book-5]]). The book's story is told under [[the-deception-of-zeus]].

**The words.** Like “the curtailed battle” of Book 8, the title is made of Homer's own words (see [[title-of-book-8]]). ἀπάτη is “deceit, trickery”, and the genitive names the one deceived (see [[apate]]). Elsewhere in the Iliad the deceiver is Zeus: Agamemnon complains that he “has devised an evil deceit” (κακὴν ἀπάτην, 2.114 = 9.21). The noun is not used in Book 14, but the deed is named at both ends of the deception with verbs of beguiling. Hera ponders “how she might beguile the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus” (ὅππως ἐξαπάφοιτο Διὸς νόον, 14.160), and Sleep tells Poseidon that “Hera has beguiled him into going to bed with her in love” (παρήπαφεν, 14.360). The noun comes in the next book, from Zeus himself. Waking on Ida beside Hera and seeing Hector lying on the plain, he calls her trick evil-working, reminds her how he punished her for the storm she raised against Heracles, and says that he will remind her of it so that she may cease from her deceits (ἀπατάων) and see whether the love and the bed in which she lay with him, and deceived him (ἀπάτησας), will help her (15.4–33). The title gives the episode the name that its victim gives it.

**The older readers.** The ancient readers who discuss the scene describe it without the name. Plato's Socrates speaks of Zeus forgetting all his plans for desire of Hera (*Republic* 3.390b–c; see [[plato-zeus-deceived]]). Plutarch speaks of “Hera's beautifying of herself for Zeus” and the enchantments of the strap (*How the Young Man Should Study Poetry* 19f; see [[plutarch]]). Proclus heads his chapter on it “what the union of Zeus with Hera means”, and asks among other things what is “the manner of the deception (τῆς ἀπάτης) with which the greatest goddess is said in the myth to deceive him” (*On the Republic* I 132–133 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). None of them uses the title.

**What the title covers.** The deception fills the middle of the book, from Hera's look down from Olympus to Sleep's message to Poseidon (14.153–360). Before it come the wounded kings. Nestor leaves his wine to see the wall thrown down; Agamemnon proposes to launch the ships and flee by night; Odysseus rebukes him; Diomedes urges them back to the battle, wounded as they are; and Poseidon, in the likeness of an old man, puts strength into the Achaeans with a shout like that of ten thousand men (14.1–152; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]] and [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). After it comes what it was for. Poseidon leads the Argives openly with his terrible sword, Ajax fells Hector with a stone, his companions carry him to the ford of the Xanthus, and the Trojans are routed; the book ends with the swift son of Oileus, who killed the most in the pursuit (14.361–522; see [[hector]], [[ajax]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). The title names the cause and leaves out its effect.

**More deceptions.** The book holds more deceit than its title names. Hera lies to Aphrodite to get the strap, with a tale of a journey to end the quarrel of Oceanus and Tethys, and tells the tale again to Zeus (14.190–223, 14.300–311; see [[aphrodites-band]] and [[oceanus]]). Sleep remembers an earlier deception of Zeus, when he lulled him so that Hera could drive Heracles over the sea to Cos (14.249–262; see [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]]). In Book 19 Agamemnon tells of a third, on the day of Heracles' birth, when Hera deceived Zeus with her wiles (δολοφροσύνῃς ἀπάτησεν, 19.97). Each time the deceiver is Hera.

As with the battle at the ships, the title names the action of one book, and its consequences run into the next, where Zeus wakes and sets out his plan to the fall of Ilios (15.4–77; see [[title-of-book-13]], [[title-of-book-15]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).
