The traditional title of Book 15 is Παλίωξις παρὰ τῶν νεῶν, “the counterattack from the ships”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter, this one is Ο, omicron (see [[title-of-book-5]]). The book's fighting belongs to [[the-battle-at-the-ships]] and [[the-third-day-of-battle]], and Zeus' design for it to the [[plan-of-zeus]].

**The words.** Like “the Deception of Zeus”, the title is made of Homer's own words (see [[title-of-book-14]]). παλίωξις is formed from πάλιν, “back”, and the root of ἰωκή, “pursuit, onslaught”, a word that Homer uses of the Trojans' onslaughts (5.521) and makes a figure on the aegis (5.740; see [[onslaught]]). A Byzantine lexicon defines it as “pursuit after a turn, when those who are fleeing turn about and pursue” (Suda π 110). The Iliad has the word three times, and each time it is the Achaeans who are to turn. Polydamas fears it when he advises the Trojans not to drive their chariots into the ditch: “but if they turn back, and a counterattack comes / from the ships” (12.71–72), the Trojans will be caught in the ditch, and not even a messenger will get back to the city. Zeus promises it to Hera when he wakes on Ida: Patroclus will kill Sarpedon, Hector will kill Patroclus and Achilles Hector, and “From then on I would bring about a counterattack from the ships, / always and without a break, until the Achaeans / take steep Ilios through the counsels of Athena” (15.69–71). Late in the book the narrator says it again: “For from then on he was going to bring about a counterattack / against the Trojans from the ships” (15.601–602). The title keeps Homer's παρά with the genitive, “from beside”, and gives the ships the article and the Attic form, τῶν νεῶν for νηῶν, as the title of Book 13 gives them the article (see [[title-of-book-13]]). Outside the Iliad the word is a figure on the shield of Heracles in the Hesiodic poem, where Pursuit and Counter-pursuit (Προΐωξίς τε Παλίωξίς τε) are wrought beside Tumult, Rout and Slaughter (*Shield* 154–155; see [[hesiod]]).

**What the title names.** The title names what the book foretells, not what it tells. The narrator says why Zeus drives Hector on: he wished to give him glory, so that he might cast fire on the ships and fulfill Thetis' prayer in full, “for Zeus the counselor was waiting for this, / to see with his eyes the blaze of a burning ship” (15.599–600; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). The counterattack was to follow the fire. In Book 15 no ship burns. Hector takes hold of the stern of the ship that brought Protesilaus to Troy and calls for fire, and Ajax, driven from the deck to the seven-foot bench, keeps off with his spear every Trojan who brings it, “and twelve he stabbed hand to hand in front of the ships” (15.704–746; see [[protesilaus]], [[stern]] and [[ajax]]). The fire falls on the ship early in the next book, when Hector has cut off the head of Ajax's spear (16.112–124). Then the counterattack begins: Patroclus, in Achilles' armor, makes his first cast by the stern of Protesilaus' ship, drives the Trojans from the ships and puts out the fire, and the ship is left half-burned (16.284–294; see [[patroclus]]). The counterattack from the ships is the action of Book 16.

**The turn in Book 15.** The book has a turn of its own, in the other direction. At the end of Book 14 the Trojans are routed, and at the start of Book 15 they have crossed the stakes and the ditch “in their flight” and halt beside their chariots, “pale with fear” (15.1–4). Then Zeus wakes, Apollo puts might into Hector and goes before the Trojans with the aegis, and the fugitives turn on their pursuers, drive them back over the ditch and the wall, and fight them at the sterns of their ships (15.220–389; see [[apollo]], [[hector]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). By the lexicon's definition the Trojans' turn is a παλίωξις too, but it runs toward the ships, not from them, and the poem never gives the word to the Trojans. The book that bears the title tells the Trojans' rally; the Achaeans' is foretold in it.

**The older readers.** The ancient writers who use the book do not name it. Plato opens the myth of the *Gorgias* with Poseidon's division of the world, “as Homer says” (523a; see [[plato]]). Lycurgus introduces Hector's exhortation at the ships as what Hector said “exhorting the Trojans on behalf of their country” (*Against Leocrates* 103; see [[lycurgus-quotes-hector]]). Aristotle quotes two of its verses, without saying where they stand, among Homer's metaphors that make lifeless things alive (*Rhetoric* 3.11, 1412a1–2; see [[homers-lifeless-things-alive]]). Strabo quotes its lines on Otus of Cyllene, on Meges' corselet from Ephyra and on Melanippus' herds at Percote (*Geography* 8.3.4–5, 10.2.14, 13.1.7; see [[strabo]]). Aelian's list of the parts once sung on their own has “the battle at the ships” (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14, as reported under [[title-of-book-13]]), a name that suits this book, where the Trojans first bring fire to the ships and lay hold of one (15.419–420, 15.704–705).

**What it leaves out.** The title passes over the gods, who fill the first half of the book: Zeus' threat to Hera and the tale of her hanging (15.4–33; see [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]]), Ares' grief for his son and Athena's restraint (15.100–142), Poseidon's protest that the world was divided by lot among three brothers (15.184–217; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]), and Apollo, who bridges the ditch and throws down the wall “very easily, as when a boy beside the sea throws down the sand” (15.355–364). It passes over the two men who carry the book's end: Hector, whom Zeus honored because he was to be short-lived (15.610–614), and Ajax on the decks with his great spear for fighting at sea (15.674–746).

As with the titles of the two books before it, the title names one moment of a longer action. Zeus foretells the counterattack in this book, and it comes in the next; the battle at the ships runs from Book 13 into Book 16 (see [[title-of-book-13]] and [[title-of-book-14]]).
