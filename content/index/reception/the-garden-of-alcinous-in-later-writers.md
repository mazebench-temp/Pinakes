summary: The orchard of Alcinous, whose fruit never fails in winter or in summer, became for the Latin poets the type of the rich man's orchard and of a year that is always autumn. Virgil names “the woods of Alcinous” among the kinds of fruit trees, and Propertius' orchards do not equal the Phaeacian woods; Ovid and Martial use fruit for Alcinous as a proverb for a needless gift; Martial and Statius set their friends' gardens above his; and Juvenal's rich patron eats fruit “such as the perpetual autumn of the Phaeacians had”.

**Homer's garden.** Outside the court of Alcinous, near the doors, is an orchard of four acres with a fence around it. The fruit of its pears, pomegranates, apples, figs and olives never perishes or fails, in winter or in summer, for the West Wind always brings some to growth and ripens others; in the vineyard some grapes are drying in the sun while others are gathered and others trodden, and in front are the unripe ones; there are garden beds of every kind, and two springs, one for the garden and one that runs under the threshold of the court, from which the citizens draw water. “Such were the splendid gifts of the gods at the house of Alcinous” (*Odyssey* 7.112–132; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]]). Odysseus stands and gazes at it all before he steps over the threshold (*Odyssey* 7.133–135; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-7]]).

**The Phaeacian woods.** Virgil, telling how trees of one name are not all of one kind, says that the olives grow in more than one shape, and so do “apples and the woods of Alcinous” (*pomaque et Alcinoi silvae*), and that the cutting is not the same for every pear (*Georgics* 2.83–88; plain renderings of the Latin throughout): the king's orchard stands for the orchard trees themselves. For Propertius it is a rich man's possession. His house does not rest on columns of Taenarian marble, it has no ivory vault among gilded beams, “nor do my orchards equal the Phaeacian woods” (*nec mea Phaeacas aequant pomaria silvas*), and no Marcian water washes his grottoes; but the Muses are his companions (*Elegies* 3.2.11–16).

**Fruit for Alcinous.** Ovid tells a girl that it is no shame to ask gifts of a rich man, who has what he can give: “Pick the grapes hanging from full vines; let the kindly field of Alcinous furnish its fruit!” (*carpite de plenis pendentes vitibus uvas; / praebeat Alcinoi poma benignus ager!*, *Amores* 1.10.53–56). In exile he excuses himself for sending no verses to the poet Severus with a list of needless gifts: “Who would give honey to Aristaeus, Falernian wine to Bacchus, grain to Triptolemus, fruit to Alcinous?” (*Quis mel Aristaeo, quis Baccho vina Falerna, / Triptolemo fruges, poma det Alcinoo?*, *Letters from Pontus* 4.2.9–10); to send a poem to such a man “was to add leaves to the woods” (*Letters from Pontus* 4.2.13). Martial turns the proverb round. Castricus outdoes him in gifts and in verse, and Martial is content to be beaten; why then has he sent such poor poems? “Do you think no one has given fruit to Alcinous?” (*Alcinoo nullum poma dedisse putas?*, *Epigrams* 7.42.5–6).

**Better than Alcinous.** Martial also measures other gardens by the king's. Entellus keeps his vines under transparent stone, so that the grape is covered and not hidden, and “whoever has seen the orchards of the Corcyraean king will prefer your house's country place”; “barren winter is bidden to bear autumn” (*Epigrams* 8.68.1–2, 8.68.10). The king of Corcyra is Alcinous, for Corcyra was taken to be Scheria (see [[scheria]]). He ends his praise of the house and “small kingdom” that Marcella gave him when he came home after thirty-five years, with its grove and springs, the shade of a trailing vine, a channel of water, rose beds and greens that do not freeze in January: “If Nausicaa offered me her father's gardens, I could say to Alcinous, ‘I prefer my own’” (*Si mihi Nausicaa patrios concederet hortos, / Alcinoo possem dicere 'Malo meos'*, *Epigrams* 12.31.9–10). His own orchards, by contrast, are guarded by no serpent and served by no “royal field of Alcinous”, and the waxen fruit of “my autumn” that he sends was “born just now in the middle of the Subura”, a crowded quarter of Rome (*Epigrams* 10.94.1–6). Statius, describing the villa of Manilius Vopiscus at Tibur, asks, “Why should I praise the twice-bearing orchards of Alcinous, and you, branches that never rose empty into the air?”, and bids the lands of Telegonus, of Turnus, of bloody Antiphates and of Circe yield to it (*quid bifera Alcinoi laudem pomaria vosque, / qui numquam vacui prodistis in aethera, rami?*, *Silvae* 1.3.81–85).

**The perpetual autumn.** Juvenal's rich Virro has fruit served to himself and his like “whose scent alone would feed you, such as the perpetual autumn of the Phaeacians had, which you could believe stolen from the African sisters”, the Hesperides, while his poor guest gets a scabby apple (*qualia perpetuus Phaeacum autumnus habebat*, *Satires* 5.149–153). Juvenal's endless autumn, Statius' orchards that bear twice and Martial's winter bidden to bear autumn all take up the line that sets Homer's garden apart, fruit that never fails, “in winter or in summer, lasting all the year” (*Odyssey* 7.118).
