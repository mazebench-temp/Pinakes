**The episode.** While Aeneas is away seeking allies, Turnus' Rutulians besiege the Trojan camp. Nisus, a sentry at one of its gates, and his young friend Euryalus, the most beautiful of Aeneas' men, are bound by love: “one love was theirs” (*Aeneid* 9.176–183). Nisus sees that the enemy's fires are few and the Rutulians sunk in sleep and wine, and resolves to get through to Aeneas; Euryalus insists on coming (*Aeneid* 9.184–223). The Trojan leaders, holding council in the night, accept the plan and promise rewards (*Aeneid* 9.224–313). The two go out, kill many sleeping Rutulians, take spoils, and leave (*Aeneid* 9.314–366). On their way a troop of Latin horsemen sees the gleam of a helmet Euryalus has taken, and he is caught in the wood; Nisus kills two of the horsemen with spears and dies avenging his friend (*Aeneid* 9.367–445). The Rutulians carry their heads on spears, and Euryalus' mother laments on the wall (*Aeneid* 9.450–502).

**The model.** Virgil follows the Doloneia at almost every step.

- *The volunteers.* Diomedes goes because “my heart and my proud spirit urge me” (10.220); Nisus wonders whether the gods put this ardor in men's minds, or each man's fierce desire becomes his god (*Aeneid* 9.184–185). Diomedes asks for a companion (10.222–226; see [[when-two-go-together]]); Euryalus will not let Nisus go alone (*Aeneid* 9.199–206).
- *The council.* The Trojan chiefs sit in council while all other creatures sleep (*Aeneid* 9.224–228), as the Achaean kings sit beyond the ditch (10.194–202), and an old man, Aletes, “heavy with years and ripe in mind”, praises the young men (*Aeneid* 9.246–256), where Nestor calls for them.
- *The prizes.* Nestor promises fame and a black ewe from every chief (10.211–217). Ascanius promises silver cups, tripods, two talents of gold and a mixing bowl that Dido gave, and, if Italy is won, the horse and the armor of Turnus himself, twelve women and captives, and the fields of King Latinus (*Aeneid* 9.257–274). The enemy leader's horse as a prize recalls Dolon, who asked for the horses of Achilles (10.319–323; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]).
- *The arming.* Ascanius gives Euryalus a gilded sword; Mnestheus gives Nisus the skin of a shaggy lion, and Aletes exchanges helmets with him (*Aeneid* 9.303–307). The Achaeans too are armed by others, Diomedes by Thrasymedes and Odysseus by Meriones, who gives him the helmet of boar's tusks (10.255–271; see [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]]), and Diomedes has already put on a lion's skin (10.177–178).
- *The sleeping camp.* The Rutulians lie in the grass among their chariots and arms, overcome by sleep and wine (*Aeneid* 9.316–319), as the Thracians sleep worn out beside their armor and their horses (10.471–475). Nisus kills first a king who was also an augur, Rhamnes, snoring on piled rugs, whose augury could not save him (*Aeneid* 9.324–328); the Thracian king dies “as he lay gasping” (10.496). Virgil's simile is Homer's: Nisus is like a starving lion in full sheepfolds (*Aeneid* 9.339–341), as Diomedes falls on the Thracians like a lion on flocks with no herdsman (10.485–488).
- *The halt.* Euryalus is making for the men of Messapus, where the horses stand tethered and grazing (*Aeneid* 9.351–353). But Nisus, who “felt that they were carried away by too much slaughter and greed”, calls a halt, for hostile daylight is near (*Aeneid* 9.353–356). In Homer it is Athena who calls Diomedes away, while he ponders whether to take the chariot or kill more Thracians (10.503–511), and the horses go to the ships.
- *The spoils.* The two leave silver, arms and mixing bowls behind; Euryalus takes the trappings and gold-studded belt of Rhamnes and puts on the plumed helmet of Messapus (*Aeneid* 9.357–366). Odysseus hangs Dolon's cap, wolfskin, bow and spear on a tamarisk, marks the place, and later takes them to the ships for Athena (10.458–468, 10.570–571).

**The reversal.** The Achaean pair come home, are welcomed with handclasps and laughter, and bathe and eat (10.540–579). Virgil's pair do not come home. In the half-light of the night the helmet betrays Euryalus, flashing back the light (*Aeneid* 9.373–374), and his plunder weighs him down in the dark wood (*Aeneid* 9.384–385). Nisus prays to the Moon before he throws his spear (*Aeneid* 9.403–409), as the Achaeans prayed to Athena at the start of their night (10.277–294), but no goddess guides these spears home. Euryalus dies like a flower cut by the plow or a poppy bowed by the rain (*Aeneid* 9.433–437; see [[the-poppy-in-later-poets]]), and Nisus dies on his friend's body. The poet turns to them: “Fortunate both! If my songs have any power, no day will ever take you from the memory of time” (*Aeneid* 9.446–447; see [[apostrophe]]).

The Trojans are Virgil's own people, the side that in Homer lost Dolon and its ally Rhesus in a single night. The raid that wins the Achaeans glory in the Iliad brings the Trojans' young men to their deaths in the Aeneid, and the taking of spoils, a triumph in Homer, is what undoes Euryalus. The story of Dolon's son in the last book of the poem keeps the Doloneia in view from the Trojan side (see [[virgil-rhesus-and-dolon]]).
