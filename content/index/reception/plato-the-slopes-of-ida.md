**Homer.** Aeneas begins his lineage with Dardanus, son of Zeus, who “founded Dardania, since sacred Ilios was not yet / built in the plain, a city of mortal men, / but they still dwelt on the slopes of Ida of the many springs” (20.216–218; see [[dardanus]], [[dardania]] and [[ida]]). ὑπώρεια, found only here in Homer, is the hill country at the foot of a mountain. The poem does not say who built Ilios in the plain.

**Plato.** In the third book of the *Laws* the Athenian stranger asks how cities and constitutions began. The old stories of many destructions of mankind, by floods and plagues and much else, have some truth in them, he says, and he imagines the few who escaped one great flood as herdsmen on the mountain tops, small embers of the human race, while the cities of the plains and of the coast perished (677a–c). The first constitution is the rule of the eldest in scattered households, and Homer is a witness to it in the Cyclopes, who “have no assemblies for counsel, nor laws”, but live on the peaks of high mountains in hollow caves, each one laying down the law for his children and wives (680b–c, quoting *Odyssey* 9.112–115; see [[cyclopes]]). Then larger communities turn to farming in the foothills, build rough walls against wild beasts, and choose men to collect their customs and set rulers over them (680e–681d). Of the third form, in which all kinds and conditions of constitutions and of cities come about together, Homer too gave a sign, telling how it came after the second; and the Athenian quotes Aeneas' verses (681d–e, quoting 20.216–218). His text has the Attic ᾤκουν for ᾤκεον, “they dwelt”, and πολυπιδάκου for πολυπίδακος, “of many springs”.

Homer said these verses, and the verses on the Cyclopes, in accordance with god and with nature, the Athenian goes on, “for the race of poets, being divine and inspired when it sings, lays hold each time, with the help of certain Graces and Muses, of many things that truly happened” (682a). Ilion, he says, was settled from the heights in a great and fair plain, on a hill that was not high, with many rivers rushing down from Ida above; its founders must have long forgotten the flood, to set a city beneath so many rivers coming down from the heights and to trust in hills of no great height (682b–c). Other cities grew up and made war on it, by sea as well, and the Achaeans destroyed Troy after ten years. In those ten years there was much trouble at home with the young men, who did not receive the returning soldiers well or justly, so that there were many deaths and exiles; the exiles came back under a new name, Dorians instead of Achaeans (682c–e). When the speakers look back on their argument, they name among the settlements they have studied “the foothills of Dardanus and the settlement by the sea” (τὰς τοῦ Δαρδάνου ὑπωρείας τε καὶ τὴν ἐπὶ θαλάττῃ κατοίκισιν, 702a; see [[plato]]).

**Strabo.** Strabo retells the argument in his account of the Troad. The Dardania that the poet names was probably founded in the foothills of Ida under Aeneas, he thinks, though in his day not even a trace of a city was left there (*Geography* 13.1.24; see [[dardanians]]). Plato conjectures, he says, three forms of constitution after the floods: the first on the mountain tops, simple and wild, while men still feared the waters lying on the plains; the second on the slopes, as they took heart little by little; the third in the plains. One might add a fourth and a fifth, he suggests, and last the settlements on the coast and in the islands, when all such fear was gone. He gives the first to the Cyclopes, the second to Dardanus, quoting 20.216–218 with πολυπιδάκου, and the third to Ilus, who is said to have founded Ilion and given it his name; and he thinks it likely that Ilus was buried in the middle of the plain because he was the first to dare to settle there, quoting the rout “past the mound of ancient Ilus, descendant of Dardanus, / through the middle of the plain” (11.166–167; *Geography* 13.1.25; see [[ilus]], [[the-tomb-of-ilus]] and the *Iliad* 11 note). Even Ilus, Strabo adds, did not dare fully, for he built his city not where the Ilium of Strabo's day stood but about thirty stades higher up, toward the east, toward Ida and Dardania, at the place called the Village of the Ilians (13.1.25; see [[troy]]).

Walter Leaf, who cites the *Laws* for the word ὑπωρείας, cannot say whether the verses embody a real local tradition or are merely an ancient speculation on the growth of cities. He takes the word for the low hill country that fringes Ida, since the place where the mountains rise from the plain, the site of Ilios, is “in the plain” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.218).
