Ovid's *Heroides* are letters in elegiac verse from women of myth to the men who have left them. The last six are pairs, a man's letter with the woman's answer; their authorship has been doubted, but many scholars accept them as a later work of Ovid's. In the sixteenth, Paris writes to Helen from Sparta, where he is Menelaus' guest. Venus promised her to him when he judged the goddesses; he has come for her; and Menelaus has sailed off to Crete and left them together. In the seventeenth Helen answers. She protests her virtue, reproaches him for abusing her husband's hospitality, and fears the war that will follow, but she lets herself be persuaded.

Ovid writes the beginning of the story that Iliad 3 looks back on, and his Helen already sees the Paris of Book 3. His body, she tells him, is better suited to Venus than to Mars: "let the brave wage wars; you, Paris, always love", and let Hector, whom he praises so much, do his fighting for him. That is Hector's rebuke of Paris (3.39–57) and Helen's own after the duel (3.428–436).

The letter's last couplet is a learned joke. The rest, Helen writes, they can arrange through her companions Clymene and Aethra, who are her attendants and her counsel. These are the two handmaids who follow Helen to the Scaean gates in Iliad 3, "Aethra, daughter of Pittheus, and ox-eyed Clymene" (3.143–144). In Homer they walk with her to the gates in the tenth year of the war; in Ovid they carry the messages that begin it. On the problem of Aethra's presence in Helen's household, see [[aethra-line]].
