summary: Odysseus' first words in his tale to the Phaeacians, that there is no fulfillment more gracious than a feast at which the whole people is glad and the banqueters listen to a singer, were censured by Plato's Socrates as unfit for the young, cited by Aristotle as proof that music was meant for the leisure of free men, and defended by Proclus, who found in them gladness and music, not pleasure. Two of the verses stood under the bronze statue of a citharode at Magnesia.

**Homer.** Alcinous has asked the stranger his name and why he weeps at the song of Troy (*Odyssey* 8.536–586). Odysseus begins his answer with praise of the singer, “truly it is a fine thing to listen to a singer / such as this one is, like the gods in his voice” (*Odyssey* 9.3–4, the words of Telemachus at *Odyssey* 1.370–371; see [[singers]] and [[demodocus]]), and of the feast at which the whole people is glad (*Odyssey* 9.5–11; see [[feast]]). Then he turns to his own sorrows (*Odyssey* 9.12–15). τέλος, rendered “fulfillment”, is the realization or highest perfection of a thing rather than its end; in the later language of philosophy τὸ τέλος came to mean the chief good, but a scholium, Merry notes, already observes that Odysseus called pleasure the end not of all life but of a symposium; and Eustathius says rightly, in Merry's judgment, that Odysseus is not propounding a philosophy but chiming in with what his host had said (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 9.5; see the translation's key words). Alcinous had told him what the Phaeacians love: “always dear to us are the feast and the lyre and the dances / and changes of clothing and warm baths and beds” (*Odyssey* 8.248–249; see [[different-gifts]]).

**Plato.** In the third book of the *Republic*, Socrates, reviewing what the young guardians may hear, says that for most men self-control means obeying their rulers and ruling their own pleasures of drink, sex and food. “What then? To make the wisest of men say that it seems to him the finest thing of all, when ‘beside them the tables are laden / with bread and meat, and drawing wine from the mixing bowl / the wine-pourer carries it round and pours it into the cups’, does that seem to you fit for a young man to hear, for his self-mastery?” (*Republic* 389d–390b, a plain rendering, quoting *Odyssey* 9.8–10). Plato's verse has πλεῖαι ὦσι, “are full”, where our text has πλήθωσι, “are laden”. The next examples are the verse that to die of hunger is the most pitiable fate (*Odyssey* 12.342) and Zeus' desire for Hera on Ida (see [[plato-zeus-deceived]] and [[plato]]). Merry sets beside Plato's censure a chapter of Lucian's *Parasite* (Merry on *Odyssey* 9.5); and in the manuscript that Dindorf calls Q, *Odyssey* 9.5–8 are marked with obeli, which Merry takes for an objection to the sentiment rather than a rejection of the verses (his critical note on *Odyssey* 9.5–8).

**Aristotle.** In the last book of the *Politics* Aristotle asks why the ancients made music part of education. Not as necessary, nor as useful, as letters are useful for business, for managing a household, for learning and for public life; nor, as gymnastics, for health and strength. What is left is the pastime of leisure, which they count the pastime of free men. “That is why Homer wrote thus”, Aristotle goes on, quoting two verses that are not in our text, the second of them close to Eumaeus' words on the craftsmen whom men call in from abroad, among them “a divine singer, who may delight by singing” (*Odyssey* 17.382–386, a plain rendering); “and elsewhere Odysseus says that this is the best pastime, when, men being glad, ‘the banqueters throughout the house listen to a singer, / sitting in order’” (*Politics* 8.3, 1338a13–30, a plain rendering, quoting *Odyssey* 9.7–8). His “men being glad” takes up the gladness of the verse before (*Odyssey* 9.6; see [[aristotle]]).

**Proclus.** Proclus, in his essays on the *Republic*, restates the charge: Odysseus, defining the finest of lives, approves above all the polity in which “gladness holds the whole people”, and so sets up pleasure and the filling of desires as the end of life (*On the Republic* I 129 Kroll, quoting *Odyssey* 9.6–10). He answers it in two ways. Those who read the wanderings allegorically, he says, place the Phaeacians and their happiness above mortal nature, and read their feast and their harmonious song in another sense. For those who follow the surface of the poem, the wisest of the Greeks does not say that pleasure rules in a right polity, but gladness, εὐφροσύνη, and Plato himself teaches how the two differ; he approves a city brought into harmony by music and made of one mind, with a singer who shares in divine inspiration set over the multitude; and he adds plenty of necessities, praising not a life stuffed with them but one that lacks nothing that makes a life complete. Only if one took away the gladness and the education by inspired music, and supposed that he approved tables and unmeasured enjoyments aimed at pleasure, would Socrates rightly say that such things are far from his city (*On the Republic* I 131–132 Kroll, plain renderings; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).

**A statue at Magnesia.** Strabo, describing Magnesia on the Maeander, tells of the citharode Anaxenor, a Magnesian, whom the theaters exalted and Antony most of all, making him collector of the tribute of four cities and giving him soldiers; his own city clothed him in purple as priest of Zeus Sosipolis. In the theater stood a bronze statue of him inscribed with the two verses on the singer, beginning with Odysseus' ἤτοι μέν, “truly”, not Telemachus' “since”. The engraver, short of room on the base, left off the last letter of the second verse, and so laid the city open to a charge of ignorance, since the last word could be read as a nominative or as a dative, many writing their datives without the iota (*Geography* 14.1.41, plain renderings; see [[strabo]]). Strabo's text of the verse ends with the dative αὐδῇ; ours has the accusative αὐδήν, “in his voice”.

**Eratosthenes' reading.** Merry quotes from Athenaeus the reading of Eratosthenes in *Odyssey* 9.6, ἢ ὅταν εὐφροσύνη μὲν ἔχῃ, κακότητος ἀπούσης, “or when gladness holds, with evil absent”: by evil, Eratosthenes said, the poet meant folly, since the Phaeacians, “very dear to the immortals” as Nausicaa says, could not be without sense (Merry's critical note on *Odyssey* 9.6; *Odyssey* 6.203; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 9.6).
