**Homer.** Achilles would send the army to fight unfed, and will not eat himself while Patroclus lies unavenged in his hut (19.199–214; see [[fasting]]). Odysseus answers that men soon have their fill of battle and must eat to bear it (19.221–224; see [[satiety-of-battle]]), and then turns to the dead: “But the Achaeans cannot mourn a corpse with the belly” (19.225). Too many fall, “day after day” (19.226). The dead must be buried “having a pitiless heart, after weeping for him a single day” (19.229), and the living must remember drink and food so as to fight on (19.230–233; see [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]] and [[odysseus]]).

**Cicero.** The third book of the *Tusculan Disputations* is about grief, which Cicero takes to be a matter of opinion: men grieve because they think it right and fitting to grieve. He gives the signs of it. Aeschines attacked Demosthenes for sacrificing on the seventh day after his daughter's death; Homer's Bellerophon, in Cicero's Latin, wandered alone over the Aleian plain, eating out his heart and avoiding the paths of men (6.201–202; see [[bellerophon]]); the mourner who has laughed or spoken cheerfully calls himself back to sadness and blames himself for leaving off, and mothers and teachers beat children to make them weep in a house of mourning; and when mourning at last abates, because nothing is gained by it, that shows that the whole of it was voluntary (3.63–64). Terence's self-tormentor decides to be miserable, and no one decides anything against his will (3.65). “What of those whom the situation itself forbids to mourn? As in Homer daily killings and the deaths of many bring an abatement of grief”, and Cicero gives the lines in Latin verse: *Namque nimis multos atque omni luce cadentis / Cernimus, ut nemo possit maerore vacare. / Quo magis est aequum tumulis mandare peremptos / Firmo animo et luctum lacrimis finire diurnis*, “For we see too many falling, and every day, for anyone to be free of grief; all the more right, then, to commit the slain to the tomb with a firm heart, and to end mourning with a day's tears” (*Tusculan Disputations* 3.65; see [[cicero]]).

**Cicero's Homer.** The four Latin lines render 19.226–229, and leave out the belly of 19.225 and the advice that follows to remember food and drink. *Nimis multos* is λίην … πολλοί, “too many”, *omni luce* is ἤματα πάντα, “day after day”, and *lacrimis diurnis*, “a day's tears”, is ἐπʼ ἤματι δακρύσαντας, “after weeping for him a single day”. *Firmo animo*, “with a firm heart”, softens νηλέα θυμὸν ἔχοντας, “having a pitiless heart”, and *maerore*, “grief”, stands for πόνοιο, “toil”, in the question “when could anyone draw breath from toil?” (19.227). Walter Leaf, who quotes Cicero's lines, notes that *maerore* hardly gives πόνοιο, which to him is the toil of fasting; Monro took it for the toil of battle (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.226; see the translation's note on 19.225–229). In Homer the rule is an argument for eating; in Cicero it is evidence that grief is a matter of will, since circumstances can end it.

**The belly.** The Byzantine lexicon called the Suda keeps the thought as a saying, “You could not mourn a corpse with your belly”, and adds Homer's line, 19.225 (Suda ο 848).
