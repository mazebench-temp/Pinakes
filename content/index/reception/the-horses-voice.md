summary: Hera gives Achilles' horse Xanthus a human voice, and when he has foretold his master's death the Erinyes check it. Aristarchus struck out the line about Hera, since the goddess who gave the voice ought to have taken it away, and Walter Leaf could not see why Hera should have worked the miracle. Proclus had an answer: Hera is the air, and voice is struck air. For the Erinyes Leaf looked to Heraclitus, whose Erinyes would find out the sun if he overstepped his measures, and to the Fates and Juno who silence Virgil's Helenus.

**Homer.** Achilles calls to his father's horses, Xanthus and Balius, to bring their charioteer back safe and not to leave him dead on the field, as they left Patroclus (19.400–403). Xanthus answers from under the yoke, bowing his head until his mane reaches the ground, “and the white-armed goddess Hera gave him speech” (19.407; see [[xanthus-the-horse]] and [[hera]]). The day of Achilles' destruction is near, he says; it was not through their slowness but by Apollo that Patroclus died, and Achilles himself is fated to be brought down by a god and a man (19.408–417; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). “So he spoke, and the Erinyes checked his voice” (19.418; see [[erinyes]]). Hera is not named again, and the poem does not say why she gave the voice or why the Erinyes took it away.

**Aristarchus.** Aristarchus athetized 19.407 as superfluous and as contradicting 19.418, since the god who gave the voice ought to have taken it away; he also struck out the horse's words about the West Wind, “which they say is the swiftest of all”, since φασί, “they say”, is used of what is not certainly known and does not suit a horse (19.416–417; see the translation's notes on 19.407 and 19.416–417 and [[aristarchus]]). Walter Leaf answers that the objection to 407 is perfectly true by the mythological rules of etiquette, but tells equally against 418, and confesses: “Why Hera should have worked the miracle it is not easy to see” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.407).

**Proclus.** Proclus has an answer, given in his commentary on the *Cratylus*. There Socrates suggests that the lawgiver named the air (ἀήρ) Hera in disguise, putting the beginning at the end, as anyone will see who says the goddess's name over and over (*Cratylus* 404b–c; see [[plato]]). Proclus explains that air is the symbol of soul, which is therefore called breath, as fire is the image of mind, water of the nature that nourishes the world, and earth of the body. Homer hints at this, he says, when he makes Hera hang from the sky with two anvils beneath her feet, since the air has the two heaviest elements below it (15.18–19), when Hera spreads a deep mist before the fleeing Trojans (21.6–7), and when she sends the tireless Sun down to the streams of Oceanus, for what he calls the setting of the sun is the thickest mist, made through Hera (18.239–240). “And since voice is struck air, the voice of rational beings is dedicated above all to the goddess, who even made the horse of Achilles speak” (*On the Cratylus* 170; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). Proclus' Hera gives the voice because the voice is made of her element.

**The Erinyes.** Elsewhere the Erinyes punish wrong: they hear a father's curse and a mother's (9.454, 9.571), and in this book they punish perjurers beneath the earth (19.259–260). Leaf thought that here they had become almost the law that orders the world, physical as well as moral, a conception too refined, he says, for early epic, but one that he finds in Heraclitus (on 19.418). Plutarch, consoling an exile, argues that confinement to an island is no hardship, for each of the planets keeps its station, circling in one sphere as on an island: “for the sun will not overstep his measures, says Heraclitus; otherwise the Erinyes, helpers of Justice, will find him out” (*On Exile* 11, 604a; see [[heraclitus-of-ephesus]] and [[plutarch]]). Leaf reports that a scholion explains that everything against reason and portentous seems to be done by the Erinyes, which only shows, he says, that its author felt the difficulty; he would sooner lose 418 than 407 (on 19.418; see the translation's note on 19.418).

**Virgil.** Leaf compares the seer Helenus in the third book of the *Aeneid*. At Buthrotum Helenus will tell Aeneas only a few things out of many, “for the Parcae forbid Helenus to know the rest, and Saturnian Juno forbids him to speak” (*Aeneid* 3.377–380; see [[helenus]] and [[virgil]]). Leaf finds the Fates intelligible enough there (on 19.418). In Homer Hera gives a voice and the Erinyes take it away; in Virgil the Fates limit what the seer may know, and Juno what he may say.
