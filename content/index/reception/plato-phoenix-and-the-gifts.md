**Homer.** Phoenix's plea moves from the gods to the gifts. Even the gods can be turned by offerings and prayers, and whoever refuses the Prayers, daughters of Zeus, is sent Blind Folly in his turn (9.496–512; see [[litai]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]). If Agamemnon were not bringing gifts, “I would not bid you cast aside your wrath”; but he gives much and has sent the dearest of the Achaeans (9.515–523). And after Meleager: come while the gifts are offered, for without them “you will no longer be held in the same honor” (9.600–605; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]] and [[time]]).

**Gods for sale.** In the second book of the *Republic* Adeimantus complains that justice is praised only for its rewards, and worst of all is what is said of the gods. Begging priests and seers persuade the rich that they have a power from the gods, got by sacrifices and incantations, to make good any wrong done by a man or his ancestors, and to harm an enemy with spells. They bring the poets as witnesses: Hesiod for the easy road to vice (*Works and Days* 287–289), and for the gods' being led aside by men, Homer, who said, “Even the gods themselves can be entreated, and with sacrifices and gentle vows, with libation and the savor of the burnt fat, men turn them aside, entreating them, whenever someone oversteps and does wrong.” What, Adeimantus asks, will the souls of the young make of such things? (364b–365a). Plato's text begins in the middle of 9.497 and passes over 9.498, “and theirs is the greater prowess and honor and strength”. It has λιστοί, “can be entreated”, where ours has στρεπτοί, “can be turned”, a word not found in our text of Homer and formed from λίσσομαι, the verb of the Prayers; and it has θυσίαισι, “sacrifices”, for θυέεσσι, “burnt offerings”.

**The advice.** In the third book Socrates sets the rules for the stories told to the city's guardians, who must not be open to bribes or lovers of money. They are not to hear the verse “Gifts persuade gods, gifts persuade reverend kings”, which some ascribed to Hesiod (Suda δ 1451). Nor is Phoenix, Achilles' tutor, to be praised as speaking with measure (μετρίως) when he advised him to take the gifts and help the Achaeans, but without gifts not to give up his wrath. Nor will they believe that Achilles was so fond of money as to take gifts from Agamemnon, as he does in Book 19, or to release a corpse only for a price, as in Book 24, nor that the son of a goddess, raised by the wise Chiron, had in him both meanness with love of money and contempt for gods and men (390d–391c; see [[plato-achilles-insults]]). In Phoenix's speech the gifts are the measure of honor; Plato reads them as payment.

**No god for sale.** The *Laws* give Plato's own answer. The Athenian names three errors about the gods: that they do not exist, that they do not care for men, and that they are easily appeased, “led aside by sacrifices and prayers”. Young men who hear such things from poets and priests do wrong and then try to make it good (ἐξακεῖσθαι, the verb of the Prayers who “heal the harm”, ἐξακέονται, 9.507; *Laws* 10.885b–e). Later the Athenian asks whether anyone would liken the gods to steersmen who are “turned aside by libation of wine and the savor of fat” and then overturn the ship and its crew, or to charioteers bribed to throw the race, or to watchdogs charmed by wolves. The gods are the greatest of guardians, and they cannot be won over against justice (905d–907b). The steersman's libation and savor are Homer's words, λοιβῇ τε κνίσῃ τε (9.500), and he is “turned aside” (παρατρεπομένοις) as Phoenix's gods are (παρατρωπῶσʼ).
