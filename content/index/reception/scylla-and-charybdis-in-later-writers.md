summary: The two dangers of Homer's strait became the strait of Messina, a pair of names for any devouring peril, and a byword for monsters that could never have been. Polybius and Strabo found Scylla's fishing in the swordfish hunt of the Scyllaeum and Charybdis in the tides; the Seventh Letter quotes Odysseus' return to Charybdis for its author's third voyage to Sicily; Aeschylus and Euripides call murderous women Scyllas; Plato, Lucretius and Cicero count her among impossible composite creatures; Virgil gave her a maiden's body ending in dolphins and wolves, and Ovid a maiden's story and Circe's jealousy; and Cicero made Charybdis a name for plunderers and spendthrifts.

**Homer's strait.** Circe describes two crags a bowshot apart. In a misty cave high on the one lives Scylla, yelping with a newborn puppy's voice, with twelve dangling feet and six heads on long necks; she fishes for dolphins and dogfish, and from every ship that passes she takes a man with each head. Under a wild fig tree on the other, Charybdis sucks down the black water three times a day and sends it up three times, and not even the earth-shaker could save a ship caught there. It is far better, Circe says, to lose six companions than all of them, and Scylla cannot be fought (*Odyssey* 12.73–126; see [[scylla]] and [[charybdis]]). The ship passes between them, and while the men look toward Charybdis, Scylla takes six of the strongest (*Odyssey* 12.234–259; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). After the wreck the South Wind carries Odysseus back to Charybdis on his keel and mast, and he saves himself by clinging to the fig tree above her until she gives back the timbers (*Odyssey* 12.426–446; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and [[the-fig-tree-of-charybdis]]). Homer names no sea and no coast for the strait.

**The strait of Messina.** The ancients placed the two in the strait between Italy and Sicily, Scylla on the Italian side and Charybdis on the Sicilian (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 12.85). Strabo, following Polybius, held that Homer took the beginnings of his fables from history: the region of the strait was then unapproachable, “and Charybdis and the Scyllaeum were held by pirates” (*Geography* 1.2.9, plain renderings; see [[strabo]]); as the Wanderers were suggested by the Clashing Rocks, so “the voyage through the crags” was suggested by Scylla and Charybdis (*Geography* 1.2.10; see [[the-wanderers]]), though “the shape of Scylla” is one of the poet's plain marvels (*Geography* 1.2.11). Polybius, as Strabo reports him, found Scylla's fishing in the hunt for swordfish that is carried on at the Scyllaeum. The tunnies, swimming in shoals along Italy and kept from Sicily by the current, fall among dolphins, dogfish and other sea beasts, and the swordfish, also called dogfish, grow fat on them. A lookout on the heights signals to many small boats, two men in each, one rowing and the other standing in the bow with a harpoon whose barbed head comes away from the shaft on a long line, and the fish, which swims with a third of its body out of the water, is played until it tires and is hauled in; at times it strikes through the boat and wounds the rower, fighting like a wild boar. From such things, and from what is said of Charybdis, so like what happens in the strait, one may guess that Homer set the wandering around Sicily (*Geography* 1.2.15–16; see [[polybius]]). Merry reports from Polybius that Homer's κύνας, the “dogfish” that Scylla catches, are a kind of swordfish (on *Odyssey* 12.96). To those who asked how Odysseus came to Sicily three times without once sailing through the strait, Polybius answered that all later sailors avoided that passage too (*Geography* 1.2.17). Strabo describes the Scyllaeum as a high rock that forms a peninsula, joined to the land by a low isthmus, which Anaxilaus, tyrant of Rhegium, fortified against the Tyrrhenians with a naval station, taking the passage of the strait from the pirates (*Geography* 6.1.5), and Charybdis as a place of extraordinary depth in the channel a little before Messana, into which the counter-currents draw ships, turning them over with a great whirling; the wreckage drifts to the shore of Tauromenium, which is called Copria, “the dung heap”, from this (*Geography* 6.2.3). Ovid's Glaucus, too, saw Scylla “on the Italian shore, opposite the walls of Messana” (*Metamorphoses* 14.17–18, a plain rendering).

**Three times or twice.** Circe's Charybdis sends the water up three times a day and sucks it down three times (*Odyssey* 12.105). Polybius called the number an error of the copyist or of the information, since the tide turns twice (*Geography* 1.2.16). Strabo defends the poet: the myth of Charybdis was made from the ebb and flow of the sea, and the number is not ignorance but the tragedy and fear that Circe heaps into her words to turn Odysseus away, an exaggeration as common as “thrice blessed” and “thrice wretched”; and the late hour at which the timbers come up hints at the truth (*Geography* 1.2.36; see [[like-a-bat]], [[the-judge-rising-for-supper]] and the translation's note on 12.104–105). Virgil kept Homer's number. Helenus' Charybdis “three times sucks the vast waves sheer into the depth of her abyss, and again lifts them in turn to the air, and the wave lashes the stars” (*ter gurgite vastos / sorbet in abruptum fluctus rursusque sub auras / erigit alternos*, *Aeneid* 3.421–423, plain renderings), and when the Trojans pass, “three times the crags gave a cry among the hollow rocks, three times we saw the foam dashed out and the stars dripping” (*Aeneid* 3.566–567); Ovid's Charybdis “three times drinks up and three times vomits the strait” (*Letters from Pontus* 4.10.28, a plain rendering). Merry cites the Latin for the number, and observes that it has nothing for Homer's “a day” (on *Odyssey* 12.105).

**The third voyage.** The *Seventh Letter*, handed down among Plato's works, tells how its writer's last stay with Dionysius the Younger at Syracuse ended. Dionysius stopped sending Dion his revenues, saying that the property belonged not to Dion but to his son, Dionysius' own nephew and ward; it was summer, and the ships were sailing, and the writer thought he should be angry less with Dionysius than with himself and with those who had forced him to come “for the third time to the strait by Scylla, / that I might measure out again the way to deadly Charybdis” (εἰς τὸν πορθμὸν τὸν περὶ τὴν Σκύλλαν, / ὄφρʼ ἔτι τὴν ὀλοὴν ἀναμετρήσαιμι Χάρυβδιν), and he resolved to tell Dionysius that he could not stay when Dion had been so outraged (*Seventh Letter* 345c–e, plain renderings; see [[plato]] and [[dion-of-syracuse]]). The verse is Odysseus' own, when the South Wind carries him back on the wreckage to Charybdis (*Odyssey* 12.428). Merry reports Nauck's note that Apollonius the Sophist read θάλασσαν, “the sea”, for Χάρυβδιν, which Nauck thought right, although Plato's letter and Plutarch's *Dion* have Χάρυβδιν (Merry's critical note on *Odyssey* 12.428; see the translation's note on 12.428). The Suda, in a list of philosophers who went after money, says that “Plato is said to have measured out Charybdis three times for the sake of Sicilian wealth”, with Odysseus' verb (αι 349, a plain rendering). Whether Plato wrote the letter is disputed.

**Monsters of tragedy.** Aeschylus' Cassandra seeks a name for the queen who will kill Agamemnon: “What hateful beast should I call her and hit the mark? An amphisbaena, or some Scylla dwelling in the rocks, the bane of sailors, a raging mother of Hades, breathing truceless war against her own?” (*Agamemnon* 1232–1236, plain renderings; see [[aeschylus]] and [[clytemnestra]]). The words recall Homer's “Scylla of the rock” and the sailors she snatches from every ship (*Odyssey* 12.231, 12.98–100). Euripides' Jason, at the end of the *Medea*, calls his wife “a lioness, not a woman, with a nature more savage than Tyrrhenian Scylla”, and Medea takes the names: “call me a lioness, if you like, and a Scylla who dwelt in the Tyrrhenian plain” (*Medea* 1341–1343, 1358–1359, plain renderings; the end of the second passage is corrupt; see [[medea]] and [[euripides]]). Euripides' Cassandra, foretelling the sufferings of Odysseus, names first “dread Charybdis”, who has made her dwelling in the narrow channel of the rock (*Trojan Women* 431–436, a plain rendering; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-12]]). In the *Suppliants* the Theban herald uses the name for the chasm of earth that swallowed the seer Amphiaraus: “nor did a charybdis snatch the seer, throwing its chasm round his four-horse chariot?” (*Suppliants* 500–501, a plain rendering; see [[amphiaraus]]). The dithyramb *Scylla*, generally ascribed to Timotheus, gave Odysseus a lament for his companions, which Aristotle names as an example of “the unseemly and inappropriate”; bad pipers, he says, drag the chorus leader about “if they are piping the Scylla” (*Poetics* 1454a30–31, 1461b31–32; see [[scylla-of-timotheus]]).

**A creature that never was.** Plato, shaping an image of the soul, bids his hearer imagine one of the natures that the myths say existed of old, “like that of the Chimaera and of Scylla and of Cerberus”, in which many forms grew together into one, and builds from it a many-colored, many-headed beast, with the heads of tame and of wild animals in a ring (*Republic* 9.588c, plain renderings; see [[chimaera]]). Lucretius denies that such creatures can be: there never were Centaurs, nor can there be beings of a double nature joined from alien limbs, “nor Scyllas girt about their half-marine bodies with ravening dogs”, for their parts would neither flower nor grow old together (*aut rapidis canibus succinctas semimarinis / corporibus Scyllas*, Lucretius 5.878–894, plain renderings; see [[lucretius]] and [[centaurs]]). Cicero's Academic Cotta turns the argument against the Epicureans, who explain thought by images coming from things: what of the images “of things that never were at all and never could be, like Scylla, like the Chimaera”? (*On the Nature of the Gods* 1.108, a plain rendering; see [[cicero]]).

**Virgil.** In the third book of the *Aeneid* the seer Helenus warns Aeneas to go round Sicily by the long way. When the narrows of Pelorus open, he must keep to the land and the sea on the left and shun the shore and the waves on the right, for here, they say, Italy and Sicily were once one land, until the sea broke through between them. “Scylla holds the right side, implacable Charybdis the left”; Charybdis sucks the waves into her abyss and throws them up to the stars, and a cave in dark hiding places holds Scylla, thrusting out her mouths and dragging ships onto the rocks: “above, she has a human face, and is a maiden fair-breasted to the groin; below, a sea-monster of huge body, with dolphins' tails joined to a belly of wolves”. It is better to round Pachynus, the cape of Trinacria, slowly and by a long course, “than once to have seen shapeless Scylla under her vast cave, and the rocks that echo with her sea-blue dogs” (*Aeneid* 3.410–432, plain renderings; see [[virgil]] and [[helenus]]). When the Trojans come there, they hear the great groaning of the sea far off, and Anchises cries: “Surely this is that Charybdis: these are the crags, these the dreadful rocks Helenus foretold”; the oarsmen pull to the left, lifted to the sky on the arching whirlpool and sunk to the shades below as the wave is drawn away (*Aeneid* 3.554–567, plain renderings; see [[anchises]]). Later, fleeing the Cyclopes, Aeneas remembers Helenus' warning against the course “between Scylla and Charybdis”, death on either side by a narrow margin, and resolves to turn his sails back (*Scyllamque Charybdinque / inter, utrimque viam leti discrimine parvo*, *Aeneid* 3.684–686). In the sixth *Eclogue* Silenus sings of “Scylla daughter of Nisus, of whom the story went that, her white groin girt with barking monsters, she harried the Dulichian ships, and in the deep whirlpool, alas, tore the frightened sailors with her sea-dogs” (*Eclogues* 6.74–77, plain renderings): the ships are Odysseus', named after Dulichium, and Homer's monster has become one with the daughter of Nisus who, as Strabo tells the story, betrayed Nisaea to Minos for love and was drowned, and gave her name to a Scyllaeum near Hermione (*Geography* 8.6.13). Merry thinks that the likeness of Σκύλλη to σκύλαξ, “puppy”, may account for the sea-blue dogs and the barking monsters, *caerulei canes* and *latrantia monstra*, of the Latin poets (on *Odyssey* 12.85).

**Ovid.** Ovid gives Scylla a story. “Scylla infests the right side, restless Charybdis the left; this one devours the ships she has seized and vomits them up again; that one is girt about her black belly with savage dogs, and has a maiden's face, and, if the poets have not left everything invented, she too was once a maiden”, who refused her many suitors and told the sea nymphs of their disappointed love; she is “the daughter of Crataeis” (*Crataeide natam*, *Metamorphoses* 13.730–749, plain renderings; see [[crataeis]] and [[ovid]]). The sea god Glaucus loved her, and when she fled him he went to Circe for a charm that would make her share his passion. Circe, who loved him herself and was refused, poisoned the pool where Scylla bathed; the girl waded in to her middle and saw her groin foul with barking monsters, the jaws of Cerberus in place of thighs and legs and feet. She stayed in her place, and “as soon as she was given the chance, in hatred of Circe she robbed Ulysses of his companions”; she would have sunk the Trojan ships too, had she not first been turned into a crag, which stands of rock still, and which the sailor still shuns (*Metamorphoses* 13.898–968, 14.1–75, plain renderings; see [[circe]]). Homer's Circe warns Odysseus of Scylla; Ovid's made her. From exile Ovid finds his own dangers worse: Scylla, “barking with savage monsters from her mutilated groin”, has done sailors less harm than the ships of the Heniochi, and Charybdis cannot compare with the hostile Achaeans of those shores (*Letters from Pontus* 4.10.25–28, plain renderings); and he will go anywhere, “into the middle of the Syrtes, into the middle of Charybdis”, if only he may leave the land he lives in (*Letters from Pontus* 4.14.9–10, a plain rendering). Propertius' Ulysses survives “Scylla, and Charybdis torn by alternate waters” (*Elegies* 3.12.28, a plain rendering; see [[propertius]]), and Horace names “Scylla, and with the Cyclops Charybdis” among the striking wonders that Homer keeps for after his modest beginning (*Ars Poetica* 145, a plain rendering; see [[horace-on-the-opening-of-the-odyssey]]).

**Charybdis the devourer.** Cicero made the pair a figure of speech. He compares Verres in Sicily to those of whom the poets tell, who held bays and headlands and sheer rocks to kill all who were driven ashore: “I do not think that Charybdis or Scylla was so hostile to sailors as he was in the same strait; and he was the more dangerous in this, that he had girded himself with many more and far more savage dogs, a second Cyclops, far more ruthless; for he besieged the whole island, while the other is said to have held only Aetna and that part of Sicily” (*Against Verres* 2.5.145–146, plain renderings; see [[cicero]] and [[cicero-polyphemus-and-the-ram]]): the dogs of Scylla's girdle have become Verres' attendants. Of Clodius he asks what Charybdis the poets could invent so monstrous as to drain such gulfs as the plunder he has swallowed, or what Scylla with dogs so prominent and so hungry as the men with whom he is seen “gnawing the very rostra” (*On the Responses of the Haruspices* 59, a plain rendering); of Antony, who ran through the property of Pompey in a few days, “What Charybdis was ever so voracious? Charybdis, do I say? If there was such a creature, it was a single animal; the Ocean itself, by heaven, could scarcely have swallowed so quickly so many things, so scattered and in places so far apart” (*Philippics* 2.66–67, a plain rendering); and Gabinius, driven by his creditors, fled into the harbor of the tribunate “so as not to stick fast at the Column in that Scyllaean strait of debt” (*On Behalf of Sestius* 18, a plain rendering). In *On the Orator* Crassus warns against metaphors fetched from too far: “‘the Syrtis of his patrimony’: I would rather say ‘the rock’; ‘the Charybdis of his goods’: rather ‘the whirlpool’; for the eyes of the mind are drawn more easily to things we have seen than to things we have only heard” (*On the Orator* 3.163, a plain rendering). A line of Aristophanes quoted in the Suda cries “strike, strike the villain”, the tax-farmer, “the gullet and Charybdis” (π 873, plain renderings; see [[aristophanes]]), and the Suda says that everything that leads down into chaos and destruction is called Charybdis (χ 144).

**The Suda.** The Suda describes Scylla as a beast told of in the Tyrrhenian sea, in part a very beautiful woman, with six dogs' heads on either side and the rest of her body like a snake's (σ 709). It gives Charybdis two places, the sea that is sucked down around Gades and turns back more violently, and the strait by Messana, where, it says, Odysseus lost all his companions with their ships and was carried alone in the currents, sitting astride a plank, until Phoenicians who saw him floating took him up and brought him naked to Idomeneus in Crete, who entertained him through the winter and sent him on to Phaeacia, “now called Corcyra”, and the Phaeacians sent him on with two ships and companions (χ 144; see [[idomeneus]] and [[phaeacians]]). The version has no gods in it, and no fig tree.

**Circe's choice.** In the poem the pair is a choice between two evils, and Circe makes it: “But be sure to draw close to Scylla's crag, and quickly / row your ship past, since it is far better / to miss six companions in your ship than all of them together” (*Odyssey* 12.108–110). Virgil's Helenus warns against the course “between Scylla and Charybdis” as death on either side by a narrow margin (*Aeneid* 3.684–686).
