summary: Euripides' Cyclops, the one satyr play that survives whole, stages the story of Odyssey 9 under Etna, with Silenus and the satyrs as the Cyclops' slaves. It keeps Maron's wine, the ivy cup, the two men eaten at a time, the guest-gift of being eaten last, the name Nobody, the stake of olive wood twirled like a shipwright's drill, the oracle and the rock; it makes Maron a son of Dionysus, has the Cyclops boil and roast his guests, gives him a creed of wealth and the belly beside his contempt for Zeus, and replaces the escape under the rams with the satyrs' mockery of the blind giant.

**The setting.** Silenus speaks the prologue. When Hera set Tyrrhenian pirates on Dionysus, he sailed with his sons, the satyrs, to look for the god, and as they were already near Malea an east wind cast them on the rock of Etna, where the one-eyed sons of the sea god, the Cyclopes, live in lonely caves and kill men (*Cyclops* 11–22). One of them, Polyphemus, holds them as slaves, and they tend his flocks (23–35). Homer's Odysseus too was driven off course as he rounded Malea (*Odyssey* 9.80–81; see [[malea]]); the play sets his Cyclops where later writers placed the Cyclopes, in Sicily under Etna (see [[sicily]] and [[cyclopes]]). Odysseus lands for water and food and names himself at once, “Odysseus of Ithaca, lord of the land of the Cephallenians” (103), and Silenus knows him for a sharp rattle, “of the race of Sisyphus” (104; plain renderings throughout).

**What the play keeps.** Silenus' answers describe the Cyclopes of the Odyssey: no walls and no city, caves and not houses, no rule, “nomads; no one listens to anyone at all” (115–120), as Homer's have no assemblies and no ordinances (*Odyssey* 9.112–115; see [[plato-the-cyclopes]]). Asked whether they are kind to strangers, as Homer's Odysseus wondered (*Odyssey* 9.175–176), Silenus answers that they find strangers' flesh the sweetest (125–126). The wine is Maron's, carried in a skin (141–145; *Odyssey* 9.196–211; see [[maron]]), and the Cyclops' own cup is of ivy, σκύφος κισσοῦ (390), the play's word for Homer's κισσύβιον, the ivy-wood bowl in which Odysseus brings him the wine (*Odyssey* 9.346; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 9.346). His cheeses stand in baskets of rush, his lambs run at their mothers' sides (206–209; *Odyssey* 9.219–223), and when he sees strangers by his cave he asks whether “pirates or thieves” have come to the land (222–223; compare *Odyssey* 9.254 and see [[thucydides-and-the-pirates]]). Odysseus pleads as a suppliant to the son of the sea god, citing the law that mortals must receive shipwrecked suppliants, give them guest-gifts and clothe them (286–301), and calls on “Zeus of strangers” (354), as Homer's Odysseus reminds the Cyclops of Zeus, the god of guests (*Odyssey* 9.266–271; see [[god-of-guests]], [[xenia]] and [[supplication]]). The Cyclops answers that he does not shudder at Zeus' thunderbolt and does not know that Zeus is a stronger god than he (320–321), as Homer's says that the Cyclopes care nothing for Zeus (*Odyssey* 9.275–276), and he promises his own guest-gifts, fire and his father's cauldron (342–344), as Homer's promises to eat Nobody last (*Odyssey* 9.369–370). He seizes two men (397). Odysseus' plan is the stake of olive wood, sharpened and heated in the fire (455–459), and he describes its turning in the eye with Homer's simile of the shipwright who turns his drill with a double strap (460–463; *Odyssey* 9.384–386; see [[the-shipwrights-drill]]). He gives his name as Οὖτιν, “Nobody”, and is promised the privilege of being eaten last of all his companions (548–550; see [[nobody]]). Blinded, the Cyclops cries “Nobody has destroyed me”, and the satyrs answer, as Homer's Cyclopes did, that then no one has wronged him (672–675; *Odyssey* 9.408–410). He takes his stand in the mouth of the cave with his hands spread to catch the men (666–668; *Odyssey* 9.415–418). Odysseus, out of reach, names himself, as his father named him, and says that the Cyclops was to pay for his unholy feast (689–695; *Odyssey* 9.477–479, 9.502–505); the Cyclops recognizes an old oracle, that he would be blinded by Odysseus on his way from Troy (696–698; *Odyssey* 9.507–512; Merry on *Odyssey* 9.507; see [[telemus]]), and threatens to break off a rock and crush the ship and its crew (704–705; *Odyssey* 9.481–486, 9.537–542).

**What it changes.** Maron is no longer the priest of Apollo at Ismarus but “the son of the god”, of Bacchus, whom Silenus once nursed in his arms (141–143; *Odyssey* 9.196–201; see [[ismarus]]); the scholia on Apollonius, as Merry reports, make Euanthes, Maron's father in Homer, the son of Dionysus and Ariadne (Merry on *Odyssey* 9.197; see [[dionysus]]). Odysseus offers the wine in trade for food, and Silenus sells his master's lambs for it (137–192). The Cyclopes have no wine at all and live on milk, cheese and the flesh of their flocks (122–124), where Homer's land bears them “wine of great clusters” (*Odyssey* 9.110–111, 9.357–358); this Cyclops also hunts with dogs and keeps cattle (130, 136; Merry on *Odyssey* 9.234). He cooks his guests: one he throws into the cauldron, the other he dashes against a rock and roasts in the fire (397–404), where Homer's dashes them to the ground like puppies, cuts them limb from limb and eats them like a mountain-bred lion (*Odyssey* 9.288–293; see [[like-puppies]] and [[the-devouring-lion]]); “the cooking”, Merry says, “is a later refinement, not found in the Homeric story” (Merry on *Odyssey* 9.288). His creed is the belly. The earth, willing or not, must grow grass for his flocks; he sacrifices to no one but himself, “and to the greatest of the divinities, this belly” (332–335), and calls wealth the god of the wise (316). The stake is a branch of olive, ἀκρεμὼν ἐλαίας (455), not a length of the Cyclops' club, which Merry thinks spoils the Homeric picture (Merry on *Odyssey* 9.326; *Odyssey* 9.319–328). There are no other Cyclopes at the cave and no escape under the rams. The satyrs, who had begged to share in the blinding, find their legs lame and their eyes full of dust when the time comes, and Odysseus does it with his own companions (469–475, 630–655); afterwards they lead the blind giant astray until he strikes his head on the rock (680–688). In the prophecy Odysseus too pays, “drifting a long time on the sea” (698–700), which Homer leaves to the Cyclops' prayer to his father (*Odyssey* 9.528–536). At the end Odysseus sails for home on the Sicilian sea, and the satyrs go with him to serve Bacchus again (702–709).

**The pun.** The play uses the pun before the false name is given. When Odysseus would keep the wine from the Cyclops' brothers, the drunken Cyclops boasts, μεθύω μέν, ἔμπας δʼ οὔτις ἂν ψαύσειέ μου, “I am drunk, but all the same no one could touch me” (535), and οὔτις, “no one”, is the name that Odysseus gives him a few lines later (549). When the blinded Cyclops threatens the men in his cave, he calls them οὐδὲν ὄντες, “nothings” (667; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]).
