In *Republic* 3 Socrates distinguishes three ways of telling a story:

- **simple narration** (ἁπλῆ διήγησις), in which the poet speaks as himself;
- **imitation** (μίμησις), in which he speaks as if he were the character;
- **a mixture** of the two, which is Homer's way.

He takes the opening of the Iliad as his example. Up to 1.16 Homer narrates, but from 1.17 he "speaks as if he were Chryses himself".

To show what pure narration would be, Socrates rewrites the scene in prose, turning every speech into reported speech: the priest came, prayed that the gods grant them to take Troy and return safely, and begged them to release his daughter out of reverence for the god (393d–394a). He says he will do it "without meter, for I am not a poet" (οὐ γάρ εἰμι ποιητικός).

The passage is one of the earliest pieces of literary theory in Greek, and the Iliad's first scene is its specimen. Socrates goes on to argue that the guardians of his ideal city should imitate only good men, if they imitate at all.
