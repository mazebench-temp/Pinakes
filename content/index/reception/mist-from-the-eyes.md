Athena's answer to Diomedes' prayer is a double gift. She puts his father's might in his breast, and she takes from his eyes the mist that was on them before, so that he may tell god from man (5.125–128). In the Iliad the second gift has a use in battle. Diomedes may wound Aphrodite but must not fight any other god, so he has to know them when he sees them (5.129–132; see [[achlys]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). Later writers kept the image and changed what the cleared eyes see.

**Alcibiades II.** In this dialogue, handed down among Plato's works but probably not his, Socrates meets Alcibiades on his way to pray and persuades him that he does not know what to pray for: he might ask the gods for evils, thinking them goods. He must wait until he has learned how to behave toward gods and men. Who will teach him? The man who cares for him, says Socrates, and he quotes Homer. As Athena took the mist from Diomedes' eyes, “so that he might know well both god and man”, this teacher must first take the mist from Alcibiades' soul, and then give him the means by which he will know “both evil and good” (150d–e). The second phrase is made on the pattern of Homer's. Alcibiades answers that the teacher may take away the mist or anything else he likes (150e; see [[plato]]).

**Virgil.** On the night Troy falls, Venus appears to her son and tells him that it is not Helen or Paris who is overturning the city, but the harshness of the gods. Then she clears his sight: “Look, for I will tear away the whole cloud that now, drawn over your eyes, dulls your mortal sight and darkens it with mist” (*Aeneid* 2.604–606). He sees Neptune shaking the walls with his trident, Juno at the Scaean gate calling the Greeks from the ships, Pallas on the citadel with her Gorgon, and Jupiter himself giving the Greeks their courage: “the dreadful faces and the great powers of the gods, hostile to Troy” (*Aeneid* 2.608–623; see [[virgil]]). Diomedes' cleared eyes let him fight; Aeneas' let him see that he cannot, and he leaves the city.

**Milton.** In the eleventh book of *Paradise Lost* the archangel Michael takes Adam up a hill to show him the future of his race. First he removes from Adam's eyes the film that the forbidden fruit, which had promised clearer sight, had bred, and purges the nerve of sight with euphrasy and rue, “for he had much to see” (*Paradise Lost* 11.411–416; see [[milton]]).

In each version a god or an angel clears a mortal's eyes, and he sees what is really there. For Diomedes it is the gods on the battlefield; for Aeneas, the gods destroying his city; for Alcibiades, the difference between good and evil; for Adam, the history of the world.
