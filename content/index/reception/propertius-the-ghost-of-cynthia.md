summary: In an elegy of his fourth book Propertius dreams that the dead Cynthia stands over his bed and reproaches him, as the soul of Patroclus stands over the sleeping Achilles. She has the hair and the eyes she had in life, asks why he did not pray for winds for her pyre, foretells that her bones will soon be mixed with his, and slips from his embrace. The first words of the poem, “the shades are something”, answer Achilles' cry on waking.

**Homer.** As Achilles sleeps on the shore, the soul of Patroclus comes to him, “like him in all things, in stature and in the fair eyes / and the voice”, and wearing such clothes as he had worn; it stands above his head and speaks: “You are asleep, and you have forgotten me, Achilles. / You were not careless of me while I lived, but you are now that I am dead” (23.62–70). It asks to be buried, foretells Achilles' death, and asks that their bones lie together in one urn (23.71–92). Achilles reaches out to it, “but did not take hold of it; and the soul went beneath the earth like smoke”, and he springs up and cries that even in the house of Hades there is some soul and phantom (23.99–104; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]], [[psyche]] and [[eidolon]]). On the next night, when the pyre will not burn, Achilles prays to the North Wind and the West Wind and promises them sacrifices (23.192–198; see [[boreas]] and [[zephyrus]]).

**Propertius.** “The shades are something: death does not end all, / and a pale ghost escapes the conquered pyre” (*Sunt aliquid Manes: letum non omnia finit, / luridaque evictos effugit umbra rogos*, Propertius 4.7.1–2). Cynthia, lately buried beside the noisy road, seemed to lean over his bed as sleep hung over him after his love's funeral (4.7.3–6). She had the same hair she was carried out with, the same eyes; her dress was charred at the side, the fire had eaten the beryl ring on her finger, and the water of Lethe had worn her lips; she breathed out living spirit and voice (4.7.7–11). Her first words are a reproach for sleep: “Faithless one, … can sleep already have power over you?” (*perfide … in te iam vires somnus habere potest?*, 4.7.13–14). Then she reproaches him for her funeral: no one saw him bowed at it, “Why did you not yourself pray for winds for my pyre, ungrateful man? / Why did my flames not smell of nard?” (*cur ventos non ipse rogis, ingrate, petisti?*, 4.7.27–32). She describes the two dwellings of the dead across the river, one for the guilty and one for the faithful (4.7.55–70), charges him with tasks, for her old nurse and her maid, the burning of the verses he made in her name and the epitaph for her tomb (4.7.71–86), and tells him not to despise dreams that come from the gates of the pious, since by night the shades are set free and wander, and at dawn must go back to Lethe (4.7.87–92). She ends: “Now let others possess you: soon I alone will hold you; / you will be with me, and I will wear down bones mixed with bones” (*mixtis ossibus ossa teram*, 4.7.93–94). And when she had finished her quarrel and her complaint, “the shade slipped away from my embrace” (*inter complexus excidit umbra meos*, 4.7.95–96).

**Homer and Propertius.** The elegy follows the course of Homer's scene and turns it into a lover's quarrel. The dead stand over the sleeper in their own likeness, reproach the living for forgetting them in sleep, speak of the pyre and its due, charge the sleeper with their wishes, foretell that their bones will be joined, and vanish from the arms that reach for them. The winds that Achilles prayed for are the winds that Propertius did not ask for, and the one urn that Patroclus asks for becomes Cynthia's threat. Walter Leaf, preferring the reading τι in Achilles' cry, “the soul is something even in Hades”, says that it must have been the reading Propertius had before him when he wrote *sunt aliquid Manes* (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.103; see the translation's note on 23.103–104).
