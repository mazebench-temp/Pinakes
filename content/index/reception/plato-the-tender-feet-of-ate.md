summary: In Plato's Symposium the tragic poet Agathon, praising Love as the youngest and tenderest of the gods, calls Homer to witness that a god can be tender. Homer's Ate, Blind Folly, has tender feet, for she walks not on the ground but on the heads of men; Love, says Agathon, walks on softer things still, the characters and souls of gods and men.

**Homer.** Agamemnon tells the assembly that he is not to blame for taking Achilles' prize, “but Zeus and Fate and the Erinys who walks in darkness, / who in the assembly cast savage blind folly into my mind” (19.86–88; see [[agamemnon]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]). Then he describes the power herself. Ἄτη, Blind Folly, is the eldest daughter of Zeus, and her feet are tender (ἁπαλοί), “for she does not step / upon the ground, but walks over the heads of men, / doing harm to men” (19.92–94; see [[ate]] and [[tender]]). In Phoenix's story of the Prayers her feet are her strength: “But Blind Folly is strong and sound of foot, and so she far / outruns them all” (9.505–506; see [[litai]]). Agamemnon goes on to tell how she blinded Zeus himself, who seized her by the hair and hurled her from the sky to the works of men (19.95–131; see [[the-birth-of-heracles]]). Hesiod gives her another parent: she is a daughter of Strife, born with Lawlessness, “close companions of each other” (*Theogony* 226–230; see [[hesiods-two-strifes]]).

**Agathon.** In the *Symposium* Agathon, the tragic poet whose victory the party celebrates, speaks after Aristophanes (see [[agathon]]). Love, he says, is the youngest of the gods, for he flees old age; and he is not older than Cronus and Iapetus, as Phaedrus said, since the old violence among the gods that Hesiod and Parmenides tell of, the gelding and the binding, came about through Necessity and not through Love (195a–c). Besides being young he is tender, but it needs a poet like Homer to show a god's tenderness: “For Homer says that Ate is a goddess and tender, her feet at least”, and Agathon quotes the two lines. It seems to him a fine proof of her tenderness that she walks not on what is hard but on what is soft. He will use the same proof for Love. Love walks neither on the earth nor on skulls, which are not very soft, but in the softest of all things: he has made his home in the characters and souls of gods and men, and not in every soul, for he leaves one whose character is hard and settles where he finds one soft (195c–e; see [[plato]]).

**Plato's text.** Plato's lines differ from ours in two small things, both of case: τῆς μένθʼ, “of her”, for τῇ μέν θʼ, and ἐπʼ οὔδεος, “on the ground”, for ἐπʼ οὔδει. The sense is the same. Walter Leaf, who counted the personified Ate of this book, with the Prayers of Book 9, among the latest parts of the poems, notes that Plato quotes the lines simply as Homer's, in a tone that shows no awareness that anyone else might have written them (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.91; see the translation's note on 19.91–94).

**Later readers.** Leaf adds that the idea of the tender feet was worked out by the Hellenistic poet Rhianus, whose lines he quotes: his Ate runs on tender feet over the tops of men's heads, unforeseen and unseen, doing service to Zeus, lord of the gods, and to Justice (on 19.92). Agamemnon's Ate is flung out of heaven by Zeus; Rhianus' works for him. The Byzantine lexicon called the Suda, under the word ἄτη, quotes the verse in which Zeus seizes her “by her head of shining tresses” (19.126) and explains that he took her from his own head, since she walks over the heads of men (Suda α 4350). Leaf found the same explanation in a scholion and called it amusing (on 19.126).
