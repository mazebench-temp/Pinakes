summary: In the sixth book of the Aeneid the Sibyl tells Aeneas of the punishments behind the walls of Tartarus, which he may not enter himself. Tityos lies stretched over nine iugera with a vulture at his immortal liver, a black rock hangs over Ixion and Pirithous before a feast they may not touch, sinners roll huge stones, and Theseus sits and will sit forever. Virgil takes the sinners of Odysseus' last sights in Odyssey 11, gives Tantalus' punishments to others, and makes Minos the judge of the dead that Homer's Minos is not.

**Homer.** After the heroes of the war, Odysseus sees Minos giving judgment to the dead, Orion hunting, and three men punished: Tityus, whom two vultures tear for the violence he did to Leto, Tantalus in the lake, and Sisyphus with his stone (*Odyssey* 11.568–600; see [[minos]], [[orion]], [[tityus]], [[tantalus]] and [[sisyphus]]). He sees them from where he sits by the pit, and W. W. Merry, who doubted the passage, contrasts Aeneas: “Odysseus did not, like Aeneas, make a descent into the netherworld, but he sits at the edge of the trench, and questions the shades as they come forward and taste the blood” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.565; see the Odyssey translation's note on 11.565–567).

**Charon and Minos.** At the Styx Charon refuses to carry the living: he was not glad, he says, to take on his lake Alcides, or Theseus and Pirithous, “though they were born of gods and unconquered in strength”; the one came to put the warder of Tartarus in chains and dragged him trembling from the king's own throne, the others set out to carry off the queen from the chamber of Dis (*Aeneid* 6.388–397, plain renderings throughout; see [[heracles-and-cerberus]], [[theseus]] and [[peirithous]]). In Homer the shade of Heracles tells how he brought the hound up out of Hades, and Theseus and Peirithous are the “glorious children of the gods” whom Odysseus would have stayed to see (*Odyssey* 11.623–626, 11.631; see [[heracles]]). Beyond the river the seats of the dead are not assigned without lot or judge: *quaesitor Minos urnam movet; ille silentum / consiliumque vocat vitasque et crimina discit*, “Minos, the examiner, shakes the urn; he calls the council of the silent and learns their lives and their crimes” (*Aeneid* 6.431–433). Merry warns against reading this later judge of the dead into Homer's Minos, who gives judgment among the dead as he did among the living (on *Odyssey* 11.568).

**The walls.** Where the road divides, the right-hand way leads under the walls of Dis to Elysium, and the left exacts the punishments of the wicked and sends them to Tartarus (*Aeneid* 6.540–543). Aeneas sees the walls, girt with a triple rampart and the flaming river Phlegethon, and hears the groans and the clank of chains, but the Sibyl tells him that no pure soul may set foot on the accursed threshold: Hecate herself showed her the punishments of the gods, and Cnossian Rhadamanthus rules there, chastising and hearing frauds (*Aeneid* 6.548–569; see [[rhadamanthus]] and [[tartarus]]). Within lie the Titans, the twin sons of Aloeus, who tried to tear down heaven and thrust Jupiter from his kingdom, and Salmoneus, who aped his thunder (*Aeneid* 6.580–594), men whom Homer names in this book only as the giants Apollo killed before they were grown and as Tyro's father (*Odyssey* 11.235–236, 11.305–320; see [[otus]], [[ephialtes]] and [[salmoneus]]).

**The punished.** Then Tityos, “the nursling of Earth, the mother of all”, whose body is stretched over nine whole *iugera*, while a monstrous vulture with a hooked beak crops his immortal liver and his entrails, fertile for punishment, searches them for its feasts and dwells deep in his breast, and the fibers that grow again are given no rest (*Aeneid* 6.595–600). Homer's Tityus is “the son of glorious Earth”, stretched over nine plethra, with two vultures (*Odyssey* 11.576–579), and Merry cites Virgil's lines on δέρτρον, the caul (on *Odyssey* 11.579). Then the Lapiths, Ixion and Pirithous, *quos super atra silex iam iam lapsura cadentique / imminet adsimilis*, “over whom a black rock, just about to slip, hangs like one falling”, while golden couches gleam and a feast of royal luxury is set before their faces, and the eldest of the Furies lies beside it and forbids them to touch the tables (*Aeneid* 6.601–607; see [[ixion]] and [[lapiths]]). These are the punishments of Tantalus, given to others: the rock that the tragedians hang over his head, and the food and drink that Homer's Tantalus can never reach (*Odyssey* 11.582–592; see [[tantalus]]). Merry quotes the rock “ever about to fall” for Homer's Heracles, “like one ever about to shoot” (on *Odyssey* 11.608). Others, of the many kinds of sinners, *saxum ingens volvunt*, “roll a huge stone”, as Sisyphus does alone in Homer (*Aeneid* 6.616; *Odyssey* 11.593–600), or hang stretched on the spokes of wheels; *sedet aeternumque sedebit / infelix Theseus*, “unhappy Theseus sits, and will sit forever”, and Phlegyas, most wretched, warns them all with a great voice through the shades: “Learn justice, being warned, and not to scorn the gods” (*Aeneid* 6.616–620).

**A hundred tongues.** The Sibyl ends: if she had a hundred tongues and a hundred mouths and a voice of iron, she could not tell all the forms of crime or run through all the names of the punishments (*Aeneid* 6.625–627). It is the Iliad's prayer before the Catalogue, “not even if I had ten tongues and ten mouths, / a voice that could not break, and a heart of bronze within me” (2.489–490; see [[catalogue-of-ships]]), and the verse before it, with its first word changed, is the one with which Odysseus breaks off his own catalogue of the dead women: “But all of them I could not tell or name” (*Odyssey* 11.328, as *Iliad* 2.488; again *Odyssey* 11.517).
