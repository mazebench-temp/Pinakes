The Iliad names Hypsipyle once, as the mother of Euneus (7.469; see [[hypsipyle]] and [[euneus]]), and says nothing of how Jason came to Lemnos or of what the women of the island had done. Pindar's odes, of the fifth century BCE, give two of the oldest glimpses of that story that survive.

**The race in armor.** The fourth Olympian, for Psaumis of Camarina, ends with a tale of Hypsipyle's games. Trial, Pindar says, is the test of mortals, and it was trial that freed Erginus, son of Clymenus, from the scorn of the Lemnian women, who laughed at one of the Argonauts for his gray hair. He won the race in armor, and as he went to take his crown he said to Hypsipyle: “Such am I for speed; my hands and heart are equal. Gray hair grows often on young men too, before the time that suits their age” (*Olympian* 4.19–27).

**The seed of Cyrene.** The fourth Pythian, for Arcesilas, king of Cyrene, tells the voyage of the Argo at length. On the way home the heroes came to “the race of the man-slaying Lemnian women”, held games with a garment for the prize, and lay with them; and in those foreign furrows the fated day or night received the seed from which the king's house would grow (*Pythian* 4.251–257). The line ran from the Argonaut Euphamus through Lacedaemon and Thera to the founding of Cyrene. Herodotus knew a related tradition. The Minyans, descendants of the heroes who sailed in the Argo, born to them when they put in at Lemnos, were driven from the island by the Pelasgians and came to Sparta, and from there to Thera (4.145; see [[herodotus]]).

Pindar calls the women ἀνδροφόνοι, “man-slaying”, the epithet that Homer gives most often to Hector. Homer's Lemnos has no such past. It is a place of trade, whose king sells wine to the Achaeans and buys a son of Priam (7.467–475, 21.40–41; see [[lemnos]]). For the story itself, see [[the-lemnian-women]]; for its later tellings, [[apollonius-lemnos]] and [[euripides-hypsipyle]].
