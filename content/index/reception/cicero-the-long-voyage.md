**Nestor's voyage.** After the second strife at Tenedos, Nestor sailed on with Diomedes, and Menelaus overtook them at Lesbos as they pondered the long voyage (δολιχὸν πλόον ὁρμαίνοντας): whether to go home above Chios, by the island of Psyria, keeping it on their left, or below Chios, past windy Mimas (*Odyssey* 3.165–172). The god whom they asked for a sign sent them neither way, but straight across the open sea to Euboea, and they put in at Geraestus by night (*Odyssey* 3.173–179; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]], [[psyria]], [[mimas]] and [[geraestus]]). The three phrases that Cicero takes from the passage occur nowhere else in Homer.

**Vibo and Regium.** Writing to Atticus from Vibo, where he is staying with Sicca, Cicero says that he has sailed so far more comfortably than briskly, mostly under oars, and that when he reaches Regium “we shall consider there the long voyage” (δολιχὸν πλόον): by merchant ship to Patrae, or in small boats to Leucopetra of the Tarentines and on to Corcyra, and, if in a cargo ship, whether straight through the strait or by way of Syracuse (*Letters to Atticus* 16.6.1). Like Nestor at Lesbos, he has to choose his course before the open sea.

**The road from Sinuessa.** In a later letter the phrase comes back with its participle. Cicero had risen before dawn at his villa near Sinuessa and come at daybreak to the bridge at Minturnae where the road turns off toward Arpinum, when a courier met him, “who found me pondering the long voyage” (δολιχὸν πλόον ὁρμαίνοντα). Then: “here is your second letter, in which you urge me ‘past windy Mimas’, keeping, that is, the Appian Way ‘on the left’” (παρʼ ἠνεμόεντα Μίμαντα, *Appiam scilicet* ἐπʼ ἀριστέρʼ ἔχοντα). So he spent that day at Aquinum, after “a rather long journey and a bad road” (*Letters to Atticus* 16.13.1–2). The sea route has become a road, and the phrases come from both of Nestor's courses: “past windy Mimas” is the course below Chios, and “keeping it on our left” belongs to the course above Chios, by Psyria (*Odyssey* 3.171–172).

**Cicero's Homer.** These are not the only verses of *Odyssey* 3 in the letters. Waiting for Caesar, Cicero wished for Homer's Minerva disguised as Mentor, to ask her Telemachus' question, “Mentor, how then shall I go?”, and when Caesar came on him suddenly he gave himself her answer, in her own words, ἄλλα μὲν αὐτός … ἄλλα δὲ καὶ δαίμων ὑποθήσεται, “as he says” (*Letters to Atticus* 9.8.2, 9.15.4, quoting *Odyssey* 3.22 and 3.26–27; see [[athena-as-mentor-in-later-writers]]). In *On Old Age* he gives Nestor's “third generation of men” as his license to praise himself (see [[nestors-three-generations]]).
