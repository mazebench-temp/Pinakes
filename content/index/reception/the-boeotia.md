The episodes of the Iliad had names long before the poem was divided into twenty-four books. Herodotus quotes lines of our Book 6 as coming from "the Aristeia of Diomedes" (2.116). Aelian lists "the Catalogue of Ships" among the old titles of the episodes that rhapsodes performed separately (*Varia Historia* 13.14), and Thucydides already refers to Homer's "catalogue of ships" (1.10.4). The traditional titles of Book 2 are "the Dream" (Ὄνειρος) for its first part and "the Boeotia" (Βοιωτία) for the Catalogue, which is named after its first entry.

The Boeotian entry has more leaders and more towns than any other: five leaders (2.494–495) and twenty-nine towns. Its fifty ships carry 120 young men each (2.509–510), the largest crews the Catalogue records. Yet the Boeotians play a small part in the rest of the poem.

Why begin with Boeotia? The simplest answer is Aulis. The fleet gathered at Aulis on the Boeotian coast (2.303), and Aulis is among the first towns named (2.496). Many scholars have concluded that the Catalogue began as a list of the fleet assembled at Aulis, and was adapted, not always smoothly, to the tenth year at Troy. Protesilaus is dead and Philoctetes left on Lemnos, and their men have new leaders (2.698–709, 2.721–728). Others have linked the prominence of Boeotia to the Boeotian tradition of catalogue poetry associated with Hesiod. Neither explanation can be proved.

The title raised a historical problem that Thucydides noticed. By tradition the Boeotians came into the land from Arne in Thessaly, driven out by the Thessalians, only sixty years after the fall of Troy. How then could Boeotians sail from Boeotia? Thucydides answers that a portion of them was already settled there, "and some of these went to Troy" (1.12.3). The Catalogue itself has a Boeotian Arne (2.507).
