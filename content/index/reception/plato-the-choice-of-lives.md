summary: At the end of the Republic the souls of the dead choose their next lives, most of them by the habits of the life before. The soul of Ajax, still remembering the judgment of the arms, will not be a man again and takes the life of a lion; Agamemnon's, hating mankind for what it suffered, takes an eagle's; and Odysseus', choosing last and cured of ambition by the memory of its toils, looks for the quiet life of a private man. Proclus read the myth as Plato's own Nekyia, modeled on Homer's.

**Er's tale.** Socrates ends the *Republic* with a story of the dead, and begins it with a jest on Homer: “But it is not a tale to Alcinous that I will tell you, but the tale of a valiant man, Er the son of Armenius, a Pamphylian by birth” (*Republic* 10.614b, plain renderings throughout; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-9]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). Er was killed in battle; when the dead were taken up on the tenth day, already decayed, his body was taken up sound, and on the twelfth day, as he lay on the pyre, he came back to life and told what he had seen in the other world (614b). There judges sit between two openings in the earth and two in the sky, and send the just to the right and upward, the unjust to the left and down (614c). After their journeys the souls come to choose their next lives from the patterns set before them, and the prophet who sets them out promises that “even for the one who comes last, if he chooses with intelligence and lives earnestly, there lies a life content, not bad” (619b).

**The heroes.** The sight, Er said, was pitiable, laughable and wonderful, for most chose by the habit of their former life (619e–620a). Orpheus' soul chose the life of a swan, from hatred of the race of women, at whose hands he had died, and Thamyras' the life of a nightingale (620a; see [[plato-thersites]] and [[thamyris]]). “The soul that drew the twentieth lot chose the life of a lion: it was the soul of Ajax, son of Telamon, which shunned becoming a man, remembering the judgment of the arms. After it came Agamemnon's; it too, in hatred of the human race because of what it had suffered, took in exchange the life of an eagle” (620b; see [[ajax]], [[agamemnon]], [[lion]] and [[eagle]]). Atalanta's soul took the honors of a male athlete; Epeius', the nature of a craftswoman; and far off among the last, the soul of the jester Thersites put on an ape (620b–c; see [[epeius]] and [[thersites]]). “By chance the soul of Odysseus had drawn the last lot of all, and came to choose; and from the memory of its former toils it had recovered from its love of honor, and went about for a long time looking for the life of a private man who minded his own business; and with difficulty it found one lying somewhere, neglected by the others, and said when it saw it that it would have done the same if it had drawn the first lot, and chose it gladly” (620c–d; see [[odysseus]]).

**Homer's souls.** The choices go on from the souls of *Odyssey* 11. There the soul of Ajax alone stands apart, still angry over the victory that Odysseus won “when I pleaded my case beside the ships / for the armor of Achilles”, with the sons of the Trojans and Pallas Athena for judges, and goes off without a word (*Odyssey* 11.543–564; see [[the-judgment-of-arms]] and [[virgil-the-silence-of-dido]]). Agamemnon's soul tells how Aegisthus with his ruinous wife killed him at a feast, “as one kills an ox at the manger”, and Clytemnestra killed Cassandra beside him, and warns Odysseus that “there is no trusting women any more” (*Odyssey* 11.405–456; see [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]). Odysseus' soul, choosing last, takes the obscure life that the others passed by; in the same book Achilles' soul would rather serve for hire on earth than be lord over all the dead (*Odyssey* 11.489–491; see [[rather-a-hired-laborer]]).

**Plato's Nekyia.** Proclus, who calls both Homer's book and Plato's myths of the dead Nekyias, takes the jest about the tale to Alcinous as Plato all but saying outright that he has set Homer's Nekyia before himself as his model (*On the Republic* I 169 Kroll; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-11]] and [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). On the choices he reads each soul as a form of life. Ajax's is a manly life carried to excess in spirit by a contentious habit of soul, and so it puts on a manly animal that is without reason; its twentieth lot, by his master's reckoning, marks the life of spirit that stands next below the life of reason, since ten belongs to reason as the one to intellect. Agamemnon's soul pursued the kingly life raised above the many, but through another passion, hatred of men, it moves into the eagle, the bird of Zeus, flying on high and king of the birds, but without reason (II 316–318 Kroll). Odysseus' soul shows prudence in choosing: the private man is the opposite of the tyrant and the ambitious, the man who minds his own business the opposite of the man of many toils, and its choice made good the prophet's word that even the last comer, choosing with intelligence, finds a life content and not bad (II 320–321 Kroll).

**The Apology.** In his speech at his trial Socrates imagines death as a journey to the place where all the dead are, and the meetings there: the true judges, Minos, Rhadamanthys, Aeacus and Triptolemus; Orpheus, Musaeus, Hesiod and Homer; Palamedes and Ajax son of Telamon, and any other of the ancients who died by an unjust judgment, with whose sufferings he would compare his own; and the leader of the great army against Troy, or Odysseus, or Sisyphus, whom he would examine as he examined men here (*Apology* 40e–41c; see [[palamedes]], [[minos]] and [[sisyphus]]). Cicero renders the passage in Latin, with Palamedes and Ajax “overcome by an unjust judgment” and “the prudence of Ulysses and Sisyphus” (*Tusculan Disputations* 1.98, plain renderings; see [[cicero]]).
