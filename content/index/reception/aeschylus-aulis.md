The first choral song of the *Agamemnon* (458 BCE) looks back ten years to the fleet's departure. At Aulis two eagles, kings of birds appearing to the two kings of the ships, one black and one white-tailed, swooped on a hare heavy with young and devoured her with her unborn brood (104–120). Calchas read the sign. The eagles were the sons of Atreus, and in time their expedition would take Priam's city. But Artemis hates "the eagles' feast", and he prayed that she might not send contrary winds to hold the fleet and demand another sacrifice (122–159). Each of the three stanzas ends with the refrain "Cry sorrow, sorrow, but may the good prevail".

The Iliad's portent at Aulis, which Odysseus recalls in Book 2, has the same setting, the same interpreter and the same shape. A beast of prey devours a helpless mother and her young, and Calchas turns the sign into a forecast of the war (2.299–332). In Homer the sign comes from Zeus and is wholly good. The victims are counted, nine birds for nine years, and Troy falls in the tenth; the serpent is turned to stone as a sign for all to see. Aeschylus keeps the promise of victory but makes the omen double-edged. The devoured young point ahead to Iphigenia, whom Agamemnon sacrifices when Artemis holds back the fleet (184–247), and to the children who die in the house of Atreus.

The Iliad knows nothing of that sacrifice. In Book 9 Agamemnon offers Achilles any of his three daughters, one of them Iphianassa (9.145), and nothing suggests that a daughter has been killed. Homer's Aulis is a place of good signs: the portent, and Zeus' lightning on the right as the fleet set sail (2.350–353). The sacrifice was told in the *Cypria*, where, in [[proclus|Proclus' summary]], Artemis snatches Iphigenia away from the altar and puts a deer in her place.
