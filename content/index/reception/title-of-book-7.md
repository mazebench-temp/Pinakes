The traditional titles of Book 7 are Ἕκτορος καὶ Αἴαντος μονομαχία, “the single combat of Hector and Ajax”, and νεκρῶν ἀναίρεσις, “the taking up of the dead”. Like the two titles of Book 4, they divide the book in two (see [[titles-of-book-4]]). The first names the challenge and the duel (7.17–312). The second names the truce in which both sides gather and burn their dead, with the councils that lead to it (7.313–432). Neither names the book's last scenes, the building of the Achaean wall and the gods' quarrel over it (7.433–464; see [[the-achaean-wall]]), or the wine from Lemnos (7.465–482).

**Single combat.** μονομαχία, “fighting alone”, is later Greek. Book 3's duel has the same noun in its title, “the single combat of Alexander and Menelaus” (see [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]]). Book 7 has its own phrase for the thing. Apollo proposes that Hector challenge one of the Danaans οἰόθεν οἶος, “man to man, alone” (7.39), and Ajax takes up the phrase when he faces Hector: “now you will know clearly, man to man, what kind of chiefs are among the Danaans too” (7.226–227). It occurs nowhere else in Homer. See [[single-combat]] and [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]].

**The taking up of the dead.** ἀναίρεσις is the noun of ἀναιρέω, “take up”. In classical Greek it was the word for recovering the bodies of the fallen after a battle, under a truce that the side which had lost the field asked of the victors. Thucydides uses it in describing the Athenian public funeral, at which an empty bier was carried for the missing, “who could not be found for recovery” (ἐς ἀναίρεσιν, 2.34.3). Euripides' *Suppliants* turns on such a request: the mothers of the Seven who fell at Thebes beg Athens to win back their sons' bodies (see [[euripides]] and [[the-seven-against-thebes]]). The *Cypria*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, told of the same act at the start of the war: after Achilles routed the Trojans at the landing, the Achaeans took up their dead, and then sent envoys to demand Helen and the possessions (see [[the-embassy-to-troy]]).

In Book 7 no side concedes defeat. Nestor proposes that the Achaeans gather and burn their own dead (7.331–335; see [[nestor]]). Priam then sends Idaeus to ask both armies “to cease from grim-sounding war until we have burned the dead” (7.375–377, 7.394–396), and Agamemnon grants it without bargaining, “for one should not grudge the corpses of the dead, once they have died, the quick comfort of fire” (7.409–410; see [[idaeus]] and [[agamemnon]]). The poem's own words for the work are plain. On both sides some go “to bring the dead”, others for wood (νέκυάς τʼ ἀγέμεν, 7.418; νέκυς τʼ ἀγέμεν, 7.420). The two parties meet on the plain, wash the blood from bodies they can hardly tell apart, lift them onto the wagons weeping, and burn them (7.421–432). See [[the-truce-for-the-dead]] and [[cremation]].
