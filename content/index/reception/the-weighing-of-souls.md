**Homer.** At noon on the second day of battle Zeus holds out his golden scales with two fates of death in them, one for each army. The pan that sinks carries death: “the fated day of the Achaeans sank” (8.72), and the Achaeans' fates settle on the earth while the Trojans' rise toward the sky. In Book 22 he weighs two men, Achilles and Hector, in the same verses, and Hector's day sinks and goes down to Hades (22.209–213; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). Later poets took up the second form, two heroes and their fates in the balance.

**Aeschylus.** Plutarch, showing the young how to read poetry, quotes the weighing of Achilles and Hector from Book 22, and goes on: Aeschylus built a whole tragedy around the story, entitling it *Weighing of Souls* (Ψυχοστασία), and set beside the pans of Zeus Thetis on one side and Eos on the other, praying for their sons as they fought. The sons are Achilles and Memnon, the son of the Dawn, whom Achilles killed after the end of the Iliad (see [[ethiopians]]). “This is plain to everyone,” Plutarch adds, that it is a fiction made up “for the pleasure or the astonishment of the hearer” (μυθοποίημα καὶ πλάσμα πρὸς ἡδονὴν ἢ ἔκπληξιν ἀκροατοῦ, *How the Young Man Should Study Poetry* 2, 16F–17A). The play is lost. Its title puts souls (ψυχαί) in the pans where Homer has κῆρες, fates of death (see [[ker-doom]] and [[aeschylus]]).

**Virgil.** At the end of the *Aeneid* Aeneas and Turnus close in single combat and clash shields like two bulls fighting while the herd waits in fear to see which will lead it (12.715–724). Then “Jupiter himself holds up two scales, their beam made level, and sets in them the different fates of the two: whom the struggle dooms, and with whose weight death sinks” (*Aeneid* 12.725–727). Virgil does not say which pan sinks. Turnus springs up and puts his whole body into a blow, and his sword breaks: in his haste, it is said, he had snatched up his charioteer's sword instead of his father's, and the mortal blade shatters like ice on the armor Vulcan made. He flees (12.728–746). Virgil keeps Homer's sign, the sinking scale of death, and leaves the reading of it to the fight.

**Milton.** In the fourth book of *Paradise Lost* Gabriel's angels have found Satan in Eden by night, and the two leaders are about to fight. “The Eternal, to prevent such horrid fray, / Hung forth in Heaven his golden scales”, now the sign of the Balance in the zodiac, seen “Betwixt Astrea and the Scorpion sign”, in which he first weighed all things created and now weighs all events, “Battles and realms”. In them he puts two weights, “The sequel each of parting and of fight”, and “The latter quick up flew, and kicked the beam”. Gabriel tells Satan to look up and read his lot in the sign, “Where thou art weighed, and shown how light, how weak, / If thou resist”; Satan, seeing “His mounted scale aloft”, flees, and the shades of night flee with him (*Paradise Lost* 4.990–1015). Milton turns Homer's sign upside down. In the Iliad the pan that sinks carries death; in Milton the scale that flies up shows a cause too light, as the writing on Belshazzar's wall did, “Thou art weighed in the balances, and art found wanting” (Daniel 5:27). And where Homer's scales decide a battle, Milton's prevent one (see [[milton]]).
