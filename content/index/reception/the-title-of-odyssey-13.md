summary: Book 13 of the Odyssey is headed Ὀδυσσέως ἀπόπλους παρὰ Φαιάκων καὶ ἄφιξις εἰς Ἰθάκην, “the departure of Odysseus from the Phaeacians and his arrival in Ithaca”, a heading that answers the sixth book's “arrival of Odysseus among the Phaeacians”. The arrival comes in stages, the landing while he sleeps, the waking in a land he does not know, and the moment when Athena scatters the mist, and the phrase of the title is spoken in the book only by Odysseus, to deny that he has come. Ancient writers named the event the putting ashore, ἔκθεσις, or the sailing in, κατάπλους, and Monro saw in it the turn of the poem from fairy tale to the world of the Trojan story.

The title of *Odyssey* 13 in Monro's edition is Ὀδυσσέως ἀπόπλους παρὰ Φαιάκων καὶ ἄφιξις εἰς Ἰθάκην, “the departure of Odysseus from the Phaeacians and his arrival in Ithaca” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, heading of Book 13); this library calls the book “The Departure from the Phaeacians and the Arrival in Ithaca”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter of the alphabet, this one is ν, nu (see [[title-of-book-5]]). Monro's volume begins with it, as Merry's ends with the book before (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-12]]). The voyage and the landing are told under [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]], the fate of the ship that brought him under [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]], the people he leaves under [[phaeacians]] and the island he comes to under [[ithaca]].

**The words of the title.** Neither noun is Homer's. The verb of the first, ἀποπλείειν, “sail away”, occurs three times, and never of this voyage: Achilles advises the Achaeans “to sail away home” (*Iliad* 9.418, again 9.685), and Telemachus' ship is told to sail on to the city (*Odyssey* 16.331, a plain rendering). The book tells the departure in the words of escort. Odysseus asks the Phaeacians to “send me on my way unharmed” (πέμπετέ με … ἀπήμονα, *Odyssey* 13.39), counts as accomplished “an escort and gifts of friendship” (πομπὴ καὶ φίλα δῶρα, *Odyssey* 13.41; see [[pompe]]), and takes leave of Arete with “But I am going home” (αὐτὰρ ἐγὼ νέομαι, *Odyssey* 13.61). The verb of ἄφιξις, “arrival”, comes once in the book, and for the next stage of the journey: “But you yourself go first of all to the swineherd” (συβώτην εἰσαφικέσθαι, *Odyssey* 13.404; for the noun see [[the-title-of-odyssey-6]]). It is Aristotle's verb for this point of the story when he sums up the whole Odyssey in a sentence: “he himself arrives, storm-tossed (αὐτὸς δὲ ἀφικνεῖται χειμασθείς), and after making himself known to certain people he attacks” (*Poetics* 1455b21–22; see [[aristotle-the-story-of-the-odyssey]]). εἰς Ἰθάκην, “to Ithaca”, is the poem's own phrase, but in this book only Odysseus says it, and both times to deny that he has arrived. The Phaeacians, he complains on waking, “told me / they would bring me to clear-seen Ithaca, and they have not done it” (ἄξειν εἰς Ἰθάκην εὐδείελον, *Odyssey* 13.211–212), and to the goddess he says, “I do not think / that I have come to clear-seen Ithaca” (*Odyssey* 13.324–325; see [[clear-seen]]). The verb and the phrase stand together twice in Homer, both times in the mouths of the companions, who never came there: on Aeaea they welcome Odysseus back “as if we had come to Ithaca, our native land” (ὡς εἴ τʼ εἰς Ἰθάκην ἀφικοίμεθα πατρίδα γαῖαν, *Odyssey* 10.420), and on Thrinacia Eurylochus vows a temple to the Sun, “if we ever come to Ithaca, our native land” (*Odyssey* 12.345). The heading gives the people's name in its later form, Φαιάκων, where Homer's is Φαιήκων (*Odyssey* 13.12, 13.149, 13.175), as Merry's heading of Book 6 has Φαίακας. The book joins the heading's preposition to the Phaeacians once, when Odysseus wishes on waking that his goods “had stayed there among the Phaeacians” (παρὰ Φαιήκεσσιν, *Odyssey* 13.204).

**Two arrivals.** The heading answers the one that Merry gives the sixth book, Ὀδυσσέως ἄφιξις εἰς Φαίακας, “the arrival of Odysseus among the Phaeacians” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, heading of Book 6; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-6]]). Between the two stand Merry's headings for the entry into the house of Alcinous and the introduction to the Phaeacians, and the four books of the tale (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-7]], [[the-title-of-odyssey-8]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]), so that the stay among the Phaeacians begins with one ἄφιξις and ends with an ἀπόπλους and another ἄφιξις. The two arrivals are told alike. On Scheria Odysseus crept under two bushes, “one of wild olive, one of olive”, and slept (*Odyssey* 5.476–493); here he is laid asleep on the sand, and his gifts are set by the trunk of an olive (*Odyssey* 13.119–122). Both times he wakes in a land he does not know and asks the same question: “Ah me, to the land of what mortals have I come this time? / Are they arrogant and savage and unjust, / or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods?” (*Odyssey* 13.200–202 = 6.119–121; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]). Both times Athena comes to him in disguise, on Scheria “like a young girl, a maiden, carrying a water jar” (*Odyssey* 7.20), here “in form like a young man, a herdsman of sheep” (*Odyssey* 13.222; see [[athena]]). Monro's heading of the next book names the stage that this one prepares: Ὀδυσσέως πρὸς Εὔμαιον ὁμιλία, “the talk of Odysseus with Eumaeus” (Monro, heading of Book 14; see [[eumaeus]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-14]]).

**What the title covers.** The departure takes the first 125 of the book's 440 lines. It begins in the hall of Alcinous, at the end of the tale, where the Phaeacians sit spellbound (*Odyssey* 13.1–2 = 11.333–334), and goes on through the further gifts, a day spent waiting for the sun to set, the farewell to Alcinous and the cup put into Arete's hand (*Odyssey* 13.3–69; see [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]), and the voyage by night (*Odyssey* 13.70–95). Then “the seafaring ship drew near to the island” (*Odyssey* 13.95), and the arrival is made before Odysseus knows it: the Phaeacians row into the harbor of Phorcys, lift him out, “and laid him down on the sand, overcome by sleep” (*Odyssey* 13.113–124; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]). The heading has no place for the sixty lines that follow, in which the poem leaves him asleep and turns to Poseidon's complaint, Zeus' advice, the ship turned to stone on its way home and the Phaeacians' sacrifice to avert the old prophecy (*Odyssey* 13.125–187; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]). The rest of the book, more than half of it, is the arrival in its other senses. Odysseus wakes “where he slept in the land of his fathers—and he did not know it” (*Odyssey* 13.187–188), for Athena has poured a mist around him and “all things appeared in another shape to their lord” (*Odyssey* 13.194; see the translation's note on 13.189–191); he meets the goddess and lies to her (*Odyssey* 13.221–286); and the land is his again only when she scatters the mist and shows him the harbor, the olive, the cave and Mount Neritum (*Odyssey* 13.344–354; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]] and [[neritum]]). The last eighty lines prepare the homecoming that is still to come: the gifts are hidden in the cave, the two plan the suitors' death beneath the olive, and she changes him into a beggar (*Odyssey* 13.361–438; see [[the-suitors]]). Monro explains the delay: had Athena not first consulted with him and changed his appearance, “Ulysses would have gone straight to his palace, and all would have been lost” (Monro on *Odyssey* 13.191).

**Ancient names for the landing.** The event has names in the critics. Aristotle calls it τὰ περὶ τὴν ἔκθεσιν, “the things about the putting ashore”, and counts it among the irrational things in the Odyssey that “would clearly be intolerable, if a bad poet had made them; as it is, the poet conceals the absurdity, sweetening it with his other virtues” (*Poetics* 1460a35–b2; see [[alogon]] and [[aristotle-the-putting-ashore]]). The *Divisions* handed down under Aristotle's name take the same scene as their example of a rhetorical problem, the kind that arises “when someone accuses or defends the poets”: “why did the companions put Odysseus, most prudent as he was, ashore on the island asleep?” (διατί τὸν Ὀδυσσέα οἱ ἑταῖροι φρονιμώτατον ὄντα ἐξέθηκαν εἰς τὴν νῆσον καθεύδοντα, *Divisions* 2.42, a plain rendering), where the poem's “companions” are the Phaeacian crew, “the noble escorts” (*Odyssey* 13.71). The noun and the verb are compounds of the poem's own verb, ἔθεσαν, “laid him down” (*Odyssey* 13.119). Strabo calls it a sailing in, τὸν εἰς τὴν Ἰθάκην κατάπλουν τοῦ Ὀδυσσέως, “the sailing in of Odysseus to Ithaca”, and names it with “the killing of the suitors” (τὴν μνηστηροφονίαν) and the battle that the Ithacans fought against him in the country. A man who takes the poet's marvels for history, Ocean and Hades and the cattle of the Sun, so plainly falsifies the poet, he says, that he is not worth arguing with, any more than if he said that the sailing in, the killing of the suitors and the battle happened in just that way (*Geography* 1.2.11, plain renderings; see [[strabo]]). Strabo's second name is Monro's heading of the twenty-second book, Μνηστηροφονία (Monro, heading of Book 22).

**Between the two halves.** The book stands at the turn of the poem. The ship carries “a man whose counsels were like the gods', / who before had suffered very many sorrows in his heart, / passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves; / but now he slept motionless, forgetful of all he had suffered” (*Odyssey* 13.89–92), the sorrows of the opening lines, where “on the sea he suffered many sorrows in his heart” (*Odyssey* 1.4; see [[proem]]). Monro finds the change in the poem itself. The Phaeacian episode is “distinctly märchenhaft”, a fairy tale, “though the genius of the poet has given it a human interest which rises far above that level” (Monro, Appendix I §2). “From the thirteenth book onwards the character of the narrative perceptibly changes. … Like Ulysses after his landing in Ithaca, when Athene removed the mist from his eyes, we find ourselves in a familiar world—the world of the Trojan story” (Monro, Appendix I §3), and the false stories that Odysseus tells from now on “all turn upon events and characters in the Trojan war”, the first of them in this book, to the goddess, about the spoil from Troy (Monro, Appendix I §3; *Odyssey* 13.256–286). In the latter half of the poem, he writes, “the story is by no means in the fanciful vein which characterizes most of the earlier books”, though among the incidents “which may be thought to be of the fairy-tale order” he places the changes of form that Athena works on Odysseus, one of which ends this book (Monro, Appendix I §6; *Odyssey* 13.429–438; see [[shape-changing]]).

**The end of the book.** The book does not end with the arrival. Odysseus and the goddess part, “When the two had made their plans, they parted. She then / went to shining Lacedaemon, after the son of Odysseus” (*Odyssey* 13.439–440; see [[parted-in-friendship]]), and the next book begins, “But he went up from the harbor by the rugged path” (*Odyssey* 14.1; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]). “The book ends in the middle of a sentence”, Monro notes: ἡ μὲν … αὐτὰρ ὁ, “she went to Sparta, while he &c.” (Monro on *Odyssey* 13.440). The division between the books falls between the halves of one sentence, as the title joins a departure and an arrival.

**The landing retold.** The poem tells the landing again, once in this book and three times later. Poseidon complains that the Phaeacians “carried him asleep in a swift ship over the sea / and set him down in Ithaca, and gave him untold gifts” (*Odyssey* 13.134–135), and Odysseus tells his son “the truth” in nearly the same words: the Phaeacians “carried me asleep in a swift ship over the sea / and set me down in Ithaca, and gave me splendid gifts, / bronze and gold in plenty, and woven clothing”, which lie hidden in caves (*Odyssey* 16.226–232, plain renderings; see [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]). In his false tale to Penelope the departure is put off: the Phaeacians honored Odysseus like a god, gave him many gifts and were willing to send him home, but he thought it better to collect goods as he went far over the earth (*Odyssey* 19.278–284, plain renderings). When at last he tells her his true story, it ends with the Phaeacians, who “sent him in a ship to his dear native land, / giving him bronze and gold in plenty, and clothing”, and with that he falls asleep (*Odyssey* 23.338–343, plain renderings).

**Later summaries.** Hyginus, in the fable headed *Odyssea*, ends the wanderings in a few words and adds a wreck that Homer does not have: “Alcinous received him generously as a guest, honored him with gifts and sent him home to his native Ithaca. By Mercury's anger he suffered shipwreck a second time. After the twentieth year, his companions lost, he returned alone to his native land” (*Alcinous hospitio liberaliter acceptum donisque decoratum in patriam Ithacam dimisit. Ira Mercurii iterum naufragium fecit. Post vicesimum annum sociis amissis solus in patriam redit*; Hyginus, *Fabulae*, plain renderings throughout). The *Panegyric of Messalla*, transmitted among the poems of Tibullus, runs through the wanderings from the Cicones to Calypso and ends them with “the Phaeacian land, the end of his wretched wandering” (*finis et erroris miseri Phaeacia tellus*), leaving open whether these places “are known among our lands, or fable gave a new world to his wanderings” ([Tibullus] 3.7.54–80). Ovid, measuring his exile against the wanderings of “the Neritian leader”, grants that a warrior goddess brought Ulysses help, that “the greatest part of his labors is fiction”, and that “at last he touched the household gods he sought, and reached the fields he had long sought” (*denique quaesitos tetigit tamen ille Penates, / quaeque diu petiit, contigit arva tamen*, *Tristia* 1.5.57–58, 1.5.76–82; see [[ovid]]). The Suda keeps no gods and no stone ship: Phoenicians found Odysseus floating on a plank after Charybdis and brought him naked to Idomeneus in Crete, who kept him through the winter and sent him to Phaeacia, “now called Corcyra”, and the Phaeacians sent him on “with two ships and companions” (Suda χ 144; see [[scylla-and-charybdis-in-later-writers]]). The Phoenicians and Idomeneus are those of the story Odysseus invents in this book, where Phoenician sailors set the stranger down asleep on the sand (*Odyssey* 13.272–286; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]).
