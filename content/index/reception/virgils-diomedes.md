In Book 5 Diomedes nearly kills Aeneas. He crushes his hip with a stone that two men of today could not carry (see [[men-of-today]]), and only Aphrodite, and then Apollo, carry him out of the battle; Diomedes wounds the goddess in the hand as she goes (5.297–351; see [[aeneas]] and [[diomedes]]). The Aeneid remembers the scene twice.

**Aeneas' wish.** The poem begins with a storm. As his ships are wrecked, Aeneas groans that those who died before their fathers' eyes beneath the walls of Troy were three and four times blessed, and he calls on Diomedes: “O bravest of the race of the Danaans, son of Tydeus! Could I not have fallen on the plains of Ilium and poured out this life by your right hand, where fierce Hector lies by the spear of Achilles, where huge Sarpedon lies” (*Aeneid* 1.94–101). The model is Odysseus in his storm, who calls the Danaans who died at Troy three and four times blessed and wishes he had died there (*Odyssey* 5.306–312). Virgil adds the name of the man at whose hands Aeneas nearly did die, in Iliad 5.

**Diomedes' refusal.** After Troy Diomedes founded a city in southern Italy. When the war with Aeneas begins, the Latins send an envoy to ask for his help (*Aeneid* 8.9–17), and in Book 11 the envoys return with his answer (11.225–295). All the Greeks who violated the fields of Ilium, he says, have paid for it; the gods begrudged him the sight of his longed-for wife and lovely Calydon; his companions have been turned into birds that fill the rocks with tearful cries (see [[birds-of-diomedes]]). “These things I should have expected from the day when, out of my mind, I attacked heavenly bodies with the sword and violated the hand of Venus with a wound” (*Aeneid* 11.275–277). He will not fight the Trojans again. “We have stood against his fierce weapons and met him hand to hand: believe one who has tried how great he rises to his shield, with what whirlwind he hurls his spear” (*Aeneid* 11.282–284). If Ida had borne two more such men, the Trojans would have come to the cities of Greece; it was the hand of Hector and Aeneas that held back the Greek victory for ten years, both outstanding in courage and in arms, Aeneas the first in piety (*Aeneid* 11.285–292). Let the Latins make peace.

Virgil's Diomedes remembers the fight of Iliad 5 as a meeting of equals, which in Homer it was not. And he gives his own life the shape of Dione's warning. The man who fights the immortals, she said, does not live long, and his wife will wake her household with her wailing, longing for her husband, the best of the Achaeans (5.406–415; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]). Diomedes lived, but he did not come home to her.
