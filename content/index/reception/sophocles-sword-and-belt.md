summary: In Sophocles' Ajax the gifts that Hector and Ajax exchange after their duel become the instruments of their deaths. Ajax falls on the sword Hector gave him, and his brother Teucer says that Hector was dragged to his death by the belt Ajax gave in return. Teucer also recalls how Ajax's lot leapt first from the helmet.

In the Iliad the gifts close a drawn fight. Hector proposes them so that men of both armies will say that the two “fought in heart-devouring strife, and then parted in friendship, reconciled” (7.301–302). He gives a silver-studded sword with its sheath and strap, and Ajax gives “a belt bright with purple”, in the words used of the belt Oeneus gave Bellerophon when the two became guest-friends (7.303–305; 6.219; see [[guest-gifts]], [[sword]] and [[belt]]). The poem does not mention the gifts again.

Sophocles' *Ajax* follows them to the end. Ajax has lost the arms of Achilles to Odysseus. Setting out by night to kill the Greek leaders, he was driven mad by Athena and slaughtered the army's cattle instead; when he comes to himself he resolves to die (see [[ajax]]). In a speech that seems to promise submission, he says that he will go to the meadows by the shore and bury “this sword of mine, most hateful of weapons” where no one will see it: “for since I received this in my hand as a gift from Hector, my worst enemy, I have had nothing good from the Argives. The proverb of mortals is true: the gifts of enemies are no gifts, and bring no profit” (*Ajax* 654–665).

He does bury it, hilt down, in the Trojan earth, and falls on it. His last speech begins with the sword: “The slayer stands where it will cut most surely … the gift of Hector, the guest-friend I have hated most, most hateful to my sight; it is fixed in the enemy soil of Troy, newly sharpened on the iron-devouring whetstone” (815–820). He calls Hector ξένος, “guest-friend”. The exchange of Book 7 made the two men guest-friends, as Glaucus and Diomedes are (see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]), and in Sophocles the bond has become a curse.

Teucer, finding his brother's body, draws the moral from both gifts. “Do you see how in time Hector, even dead, was to destroy you? Look, by the gods, at the fortune of two men. Hector, with the belt this man gave him, was bound fast to the chariot rail and mangled until he breathed out his life; and this man, who had this gift from him, has perished by it in a deadly fall. Was it not a Fury that forged this sword, and Hades, that savage craftsman, the belt?” (1026–1035). Some editors have doubted that the lines from 1028 on are Sophocles'. In the Iliad Achilles drags Hector after his death, with oxhide straps drawn through the tendons of his feet, and the belt plays no part (22.395–404; see [[hector]] and [[teucer]]).

**The lot.** Near the end of the play Agamemnon scorns the dead man, and Teucer answers with Ajax's services to the army. The lot is one of them. When Ajax went out to face Hector, man against man, he went “by lot and unbidden” (λαχών τε κἀκέλευστος), and the lot he cast into the crested helmet was no runaway, “a clod of wet earth”, but one that would leap out first (1283–1287). In the Iliad nine men volunteer, each marks his own lot, and the lot that leaps from Agamemnon's helmet is “the lot that they themselves wanted”, the lot of Ajax (7.161–191; see [[lots]]). Teucer claims both honors for his brother, the volunteer's and the lot's. A clod of earth was the trick later told of Cresphontes: when the sons of Heracles drew lots for the Peloponnese, he cast a clod into the water jar so that it dissolved, the other lots came out first, and the land he wanted was left to him (Apollodorus 2.8.4; see [[apollodorus]]).
