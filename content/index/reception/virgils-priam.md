**The death of Priam.** Aeneas, telling Dido of the fall of Troy, comes to the fate of Priam. The old man sees the city taken and the enemy in the heart of his house, puts on the armor he has long given up, and goes to die among the enemy (*Aeneid* 2.506–511). In the open court of the palace stands a great altar under an ancient laurel, where Hecuba and her daughters sit huddled like doves driven by a storm; when Hecuba sees her husband in arms she asks him what madness drives him, for the time does not need such help, “not even if my Hector himself were here now”, and draws him to the altar beside her (*Aeneid* 2.512–525). Polites, one of Priam's sons, wounded, flees from Pyrrhus through the colonnades and the empty halls, and falls dead before his parents' eyes (*Aeneid* 2.526–532). Priam, though death holds him already, does not spare his voice or his anger. May the gods repay Pyrrhus for making a father watch his son's death and defiling a father's eyes with it (*Aeneid* 2.533–539). “But that Achilles, whom you falsely claim as your father, was not such to Priam his enemy; he respected the rights and the trust of a suppliant, and gave back Hector's bloodless body for burial, and sent me back to my kingdom” (*at non ille, satum quo te mentiris, Achilles / talis in hoste fuit Priamo; sed iura fidemque / supplicis erubuit corpusque exsangue sepulcro / reddidit Hectoreum meque in mea regna remisit*, *Aeneid* 2.540–543). He throws a feeble spear, which hangs from the boss of the shield (*Aeneid* 2.544–546). Pyrrhus answers: “You will report this, then, and go as a messenger to my father, the son of Peleus; remember to tell him of my sorry deeds and of his degenerate Neoptolemus. Now die.” He drags him, trembling and slipping in his son's blood, to the altar, winds his hair in his left hand, and drives the sword into his side to the hilt (*Aeneid* 2.547–553). “This was the end of Priam's fortunes”, the lord of so many peoples and lands of Asia; “he lies a huge trunk on the shore, the head torn from the shoulders, a body without a name” (*Aeneid* 2.554–558).

**The ransom remembered.** Priam's three clauses are Book 24 in brief. Achilles respected the suppliant: when Priam pressed him too hard he warned him not to stir his heart, “lest I do not spare even you, old man, in my huts, / suppliant though you are, and sin against the commands of Zeus” (24.568–570; see [[supplication]]), and when they had agreed on the truce he took the old man's right hand at the wrist, “so that he might feel no fear in his heart” (24.671–672). He gave back the body: he had the slave women wash and anoint it, lifted it onto the bier himself and set it on the wagon (24.582–590; see [[the-ransom-of-hector]]). And he sent Priam home, with eleven days of peace for the funeral (24.656–670), though in the poem it is Hermes who wakes the old man before dawn and drives him out of the camp (24.677–697; see [[hermes]]). Among the pictures of the war on the walls of Carthage, Aeneas had seen the same scene from its other side: Achilles selling Hector's body for gold, and Priam stretching out unarmed hands (*tendentemque manus Priamum conspexit inermis*, *Aeneid* 1.487; see [[virgils-hector]]).

**Father and son.** In the Iliad Hermes tells Priam to clasp Achilles' knees “and beg him by his father and his mother of the lovely hair / and his child” (24.465–467), and Priam begins with the father: “Remember your father, Achilles, like the gods” (24.486; see [[peleus]]). Achilles' child is the one son that the Iliad gives him, being reared on Scyros (19.326–327; see [[neoptolemus]]). Virgil sets the father against the child. Priam appeals to Achilles' conduct as a father's example, and the son answers by sending the old man to Peleus' son in the house of the dead to tell him how his son has fallen short. Hecuba, too, tries in both poems to hold her husband back. In Book 24 she cries out against his going alone to the ships, to “that eater of raw flesh, that faithless man” who will not pity him (24.200–208; see [[hecuba]]), and he goes and comes home; in the *Aeneid* she keeps him at the altar, and he dies there.

**Priam's end.** The Iliad does not tell Priam's death. In Book 22 Priam foresaw it, begging Hector to come inside the wall: “And myself, last of all, at the outer doors the dogs / that eat raw flesh will tear at, when someone with the sharp bronze / has struck or hit me and taken the life from my limbs” (22.66–68; see [[priam]]). Virgil keeps the bloody death and the body left unburied, and moves them from the doors to the altar of the house and to the shore. Polites, in the Iliad, is among the nine sons left to Priam at the end, whom he rebukes as he sets out for the ships (24.248–251; see [[polites]] and [[sons-of-priam]]). For the altar and the palace see [[priams-palace]].
