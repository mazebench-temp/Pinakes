Strabo, describing the temple of Zeus at Olympia, tells the story. When Phidias' collaborator, the painter Panaenus, asked what model he would use for the image of Zeus, Phidias answered that he would make it after the image Homer gives in these three lines. Strabo adds that the poet was thought to have captured the god's majesty best of all. The statue itself, of gold and ivory, was so large that if Zeus had stood up he would have lifted the roof.

The anecdote became famous as an example of poetry shaping the visual arts. It was repeated by Latin writers, and Dio Chrysostom's *Olympic Oration* (Oration 12) imagines Phidias defending his statue by appeal to Homer.

The statue, finished around 430 BCE, was one of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world. It is lost. Its likeness survives on coins of Elis, and the workshop where Phidias made it has been excavated at Olympia.
