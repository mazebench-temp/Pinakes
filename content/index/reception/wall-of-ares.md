Monro and Allen print παυρότερον λαὸν ἀγαγόνθʼ ὑπὸ τεῖχος ἄρειον, and the translation follows them: “leading a smaller army under a stronger wall” (4.407). ἄρειον is then the neuter of ἀρείων, “better, stronger”, the comparative that goes with ἀγαθός (“good”). Sthenelus' two comparatives, παυρότερον and ἄρειον, make a single boast. The sons came with fewer men against a stronger wall, and still they took the city that their fathers could not. Hesiod uses the same form of the race of heroes, “more just and better” (δικαιότερον καὶ ἄρειον, *Works and Days* 158), in the passage that goes on to their wars at Thebes and at Troy.

Written with a capital, Ἄρειον is the adjective of Ἄρης, “of Ares”, and the phrase becomes “under the wall of Ares”. The accent is the same, and ancient texts were written without the distinction between capital and small letters, so the choice is an editor's. The epithet would suit Thebes. In later myth the city was bound to the god from its founding: Cadmus killed a serpent of Ares at its spring before he founded the city, and married Harmonia, the daughter of Ares and Aphrodite (Apollodorus 3.4.1–2). The Iliad says nothing of this.
