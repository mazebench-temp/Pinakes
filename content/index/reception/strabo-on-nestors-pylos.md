**Three Pyloi.** Strabo knew three places called Pylos in the western Peloponnese: one in Hollow Elis near Mount Scollis, one in Triphylia near Lepreum, and one in Messenia by Coryphasium, the Pylos of his own day. Hence the verse “There is a Pylos before Pylos; and there is yet another Pylos.” Each people, he says, tried to show that its own Pylos was the “sandy” one and Nestor's home. Most of the later prose writers and poets made Nestor a Messenian, siding with the Pylos that had survived into their own time; but “the more Homeric”, following the verses, held that Nestor's Pylos was the one whose land the Alpheus crosses, and the Alpheus crosses Pisatis and Triphylia (*Geography* 8.3.7; see [[pylos]], [[sandy]] and [[alpheus]]). His own conclusion is that Nestor's land lay on both sides of the Alpheus, “all of which he calls the land of the Pylians”, while the river touches neither Messenia nor Hollow Elis; Nestor's home was “the Pylos we call Triphylian, Arcadian and Lepreatic”, more than thirty stades from the sea (*Geography* 8.3.26, arguing from Telemachus' visit in the Odyssey; compare 5.545).

**Nestor's story.** The argument rests above all on Book 11. Nestor's account to Patroclus of the war between the Pylians and the Eleans, Strabo says, supports his case “if one looks at the verses” (*Geography* 8.3.28), and he retells it step by step. Heracles had ravaged the Pylian land and killed eleven of Neleus' twelve sons (see [[heracles-sacks-pylos]]), so that the Epeians, despising the old king, treated the Pylians “haughtily and with outrage”, the words of the poem (ὑπερηφανέοντες, ὑβρίζοντες, 11.694–695). Nestor raided Elis by day, killed Itymoneus and drove the plunder home, reaching the city by night (11.670–684). On the third day, while the spoil was being divided and the sacrifices made, the Epeians marched out in force and besieged Thryon on the Alpheus (11.706–713). The Pylians camped by the river Minyeios near Arene, reached the Alpheus at midday, sacrificed and spent the night there, joined battle at dawn, and pursued the Epeians as far as Buprasium, the Olenian Rock and the hill of Alesium (11.714–760; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]).

**The argument.** From this, Strabo asks, how could the Pylos be the Elean or the Messenian one (*Geography* 8.3.29)? Not the Elean: when Heracles sacked it, he sacked the land of the Epeians with it, and fellow sufferers of the same stock would not have shown such arrogance to each other, or raided their own country. Nor could Augeas and Neleus, who were enemies, have ruled the same people at once, if a debt was owed to Neleus “in shining Elis” (11.698–702; see [[augeas]] and [[neleus]]); and if Neleus had lived there, Nestor would have ruled there, whereas the Catalogue gives the Eleans and Buprasians four leaders of their own and Nestor other towns (2.591–624). Why, too, would the Epeians march south to the Alpheus and Thryon, and flee from there to Buprasium? Not the Messenian Pylos either: men living so far off could hardly have wronged the Pylians in so many dealings, and Nestor could not have driven pigs and sheep, which can go neither fast nor far, more than a thousand stades to the Pylos by Coryphasium, while the Epeians on the third day came all together to Thryoessa and the Alpheus to besiege it. In Strabo's view those lands belonged to the Caucones, Triphylians and Pisatans, and Messenia was subject to Menelaus. As for the epithet Gerenian, the Messenians' Gerena may have been given its name on purpose, or by chance (8.3.29). In Hollow Elis, too, people pointed to a place Gerenus and to rivers called Geron and Geranius to claim the epithet for their Pylos, and the Messenians did the same, more plausibly (8.3.7; see [[gerenian-horseman]]).

**Thryoessa and the Minyeios.** Strabo applies the story's place names to the ground. Of the Catalogue's Thryum (2.592) he writes, “what he now calls Thryon, he elsewhere calls Thryoessa”, and quotes 11.711–712. The ford is called “the ford of the Alpheus” because the river can be crossed on foot there, and the place is now called Epitalium, in Macistia; the name fits the rushes (θρύον) that grow along the rivers of that country. Some, he adds, took Thryon for the ford itself and made “well-built Aepy” the town of Epitalium, a natural stronghold, because Homer calls Thryoessa “a steep hill” (αἰπεῖα κολώνη; *Geography* 8.3.24; see [[thryum]] and [[aepy]]). In his quotation the verse ends πυμάτη Πύλου ἠμαθόεντος, “last of sandy Pylos”, where our text has νεάτη, “on the border of”. Arene, he says, could be found nowhere for certain, but people placed it at Samicum, where the Anigrus, “formerly called Minyeios”, flows past and gives “no small sign”, since Homer says the Minyeios falls into the sea near Arene (*Geography* 8.3.19, quoting 11.722–723; see [[arene]] and [[minyeios]]). The stagnant, foul-smelling Anigrus, the cave of its nymphs and the healing of skin diseases in its waters take up the rest of the section.

**Afterward.** Strabo's Triphylian Pylos had a modern champion in Wilhelm Dörpfeld, who looked for Nestor's palace there early in the twentieth century. Since the excavation of the Mycenaean palace at Ano Englianos in Messenia, begun in 1939, most scholars have placed Nestor's home there; its Linear B tablets name the place Pylos (see [[pylos]] and [[strabo-and-the-catalogue]]). Nestor's story still sets the northern frontier of his people on the Alpheus, as Strabo saw (see [[pylians]]).
