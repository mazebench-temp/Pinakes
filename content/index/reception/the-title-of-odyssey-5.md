The title of *Odyssey* 5 in Merry's edition is Ὀδυσσέως σχεδία, “the raft of Odysseus” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, heading of Book 5); this library calls the book “The Raft of Odysseus”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter of the alphabet, this one is ε, epsilon (see [[title-of-book-5]]). The raft has its own entry, [[raft]], and its making is told under [[the-building-of-the-raft]]; the nymph is under [[calypso]], her island under [[ogygia]] and her dwelling under [[calypsos-cave]].

**Aelian's list.** Aelian, writing that the ancients sang the poems of Homer divided into parts, gives the names they used for the Odyssey: “the events at Pylos, and the events at Lacedaemon, and the cave of Calypso, and the events about the raft, and the tales to Alcinous”, and so on to “the events at Laertes' farm” (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[aelian]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-3]]). The third and fourth names both belong to this book, after the two that name the places of Telemachus' journey (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-4]]). They name what the book tells: the cave in which Hermes finds the nymph at her loom and where Odysseus spends his last evening with her (*Odyssey* 5.55–227), and the raft that he builds, sails and loses (*Odyssey* 5.228–370). Where one part ended and the next began, and whether either ran on into the next book, Aelian does not say.

**The cave.** The word σπέος, “cave”, comes seven times in the book, always of Calypso's. Hermes walks on until he comes to “the great cave, in which the nymph / of the lovely tresses lived” (*Odyssey* 5.57–58), with its wood, its roosting birds, its vine, its four springs and its meadows of violets and parsley: “There even an immortal, if he came, / would gaze at the sight and delight his mind” (*Odyssey* 5.63–74). There she feeds the god ambrosia and nectar (*Odyssey* 5.85–96); there Odysseus sleeps beside her by night, “unwilling beside the willing” (*Odyssey* 5.154–155); and there the last evening ends, “in the inner part of the hollow cave” (*Odyssey* 5.226–227). The poem had already placed her there in its first lines, holding him “in her hollow caves, longing for him to be her husband” (*Odyssey* 1.15), and Odysseus will say it of her to Alcinous in the same verse (*Odyssey* 9.29–30).

**The raft.** σχεδίη, “raft”, occurs twelve times in this book and only twice more in Homer, both in Odysseus' own account of the voyage (*Odyssey* 7.264, 7.274); the σχεδίην of *Iliad* 5.830 is another word, “at close quarters”. The raft is first named in Zeus' command, which sends Odysseus home without the escort of gods or men, “on a raft of many bindings, suffering woes” (*Odyssey* 5.33). Calypso bids him build it (*Odyssey* 5.162–164), and he suspects a trap, since “over it not even balanced ships / cross” (*Odyssey* 5.175–176), until she swears that she means him no harm (*Odyssey* 5.177–191). He builds it in four days with her axe, adze and augers, “As wide as the floor of a ship that a man marks out, / a broad freight ship” (*Odyssey* 5.228–262, 5.249–250), sails on the fifth, and on the eighteenth day of sailing is wrecked within sight of Scheria (*Odyssey* 5.263–332; see [[scheria]]). The first wave whirls the raft around and breaks the mast (*Odyssey* 5.313–318); Ino bids him leave it (*Odyssey* 5.343–344), and he will not until a wave shakes it apart (*Odyssey* 5.356–364); a second wave scatters its long beams as a blustering wind shakes a heap of husks (*Odyssey* 5.366–370). Odysseus “bestrode a single beam, as though riding a horse” (*Odyssey* 5.371), and the book does not mention the raft again.

**What the title leaves out.** The book begins on Olympus, with the second council of the gods and Hermes' flight over the sea (*Odyssey* 5.1–54; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]] and [[hermes]]). Its last 123 lines come after the raft is gone: Odysseus strips off the garments Calypso gave him, binds Ino's veil under his chest and swims for two nights and two days; he is thrown on the rocks of the Phaeacians' coast, comes to the mouth of a river whose god takes him in as a suppliant, lets the veil go into the river for Ino to take back, and sleeps under a wild olive and an olive in a bed of leaves (*Odyssey* 5.371–493; see [[leucothea]] and [[the-river-of-scheria]]).

**Odysseus' own summaries.** Odysseus tells the story of this book twice. To Arete he tells it at length: Ogygia, where crafty Calypso, the daughter of Atlas, lives apart from gods and men; nine days on the keel after Zeus' thunderbolt shattered his ship; her love and care and her promise to make him immortal and ageless, which never persuaded his heart; seven years of tears; and in the eighth her order to go, “at a message from Zeus, or her own mind was changed”. He does not know which. Then come the raft of many bindings, bread and sweet wine and immortal clothes and a warm wind; seventeen days of sailing and the shadowy mountains of Scheria on the eighteenth; Poseidon's storm, which would not let him ride the raft; the swim, the rocks, and the river where the place seemed best, smooth, free of rocks, sheltered from the wind; and sleep among the leaves all night and on through the morning and midday, until the sun was sinking (*Odyssey* 7.241–289, plain renderings; the place by the river is described in the words of *Odyssey* 5.442–443, with “me” for “him”). He says nothing of Calypso's oath, or of Ino and her veil. In the summary of his wanderings that he gives Penelope, the raft and the storm are gone: he came to Ogygia and the nymph Calypso, who held him in her hollow caves, longing for him to be her husband, cared for him and promised to make him immortal, but never persuaded his heart; and he came after much toil to the Phaeacians, who honored him like a god and sent him home in a ship with bronze and gold and clothing (*Odyssey* 23.333–341, plain renderings). The last three verses are Zeus' prophecy in this book (*Odyssey* 5.36–38), told as done.

**The raft in Plato.** Plato's Simmias would sail through life on the best of human accounts “as on a raft”, unless a divine account could carry him more safely (*Phaedo* 85c–d); his word, σχεδία, is the word of this book (see [[raft]] and [[plato]]).
