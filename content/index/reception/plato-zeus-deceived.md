**Plato.** In the third book of the *Republic* Socrates reviews the stories that the young guardians of his city may hear. For most people, he says, self-control (σωφροσύνη) means obeying their rulers and themselves ruling the pleasures of drink, sex and food (389d–e). So the poets must not make the wisest of men say that nothing is finer than tables full of bread and meat, with the wine-pourer filling the cups (*Odyssey* 9.8–10), nor that to die of hunger is the most pitiable fate (*Odyssey* 12.342). Then comes the scene on Ida. Is it fit for a young man to hear of “Zeus, who, when the other gods and men were asleep and he alone was awake, easily forgot all that he had planned because of his desire for sex, and was so struck at the sight of Hera that he was not even willing to go into the chamber, but wanted to lie with her there on the ground, saying that he was held by such desire as not even when they first went to each other ‘without the knowledge of their dear parents’” (390b–c)? Nor is the binding of Ares and Aphrodite by Hephaestus to be told (*Odyssey* 8.266–366). Adeimantus agrees: by Zeus, such things are not fit to hear.

**Plato's Homer.** Socrates' summary joins two scenes and changes the speaker.

- Zeus “alone awake” while gods and men slept is the Zeus of the opening of Book 2, who lies awake planning how to honor Achilles and destroy many Achaeans at the ships (2.1–4; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[all-the-gods-slept]]). Those are the plans he forgets. In Book 14 he sits on the topmost peak of Ida (14.157–158), and the poem does not say that he forgets anything: desire “wrapped his shrewd mind” (14.294), and he sleeps while Poseidon turns the battle (14.352–360; see [[eros]]).
- The chamber is Hera's idea. She pretends to fear that a god might see them “on the peaks of Ida, where everything is in plain view”, so that “it would be a thing for blame”, and proposes the chamber that Hephaestus built for him (14.330–340; see [[hephaestus]]). Zeus answers with a golden cloud that not even the Sun could see through (14.342–345).
- They do not lie on the bare ground. The earth sends up grass and dewy lotus, crocus and hyacinth, “thick and soft, which held them up off the ground” (14.347–349; see [[lotus]], [[crocus]] and [[hyacinth]]). Plato's χαμαί, “on the ground”, is what the flowers prevent.
- The comparison with their first union is the narrator's (14.295–296). Zeus himself measures his desire against the goddesses and women he has loved, and against Hera herself, “nor you yourself” (14.315–328; see [[the-loves-of-zeus]]). Plato gives the narrator's words to Zeus, with the verse's dual participle, φίλους λήθοντε τοκῆας.

**The objection.** Plato says nothing of the deceit. His charge is that the king of the gods is overcome by pleasure, the very thing that guardians who must rule their pleasures should not admire; Homer's narrator puts it in a phrase, “overcome by sleep and love” (14.353). Elsewhere in the *Republic* the poets may not show a god who deceives (see [[plato-lying-dream]]) or gods overcome by laughter (see [[plato-homeric-laughter]]).

**Two answers.** Later readers defended the scene in two ways. Plutarch, writing on how the young should read poetry, rejects the allegorists who would have “Hera's beautifying of herself for Zeus” and the enchantments of the strap be a cleansing of the air as it draws near the fiery element, as though the poet did not give the solutions himself. In the story of Hera, he says, Homer showed best that the intimacy and favor won from a husband by drugs, witchcraft and guile (μετὰ δόλου) are short-lived, soon cloying and unstable, and turn to enmity and anger when the pleasure fades. Such, he says, is the threat of Zeus when he wakes: she will see whether the love and the bed in which she lay with him, and deceived him (καί μʼ ἀπάτησας), will help her (15.32–33). Plutarch quotes the two verses (*How the Young Man Should Study Poetry* 19e–20b; see [[plutarch]] and [[aphrodites-band]]). The allegorists' Hera is the air, as Plato's Socrates had already suggested: the namegiver, perhaps, speaking of things on high, called the air (ἀήρ) Hera, hiding the beginning of the word at its end (*Cratylus* 404b–c).

Proclus answers as a theologian. In his essays on the *Republic* he takes up Socrates' charge, that for love of Hera Zeus forgot all he had resolved and lay with her on the ground, and explains “that whole myth”, drawing on a treatise that “our guide”, his teacher Syrianus, had written on it. The union of Zeus and Hera is that of the father and the mother of all that Zeus fathers. His waking is his providence for the world and his sleep his life apart from it, and in the one he does not forget the other. Ida is “the place of the Ideas” (τὸν τῶν ἰδεῶν τόπον). In refusing the chamber built by Hephaestus, the craftsman of the visible world, Zeus prefers the union above the world to the union within it. Hera's tresses, her girdle with its hundred tassels, her earrings and sandals, the ambrosia and the oil and the strap hidden in her bosom make her like Rhea, the mother of the gods. Read so, Homer is clear of all blasphemy; and if such things are not fit for the young on their surface, the poets might answer that they wrote them not for the young but “with a raving mouth”, in the madness of the Muses (*On the Republic* I 132–140 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).
