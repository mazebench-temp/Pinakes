Greek puts the poem's subject first: μῆνιν, "wrath". English word order makes that hard, and each translator solves it differently. The openings of the public-domain translations:

> Achilles’ baneful wrath resound, O Goddess, that impos’d
> Infinite sorrows on the Greeks, and many brave souls los’d.
> — George Chapman (1611)

> Achilles’ wrath, to Greece the direful spring
> Of woes unnumber’d, heavenly goddess, sing!
> — Alexander Pope (1715)

> Achilles sing, O Goddess! Peleus’ son;
> His wrath pernicious, who ten thousand woes
> Caused to Achaia’s host
> — William Cowper (1791)

> Sing, Ο goddess, the destructive wrath of Achilles, son of Peleus,
> which brought countless woes upon the Greeks
> — Theodore Alois Buckley (1851)

> Of Peleus’ son, Achilles, sing, O Muse,
> The vengeance, deep and deadly
> — Edward, Earl of Derby (1864)

> Sing, goddess, the wrath of Achilles Peleus’ son, the ruinous wrath
> — Lang, Leaf & Myers (1883)

> Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles son of Peleus
> — Samuel Butler (1898)

Chapman and Pope lead with "Achilles' wrath", keeping the subject first by making the hero possessive. Cowper and Derby lead with Achilles himself and push the wrath into the next line. The prose translators follow the Greek order after the verb, "Sing, goddess, the wrath". Butler alone chooses "anger", a word that loses μῆνις' divine register. The translation in Pinakes opens "Sing, goddess, the wrath of Achilles, son of Peleus", and keeps "wrath" for μῆνις throughout. See [[menis]].
