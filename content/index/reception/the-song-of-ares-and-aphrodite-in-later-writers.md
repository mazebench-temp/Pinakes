summary: Demodocus' song of Ares and Aphrodite caught in the bonds of Hephaestus was a scandal to the philosophers and a favorite of the Latin poets. Plato would not let the young hear it; Proclus read the bonds as the craftsman's binding of opposites and the song as Homer at his most inspired; a reading preserved in the Suda made it a warning to the luxurious Phaeacians. Virgil gives it to a nymph at her spinning, and Ovid told it twice, as a lesson for lovers and as a story of the Sun, and named it in exile as proof that Homer too sang of love.

**Homer's song.** The second song of Demodocus is the only one of his three that the Odyssey gives in full. The Sun sees the lovers and tells Hephaestus; he forges bonds “fine as spider's webs”, pours them around his bed and down “from the roof beam”, and makes as if to go to Lemnos; Ares comes, the bonds close on the lovers, and the husband, turned back by the Sun's report, calls the gods to see “deeds that are laughable, and hard to bear” (*Odyssey* 8.270–307). The goddesses stay at home for shame, the gods laugh, Hermes would gladly lie in three times as many bonds beside golden Aphrodite, and Poseidon's pledge sets the lovers free, Ares to go to Thrace and Aphrodite to Paphos (*Odyssey* 8.321–366). The song and its place in the poem are told under [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]], with the ancient doubts about its genuineness, and the trap under [[the-bonds-of-hephaestus]].

**The philosophers.** Plato's Socrates, reviewing what the young guardians may hear for the sake of self-control, puts it beside the scene on Ida: they must not hear of Zeus forgetting his plans in his desire for Hera, οὐδὲ Ἄρεώς τε καὶ Ἀφροδίτης ὑπὸ Ἡφαίστου δεσμὸν διʼ ἕτερα τοιαῦτα, “nor of the binding of Ares and Aphrodite by Hephaestus, for other reasons of the same kind” (*Republic* 3.390c, a plain rendering; see [[plato-zeus-deceived]] and [[plato]]). Proclus answers him in his essays on the *Republic*. The two gods work through the whole cosmos, Ares dividing its opposites and Hephaestus making its visible order, and both need Aphrodite; Hephaestus shares in her by the higher orders and so is said to have her by the will of Zeus, while Ares, by the lower, is said to commit adultery with her. The Sun who sees from above and informs the craftsman, the bonds invisible to the other gods, and the loosing at Poseidon's request, since the bonds of what is below the moon can be loosed, all have their meaning (*On the Republic* I 140–143 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). Proclus goes further. When Homer tells of the bonds of Hephaestus, or of the union of Zeus and Hera, he says, the poet is plainly inspired and speaks from his possession by the Muses; and he shows that inspiration in Demodocus himself, who began the song of the wooden horse “stirred by the god” (*On the Republic* I 193 Kroll, quoting *Odyssey* 8.499; see [[demodocus-and-the-blind-homer]]). The story Plato would keep from the young is, for Proclus, Homer at his highest.

**A song for the Phaeacians.** A third answer made the song a lesson for its audience. The Suda, under ἀοιδός, says that the singers of old were men of temperance with the disposition of philosophers: Agamemnon left one to guard Clytemnestra, and Aegisthus could not seduce her until he had killed him. “And Demodocus sings of the union of Aphrodite and Ares, not for its sweetness or in approval of this passion, but to turn them away from lawless desires, knowing that they had been brought up in a life of luxury” (Suda α 4402, a plain rendering), and it cites Alcinous' boast, “always dear to us are the feast and the lyre” (*Odyssey* 8.248; see [[phaeacians]], [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]] and [[phemius]]). The lexicographers noticed the song's word for the trap. Explaining ἀτέχνως, the Suda adds that the ancients “called deceits arts”, τέχνας, and quotes the bonds, ἀμφὶ δὲ δεσμοὶ τεχνήεντες ἔχυντο (Suda α 4348, quoting *Odyssey* 8.296–297): the gods laugh “as they saw the craft of Hephaestus”, and he caught Ares “lame as he is, by his craft” (*Odyssey* 8.327, 8.332; see [[dolos]]).

**Virgil.** In the fourth book of the *Georgics* the shepherd Aristaeus, his bees lost, calls on his mother Cyrene at the source of her river. Below the water her nymphs are spinning Milesian wool, “and among them Clymene was telling of the vain care of Vulcan, and the tricks and sweet thefts of Mars, and was counting the crowded loves of the gods from Chaos on” (*inter quas curam Clymene narrabat inanem / Vulcani Martisque dolos et dulcia furta, / aque Chao densos divum numerabat amores*, *Georgics* 4.345–347); held by her song as they unwind their spindles, they hear his lament (*Georgics* 4.348–350; plain renderings of the Latin throughout; see [[virgil]]).

**Ovid's lesson for lovers.** Ovid tells the story in the second book of the *Art of Love* as an example for the young man who has a rival: better not to know, since love grows in those who are caught (*Art of Love* 2.555–560). “A story is told, the best known in all heaven, of Mars and Venus caught by the snares of Mulciber” (*Fabula narratur toto notissima caelo, / Mulciberis capti Marsque Venusque dolis*, *Art of Love* 2.561–562). Mars, “from a terrible general made a lover”, finds Venus no prude; she laughs at her husband's feet and at his hands hardened by fire or by his craft, and mimics Vulcan in front of Mars (*Art of Love* 2.563–570), the complaint of Homer's lame god turned into a scene (*Odyssey* 8.308–311). “By the Sun's report (who could deceive the Sun?)” Vulcan learns what his wife has done, and the poet turns on the informer: “What bad examples you set, Sun! Ask a gift of her herself: if you keep quiet, she has something she can give you” (*Art of Love* 2.573–576). Vulcan sets hidden snares around and above the bed, and “the work deceives the eyes”; *fingit iter Lemnon*, “he feigns a journey to Lemnos” (*Art of Love* 2.577–579), the phrase Merry sets beside Homer's “he made as if to go to Lemnos” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 8.283). Caught naked, the lovers cannot cover their faces; Venus can scarcely keep back her tears; and “someone, laughing, said: ‘Onto me, bravest Mars, if they weigh on you, shift the chains!’” (*Art of Love* 2.580–586), the wish that Homer gives to Hermes (*Odyssey* 8.339–342). At Neptune's prayers Vulcan barely lets them go, “Mars makes for Thrace, she for Paphos” (*Art of Love* 2.587–588). Then Ovid adds an ending of his own. “This is your reward, Vulcan: what they hid before, they do more freely, now that all shame is gone”, and the god often owns that he acted like a fool, and is said to have repented of his art (*Art of Love* 2.589–592). The moral follows: Venus, caught herself, forbids the snares she suffered, so set none for a rival, and do not intercept his secret letters (*Art of Love* 2.593–596).

**Ovid's story of the Sun.** In the *Metamorphoses* the daughters of Minyas, who will not join the rites of Bacchus, tell stories as they spin and weave (*Metamorphoses* 4.32–41), and Leuconoe tells “the loves of the Sun” (*Metamorphoses* 4.169–170). “This god is thought to have been the first to see the adultery of Venus with Mars; this god sees all things first” (*primus adulterium Veneris cum Marte putatur / hic vidisse deus; videt hic deus omnia primus*, *Metamorphoses* 4.171–172; see [[helios]] and [[who-sees-all-things]]). Grieved, he shows Juno's son the theft of his bed; Vulcan drops the work in his hand and files fine chains of bronze, nets and snares that could deceive the eyes, “a work that the finest threads would not surpass, nor the spider's web that hangs from the top of the beam” (*non illud opus tenuissima vincant / stamina, non summo quae pendet aranea tigno*, *Metamorphoses* 4.178–179), Homer's bonds “fine as spider's webs” poured down “from the roof beam” (*Odyssey* 8.279–280). The Lemnian opens the ivory doors and lets in the gods; “one of the gods, not the gloomy ones, wishes he might be so shamed; the gods laughed, and for a long time this was the best known story in all heaven” (*Metamorphoses* 4.185–189). The story is a prelude: “Cytherea exacts a penalty mindful of the informing” (*Metamorphoses* 4.190), and wounds the Sun, who harmed a hidden love, with a love of his own (*Metamorphoses* 4.190–192).

**Ovid in exile.** Defending the *Art of Love* to Augustus, Ovid argues that the greatest poets wrote of love. The Iliad is the story of an adulteress, the Odyssey of a woman wooed by many suitors while her husband was away (*Tristia* 2.371–376), and “who but Maeonides tells of Venus and Mars bound, their bodies caught on a shameful bed?” (*Quis, nisi Maeonides, Venerem Martemque ligatos / narrat in obsceno corpora prensa toro?*, *Tristia* 2.377–378; see [[ovid]]). Ovid gives the song to the poet, not to the singer.

**Women's work.** In Homer the song is a man's, sung to the lyre in the place of the games before the people and the stranger, and in it the goddesses stay at home “for shame” (*Odyssey* 8.262–266, 8.324). Virgil and the *Metamorphoses* set it among women at their wool, Clymene among the nymphs who spin, Leuconoe among the sisters at the loom.
