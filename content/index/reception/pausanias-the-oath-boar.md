**Homer.** Agamemnon tells Talthybius to make ready a boar “to sacrifice to Zeus and the Sun” (19.197). When the gifts are set out in the assembly, he draws his knife, cuts the first hairs from the boar, lifts his hands to Zeus and swears by Zeus, Earth, the Sun and the Erinyes that he never laid a hand on Briseis (19.249–265; see [[oath]] and [[briseis]]). Then he cuts its throat, and Talthybius whirls it round and flings it into the sea, “food for the fishes” (19.266–268; see [[talthybius]] and [[boar]]). The victim is neither burnt nor eaten. After the oaths of the truce in Book 3 Priam puts the lambs in his chariot and takes them back to the city (3.310–313), and Walter Leaf takes the oath victim in both places to be given over to the gods below (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.267; see the translation's note on 19.267).

**Pausanias.** In the council house at Olympia, Pausanias says, stands the image of Zeus that, of all his images, is most fitted to strike terror into wrongdoers: his title is Zeus of Oaths (Ὅρκιος), and he holds a thunderbolt in each hand. Beside it the athletes, their fathers and brothers and their trainers swear over the cut pieces of a boar that they will do nothing wrong in the Olympic games, and the athletes swear besides that they have kept strictly to their training for ten months in a row. The judges of the boys and of the foals swear to judge fairly and without bribes, and to keep secret what they learn of those they examine. “What is done with the boar after the athletes' oath I forgot to ask; but among the men of old it was the rule that a victim over which someone had sworn an oath should no longer be food for any man. Homer too shows it, not least”: the boar over whose pieces Agamemnon swore that Briseis had not shared his bed, the poet has the herald throw into the sea, and Pausanias quotes the three lines. “So they thought in the old days.” Before the feet of Zeus of Oaths stands a bronze tablet with elegiac verses, meant to frighten those who swear falsely (*Description of Greece* 5.24.9–11; see [[pausanias]] and [[olympia]]).

**His text.** Pausanias' first line has σφάραγον, “throat”, where ours has στόμαχον: “He spoke, and cut the boar's throat with the pitiless bronze” (19.266). He speaks of the boar's τόμια, the cut pieces of a victim on which an oath is sworn, a word Homer does not use. In the Iliad Agamemnon cuts only the first hairs from the boar, as he cuts hair from the lambs' heads before the truce of Book 3 (19.254; 3.273). His boar is for Zeus and the Sun (19.197), the athletes' for Zeus of Oaths (see [[zeus]] and [[helios]]).
