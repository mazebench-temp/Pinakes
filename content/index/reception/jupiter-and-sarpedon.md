summary: Roman readers took Zeus' grief for Sarpedon as a lesson in fate. Cicero cites it to show that nothing can be averted if all things happen by fate, and in the Aeneid Jupiter consoles Hercules, who weeps for Pallas, with the sons of gods who fell at Troy, his own Sarpedon among them; later in the same book Juno pleads for Turnus as Zeus had thought of saving his son. Homer's Zeus could have saved Sarpedon, and does not.

**Homer.** When Sarpedon and Patroclus leap down from their chariots to fight, Zeus pities them and tells Hera that his heart is divided: should he snatch Sarpedon alive out of the tearful battle and set him down in Lycia, or bring him down now at the hands of the son of Menoetius (16.426–438)? Hera answers that a mortal long since ordained to his portion is not to be set free from death: “Do it; but not all of us other gods approve” (16.443; see [[not-all-the-gods-approve]]). If he sends Sarpedon home alive, another god may wish to save his own son, “for many are fighting around the great city of Priam, / sons of immortals” (16.448–449). Let him rather leave Sarpedon to fall, and send Death and Sleep to carry him to Lycia for burial (16.450–457; see [[sleep]] and [[death]]). Zeus does not disobey, and sheds bloody raindrops on the earth in honor of his son (16.458–461). Homer does not say that Zeus cannot save him. Hera's “Do it” leaves the choice to him, and he chooses as she advises; when Sarpedon has fallen, Glaucus says only that Zeus “does not defend even his own son” (16.522; see [[the-death-of-sarpedon]], [[zeus]] and [[hera]]).

**Cicero.** In the second book of *On Divination* Cicero argues against the Stoics that if all things happen by fate, foreknowledge is of no use, since what is fated cannot be changed. The Stoics answer at last that every evil falls more lightly when the gods are appeased with rites; but if nothing happens outside fate, Cicero replies, no rite can lighten anything. Homer, he says, has this in mind when he brings in Jupiter complaining that he could not snatch his son Sarpedon from death against fate; and a Greek verse, which Cicero gives in Latin, says the same: what is prepared to be overcomes even highest Jupiter (*On Divination* 2.25; see [[cicero]]). Cicero's Jupiter cannot save his son; Homer's chooses not to.

**Virgil.** In the tenth book of the *Aeneid* young Pallas, whom his father Evander has entrusted to Aeneas, goes out against Turnus, and before he throws he prays to Hercules, once his father's guest, to help him (*Aeneid* 10.457–463). Hercules hears the young man, presses down a great groan deep in his heart and pours out vain tears. Then the father speaks to his son with kindly words: “Each has his own day; brief and beyond recall is the time of life for all; but to lengthen fame by deeds, that is the work of courage. Under the high walls of Troy so many sons of gods fell; why, with them fell Sarpedon, my own offspring. Turnus too is called by his fates, and he has reached the goal of the life he was given.” So he speaks, and turns his eyes away from the fields of the Rutulians (*Aeneid* 10.464–473). Pallas' spear grazes Turnus; Turnus' spear pierces Pallas' shield, corselet and chest (*Aeneid* 10.474–489).

Virgil has divided Homer's scene between his gods. Hercules has Zeus' grief, and weeps for a mortal he cannot save. Jupiter has Hera's argument, the many sons of gods who fight at Troy (16.448–449), and her conclusion that the mortal must die, and he gives it as consolation, with his own son for example. He gives it in the terms of Sarpedon's speech to Glaucus, that since “the fates of death stand over us, / countless, which no mortal can flee or escape”, men must go forward, and either give the triumph to another or win it themselves (12.326–328; see [[sarpedons-speech]]). And where Homer's Zeus, while the fight goes on over Sarpedon's body, never turns “his shining eyes away from the fierce battle” (16.644–645), Virgil's Jupiter turns his away before Pallas falls.

**Juno and Turnus.** Later in the same book Virgil turns the scene round. Jupiter addresses Juno as “my sister and at the same time my dearest wife” (*Aeneid* 10.607), as Homer's Zeus speaks to “Hera, his sister and wife” (16.432), and Juno begs to be allowed to take Turnus out of the fight and keep him safe for his father Daunus (*Aeneid* 10.611–620). Jupiter grants a delay of death and no more: “take Turnus away in flight and snatch him from the fates that press on him; so far I may indulge you. But if any larger favor lies hidden under your prayers, and you think the whole war can be moved or changed, you are feeding vain hopes” (*Aeneid* 10.622–627). Juno answers in tears: what if he granted in his mind what he refuses in words, and this life were left to Turnus (*Aeneid* 10.628–632)? In Homer the father wishes to snatch his son out of the battle and the wife restrains him; in Virgil the wife pleads and the father restrains her. Juno draws Turnus off the field with a phantom of Aeneas and carries him away by sea to his father's city (*Aeneid* 10.633–688), and his death is only put off.
