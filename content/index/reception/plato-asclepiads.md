In the third book of the *Republic*, after music and gymnastics, Socrates turns to doctors. He deplores a new kind of medicine that nurses disease. Its inventor, he says, was Herodicus, a trainer who fell ill and, by mixing gymnastics with medicine, tormented first himself and then many others, drawing out his death into a long old age (406a–b). Asclepius knew better. He taught medicine for people who are healthy by nature and in their way of life but have some particular disease; he cured them with drugs and surgery and let them go on living as before, and he did not try to prolong by regimens the lives of those who were sick through and through (407c–e).

His sons proved it at Troy. When Pandarus wounded Menelaus, Socrates says, they “sucked out the blood and spread soothing drugs on it”, and prescribed nothing about what he should eat or drink afterward, since the drugs were enough to heal men who had been healthy and orderly in their lives before they were wounded (408a). The words are Homer's, put into the plural: in the Iliad it is Machaon alone who “sucked out the blood and skillfully spread on it soothing drugs” (4.218).

Plato's Homer here is not quite ours. A little earlier Socrates says that the sons of Asclepius found no fault with the woman who gave the wounded Eurypylus a drink of Pramnian wine sprinkled with barley meal and grated cheese, and did not blame Patroclus who was treating him (405e–406a). A strange drink, Glaucon remarks, for a man in that condition. In the Iliad the drink is made by Hecamede for Nestor and the wounded Machaon (11.624–641), and it is Patroclus who treats Eurypylus (11.828–848). In the *Ion* Socrates quotes the lines on the drink correctly, as given to Machaon (538b–c).
