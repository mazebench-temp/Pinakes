When the Achaeans have built their wall, Poseidon complains that its fame will make men forget the wall that he and Apollo built for Laomedon. Zeus tells him to break it down and sweep it into the sea once the Achaeans have sailed home (7.442–463; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[poseidon]]). The poet tells how that was done at the beginning of Book 12, long before its time. After the fall of Troy, Poseidon and Apollo turned the rivers that run down from Ida against the wall for nine days while Zeus sent rain, and Poseidon swept its foundations into the sea and covered the shore again with sand (12.3–35). Nothing was left of it.

**Aristotle.** The remark survives only in Strabo. Following Demetrius of Scepsis, Strabo argues that the Ilium of his own day cannot be Homer's Troy, because it stands too near the Achaeans' old naval station by Sigeum. On that reckoning one would have to wonder at the recklessness of the Achaeans, who left their camp unwalled for so long so close to a great city, “for he says that the wall was built only lately”, and at the cowardice of the Trojans, who stormed the wall once it was built but never dared to attack the camp while it lay open. Into this argument Strabo drops a parenthesis: “or it never existed, but the poet who invented it made it vanish, as Aristotle says” (ἢ οὐδʼ ἐγένετο, ὁ δὲ πλάσας ποιητὴς ἠφάνισεν, ὡς Ἀριστοτέλης φησίν, *Geography* 13.1.36). Editors place the fragment among those of Aristotle's lost *Homeric Problems* (see [[aristotle]]).

The saying turns a question about the site into a statement about poetry. No visitor to the Troad could find the wall, and the poem itself explains why; the explanation belongs to the same fiction as the wall. In the *Poetics* Aristotle says that Homer above all has taught the other poets how to tell falsehoods as they should (24, 1460a18–19). Strabo adds a geographer's reason why the ground had changed: the whole plain between the city and the sea had been built up by the rivers, so that in the heroes' time the shore lay much nearer the city than it does now (13.1.36). The Iliad makes the rivers of the plain the instruments of the wall's end (12.17–24).

**The scholia.** The ancient commentators make the same point. A note on 12.4 in the Venetus A says that the poet, having invented the wall (πλάσας, the verb Strabo gives to Aristotle) in honor of Achilles, brings in its destruction “so that his falsehood may not be refuted by later men, as of a thing that never was”; another explains that he invented it to move the battle on the plain to a fight at a wall, a τειχομαχία, and gave as the cause of its disappearance the anger of the gods, who had no sacrifices for it (scholia on 12.3–4).

**Posidonius.** Strabo reports the phrase a second time. Posidonius, discussing Plato's story of Atlantis, thought it better to say that the island might really have existed and sunk than to say that “he who invented it made it vanish, as the poet did the wall of the Achaeans” (*Geography* 2.3.6). By his time the phrase stood for any fiction whose author takes away the evidence against it.

James I. Porter follows the remark and its later history in “Making and Unmaking: The Achaean Wall and the Limits of Fictionality in Homeric Criticism” (*Transactions of the American Philological Association* 141, 2011).
