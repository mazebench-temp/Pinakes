The play is probably of the 420s BCE. It is set in Phthia, at the shrine of Thetis, where Andromache has taken refuge at the goddess's image. Her first lines recall her wedding: “Glory of the land of Asia, city of Thebe, from which I came once, with the rich golden splendor of my bride-gifts, to the royal hearth of Priam, given to Hector as his wife to bear his children” (*Andromache* 1–4). Now she is the most wretched of women. She saw Hector killed by Achilles, and the son she bore him, Astyanax, thrown from the high towers when the Greeks took Troy; she, from the freest of houses, came to Greece a slave, the chosen prize of Neoptolemus' spear (8–15). What Hector foresaw has happened: the day of freedom taken from her, the day of slavery come (6.455, 6.463; see [[eleutheros]] and [[doulios]]).

Neoptolemus has made her his concubine, and she has borne him a son. His wife Hermione, the daughter of Menelaus, is childless and blames Andromache's drugs, and while Neoptolemus is away at Delphi she and her father plot to kill Andromache and the boy. Andromache laments in elegiac couplets, the only elegiacs in surviving Greek tragedy. Paris brought to steep Ilion “not a marriage but a ruin” (ἄτη) when he brought Helen to his chambers; the son of sea-born Thetis dragged Hector around the walls behind his chariot; she herself was led from her chambers to the seashore, with hateful slavery thrown over her head (103–116). ἄτη is Helen's own word for Paris' folly in Book 6 (6.356; see [[ate]]).

Answering Hermione, Andromache describes a perfect wife. For Hector's sake, she says, she even helped him in his loves, and many times gave the breast to his bastard children, so as to give him no cause for bitterness (222–225). The Iliad gives no hint of such a thing. Euripides makes her the model wife to shame the jealous one.

Old Peleus rescues her and the child. Orestes carries off Hermione and contrives Neoptolemus' death at Delphi. At the end Thetis appears. Andromache is to settle in Molossia as the wife of Helenus, and her son by Neoptolemus, the last of the line of Aeacus, is to be the father of a line of Molossian kings, for the gods care for Troy too, “though it fell by the eagerness of Pallas” (1243–1252). The widow of Hector ends as the wife of his brother, the seer who sent him into the city in Book 6 (6.73–101; see [[helenus]]).
