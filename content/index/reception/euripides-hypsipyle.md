**Homer.** The Iliad knows the royal house of Lemnos only in passing. Euneus, “son of Jason, … whom Hypsipyle bore to Jason, shepherd of the people”, sends shiploads of wine to the Achaean camp and a gift of a thousand measures to the sons of Atreus (7.467–471; see [[euneus]], [[hypsipyle]] and [[jason]]). Later he buys Lycaon, a son of Priam, from Achilles, and pays for him with a silver mixing bowl of Sidonian work, which Phoenician traders had once given to Thoas (21.40–41, 23.740–747; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[mixing-bowl]]). Homer does not say who Thoas was; later poets made him Hypsipyle's father and the king of Lemnos. Homer's Euneus is a king who trades.

**Euripides.** The *Hypsipyle* did not survive in the manuscripts of Euripides, and much of it was recovered from a papyrus found at Oxyrhynchus and published in 1908 (P.Oxy. 852). It is one of his late plays. Hypsipyle, once queen of Lemnos, has become a slave at Nemea, in the house of Lycurgus, where she nurses his infant son Opheltes. Two young strangers come to the house and are taken in as guests. The army of the Seven, marching on Thebes, passes through, and the seer Amphiaraus asks her to show him water for a sacrifice; while she leads him to a spring, a serpent kills the child. The boy's mother, Eurydice, condemns her to death, and Amphiaraus pleads for her. The strangers are her sons by Jason, Euneus and Thoas, and mother and sons know each other at last (see [[the-seven-against-thebes]]).

Euripides took the name Euneus from Homer and gave the second son his grandfather's name. The handbook of Apollodorus tells the rest. When the women of Lemnos killed their men, Hypsipyle had saved her father Thoas; when they learned of it they killed him and sold her, and so she came to serve Lycurgus. After the child's death the Argives buried him, called him Archemorus, “beginning of doom”, and founded the Nemean games in his memory (3.6.4). Apollodorus names Jason's sons by Hypsipyle Euneus and Nebrophonus (1.9.17; see [[apollodorus]]).

**Later.** Statius tells the whole story in the *Thebaid*. On the march to Thebes the Argives find Hypsipyle at Nemea, and she tells them at length how the women of Lemnos killed their men and how the Argonauts came (Books 4–6; see [[statius]]). For Jason's visit to Lemnos, see [[apollonius-lemnos]] and [[the-lemnian-women]].
