summary: Proteus promises Menelaus that he will not die but be sent to the Elysian plain at the limits of the earth. Later writers turned the plain into the Isles of the Blessed. Hesiod settles some of the heroes of Thebes and Troy there beside Oceanus; Euripides' Dioscuri promise the island to Menelaus; Plato sends the souls of the just there, judged by Rhadamanthys; Horace bids the Romans sail for the blessed fields, which Ulysses' crew never reached; and Strabo set Homer's plain in the far west, in Iberia, and the Isles off Mauretania.

**Homer.** The Elysian plain is named once in Homer, in Proteus' last words to Menelaus. It lies at “the limits of the earth”, where fair-haired Rhadamanthys is; there is no snow, no severe winter and no rain, and Oceanus sends up the breezes of the West Wind; and Menelaus will be sent there instead of dying in Argos, because Helen is his wife and he is the son-in-law of Zeus (*Odyssey* 4.561–569; see [[elysium]], [[rhadamanthus]] and [[zephyrus]]). Homer does not call it an island, and he sends one man there, for a reason of family.

**Hesiod.** In the *Works and Days* the fourth race of men is “the divine race of heroic men, who are called demigods”, destroyed by war, some at seven-gated Thebes and some at Troy, “for the sake of lovely-haired Helen”. Death enveloped some of them; to others Zeus, son of Cronus, gave a life and a dwelling apart from men and settled them “at the limits of the earth” (ἐς πείρατα γαίης), and they live with hearts free of care “in the Isles of the Blessed beside deep-eddying Oceanus”, happy heroes, for whom the grain-giving earth bears honey-sweet fruit three times a year. Another verse says that Cronus is their king, far from the immortals (*Works and Days* 156–173, plain renderings; see [[hesiod]]). The limits of the earth and Oceanus are Proteus' words, and the grain-giving earth and the three yields a year are words of the marvels of *Odyssey* 4, Egypt, “where the grain-giving earth bears the most / drugs”, and Libya, where “the flocks bear young three times in the full year's round” (*Odyssey* 4.229–230, 4.86). Merry sets Hesiod's verses beside Proteus' (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.563). Pindar's island of the blessed, where the good live under the counsels of Rhadamanthys, is told under [[rhadamanthus]].

**Euripides.** At the end of the *Helen* the Dioscuri announce that “for the wanderer Menelaus it is fated by the gods / to dwell in the island of the blessed; / for the gods do not hate the well-born”, while the toils of the countless are greater (*Helen* 1676–1679; see [[euripides-helen]]). The promise is Proteus', the island Hesiod's, and the reason new.

**Plato.** The myth at the end of the *Gorgias* begins, “as Homer says”, from the division of the world among Zeus, Poseidon and Pluto. There was a law in the time of Cronus, and still is among the gods, that the man who has lived his life justly and piously goes at his death to the Isles of the Blessed, to live in all happiness apart from evils, and the unjust and godless man to the prison of retribution that men call Tartarus (*Gorgias* 523a–b). Zeus makes three of his sons judges; they sit in the meadow at the crossroads from which two roads lead, one to the Isles of the Blessed and the other to Tartarus, and Rhadamanthys judges those who come from Asia, Aeacus those from Europe, while Minos decides where the two are in doubt (*Gorgias* 523e–524a). When Rhadamanthys sees a soul that has lived holily and with truth, above all a philosopher's, he admires it and sends it to the Isles of the Blessed (*Gorgias* 526b–c; see [[plato]], [[rhadamanthus]] and [[tartarus]]). Homer's Rhadamanthys simply lives in the Elysian plain, and the man who goes there does not die; Plato's judges the dead. In the *Republic* those who spend their lives in learning will not willingly take up affairs, thinking that they have emigrated while still alive to the Isles of the Blessed, and the guardians, when they have trained others to take their place, depart to live there (*Republic* 7.519c, 7.540b).

**Horace.** In his sixteenth *Epode*, written in the years of civil war, Horace bids the better part of the Roman people leave the city, as the Phocaeans once left theirs, and sail for the fields that wandering Oceanus keeps, “the blessed fields and the rich islands”, where the unplowed land yields grain every year, the unpruned vine always flowers, honey runs from the hollow oak, and neither rain from the East Wind washes the fields nor drought burns the seed (*Epodes* 16.1–56). No Argo came there, nor the Colchian Medea; “not here did Sidonian sailors turn their sailyards, / nor the toiling crew of Ulysses”. Jupiter set those shores apart for a pious people when he stained the golden age with bronze (*Epodes* 16.57–64; plain renderings; see [[horace]]). Merry quotes these lines with Hesiod's (on 4.563).

**Strabo.** Strabo, who treats Homer as the first geographer, takes the Elysian plain for a place in the real west. Homer shows the good fortune and the mild climate of the western peoples, he says, having heard, it seems, of the wealth of Iberia, which Heracles and after him the Phoenicians went out to win; “for there are the breezes of the west wind, and there the poet sets the Elysian plain”, and he quotes Proteus' verses (*Geography* 1.1.4). The Isles of the Blessed, he adds, lie off the farthest west of Maurusia, near the end of Iberia, and their name shows that men thought them happy because they were near such lands (*Geography* 1.1.5). Arguing over Menelaus' wanderings, he asks what limits of the earth Proteus meant, and answers that the West Wind set beside them shows a western place, the farthest of all (*Geography* 1.2.31). In his account of Iberia he explains the choice. The poet had learned of the many expeditions to the farthest parts of Iberia, and heard from the Phoenicians of its wealth and its other goods, and there he made the land of the pious and the Elysian plain where Proteus says Menelaus will dwell: the good air and the gentle breezes of the west wind belong to that western, warm country; it is at the limits of the earth, where the myths had placed Hades; and Rhadamanthys points to a place near Minos, whom Odysseus saw “giving judgment to the dead” (*Geography* 3.2.13, quoting *Odyssey* 4.563–568 and 11.568–569; plain renderings). The later poets, Strabo adds, sang of Heracles' journeys for the cattle of Geryon and the golden apples of the Hesperides, and named Isles of the Blessed, which are still shown not far from the headlands of Maurusia opposite Gadeira (*Geography* 3.2.13; see [[strabo]]).

**The West Wind.** The *Problems* ascribed to Aristotle quote the verse of the West Wind and ask why it is the sweetest of the winds (*Problems* 26.31; see [[elysium]] and [[aristotle]]).
