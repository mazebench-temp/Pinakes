The traditional title of Book 6 is Ἕκτορος καὶ Ἀνδρομάχης ὁμιλία, “the meeting of Hector and Andromache”. It names the scene at the Scaean gates (6.369–502), the longest in the book and the last of Hector's meetings in the city, after his mother (6.251–285) and Paris and Helen (6.312–368). It passes over the rest: the battle without the gods, the robe for Athena, and the meeting of Glaucus and Diomedes on the plain (see [[hector-and-andromache]] and [[hectors-visit-to-troy]]).

The episodes had names before the poem was divided into books, and the names did not keep to the later divisions. Herodotus quotes four lines of this book, on the robes made by the Sidonian women whom Paris brought from Sidon, as standing “in the Aristeia of Diomedes” (Herodotus 2.116, on 6.289–292; see [[title-of-book-5]]). The episode that bore Diomedes' name ran on into Book 6 at least as far as the robe for Athena, which is offered so that the goddess may keep Diomedes from the city (6.269–311). The later title names only the book's end.

ὁμιλία is formed from ὅμιλος, “throng, company”, and means “being together, company, converse”. It is not a Homeric word. Homer has the noun ὅμιλος, most often the throng of battle, and the verb ὁμιλέω, “be among, join with”, which is used of battalions meeting in the fight (19.158). In later Greek the noun came to mean a discourse, and through the Latin of the Church it became the English “homily”.

The poem has its own word for the scene. When Paris catches up with his brother, Hector is about to turn from the place “where he had talked intimately with his wife” (ὅθι ᾗ ὀάριζε γυναικί, 6.516). ὀαρίζω, from ὄαρ, “wife”, is the talk of a man and a woman, the ὀαριστύς worked into Aphrodite's band with love and longing (14.216; see [[oaristys]]). Where the later title says “company”, Homer says “intimate talk”. And the verb returns once more for Hector. Waiting for Achilles under the wall, he knows that there is no way now to talk with him “from an oak or from a rock”, as a young man and a girl talk together (22.126–128). The talk he had with his wife in Book 6 is the talk he cannot have with his enemy in Book 22.
