**Homer.** When Thetis comes to his house, Hephaestus is sweating at his bellows, making twenty tripods to stand around the wall of his hall. He has set golden wheels under the base of each, “so that of themselves they might enter the divine assembly / and go back again to his house, a wonder to see”; only the handles remain to be fitted (18.372–379; see [[tripod]] and [[hephaestus]]). The Iliad has αὐτόματος, “of themselves”, three times more: of the gates of heaven, which groan open of their own accord for Hera's chariot (5.749 = 8.393), and of Menelaus, who comes to Agamemnon's sacrifice unbidden (2.408). A few lines later Hephaestus walks out supported by golden handmaids, “like living young women”, who have a mind in their hearts, and voice, and strength (18.417–420; see [[the-golden-handmaids]] and [[handmaids]]).

**Aristotle.** In the first book of the *Politics* Aristotle divides the household. Property is a part of it, and each art needs its own tools to finish its work; tools are lifeless or living, as for a helmsman the rudder is lifeless and the lookout living, since in the arts an assistant ranks as a tool. So a possession is a tool for living, the slave is a living possession, and every assistant is a tool that comes before tools (*Politics* 1.4, 1253b23–33). “For if each tool could do its own work at a command, or by perceiving in advance what was wanted, as they say the works of Daedalus did, or the tripods of Hephaestus, which, the poet says, ‘enter the divine assembly of themselves’, so that shuttles wove by themselves and plectra played the lyre, master craftsmen would have no need of assistants, nor masters of slaves” (*Politics* 1253b33–1254a1). Aristotle turns Homer's verse into indirect speech, with δύεσθαι for δυσαίατο. He goes on to separate tools of production, like the shuttle, from possessions that are only used, like a garment or a bed, and puts the slave among the second: an assistant in living, not in making (*Politics* 1254a1–8; see [[aristotle]] and [[daedalus]]).

**The poet and the philosopher.** Homer's tripods go to the gods' assembly and come home again, a marvel of the divine smith's house; Aristotle makes them the measure of what human tools cannot do. The golden handmaids of the same scene, who think and speak and work, are nearer to what his argument imagines, but he does not name them.
