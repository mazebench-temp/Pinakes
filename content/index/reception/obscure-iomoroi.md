Agamemnon, going through the ranks, rebukes the men who hang back: Ἀργεῖοι ἰόμωροι ἐλεγχέες, “Argives, you arrow-fighters, you disgraces” (4.242). The same address occurs once more, when the Trojan Acamas taunts the Argives as “ἰόμωροι, insatiable in threats” (14.479). Nowhere else in Homer is the word used, and its meaning was already uncertain in antiquity. The ancient commentators could only guess.

The second element, -μωρος, recurs in two other compounds. ἐγχεσίμωρος is an epithet of warriors, formed on ἔγχος, “spear” (2.692, 2.840; see [[fighters-with-the-spear]]). ὑλακόμωρος, formed on ὑλακή, “barking”, is used in the Odyssey of Eumaeus' dogs (Odyssey 14.29, 16.4). What -μωρος means is itself disputed. Among the proposals are a word for “great, famous” found in Celtic and Germanic names, and μωρός, “foolish, frenzied”.

For the first element there are two old explanations. If it is ἰός, “arrow”, the ἰόμωροι are men who fight with arrows, from a distance, and the word is a sneer. Archers could be scorned in the Iliad: Diomedes insults Paris by calling him an archer, τοξότα (11.385). And in Book 4 the truce has just been broken by an archer. If it comes from a word for “cry”, the ἰόμωροι are men whose strength is in their shouting, braggarts, and Acamas' “insatiable in threats” would suit that sense. The translation follows the first explanation, “arrow-fighters”, without claiming to settle the question.
