summary: Book 15 of the Odyssey is headed Τηλεμάχου πρὸς Εὔμαιον ἄφιξις, “the arrival of Telemachus at Eumaeus' hut”, and the arrival comes only in its last three lines; the welcome is told in the next book. The book tells two journeys in turn, Telemachus' from Sparta by Pylos and the open sea to the coast of Ithaca, and a night in the hut, where the swineherd tells how he himself came to Ithaca, and the heading names the point where the two meet. Monro reports that the supposed Telemachia was usually taken to include the earlier part of this book.

The title of *Odyssey* 15 in Monro's edition is Τηλεμάχου πρὸς Εὔμαιον ἄφιξις, “the arrival of Telemachus at Eumaeus' hut” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, heading of Book 15); this library calls the book “Telemachus' Arrival at the Hut of Eumaeus”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter of the alphabet, this one is ο, omicron (see [[title-of-book-5]]). The journey is told under [[the-journey-of-telemachus]], the leave-taking at Sparta under [[menelaus]], [[helen]] and [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]], the seer taken on board at Pylos under [[theoclymenus]], the swineherd's own story under [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]], and the landing under [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]].

**The words of the title.** The noun ἄφιξις, “arrival”, is not Homer's (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-6]]), but its verb runs through the book. Athena gives the orders that the heading sums up: “But when you reach the first shore of Ithaca, / send the ship and all your companions on to the city, / but you yourself go first of all to the swineherd” (ἀφίκηαι … συβώτην εἰσαφικέσθαι, *Odyssey* 15.36–38). The last of these verses is the one with which she sent the father to the same man (*Odyssey* 13.404; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-13]]). Helen's farewell is the wish for another arrival, “And may you reach, rejoicing, / your well-built house and your native land” (ἀφίκοιο, *Odyssey* 15.128–129), and the book ends with the simple verb, “until he came to the fold” (ὄφρʼ ἵκετʼ αὐλήν, *Odyssey* 15.555; see [[fold]]). Between them the verb is used of exiles and wanderers. Melampus “came to the land of other men, fleeing his native land” (*Odyssey* 15.228, again 15.238; see [[melampus]]). Odysseus tells the swineherd that Zeus has set good beside the evil for him, “since, after toiling much, you came to the house of a man / who is kind” (ἀφίκεο πολλὰ μογήσας, *Odyssey* 15.489–490), a half-verse that the poem uses once more, of Odysseus' own coming to the Phaeacians (*Odyssey* 23.338); and of himself he says that he has “come here after wandering through many cities of mortals” (*Odyssey* 15.492). The heading takes its preposition and its name from the heading of the book before, Ὀδυσσέως πρὸς Εὔμαιον ὁμιλία, “the talk of Odysseus with Eumaeus” (Monro, heading of Book 14; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-14]]). As in that book, the swineherd's name is heard only in the vocative, from the beggar (*Odyssey* 15.307, 15.341, 15.381, 15.486) and once from the poet, who turns to address him (*Odyssey* 15.325; see [[apostrophe]]). Telemachus does not name him. To his crew he says only, “but I will go to the fields and the herdsmen” (*Odyssey* 15.504).

**What the title covers.** The arrival takes the last three lines. The first 181 are at Sparta: Athena's warning in the night, the gifts, the meal, and [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose|the omen of the eagle]] as the chariot sets off (*Odyssey* 15.1–181); the poem had left Telemachus there in *Odyssey* 4 (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-4]]). Then come the drive to Pherae and Pylos and the parting from Peisistratus at the ship (*Odyssey* 15.182–221; see [[peisistratus]]), the seer [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]], who comes up as a suppliant while Telemachus sacrifices at the stern (*Odyssey* 15.222–286), and the voyage north past Elis, Telemachus “pondering whether he would escape death or be caught” (*Odyssey* 15.287–300; see [[the-pointed-islands]]). There the poem turns to the hut, where the beggar tests the swineherd, asks after his master's mother and father and hears the swineherd's own story until they sleep (*Odyssey* 15.301–494; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]), and it comes back to the ship at dawn as it reaches the coast (*Odyssey* 15.495–500). Monro gives the reason for the turn: “The scene changes to the hut of Eumaeus. This digression serves a double purpose in the narrative: (1) it carries on the idyl of Ulysses in the swine-herd's hut, and gives it artistic continuity; and (2) it fills up the otherwise blank space of the night voyage of Telemachus” (Monro on *Odyssey* 15.301–495). At the landing Telemachus sends the ship on to the city and promises to come down himself in the evening (*Odyssey* 15.503–505), but, as Monro notes, “Telemachus does not in fact return till next day” (Monro on *Odyssey* 15.505). The seer is given to Peiraeus after [[the-omen-of-the-falcon|the omen of the falcon]] (*Odyssey* 15.508–546; see [[peiraeus-son-of-clytius]]), and Telemachus binds on his sandals, takes his spear from the deck and goes on foot (*Odyssey* 15.550–557). The heading names the end of the book, where its two threads meet.

**The welcome.** The arrival is completed in the next book, which opens in the hut at dawn with the verse that began the night scene in this one, “And the two of them in the hut, Odysseus and the brilliant swineherd” (*Odyssey* 16.1 = 15.301). “The barking dogs fawned around Telemachus, / and did not bark as he came near”; Odysseus hears the sound of feet and speaks to the swineherd, the first time that the poem gives the name in the accusative of the heading, “And at once he addressed Eumaeus with winged words” (*Odyssey* 16.4–8, plain renderings); and before he has finished speaking, “his dear son / stood in the doorway” (*Odyssey* 16.11–12, a plain rendering). The swineherd lets the vessels in which he was mixing the wine fall from his hands, and kisses his young master's head and eyes and hands, “as a father, in friendship, welcomes his son / who has come from a distant land in the tenth year”, “as one who had escaped from death” (*Odyssey* 16.12–21, plain renderings). Monro heads that book Τηλεμάχου ἀναγνωρισμὸς Ὀδυσσέως, “the recognition of Odysseus by Telemachus” (Monro, heading of Book 16). The arrival named by this heading is the condition of the recognition named by the next.

**The days.** The book takes up the thread that the poem dropped when Athena “went to shining Lacedaemon, after the son of Odysseus” (*Odyssey* 13.440): “The scene changes to Sparta, whither we follow Athene” (Monro on *Odyssey* 15.1). Monro counts the book from the 36th day; the drive from Pherae begins on the morning of the 37th, and the landing comes at the dawn of the 38th (Monro on *Odyssey* 15.1, 15.189, 15.495), so that the night in the hut is the night of the voyage, and not the night after the one in *Odyssey* 14. On that reckoning Odysseus spent four nights in the hut, while Eumaeus will tell Penelope, “For three nights I kept him, and three days I held him / in my hut” (*Odyssey* 17.515–516, a plain rendering). Monro explains the discrepancy by what the poem tells: “only three evenings in the hut of Eumaeus are actually described—one in each of the three books 14–16. The rest of the time spent there—the second and early part of the third day—is a blank in respect of incident, and naturally passed even from the poet's own mind” (Monro on *Odyssey* 17.515). The heading of Book 14 suits this book's evening as well as that book's: the two men again talk until late, and the talk ends in the same formula, “So they spoke such words to one another” (*Odyssey* 15.493 = 14.409; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-14]]).

**The Telemachia.** Many scholars, Monro reports, took the part of the Odyssey that tells of Telemachus for a distinct poem, or for the work of another author composing it for its place in the whole, a “Telemachia”, and “It has usually been taken to include—roughly speaking—the first four books with the earlier part of the fifteenth” (Monro, Appendix I §10). Against the argument from its chronology, by which Telemachus “must have spent twenty days in Sparta” though his visit is always represented as a hurried one, Monro answers that “the epic poet does not aim at accuracy of this kind” (Monro, Appendix I §10). The heading belongs to neither part alone. It names the end of Telemachus' journey by the swineherd's name and the swineherd's hut, where the poem has kept the father since *Odyssey* 14.

**Strabo's names.** Strabo, arguing that Nestor's city lay inland, names the stages of this book in nouns of the heading's kind. On “the return from Sparta” (κατὰ τὴν ἐκ Σπάρτης ἐπάνοδον) Telemachus does not let Peisistratus drive him to the city; “the departure of Telemachus” (ὁ ἀπόπλους τοῦ Τηλεμάχου) is told in the verses of the voyage past [[cruni|Cruni]] and [[chalcis-in-elis|Chalcis]]; and the ship, avoiding the suitors who were watching the strait, “makes its landing” on the far side of the island (ποιεῖται τὴν καταγωγήν) (*Geography* 8.3.26, plain renderings; see [[strabo]] and [[strabo-on-nestors-pylos]]). ἀπόπλους is the first word of Monro's heading of *Odyssey* 13, “the departure of Odysseus from the Phaeacians” (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-13]]). Odysseus' departure from Scheria ends with his landing on Ithaca, and his son's from Pylos ends there too.
