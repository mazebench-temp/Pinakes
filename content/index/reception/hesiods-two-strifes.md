In the *Theogony* Strife is a child of Night, named last in a line with Deceit, Love and Old Age (223–225). Her own children are Toil, Forgetfulness, Famine and Pains, Battles, Fights, Murders and Manslaughters, Quarrels and Disputes, Lawlessness and Ruin, and Oath, “who most harms men on earth whenever anyone knowingly swears a false oath” (226–232). In Book 4 the oaths sworn in Book 3 are broken, and Strife herself goes through the throng.

The *Works and Days* begins with a correction, usually taken to be a correction of the *Theogony*: “So there was not one kind of Strife after all, but on earth there are two” (11–12). One is cruel and fosters evil war and battle (πόλεμόν τε κακὸν καὶ δῆριν ὀφέλλει, 14); no mortal loves her, but men honor her under compulsion, by the will of the gods. The other was born first, of Night, and the son of Cronus, “throned on high, who dwells in the upper air”, set her in the roots of the earth; she is far better for men, for she stirs even the idle to work when he sees his neighbor growing rich (17–24). So potter is angry with potter and carpenter with carpenter, beggar envies beggar and singer singer (25–26).

The Iliad's goddess is the first kind. She is “the sister and companion of man-slaying Ares” (4.441), and she goes through the ranks “increasing the groaning of men” (ὀφέλλουσα στόνον ἀνδρῶν, 4.445), with the same verb that Hesiod uses of the Strife who fosters war. Hesiod calls Zeus by the same words Agamemnon uses when he foresees the fall of Troy, Κρονίδης ὑψίζυγος, αἰθέρι ναίων (*Works and Days* 18; Iliad 4.166). The word ἔρις (“strife”) in Homer covers rivalry of every kind (see [[eris]]), but it is Hesiod who makes the rivalry of craftsmen a goddess.
