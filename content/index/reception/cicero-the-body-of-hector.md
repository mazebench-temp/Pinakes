**Cicero.** The first book of the *Tusculan Disputations* argues that death is not an evil. Near its end Cicero turns to burial, a matter, he says, that is not difficult once it is known that the dead feel nothing (1.102). Socrates told Crito to bury him as he liked, since none of his friends would catch him once he had gone; Diogenes the Cynic told his friends to throw him out unburied, and asked what harm the mauling of beasts could do him if he felt nothing; Anaxagoras, dying at Lampsacus, said that the road to the underworld is the same length from everywhere (1.103–104). Burial, Cicero concludes, concerns the body alone, whether the mind has perished or lives on, and no sensation remains in a body once the mind is gone (1.104).

Then Homer's dragging. “Achilles drags Hector, tied to his chariot: he thinks, I suppose, that Hector is being torn and feels it. So he takes vengeance, as it seems to him; but she mourns as at the bitterest thing: ‘I saw what I suffered most grievously to see, Hector dragged by the four-horse chariot’ (*Hectorem curru quadriiugo raptarier*). What Hector? Or how long will he be Hector? Better Accius, and Achilles wise at last: ‘No, indeed, I have given the body back to Priam; Hector I took away.’ So it was not Hector you dragged, but the body that had been Hector's” (*non igitur Hectora traxisti, sed corpus quod fuerat Hectoris*, 1.105). Cicero names neither the tragedy from which he takes the first quotation nor the woman who speaks it.

**Homer.** The poem speaks of the body as the man himself. Its first lines send the souls of heroes to Hades, “and made the men themselves the spoil of dogs” (1.3–4), and in this book, when Achilles has pierced the tendons and tied Hector to his chariot, “all his head lay in the dust, / that once was handsome; but then Zeus gave him to his enemies / to defile in his own native land” (22.402–404), and it is “his mother” and “his dear father” who see it and wail (22.405–409). Andromache, coming to the wall, sees him being dragged toward the ships (22.463–465), as the woman of Cicero's tragedy saw him. Achilles had threatened that “the dogs and the birds / will tear” him shamefully, and Hector, dying, had begged that his body be given back so that the Trojans might give him his portion of fire (22.335–343; see [[honoring-the-dead]]). The poem's horses, too, are a pair, not a team of four (22.400; see [[virgils-hector]]).

The nearest thing to Cicero's argument in Homer is Apollo's complaint to the gods, in the last book of the poem, that Achilles in his fury defiles the dumb earth (κωφὴν … γαῖαν ἀεικίζει, 24.54; see [[apollo]]). Aeschylus put the thought into his *Phrygians*, the play of the ransom of Hector: whether one wishes to do good to the dead or to harm them, it comes to the same, since the dead feel neither joy nor pain; “yet Nemesis stands above us, and Justice exacts the dead man's anger” (fr. 266 Nauck, quoted by Stobaeus; see [[aeschylus]]). Cicero's argument stops at the first half. Hector's last words carry the warning of the second, “lest I become a cause of the gods' wrath against you” (22.358).
