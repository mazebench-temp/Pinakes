In the Iliad these are words for a common soldier. Odysseus, carrying Agamemnon's scepter, is driving the fleeing army back to the assembly. To the men of the people he gives orders, not reasons: not every Achaean can be king, and the scepter and the ordinances are Zeus' gifts to one man. See [[one-ruler]] and [[the-test-of-the-army]].

Aristotle gave the half-line its most famous second life. Book Λ of the *Metaphysics* is his account of the unmoved mover, the one eternal principle on which heaven and nature depend. Its last chapter opens by comparing the universe to an army, whose good lies both in its order and in its general, and more in the general, since the order exists because of him. It closes by criticizing philosophers who posit a separate principle for each kind of being, and so make the universe "episodic" (ἐπεισοδιώδης), a string of unconnected episodes. The target is usually taken to be Speusippus, Plato's successor in the Academy. "But the things that are do not wish to be governed badly," Aristotle concludes, and quotes Homer: "the rule of many is not good; let there be one ruler." Odysseus' rebuke now speaks of the cosmos.

In the *Politics* he quotes it again, with a question. Discussing a democracy in which the people rule by decree rather than by law, and so become a single "monarch composed of many", he remarks that it is unclear which kind of rule by many Homer calls "not good": this kind, or rule by several men each acting alone (4.4, 1292a13–15).

The line became a motto of monarchy. Suetonius reports that Caligula, hearing the client kings who had come to Rome argue at his table about the nobility of their families, cried out in Greek the words of 2.204–205, "let there be one ruler, one king" (*Caligula* 22.1).
