In Book 3 Paris proposes that the two armies sit down while he and Menelaus fight for Helen and her possessions, the winner to take the woman home (3.67–75). In the *Aeneid*, Turnus makes the same offer to King Latinus. Let the Latins "sit and watch" (*sedeant spectentque Latini*) while he meets Aeneas alone, and let Lavinia go as wife to the winner (12.11–17). The prize is again a woman.

The treaty follows Homer's oath closely (12.161–215). Victims are brought, and the priests mark them by cutting hair from their foreheads, as Agamemnon cut hair from the heads of the lambs (3.273). Aeneas prays first: "Now let the Sun be my witness, and this Earth, as I call on them" (12.176), as Agamemnon called on Zeus, Helios, the rivers and the earth (3.276–280). Each side states the terms for victory and defeat, and Latinus swears by earth, sea and stars, and by a scepter that will never again put out leaves, the oath of Achilles in Iliad 1 (1.234–239).

Then the truce is broken (12.216–323). The Rutulians think the fight unequal, and Turnus' sister, the nymph Juturna, stirs them up in disguise; an augur hurls a spear, the armies clash, and Aeneas, trying to hold his men back, is wounded by an arrow from an unknown hand. This is Iliad 4, where Athena in disguise persuades Pandarus to shoot, and Menelaus is wounded by his arrow in the middle of the truce. Virgil has joined the duel, the oaths and their breaking into a single episode.

The ends differ. In Iliad 3 the duel settles nothing: Aphrodite snatches Paris away in a mist, and the war goes on. In the *Aeneid* the single combat, delayed for most of the book, comes at last, and ends the poem with Turnus' death.
