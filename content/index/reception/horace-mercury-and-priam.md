**Horace.** The tenth poem of Horace's first book of *Odes* is a hymn to Mercury, the eloquent grandson of Atlas, who shaped the savage ways of the first men with speech and with the wrestling school, the messenger of Jupiter and the gods and father of the curved lyre, clever at hiding in playful theft whatever he pleases; as a boy he stole Apollo's cattle, and while Apollo threatened him, he took his quiver too, and Apollo laughed (*Odes* 1.10.1–12). Then: “Indeed, with you as his guide, rich Priam, leaving Ilium, slipped past the proud sons of Atreus, the Thessalian fires and the camp hostile to Troy” (*Quin et Atridas duce te superbos / Ilio dives Priamus relicto / Thessalosque ignis et iniqua Troiae / castra fefellit*, *Odes* 1.10.13–16). The hymn ends with Mercury's work among the dead: “You set pious souls in the happy seats, and with your golden wand you drive the weightless throng, welcome to the gods above and to those below” (*Odes* 1.10.17–20).

**Homer.** Every word of the Priam stanza has its place in Book 24. Zeus sends Hermes, “since to you above all it is dearest / to keep company with a man”, to lead Priam to the ships so that none of the Danaans sees him until he comes to the son of Peleus (24.334–338; see [[hermes]] and [[zeus]]). The god goes as a guide, διάκτορος, “the guide, the slayer of Argus” (24.339; see [[guide]]); he takes the reins, and when they reach the ditch the sentries are busy with their supper, and he pours sleep over them, opens the gates and brings Priam and the gifts in (24.440–447; see [[night-watch]]). The proud sons of Atreus are the danger that both Achilles and Hermes name. Achilles has Priam sleep outside the hut, lest a counselor see him and tell Agamemnon (24.650–655), and Hermes wakes him before dawn with the same fear: his sons would pay three times as much to ransom him alive, “if Agamemnon, / son of Atreus, learns of you, and all the Achaeans know it” (24.686–688; see [[agamemnon]]). Priam is rich in the poem too. Achilles tells him that once, they say, he excelled all men from Lesbos to the Hellespont “in wealth and sons” (24.543–546), and his ransom comes out of his chests (24.228–237; see [[boundless-ransom]]). Homer never names Thessaly; Horace's Thessalian fires are those of Achilles' Myrmidons, who came from Phthia, in what later Greeks called Thessaly (see [[myrmidons]] and [[phthia]]).

**The wand.** Horace's last stanza turns from the Iliad to the Odyssey. At the start of its last book Hermes calls out the souls of the suitors, holding in his hands “a fine golden wand”, and leads them gibbering to the meadow of asphodel (*Odyssey* 24.1–14). The verses that describe the wand there are the verses of the Iliad, where Hermes sets out to meet Priam: “and he took the wand with which he bewitches the eyes of men, / of whomever he will, and again wakes others who are sleeping” (24.343–344; from τῇ τʼ ἀνδρῶν on, the words are *Odyssey* 24.3–4; see [[wand]]). In the Iliad the god who bewitches the eyes of men pours sleep over the sentries, and wakes Priam before dawn to bring him out of the camp again (24.445, 24.677–691). Horace's hymn puts the two last books together, the guide of the living through the enemy camp and the guide of the dead.
