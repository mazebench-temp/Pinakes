**Homer.** In the Iliad Nestor is the clear-voiced speaker “from whose tongue the voice flowed sweeter than honey” (1.249), and Odysseus the man whose words came “like the snowflakes of winter” (3.222; see [[nestor]] and [[odysseus]]). In Book 3 of the Odyssey Nestor makes the two of them the counselors of the Achaeans at Troy: no one there wished to match himself with Odysseus in counsel, and he and Nestor never spoke at odds, in the assembly or in the council (*Odyssey* 3.120–129). Plato's dialogues come back to the pair, and to Nestor's speech.

**The Phaedrus.** Socrates asks whether the art of speech serves only in the law courts and in public speaking, or wherever men speak. Phaedrus has heard of it only for lawsuits and the assembly, and Socrates asks: “Have you heard only of the arts of speech of Nestor and Odysseus, which the two of them composed in their leisure at Troy, and never of those of Palamedes?” “No, by Zeus,” Phaedrus answers, “nor of Nestor's either, unless you are dressing up Gorgias as a kind of Nestor, or Thrasymachus and Theodorus as an Odysseus” (*Phaedrus* 261b–c). The three were teachers of rhetoric in Socrates' lifetime (see [[gorgias]], [[thrasymachus]] and [[theodorus-of-byzantium]]). Socrates lets them be, and turns to an “Eleatic Palamedes” who can make the same things seem to his hearers like and unlike, one and many, at rest and in motion (261d). The joke takes the two counselors of Homer's war for the first authors of handbooks, written ἐν Ἰλίῳ σχολάζοντες, “at their leisure at Troy”; Nestor's own account of those years is of nine years spent “busy all around” the Trojans “with tricks of every kind” (*Odyssey* 3.118–119).

**The Laws.** The Athenian, asking how the best constitution could arise, says that it comes about only when a divine love of temperate and just ways arises in men of great power, “or if ever someone brings back the nature of Nestor, who, they say, surpassing all men in the strength of his speech, surpassed them still more in self-control (τῷ σωφρονεῖν)”. That, they say, came about at Troy, but in our time not at all; if such a man has lived or will live or lives now among us, blessed are those who hear the words that come from that temperate mouth (*Laws* 4.711d–e).

**The Hippias Major.** Hippias tells Socrates of a speech that won him fame at Sparta, on the fine pursuits that a young man should follow. “When Troy had been taken, the speech says, Neoptolemus asked Nestor what pursuits are fine, by which a young man who pursued them would win the greatest fame; and after that it is Nestor who speaks, and lays down for him a great many lawful and most beautiful things” (*Hippias Major* 286a–b; see [[hippias-of-elis]]). Homer's Nestor, after Troy, advises the son of another hero. He names Neoptolemus in *Odyssey* 3 only as “the shining son of great-hearted Achilles”, who led the Myrmidons home, and he tells Telemachus to be valiant “so that some one of those born hereafter may speak well of you” (*Odyssey* 3.188–189, 3.199–200; see [[neoptolemus]]).

**The wisest.** In the *Hippias Minor* Hippias says that Homer made Achilles the best of those who came to Troy, Nestor the wisest (σοφώτατον) and Odysseus the most versatile (*Hippias Minor* 364c; see [[plato-hippias-minor]]). In the *Symposium* Alcibiades says that one might liken Brasidas and others to Achilles, and Nestor and Antenor to Pericles, but Socrates to no one, ancient or living (*Symposium* 221c; see [[antenor]]). Athena's word for Nestor is πεπνυμένος: “he will not tell a lie, for he is very wise” (*Odyssey* 3.20; see [[wise]]), the verse with which Nestor in turn commends Menelaus (*Odyssey* 3.328).
