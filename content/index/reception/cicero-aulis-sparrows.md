The second book of Cicero's *On Divination* (44 BCE) is his answer, in his own person, to the case for divination put by his brother Quintus in the first. Among the famous examples he examines is Calchas' reading of the sign at Aulis. He quotes Odysseus' speech in Latin hexameters, a version generally taken to be his own, from "Bear up, men, and endure hard labors with courage" to the promise of Troy in the tenth year (2.63–64).

Then comes the critique (2.65). What augur would infer from sparrows a number of years, rather than of months or days? Why does Calchas interpret the sparrows, in which there was nothing portentous, and say nothing of the serpent, which is said to have been turned to stone, a thing that cannot happen? And what, in the end, do sparrows have in common with years?

The objections go to the center of Homer's scene. In the Iliad the marvel is the serpent. Zeus sent it, and Zeus turned it to stone, making it "a sign for all to see" (2.318–319). Calchas' reading rests on the number, nine, and on a correspondence that only a seer can supply. For Odysseus' listeners the proof is the war itself, now in its tenth year: "and now it is all being fulfilled" (2.330). For Cicero it showed that the interpretation of signs is guesswork.
