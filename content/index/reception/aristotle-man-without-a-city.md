**Homer.** Nestor speaks after Diomedes has defied Agamemnon's proposal to flee and the army has shouted its approval (9.29–51). He praises the young man, takes the argument further, and in the middle of his speech condemns the man who loves “war among his own people”, the strife among the Achaeans that he means to end (9.53–64). Then he turns to practical things, the watch and the council of the elders (9.65–78; see [[nestor]]). Each of the three adjectives denies a bond. ἀφρήτωρ is “without a φρήτρη”, a clan or brotherhood, the unit by which Nestor told Agamemnon to array the army in Book 2, “so that clan may help clan” (2.362–363). ἀθέμιστος is “without θέμιστες”, the ordinances that Zeus gives to kings (9.99; see [[themis]]); the *Odyssey* uses it of the Cyclopes (*Odyssey* 9.106). ἀνέστιος is “without a hearth” (ἑστία), without a home. ἀφρήτωρ and ἀνέστιος occur nowhere else in Homer (see [[without-clan-law-or-hearth]]).

**Aristotle.** The first book of the *Politics* traces the city from its parts. The household comes first, then the village, and in the oldest times each household was ruled by its eldest, as Homer says of the Cyclopes: “each lays down the law for his children and his wives” (1252b19–23, quoting *Odyssey* 9.114–115). The city, made of villages, is the end of these partnerships and is self-sufficient, and so it exists by nature:

“From these things it is clear that the city belongs among the things that exist by nature, and that man is by nature a political animal; and the man who is without a city (ἄπολις) through nature and not through chance is either a poor creature or greater than a man, like the one reviled by Homer, ‘without clan, without law, without hearth’. For one who is such by nature is at the same time a lover of war, being like an isolated piece in a game of draughts” (1253a1–7).

Man alone has speech, Aristotle goes on, to show what is useful and harmful, just and unjust, and the city is prior to each of its members. Whoever cannot share in a community, or needs nothing because he is sufficient to himself, is no part of a city, “so that he is either a beast or a god” (1253a25–29).

**The two lines.** Aristotle quotes only 9.63, but his “lover of war” (πολέμου ἐπιθυμητής) takes up the next line, the man “who loves war” (πολέμου ἔραται, 9.64). He also changes its sense. Nestor condemns a man who chooses civil strife and so puts himself outside clan, law and hearth. Aristotle describes a man who is outside the city by his nature, and finds the love of war in him as a consequence, the solitary piece on the board with no partner (ἄζυξ). Nestor's three privations become Aristotle's portrait of the man without a city. Aristotle uses two more verses of Book 9 in the *Politics*, on equal honors and on the dishonored stranger: see [[aristotle]].
