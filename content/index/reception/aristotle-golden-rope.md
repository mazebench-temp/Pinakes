The fourth chapter of the *Movement of Animals* turns from animals to the heavens. Every animal that moves itself needs something at rest to push against. Must there also be, Aristotle asks, something unmoved and at rest outside whatever is moved, which is no part of it, and must this hold of the whole universe too? It would seem strange if the origin of its motion were within it. “Hence,” he writes, “to those who take this view Homer would seem to have spoken well”, and he quotes Zeus' challenge, 8.21–22 and then 8.20: “but you could not drag Zeus, highest of all, down from the sky to the plain, not even if you toiled very hard; lay hold of it, all you gods and all you goddesses.” “For what is altogether unmoved cannot be moved by anything.” From this, he goes on, the old problem is also solved, whether the frame of the heavens can be dissolved or not, if it hangs from an unmoved principle (εἰ ἐξ ἀκινήτου ἤρτηται ἀρχῆς).

**The text.** Aristotle's lines differ from ours. He puts the challenge before the command to take hold of the rope, and where the manuscripts call Zeus ὕπατον μήστωρʼ, “the highest counselor” (8.22), he has ὕπατον πάντων, “highest of all”. He may have quoted from memory, or from a text that read so. His quotation of the opening of Book 2 also differs from the manuscripts (see [[all-the-gods-slept]]).

**The reading.** Aristotle uses only the first half of Zeus' boast. The gods hanging on the rope are all that moves; Zeus, whom they cannot move, is the fixed point outside them. He leaves aside the second half, in which Zeus hauls up earth and sea and hangs the world from Olympus. But his own word for the dependence of the world on its first mover is the one he uses here of the heavens: on such a principle, he says in the *Metaphysics*, “hang the heavens and nature” (ἐκ τοιαύτης ἄρα ἀρχῆς ἤρτηται ὁ οὐρανὸς καὶ ἡ φύσις, Λ 7, 1072b13–14). Homer's Zeus would hang the world from a peak of Olympus; Aristotle's heavens hang from the unmoved mover.

Plato's Socrates had read the same rope as the sun, whose motion keeps all things in being (see [[plato-golden-rope]]). For the later chain of causes reaching down from heaven, see [[homers-golden-chain]]. See also [[aristotle]] and [[zeus]].
