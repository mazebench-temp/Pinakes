Gotthold Ephraim Lessing's *Laocoön, or On the Limits of Painting and Poetry* takes its name from the ancient statue of the Trojan priest and his sons strangled by serpents. Its argument is that the arts work in different media. Painting sets figures and colors side by side in space, and can show bodies. Poetry sets words one after another in time, and should show actions. A poet who lists a woman's features one by one gives the reader nothing to see, since the parts never come together into a whole.

Homer, Lessing says, knew this. He never describes Helen's beauty in detail; we learn only in passing that she has white arms and lovely hair. "What Homer could not describe in its parts, he makes us recognize in its effect." The old men of Troy sit on the wall, past fighting, and when Helen comes they say softly to one another that there is no blame in suffering so long for such a woman. Lessing asks what could give a livelier idea of beauty than to have cold old age admit that it was worth a war that cost so much blood and so many tears. His advice to poets follows: paint the delight, the attraction, the love and rapture that beauty causes, and you have painted beauty itself (chapter 21).

He knew the story that the painter Zeuxis wrote these same lines of Homer under his picture of Helen, and treats it as a contest between painting and poetry in which both deserved the crown (chapter 22; see [[zeuxis-helen]]).

The elders' verdict does not end with praise. "But even so," they go on, let her go home in the ships (3.159–160). They measure the beauty by its cost, and still want to be rid of it. Lucian's Hermes quotes the same verdict over Helen's skull: see [[lucian-helens-skull]].
