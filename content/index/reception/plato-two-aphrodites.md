The Iliad gives Aphrodite a father and a mother. She is Διὸς θυγάτηρ, “daughter of Zeus” (5.131, 5.312, 5.348), and when Diomedes has wounded her she flies to Olympus and falls on the knees of Dione, “her mother”, who takes her daughter in her arms (5.370–372; see [[dione]]). Zeus calls her “my child” when he tells her to leave war alone (5.428). Hesiod tells another birth. When Cronus cut off the genitals of Uranus and threw them into the sea, a white foam (ἀφρός) rose around the immortal flesh, and in it a girl grew, who came ashore at Cyprus; gods and men call her Aphrodite because she was nurtured in the foam (*Theogony* 188–206). Hesiod's Dione is one of the daughters of Oceanus (*Theogony* 353).

Plato's *Symposium* turns the two stories into two goddesses. Pausanias, whose praise of Love is the second that the dialogue reports, objects that the company has been told to praise Love without saying which Love. There is no Aphrodite without Eros; if there were one Aphrodite there would be one Eros, but there are two. “The elder, surely, is the motherless daughter of Uranus, whom we call Heavenly (Οὐρανία); the younger is the daughter of Zeus and Dione, whom we call Common (Πάνδημος)” (180d–e). So there are two Loves as well. The Love of the Common Aphrodite does whatever comes its way. Worthless men love by it, women no less than boys, bodies more than souls. The Love of the Heavenly Aphrodite, who has no share of the female, turns to males, loving what is by nature stronger and has more intelligence (181a–c).

Pausanias names neither poet, but his two genealogies are theirs. The elder goddess, born of Uranus without a mother, is Hesiod's; the younger, daughter of Zeus and Dione, is Homer's. In the *Cratylus*, playing with the names of the gods, Socrates declines to argue with Hesiod: Aphrodite was so called because she was born from the foam (406c–d). The Aphrodite of Book 5, whom Diomedes knows for “a feeble god” and whom Zeus sends back to “the lovely works of marriage” (5.331, 5.428–429; see [[the-works-of-war]]), is on his account the lesser of the two.
