**Homer.** Menelaus names the lands of his eight years of wandering, from Cyprus to Libya, and adds what Libya is known for: the flocks bear young three times a year, and neither master nor shepherd lacks cheese, meat or milk (*Odyssey* 4.81–89; see [[libya]] and [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). κεραός, “horned”, is in the Iliad the epithet of the stag (*Iliad* 3.24, 11.475, 15.271, 16.158); ἄφαρ is “at once”.

**Herodotus.** Herodotus, describing the Scythian winter, when the cold lasts eight months, thinks that the cold is also why the hornless breed of cattle there grows no horns, and finds a witness in Homer's verse in the Odyssey, “Libya, where the lambs are horned at once”, “rightly said, that in hot lands the horns come quickly, while in severe cold the beasts either grow no horns at all or grow them with difficulty” (*Histories* 4.28–29, plain renderings; see [[herodotus]]). His text has ὅθι, “where”, for ἵνα (see the translation's note on 4.85).

**Aristotle.** In the *History of Animals*, gathering the ways in which animals differ from place to place, Aristotle writes that in Libya the horned beasts are born with their horns at once (εὐθὺς γίνεται κέρατα ἔχοντα), “not only the lambs, as Homer says, but the others too”, while in the Pontus, about Scythia, they are born hornless (*History of Animals* 8.28; see [[aristotle]]). The manuscripts' text says “the horned among the rams”, which Schneider changed to “the horned among the animals” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.85). Aristotle takes ἄφαρ to mean “from birth”, widens Homer's lambs to all the horned beasts, and sets beside Libya the same Scythian contrast as Herodotus.

**Two readings.** Merry sets the two side by side. If we follow Herodotus, the lambs “begin at once to become horned”, the horns appearing soon after birth, as Eustathius also took it, noting only that ἄφαρ is stronger than Herodotus' ταχύ, “quickly”. But from the other places where τελέθω is used, Merry concludes that it means “be” rather than “become”, so that the lambs “are horned at once”, born with their horns grown or sprouting, which agrees best with Aristotle (Merry on 4.85). The translation follows him (see the translation's note on 4.85).

**Strabo.** At the start of his *Geography*, Strabo argues that the poets show as the wisest of the heroes those who traveled far and wandered, for they count it a great thing to have seen the cities of many men and learned their mind. Menelaus is one of his examples: he quotes the wanderings from Cyprus to Libya, “where the lambs are horned at once”, with the verse that adds the peculiarity of the land, that the flocks bear three times in the full year's round. All such things, he says, are a great preparation for practical wisdom, through learning the nature of a country and the kinds of its animals and plants (*Geography* 1.1.16, quoting *Odyssey* 4.83–86; see [[strabo]]).
