In Sophocles' *Ajax* the hero has come to his senses after slaughtering the army's cattle in his madness, and has resolved to die. Tecmessa, the Phrygian princess he took captive and made the mother of his son, pleads with him (*Ajax* 485–524), and her speech is built on Andromache's. She was born free, the daughter of a rich father, and is now a slave. If he dies, she will be dragged off that same day with his son into slavery, and one of her masters will say a bitter word: “See the bedfellow of Ajax, who was the mightiest of the army, what servitude she endures instead of what envy” (500–503). So Hector imagined a man who would see Andromache weeping and say, “This is the wife of Hector, who was always the best in the fighting” (6.459–461). “I have nothing left to look to but you,” Tecmessa says. “You destroyed my country with your spear, and another fate brought down my mother and my father to dwell in Hades” (514–517). Andromache lost her father and brothers to Achilles and her mother to Artemis, and Hector is father, mother and brother to her (6.413–430). Tecmessa lost her country to the man she is pleading with.

Ajax asks for the child. The boy will not be frightened at the sight of fresh blood, he says, if he is truly his son (545–547). Astyanax shrank from the bronze and the nodding plume of his father's helmet (6.466–470). Then Ajax prays: “My son, may you be luckier than your father, but in all else like him, and you will not be base” (550–551). Hector asked the gods that his son might be as good in strength as he, and that someone might say of him, “He is far better than his father” (6.476–479; see [[fathers-and-sons]]). Ajax wishes his son only better fortune. He knows how his own ends.

He names the boy for his shield. “Take it, my son, the thing you are named for, Eurysaces”: the unbreakable shield of seven oxhides (574–576). Hector's son was named for his father's defense of the city: the Trojans called him Astyanax, “lord of the city”, “for Hector alone was guarding Ilios” (6.402–403; see [[astyanax]]). Eurysaces is given his father's shield to keep; in Euripides, Astyanax is buried in Hector's (see [[euripides-astyanax]]).

**Virgil.** Aeneas, arming for the last battle of the *Aeneid*, embraces Ascanius in his armor and kisses him lightly through his helmet: “Learn courage from me, my son, and true labor; fortune from others” (*Aeneid* 12.433–436). When the boy is grown, he is to remember the examples of his family, “your father Aeneas and your uncle Hector” (*Aeneid* 12.438–440). The wish is Ajax's. The helmet stays on: Aeneas kisses his son through it, where Hector took his off and laid it on the ground (6.472–473).
