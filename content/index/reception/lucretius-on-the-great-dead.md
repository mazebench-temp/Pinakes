**Lucretius.** The third book of *On the Nature of Things* argues that the mind is mortal, and that death is therefore nothing to us (*nil igitur mors est ad nos*, 3.830–831). Near its end Lucretius tells the reader what he might sometimes say to himself (*hoc etiam tibi tute interdum dicere possis*). Even good Ancus has left the light, “who was better than you, wretch, in many things” (*qui melior multis quam tu fuit, improbe, rebus*); since then many other kings and lords of great peoples have died. He too who once laid a road over the great sea for his legions to march across the deep, and scorned the roaring of the sea with his horses, lost the light and poured out his soul from his dying body; Scipio, the thunderbolt of war and terror of Carthage, gave his bones to the earth as if he had been the lowest servant. Add the discoverers of the sciences and the graces, add the companions of the Muses, among whom Homer alone held the scepter, and he sleeps the same sleep as the rest. Democritus, when ripe old age warned him that his memory was failing, went of his own accord to meet death. “Epicurus himself died, when the light of his life had run its course, he who surpassed the human race in genius and put them all out, as the sun risen in the sky puts out the stars. Will you then hesitate, and resent dying?” (*ipse Epicurus obit decurso lumine vitae … tu vero dubitabis et indignabere obire?*, *On the Nature of Things* 3.1024–1045). You, he goes on, whose life is all but dead while you live and see, who sleep away the greater part of your time and snore awake (3.1046–1052).

**Achilles.** Walter Leaf, after Plutarch's story of Callisthenes, adds that Lucretius' adaptation of the line is still better known, and quotes *Ipse Epicurus obit … tu vero dubitabis et indignabere obire?* (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.107; see [[callisthenes-quotes-achilles]]). Achilles tells the suppliant Lycaon, son of Priam, that he too must die, and asks why he laments: “Patroclus died too, who was far better than you” (21.106–107). Then he turns to himself, handsome and tall, the son of a brave father and a goddess, over whom death and mighty fate hang as well; there will come a morning or an evening or a midday when someone will take his life too (21.108–113; see [[patroclus-died-too]], [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). Lucretius' first example has the shape of Achilles' line, a man better than you who has died, and his question answers Achilles' “why do you lament like this?” (21.106). His list climbs, as Achilles' speech does, from the better man to the greatest: Achilles ends with himself, Lucretius with the poet who held the scepter and the master of his own school. But the purpose has changed. Achilles speaks to a man he is about to kill; Lucretius gives the words to the reader, to free him from the fear of death.
