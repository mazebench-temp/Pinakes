summary: Book 19 of the Iliad is traditionally called Μήνιδος ἀπόρρησις, “the Renunciation of Wrath”. The title puts the poem's own words into later Greek, for Thetis bids Achilles renounce his wrath and the Achaeans rejoice that he has renounced it. The renunciation takes one short speech and the formal reconciliation fills more than half the book; the title passes over Briseis' lament, the fast, the arming and the speaking horse, and over the new anger that sends Achilles back to the war.

The traditional title of *Iliad* 19 is Μήνιδος ἀπόρρησις, “the Renunciation of Wrath”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter, this one is Τ, tau (see [[title-of-book-5]]). The assembly that it names is told under [[the-renunciation-of-wrath]], and the wrath itself under [[menis]].

**The words.** The title turns the poem's own words into later Greek. Thetis, bringing the new armor at dawn, tells Achilles to call the Achaean heroes to an assembly, “renouncing your wrath against Agamemnon, shepherd of the people” (μῆνιν ἀποειπών, 19.35). When he has spoken, “the well-greaved Achaeans rejoiced / that the great-hearted son of Peleus had renounced his wrath” (μῆνιν ἀπειπόντος, 19.74–75). μῆνις is the first word of the poem (1.1), and its end has been spoken of before. Phoenix told Achilles, “I would not bid you cast aside your wrath” unless Agamemnon brought gifts (9.517); Achilles said that he would not end it until the war cry reached his own ships (16.61–63); and the Trojans, seeing Patroclus in his armor, thought that he “had thrown away his wrath and chosen friendship” (16.282). The verb ἀπειπεῖν is “speak out” and “refuse”, and in *Iliad* 9 it told Achilles' refusal of the embassy: the envoys sat silent, “amazed at his word, for he had refused very forcefully” (9.431), and Agamemnon asked Odysseus on his return, “or has he refused, and does anger still hold his proud heart?” (9.675; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]). Phoenix used it too, of the man who “refuses them and stubbornly denies them”, the Prayers who follow Blind Folly (9.510; see [[litai]]). In this book the same verb renounces the wrath itself.

**The noun.** ἀπόρρησις, “renunciation”, is not Homeric. In Attic prose it is a refusal or a giving up: Plato uses it of Thrasymachus' withdrawal from the argument, which Glaucon would not accept (*Republic* 2.357a), and a Byzantine lexicon, the Suda, glosses it ἀπαγόρευσις, “a forbidding, a renouncing” (Suda α 3500). The title's genitive μήνιδος is not Homer's either. The Odyssey has μήνιος (*Odyssey* 3.135), and so does Plato's Socrates when he refuses to praise Phoenix for advising Achilles not to give up his wrath without gifts (*Republic* 3.390e; see [[plato-phoenix-and-the-gifts]]); the Suda writes μήνιδος (π 198). Where the name was first used is not known. Aelian's list of the parts of Homer once sung on their own has no such name (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[aelian]] and [[title-of-book-16]]).

**What the title covers.** The renunciation itself is quickly done. Achilles stands up in the assembly and asks the son of Atreus whether their strife over a girl was better for either of them; he wishes that Artemis had killed her on the day he took Lyrnessus (19.56–64; see [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]]). Then: “Now I put a stop to my anger; there is no need for me / to rage stubbornly forever” (19.67–68). He calls it χόλος, “anger” (see [[cholos]]); the narrator, in the next lines, calls it μῆνις (19.75). The rest of the assembly makes the end of the quarrel formal. Agamemnon answers from his seat that Zeus, Fate and the Erinys blinded him, and tells how Blind Folly blinded even Zeus (19.76–144; see [[ate]] and [[the-birth-of-heracles]]). Achilles leaves him to give the gifts or keep them, and wants battle at once (19.145–153). Odysseus persuades them that the army must eat first, and that the amends must be made in due form before all the Achaeans (19.154–237; see [[fasting]], [[amends-and-reconciliation]] and [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]]). The gifts are brought out, Agamemnon swears over a boar that he never laid a hand on Briseis, and Achilles lays the whole quarrel on Zeus: “Father Zeus, truly great are the blind follies you give to men” (19.238–275; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]] and [[oath]]). The assembly is dissolved, and the Myrmidons carry the gifts to his huts (19.276–281). Walter Leaf suspected that the book was once independent of the embassy of *Iliad* 9, and that the lines tying Agamemnon's offer here to the promises made there were added to bring the two into line (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.136; see the translation's note on 19.136–141).

**The arms.** The book opens with the end of the Making of the Arms. Thetis brings the armor at dawn, the Myrmidons cannot bear to look at it, Achilles delights in it, and his mother drips nectar and ambrosia into the body of Patroclus to keep it sound (19.1–39; see [[the-making-of-the-arms]]). A few manuscripts add a line after 19.39, and Leaf suggests that it survives from a time when the rhapsody of the Making of the Arms ended there and not with *Iliad* 18 (on 19.39; see [[title-of-book-18]]).

**What the title leaves out.** The renounced wrath gives way at once to another anger. At the sight of the armor “anger entered him the more” (19.16), and he will not eat while Patroclus lies unavenged (19.199–214, 19.303–308; see [[fasting]]). The book has two laments over Patroclus, Briseis' and Achilles' own (19.282–300, 19.315–337; see [[briseis]] and [[lament]]), Zeus' pity and Athena's nectar (19.340–354; see [[nectar]]), and the arming of Achilles, “raging against the Trojans” (19.364–398; see [[arming-scene]]). It ends with the horse Xanthus, given a voice by Hera, foretelling his death, and with his answer: “I will not stop until I have given the Trojans their fill of war” (19.404–423; see [[xanthus-the-horse]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). The wrath against Agamemnon is over; the fury against the Trojans that takes its place fills the books that follow (see [[achilles]]).

**The older readers.** The ancient writers who use the book do not name it. The philosophers and scholars went mostly to the assembly of reconciliation. Plato's Agathon cites the tender feet of Blind Folly as Homer's proof that a god can be tender (*Symposium* 195c–e; see [[plato-the-tender-feet-of-ate]]), and Plato's Socrates will not have the young believe that Achilles was so fond of money as to take Agamemnon's gifts (*Republic* 3.390e). A note collected among the fragments of Aristotle's *Homeric Problems* asks why Hera made Zeus swear, when his nod was pledge enough (see [[aristotle-the-oath-of-zeus]]). Pausanias quotes the oath-boar flung into the sea as proof that in the old days no one ate a victim over which an oath had been sworn (*Description of Greece* 5.24.9–11; see [[pausanias-the-oath-boar]]). Strabo quotes Agamemnon's order to choose κούρητες, “young men”, in his account of the Curetes, and Briseis' lament to show that the city of Mynes was Lyrnessus (see [[strabo-on-the-kouretes]] and [[strabo-on-the-city-of-mynes]]). Cicero puts Odysseus' rule of a single day's mourning into Latin verse (*Tusculan Disputations* 3.65; see [[cicero-on-a-day-of-mourning]]), and Proclus explains why it is Hera who gives the horse its voice (see [[the-horses-voice]]).

The poets and artists went to the arms and to the birth of Heracles. The Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles* tells the begetting of Heracles that Agamemnon passes over, and the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* has Hera keep Eileithyia from another mother in labor (see [[heracles-in-the-hesiodic-shield]] and [[eileithyia-kept-from-leto]]). A scene on the chest of Cypselus at Olympia was taken, Pausanias reports, for Thetis receiving the arms from Hephaestus with the Nereids beside her, and Aeschylus named a tragedy the *Nereids* (see [[the-nereids-and-the-arms]]); Euripides' *Scyrians* was set on the island where Achilles' son was reared (see [[euripides-scyrians]]). Virgil's Venus brings Aeneas his arms as Thetis brings Achilles his, and Aeneas cannot have his fill of looking at them (*Aeneid* 8.608–625; see [[virgil-shield-of-aeneas]]).
