summary: At the river of the underworld Virgil's Sibyl tells Aeneas that the crowd on the bank are the unburied, whom Charon may not carry across until their bones are at rest. It is the rule that the ghost of Patroclus gives Achilles, and not the Odyssey's, and Virgil's unburied helmsman Palinurus asks Aeneas, as the ghost asks Achilles, for his hand.

**Homer.** The soul of Patroclus, standing over the sleeping Achilles, asks to be buried as quickly as may be, “so that I may pass the gates of Hades. / The souls keep me far away, the phantoms of the dead, / and they do not yet let me mingle with them beyond the river, / but I wander aimlessly through the wide-gated house of Hades. / And give me your hand, in my grief; for never again / shall I come back from Hades, once you have given me my portion of fire” (23.71–76; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]] and [[psyche]]). The Odyssey has another rule. Elpenor, left unburied in Circe's house, is the first soul to meet Odysseus at the edge of the underworld; he begs to be burned with his arms and given a mound on the shore of the gray sea, with the oar he rowed with set on top, but he is not kept from the other shades (*Odyssey* 11.51–78, 12.10–15). Walter Leaf notes the difference, which he puts down to the different ages and places of the poems, and says that Virgil followed the Iliad (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.72; see the translation's note on 23.71–74). The river is presumably the Styx, he says, though it may be Oceanus (on 23.73; see [[styx]]).

**Virgil.** In the sixth book of the *Aeneid* Aeneas and the Sibyl come down to the waters of Acheron, where Charon carries the dead across (*Aeneid* 6.295–304). The dead rush to the bank, as many as the leaves that fall in the woods at the first frost of autumn, and stand begging to be the first across, stretching out their hands in longing for the farther shore; the grim boatman takes now these, now those, and keeps the others far back from the bank (6.305–316; see [[leaves-of-the-dead]]). Aeneas asks what the crowd at the river means, and the Sibyl answers: “All this crowd that you see is helpless and unburied; that ferryman is Charon; those the wave carries are the buried. He may not carry them over the dreadful banks and the hoarse streams before their bones have come to rest. They wander for a hundred years and flit about these shores; then at last they are let in and come back to the pools they long for” (*Aeneid* 6.322–330). Homer's Patroclus is kept from the other souls “beyond the river”; Virgil's unburied are kept from the river itself, and for a set term.

**Palinurus.** Among those without burial Aeneas meets his helmsman Palinurus, who fell overboard on the voyage from Africa. He swam for three nights, reached Italy, and was killed on the shore by a savage people as he clung to the rocks; now the waves hold him and the winds toss him on the beach (6.337–362). He begs Aeneas to throw earth on him, or else, “give your right hand to a wretched man and take me with you across the waves, so that at least in death I may rest in a quiet place” (*da dextram misero et tecum me tolle per undas*, *Aeneid* 6.370–371). The ghost of Patroclus asks for Achilles' hand: “And give me your hand, in my grief” (23.75). The Sibyl refuses him, since an unburied man may not cross, but promises that the neighboring peoples, driven by portents from heaven, will lay his bones to rest, raise a tomb and bring it offerings, and that the place will keep the name of Palinurus forever (6.372–381). Elpenor too had asked for a mound on the shore, the mound of an unlucky man, for men to come to hear of (*Odyssey* 11.75–76).

**Charon's old age.** Virgil's Charon is old, “but a god's old age is fresh and green”, *cruda deo viridisque senectus* (*Aeneid* 6.304). Leaf explains with this verse Antilochus' word for Odysseus in the footrace, ὠμογέρων, “in green old age”, a metaphor from unripe fruit (on 23.791; see the translation's note on 23.791).
