In *Republic* 2 Socrates lays down the patterns (τύποι) that poets in the ideal city must follow when they speak of the gods. The first is that a god is good, and the cause only of good things. The second is that a god is simple and true in deed and word. He neither changes his own form nor deceives others, "by apparitions, or by words, or by sending signs, in waking or in dreams" (382e).

Then comes the verdict: "So, though we praise many things in Homer, we will not praise this: the sending of the dream by Zeus to Agamemnon" (383a). Nor will they praise Aeschylus, in a lost play where Thetis complains that Apollo sang at her wedding of her children's long and happy lives, and was himself the killer of her son.

Plato's objection is exact. Zeus sends the Dream to bring about Achaean deaths and so honor Achilles (2.3–4). He tells it to say that Troy can be taken now, and that the gods are no longer divided (2.11–15). The narrator marks the message as false: Agamemnon goes away believing "things that were not to be fulfilled", and is called νήπιος, "the fool" (2.36–38). In the Iliad a god may deceive a man to carry out his plan. In Plato's city no god may deceive at all.

Defenders of Homer answered the charge. One solution, reported by Aristotle, read a variant line of the message with a different accent, so that the false promise came from the Dream and not from Zeus: see [[aristotle-and-hippias]].
