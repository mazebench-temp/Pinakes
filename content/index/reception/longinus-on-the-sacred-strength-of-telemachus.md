**The phrase.** ἱερὴ ἲς Τηλεμάχοιο, “the sacred strength of Telemachus”, names the man by his strength, as βίη Ἡρακληείη, “the might of Heracles”, names Heracles (see [[might-of-heracles]] and [[hieros]]). The Odyssey uses it seven times, first when Telemachus speaks to his crew on the shore before the voyage (*Odyssey* 2.409, 16.476, 18.60, 18.405, 21.101, 21.130, 22.354).

**Longinus and Origen.** At the start of the *Timaeus*, Socrates has said that neither the poets nor the sophists could imitate in word and deed the citizens of his best city, and he turns to his three hearers: “There remains the kind of your condition” (καταλέλειπται δὴ τὸ τῆς ὑμετέρας ἕξεως γένος, *Timaeus* 19e). Proclus, commenting on the passage, reports two earlier readers. Longinus, “who did not disdain to study the diction”, judged that Plato altered his expression in the clause on the sophists from a desire for solemnity, that he distorted it from the natural in the next, and that the third phrase, “there remains the kind of your condition”, is altogether strange, “for it differs in nothing from ‘the might of Heracles’ and ‘the sacred strength of Telemachus’ and such things” (μηδὲν γὰρ ἀπεοικέναι τοῦ βίη Ἡρακληείη καὶ ἱερὴ ἲς Τηλεμάχοιο καὶ ὅσα τοιαῦτα). Origen accepted the manner of these phrases as fit for prose, since such periphrases suit the prose writer as they suit the poet (*On the Timaeus* I 68 Diehl). Proclus' Longinus is usually taken to be Cassius Longinus, the critic of the third century CE, to whom the treatise *On the Sublime* was long credited (see [[longinus]]).

**Proclus.** Proclus answers both. Plato, he says, always changes his manner of speech to suit his subjects, and where the matter is out of the ordinary he seeks an expression out of the ordinary too; but this phrase is not a periphrasis. It does not mean the same as “you”, as “the might of Heracles” means Heracles; it signifies the very kind of the best condition, from which alone these men could give the imitation of the best constitution what befits it (*On the Timaeus* I 68 Diehl; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]] and [[plato]]). For Longinus and Origen, Homer's phrase is a figure, a man named by his strength; Proclus will not let Plato's words be a figure of the same kind.
