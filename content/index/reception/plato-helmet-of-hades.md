In the second book of the *Republic*, Glaucon tells the story of a shepherd who found in a chasm a gold ring that made him invisible whenever he turned its bezel toward the inside of his hand. With it he seduced the king's wife, killed the king and took his kingdom. If a just man and an unjust man each had such a ring, Glaucon argues, neither would keep his hands off what belongs to others. Each could take what he liked from the market, go into houses and lie with whom he pleased, kill and set free whom he pleased, and do all the rest among men “as the equal of a god” (ἰσόθεον). No one is just willingly (*Republic* 2.359c–360d).

The rest of the *Republic* is Socrates' answer. Near its end, before he turns to the rewards of justice, he claims that they have found justice to be best for the soul in itself, and that the soul must do what is just “whether it has the ring of Gyges or not, and, besides such a ring, the helmet of Hades” (*Republic* 10.612b). In the next breath he recalls what his friends had asked him to grant for the sake of the argument: that the just man and the unjust might escape the notice “of both gods and men” (*Republic* 10.612c). The ring hides a man from men. The helmet adds the gods. In the Iliad, the only place in Homer where it appears, it hides a god from a god: Athena puts it on “so that mighty Ares should not see her” (5.845). Ares sees Diomedes coming at him, but not the goddess who holds the reins and drives the spear into his flank (5.846–859; see [[helmet-of-hades]]).

Later poets gave the helmet to Perseus, who wears it when he flies from the Gorgons with Medusa's head ([Hesiod], *Shield* 226–227), and it became a proverb. The Suda explains “the helmet of Hades” as said of those who hide themselves by some device, since Perseus used it to cut off the Gorgon's head (α 675). It also quotes Aristophanes, who turned the phrase into a joke on the thick, shaggy hair of the poet Hieronymus (α 676, quoting *Acharnians* 390).
