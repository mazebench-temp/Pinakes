summary: In the eighth book of the Aeneid Venus asks her husband Vulcan for arms for her son, as Thetis asked Hephaestus for Achilles, and names Thetis as her precedent. Vulcan's Cyclopes forge them under Aetna, and on the shield the fire-god sets not the world and its two nameless cities but the history of Rome, from the she-wolf to Augustus' victory at Actium and his triumph. Aeneas marvels at it without knowing what it shows, and lifts onto his shoulder “the fame and fates of his descendants”.

**Homer.** Thetis comes to the house of Hephaestus and begs him, at his knees, for a shield and a helmet, greaves and a corselet for her swift-fated son, since Patroclus lost the armor Achilles had (18.457–461). Hephaestus owes her his life (18.394–407), and agrees at once. He goes to his twenty bellows, which work at his bidding, casts bronze, tin, gold and silver into the fire, and makes the shield himself (18.468–482). It shows the earth, the sky and the sea, two cities, the work of the year and a dance, with Oceanus around the rim (18.483–608). At dawn Thetis lays the arms before Achilles, who delights in them as he looks at the intricate work (19.3–19; see [[the-making-of-the-arms]] and [[the-shield-of-achilles]]).

**The request.** In the eighth book of the *Aeneid*, on the night before the war with the Latins, Venus speaks to her husband Vulcan in their golden chamber. While the Greeks were destroying Troy, she says, she asked no arms of his art for the wretched Trojans, though she owed much to the sons of Priam; now that Aeneas has reached the land of the Rutulians at Jupiter's command, she comes as a suppliant, “a mother for her son”, and asks for arms. “The daughter of Nereus could move you with her tears, and so could the wife of Tithonus” (*te filia Nerei, / te potuit lacrimis Tithonia flectere coniunx*, *Aeneid* 8.370–386). The daughter of Nereus is Thetis, and the wife of Tithonus is the Dawn, whose son killed Antilochus (*Odyssey* 4.187–188; see [[thetis]], [[eos]] and [[tithonus]]). Vulcan, taken in her embrace, promises whatever his art can do with iron or molten electrum (*Aeneid* 8.387–406).

**The forge.** In the middle of the night he goes down to his workshop on an island that rises beside Sicily and Aeolian Lipara, where the caves under Aetna roar with the Cyclopes' anvils (*Aeneid* 8.407–422). Brontes, Steropes and Pyragmon are at work on a thunderbolt for Jupiter, a chariot for Mars and the aegis for Pallas, and he bids them put everything aside: “arms must be made for a fierce man” (*arma acri facienda viro*, *Aeneid* 8.423–443). They melt bronze, gold and steel, shape one huge shield to stand against all the weapons of the Latins, with seven circles bound one on another, work the bellows, dip the hissing bronze, and turn the mass with gripping tongs (*Aeneid* 8.444–453). Homer's Hephaestus works alone, with the bellows serving him of themselves, the anvil, the hammer and the tongs (18.468–477), and the shield he makes has five layers (18.481).

**The shield.** Venus brings the arms to Aeneas in a secluded valley and sets them under an oak, and he cannot have his fill of looking at each piece: the helmet with its crests spouting flame, the sword, the corselet of bronze, blood-red and huge, the greaves of electrum and gold, the spear, “and the indescribable texture of the shield” (*Aeneid* 8.608–625). On it the fire-god, “not unaware of the prophets nor ignorant of the age to come”, had made the story of Italy and the triumphs of Rome (*Aeneid* 8.626–629): the she-wolf with the twins, the Sabine women, Mettus torn apart, Porsenna, Cocles and Cloelia, Manlius on the Capitol and the Gauls stealing up to it, the Salii and the Luperci, and, far off, Catiline punished in Tartarus and Cato giving laws to the righteous (*Aeneid* 8.630–670). Between them runs a sea of gold, with silver dolphins circling, and in the middle is Actium, with Augustus on his high stern, Agrippa, and Antony with his Egyptian wife; the monstrous gods of Egypt fight against Neptune, Venus and Minerva, Mars, the Furies and Discord rage in the battle, and Apollo of Actium bends his bow (*Aeneid* 8.671–713). Last comes Caesar's triple triumph, with the conquered nations passing in procession (*Aeneid* 8.714–728). “Such things on the shield of Vulcan, his mother's gift, he marvels at, and, not knowing the events, rejoices in the image, lifting onto his shoulder the fame and fates of his descendants” (*famamque et fata nepotum*, *Aeneid* 8.729–731).

**Homer and Virgil.** Homer's shield holds the world, and its mortals have no names: the cities, the two men at law, the king and the dancers are nameless, and the only mortals named are Daedalus and Ariadne, in a comparison (18.590–592). Its scenes could happen in any city, in war and in peace. Virgil's has one city and its history, named scene by scene, and a maker who knows the future. Some things carry over. Homer's Strife, Tumult and Doom go through the battle in the city at war (18.535–538); Virgil's Discord goes “rejoicing in her torn cloak”, with Bellona and her bloody whip (*Aeneid* 8.700–703). Homer's Ares and Athena lead the men of the besieged city out to battle (18.516–519); at Actium Neptune, Venus and Minerva fight on Augustus' side (*Aeneid* 8.699). Homer's Oceanus runs around the rim (18.607–608); Virgil's sea, with its silver dolphins, runs among the scenes, as the harbor with silver dolphins does on the Hesiodic shield (*Shield* 207–212; see [[shield-of-heracles]]). Aeneas, who rejoices in the image, is Achilles taking delight in the god's gifts (19.18–19), and the poet adds what his hero does not know.
