**Scepsis.** Old Scepsis, Strabo says, lay above Cebren at the highest part of Ida, near Polichna. Its people were later moved sixty stades lower, to the Scepsis of his own day, by Scamandrius, the son of Hector, and Ascanius, the son of Aeneas, and the two families are said to have reigned there as kings for a long time (*Geography* 13.1.52; see [[astyanax]]). Demetrius of Scepsis, a native of the place, thinks that Scepsis was also the royal seat of Aeneas, since it lay between Aeneas' own land and Lyrnessus, to which Aeneas is said to have fled when Achilles pursued him. He quotes Achilles' taunt to Aeneas, from “Do you not remember when you were alone, and I drove you / away from the cattle, down the mountains of Ida on swift feet” to “I / sacked it” (20.188–192; *Geography* 13.1.53; see [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]] and [[lyrnessus]]). His text has κεῖθεν where ours has ἔνθεν, “from there” (20.191).

**The stories of Aeneas.** The stories commonly told of Aeneas, Strabo goes on, do not agree with this account of the founders of Scepsis. Aeneas, they say, survived the war because of his grudge against Priam (Strabo quotes 13.460–461), and with Anchises his father and Ascanius his son he gathered a people and sailed. Some say he settled near Macedonian Olympus; some that he founded Capyae near Mantinea in Arcadia, naming the town after Capys; some that he landed at Aegesta in Sicily with Elymus the Trojan, took Eryx and Lilybaeum, and gave the rivers near Aegesta the names Scamander and Simoeis; and that from there he came to Latium and stayed there by an oracle that bade him stay where he should eat up his table, which came about near Lavinium, when a great loaf was set out for want of a table and eaten up with the meat on it (*Geography* 13.1.53; see the *Iliad* 13 note, [[aeneas]] and [[capys]]).

**Homer's verdict.** “Homer, however, seems to agree with neither”, Strabo writes, nor with what is said of the founders of Scepsis: he shows Aeneas staying in Troy, succeeding to the rule and handing on the succession to his sons' sons, the race of the Priamids having been wiped out, and Strabo quotes Poseidon's words, “For already the son of Cronus has come to hate the lineage of Priam; / and now mighty Aeneas shall be lord over the Trojans, / and his children's children, who will be born hereafter” (20.306–308; *Geography* 13.1.53; see [[might-of-aeneas]] and [[the-rule-of-aeneas]]). On this reading not even the succession of Scamandrius would stand, and Homer disagrees far more with those who take Aeneas' wanderings as far as Italy and make him end his life there. Strabo's text reads ἤχθηρε, with the augment, where ours prints ἔχθηρε, “has come to hate” (see the translation's note on 20.306–308).

**Over all.** “Some write: the race of Aeneas shall rule over all (Αἰνείαο γένος πάντεσσιν ἀνάξει), and his children's children, meaning the Romans” (*Geography* 13.1.53). The verse so written puts “the race of Aeneas” for “mighty Aeneas” and “all” for “the Trojans”. Walter Leaf, who takes the reading πάντεσσιν for an invention to flatter the Romans, finds it significant of the honesty of the tradition that no trace of it appears in the manuscripts (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.307). Virgil's Apollo promises the house of Aeneas rule “over all shores” (see [[virgil-the-house-of-aeneas]]).

**The Hymn.** The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* makes the promise to Anchises before Aeneas is born: “you will have a dear son who will rule among the Trojans, and children's children born in unbroken line” (σοὶ δʼ ἔσται φίλος υἱός, ὃς ἐν Τρώεσσιν ἀνάξει / καὶ παῖδες παίδεσσι διαμπερὲς ἐκγεγάοντες, *Hymn to Aphrodite* 196–197; see [[aphrodite-and-anchises]]). The name, the goddess adds, will be Aeneas, because a dread grief (αἰνὸν ἄχος) seized her for falling into a mortal's bed (198–199).
