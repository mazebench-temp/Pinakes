**Athenaeus.** The first book of the *Deipnosophistae*, which survives only in an abridgment, collects the customs of Homer's heroes at table and at leisure. “And the suitors in his poem delighted themselves ‘with game pieces in front of the doors’, not having learned the game from the great Diodorus or Theodorus, nor from Leon of Mytilene, of old Athenian family, who was unbeaten at draughts, as Phaenias says. But Apion of Alexandria says that he heard from Cteson of Ithaca what the suitors' game was like” (*Deipnosophistae* 1.29 Kaibel). Apion's account follows, in reported speech. The suitors, a hundred and eight of them, set out pebbles facing one another, equal in number on each side, as many as they themselves were, so that there were fifty-four on each side. Between the two rows they left a little empty space, and in it they set one pebble, which they called Penelope, and made it their mark. They drew lots, and the winner of the lot took aim at her with another pebble. If he hit Penelope and drove her forward, he put his own pebble in the place from which she had been knocked, and then threw again, at Penelope in her new place. If he hit her without touching any of the others, he won, and had great hopes of marrying her. Eurymachus had won the most at this game, and was hopeful of the marriage. Athenaeus adds that the suitors' hands were so soft from luxury that they could not even string the bow, and that their attendants too were costly (1.29 Kaibel).

**The numbers.** Apion's hundred and eight is the poem's own count. Telemachus later tells his father how many they are: fifty-two young men from Dulichium, twenty-four from Same, twenty from Zacynthus and twelve from Ithaca itself (*Odyssey* 16.247–251), a hundred and eight in all. The bow is the bow of the contest in Book 21, which the first suitor to try it, Leiodes, cannot string before he has tired his hands, “unworn, soft” (χεῖρας … ἀτρίπτους ἁπαλάς, *Odyssey* 21.144–151). And Eurymachus is the suitor whom Penelope's father and brothers urge her to marry, because he surpasses all the others in gifts (*Odyssey* 15.16–18; see [[eurymachus]]).

**After Athenaeus.** Merry quotes Apion's account in his note on the game pieces (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 1.107, citing it as Athenaeus 1.29). Homer says nothing of how the game was played, and the account is late: Apion claims no source but what he heard from a man of Ithaca. See [[game-pieces]] and [[athenaeus]].
