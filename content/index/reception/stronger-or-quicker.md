**The line.** Achilles welcomes the envoys as the dearest of the Achaeans, seats them on chairs with purple rugs, and calls to Patroclus for a larger mixing bowl, stronger wine and a cup for each guest (9.197–204; see [[xenia]] and [[mixing-bowl]]). The Greeks drank their wine mixed with water, and ζωρός, used of wine, is the strong, less diluted kind. Homer has the word only here, in the comparative. Achilles gives his reason in the next line: “for these are the dearest of men, and they are under my roof”. The meal that follows, with Achilles carving and Patroclus serving the bread, is one of the fullest scenes of hospitality in the poem (9.205–221; see [[feast-type-scene]] and [[wine]]).

**Aristotle.** The ancient objection was to Achilles' manners: a host who orders stronger wine for his guests seems to treat them like drunkards. Chapter 25 of the *Poetics*, a handbook for answering such “problems”, meets it with the rare word (γλῶττα). Just after the mules who may be guards (1.50; see [[aristotle-mules]]) and Dolon, “ill-favored to look at” because his face was ugly (10.316), Aristotle writes: “and ‘mix it ζωρότερον’ means not undiluted, as for drunkards, but quicker (θᾶττον)” (1461a14–15). Achilles, on this reading, tells Patroclus to mix the wine quickly.

**Plutarch's dinner.** Plutarch gives a whole question of his *Table Talk* to the line. Some of the diners, he says, thought Achilles ridiculous for telling Patroclus to pour the wine less diluted (ἀκρατότερον) and then giving his reason. Niceratus the Macedonian insisted that ζωρόν means not “undiluted” but “hot”, from ζωτικόν, “living”, and ζέσις, “boiling”. Sosicles the poet recalled a verse of Empedocles in which things that were unmixed before became ζωρά, and argued that the word means “well-mixed” rather than “undiluted” (*Table Talk* 5.4).

**The meaning.** The lexicons kept the plain sense: the Suda glosses ζωρότερον as ἀκρατότερον, “less diluted” (ζ 164), and the translation renders “mix the wine stronger”. The guests are the men Achilles loves best, come at night from a defeated army, and he honors them with the bowl as well as the meal. The ancient answers show how closely the critics read a hero's behavior at table: “quicker”, “hot” and “well-mixed” were all proposed to spare Achilles a fault that the poem does not suggest.
