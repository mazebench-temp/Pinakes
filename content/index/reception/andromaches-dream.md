Homer's Andromache has no dream. She has seen enough: her father and her seven brothers killed by Achilles, and now the Trojans hard pressed and the Achaeans strong (6.386–389, 6.407–439). She asks Hector to stay on the tower, and he answers that shame will not let him (see [[aidos]]).

The Latin Middle Ages did not read Homer in Greek. They knew the war from a short Latin epitome of the Iliad and from prose histories that claimed to be the work of eyewitnesses, above all the *History of the Fall of Troy* ascribed to Dares the Phrygian, a work of late antiquity. There Andromache dreams that Hector must not go out to fight. He dismisses it as a woman's talk, and she sends to Priam, who keeps him back; but when Hector sees the Trojans in trouble, he arms and rushes out, and Achilles kills him. The scene passed into the great Troy romances and histories of the Middle Ages, and from them into English.

**Chaucer.** In the *Nun's Priest's Tale*, the cock Chauntecleer, arguing with his wife Pertelote that dreams are to be feared, cites among his examples “Andromacha, Ectores wyf”. On the night before Hector was to lose his life she dreamed that he would be killed if he went into battle that day:

> She warned hym, but it myghte nat availle;
> He wente for to fighte natheles,
> But he was slayn anon of Achilles.

The tale is too long to tell, Chauntecleer adds, and it is nearly day.

**Shakespeare.** In *Troilus and Cressida* (act 5, scene 3) Andromache begs Hector to unarm and not to fight that day, for she has dreamed all night of slaughter. Cassandra joins her, and then Priam: Hector's wife has dreamed, his mother has had visions, Cassandra foresees, and the day is ominous. Hector will not stay. “Mine honour keeps the weather of my fate,” he says: life is dear to every man, but honor is dearer. He goes out, and before the play ends Achilles' men kill him.

The dream is the medieval storytellers' own, but the answer is Homer's. In Book 6 Hector would feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women if he shrank from the war, since he has learned to fight among the foremost, “winning great fame for my father and for myself” (6.441–446; see [[kleos]]).
