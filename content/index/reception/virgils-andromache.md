On his way to Italy Aeneas lands in Epirus and hears an incredible report. Helenus, son of Priam, rules over Greek cities, having taken the wife and the scepter of Pyrrhus, the son of Achilles, and Andromache has passed once more to a husband of her own people (*Aeneid* 3.294–297). Outside the city, in a grove by a stream she calls the Simois, a “false Simois”, she is pouring the yearly offerings to Hector's ashes at an empty tomb of green turf, with two altars (3.301–305). When she sees Aeneas in Trojan armor she faints. Then she asks whether he is real, and “if the kindly light has left you, where is Hector?” (*Aeneid* 3.310–312). Aeneas answers with a question of his own: “Hector's Andromache, are you still the wife of Pyrrhus?” (*Aeneid* 3.319). So Hector imagined a stranger pointing at her in Argos: “This is the wife of Hector” (6.460).

Her story is the captive's life that Hector foresaw. She envies Priam's daughter Polyxena, killed at the tomb of Achilles, who was never drawn by lot or taken to a conqueror's bed (3.321–324). She herself bore the pride of Achilles' son, and a child in slavery, until Pyrrhus went after Hermione and handed her over, a slave to a slave, to Helenus (3.325–329). Helenus has built a small Troy, with a Pergamus, a dry stream named Xanthus and a Scaean gate (3.349–351).

She asks after Ascanius: do his father Aeneas and his uncle Hector rouse him to the courage of old? (3.339–343). When the Trojans leave, she gives the boy embroidered robes and a Phrygian cloak of her own weaving, “to be a memorial of my hands, and a witness to the long love of Andromache, the wife of Hector”. Then: “O only image left to me of my Astyanax: so he carried his eyes, so his hands, so his face; and now he would be growing up with you, of the same age” (3.486–491). In Book 6 Hector held his son and prayed that he might rule Ilios with might, and foresaw his wife weaving at another woman's bidding in Argos (6.456, 6.476–481). Virgil's Andromache gives the work of her hands to another woman's son, in whom she sees her own.

**Baudelaire.** “Le Cygne”, in the second edition of *Les Fleurs du mal* (1861), begins with Virgil's scene:

> Andromaque, je pense à vous! Ce petit fleuve,
> Pauvre et triste miroir où jadis resplendit
> L'immense majesté de vos douleurs de veuve,
> Ce Simoïs menteur qui par vos pleurs grandit,

The poet is crossing the new Carrousel in a Paris rebuilt around him, and the false river of Buthrotum comes to his mind. He returns to her in the second part, “Veuve d'Hector, hélas! et femme d'Hélénus!”, the first of the poem's exiles.
