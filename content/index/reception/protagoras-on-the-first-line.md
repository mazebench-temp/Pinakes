## The criticism

Protagoras of Abdera was among the first to study the correctness of words, ὀρθοέπεια (Plato, *Phaedrus* 267c; see [[protagoras]]). He divided speech into four kinds, prayer, question, answer and command (Diogenes Laertius 9.53–54), and by that division the poem's first words, an imperative addressed to a goddess, are an order where a prayer was due. Aristotle reports another of his corrections of Homer's language: he held that μῆνις, “wrath”, and πήληξ, “helmet”, are masculine, so that the feminine οὐλομένην in the second line of the Iliad would be a solecism (*Sophistical Refutations* 14, 173b17–22; see [[gender-of-nouns]] and [[menis]]).

## Aristotle's answer

The charge comes at the end of the chapter on thought, where Aristotle sets aside the “forms of utterance”, command, prayer, statement, threat, question and answer, as the business of the art of delivery and of its masters: knowing them or not knowing them brings no criticism against the art of poetry “that is worth taking seriously” (1456b8–15; see [[forms-of-utterance]] and [[delivery]]). Greek prayer is in any case full of imperatives: Chryses' prayer to Apollo begins “Hear me” and asks “fulfill for me this wish” (1.37–41; see [[invocation-of-the-muses]]).
