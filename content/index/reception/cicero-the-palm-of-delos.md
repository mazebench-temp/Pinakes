**Homer.** Odysseus, begging Nausicaa for help, says that he has never seen such a mortal: once at Delos, beside the altar of Apollo, he saw “a young sapling of a palm tree springing up”, on the journey “on which I was to have evil sorrows”, and he marveled at it as he marvels at her (*Odyssey* 6.160–169; see [[the-palm-on-delos]], [[delos]] and [[apollo]]). Homer names the palm tree only here.

**Cicero.** *On the Laws* opens at Arpinum, in talk between Atticus, Quintus and Cicero himself. Atticus recognizes a grove and an oak, often read of in Cicero's poem *Marius*: if that oak still stands, this must be it, for it is certainly old. It stands, says Quintus, and always will, for it was planted by genius, and no farmer's care can sow a stock so long-lived as a poet's verse (*On the Laws* 1.1, plain renderings throughout). As long as Latin letters speak, there will be an oak in this place called the Marian oak, and, as Scaevola said of the poem, “it will grow gray through countless ages”, “unless perhaps your Athens has been able to keep an everlasting olive on its citadel, or they show today the same tall and slender palm that Homer's Ulysses said he saw at Delos” (*nisi forte Athenae tuae sempiternam in arce oleam tenere potuerunt, aut quam Homericus Ulixes Deli se proceram et teneram palmam vidisse dixit, hodie monstrant eandem*); “and many other things in many places last longer in remembrance than they could have stood by nature” (*On the Laws* 1.2). So let this be the acorn-bearing oak from which “the tawny messenger of Jove” once flew, the eagle of the poem that rose from the trunk of a tree, wounded by a snake, killed it, and was taken by Marius for a sign of his glory and his return (*On Divination* 1.106, where Quintus quotes the verses); and when storm or age has destroyed it, there will still be an oak in this place that men call the Marian oak (*On the Laws* 1.2; see [[cicero]]). Cicero's eagle, which kills its snake, turns to good the omen of the Iliad, in which the bitten eagle lets its snake fall (see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]]).

**Poetry and history.** Atticus then asks Cicero himself whether his verses planted the oak or he had the story of Marius as he wrote it (*On the Laws* 1.3). Cicero answers with questions of his own: whether Romulus really appeared after his death to Proculus Julius near Atticus' house and told him that he was a god, called Quirinus, and whether the North Wind really carried off Orithyia at Athens, near Atticus' old house, as tradition says. One should not inquire too closely, he says, into what has been handed down to memory in this way, and those who demand the truth of his *Marius* as from a witness and not from a poet are unskilled; they would believe that Numa talked with Egeria and that an eagle set the cap on Tarquin's head (*On the Laws* 1.3–4). Quintus concludes that his brother thinks one set of laws must be kept in history and another in a poem; Cicero agrees, since in history everything is referred to the truth and in a poem most things to delight, “although in Herodotus, the father of history, and in Theopompus there are countless fables” (*quamquam et apud Herodotum patrem historiae et apud Theopompum sunt innumerabiles fabulae*, *On the Laws* 1.5; see [[herodotus]]).

**The palm still shown?** Quintus' “unless perhaps” (*nisi forte*) sets the everlasting olive and the very palm of Ulysses among things that cannot be, and his point is that remembrance outlasts nature: the palm that Homer's verse planted is still shown at Delos as the oak of Marius will be shown at Arpinum, whatever tree now stands there. Merry read the sentence otherwise, as Cicero's statement that the palm was still to be seen in his day, citing Pliny's *Natural History* for the same; he thought that a palm was always kept in the precinct at Delos, as the sacred olives were kept in the Academy at Athens, comparing Sophocles, *Oedipus at Colonus* 705 (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 6.162). The scholia, as Merry reports them, refer Odysseus' palm to the “first-born palm” of Leto's childbirth, and Merry cites Euripides' *Hecuba* (458) for the words: the captive Trojan women of the play ask whether they will be carried to the island “where the first-born palm and the laurel raised their sacred shoots for dear Leto, a delight of her divine birth-pangs” (*Hecuba* 455–461; see [[leto]] and [[euripides]]).
