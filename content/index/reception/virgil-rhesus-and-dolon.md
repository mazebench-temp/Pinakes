**The tents of Rhesus.** In Carthage Aeneas finds the war of Troy painted on the walls of Juno's temple and weeps at scene after scene. Next to the battle on the plain he sees this one: “Not far from here, weeping, he knows the tents of Rhesus with their snow-white canvas, which, betrayed in their first sleep, the son of Tydeus, bloody with much slaughter, was laying waste; and he turns the fiery horses away to the camp, before they could taste the pasture of Troy and drink the Xanthus” (*Aeneid* 1.469–473).

Virgil keeps Homer's outline and moves its colors. The whiteness that Homer gives the horses, “whiter than snow” (10.437), is in the tents, and the horses are *ardentes*, “fiery”. Servius, the Latin commentator of the late fourth century CE, took the word in both of Homer's senses: “it means both white and swift” (on 1.472). Only Diomedes is named. Servius thought that Virgil kept Ulysses out of the picture for Aeneas' sake, or else because in Homer Diomedes did the killing while Ulysses dragged the bodies away (on 1.471; 10.488–493). The “first sleep” he explained three ways: the first part of the night, or the deepest sleep, or Rhesus' first night at Troy, since he had only just arrived (on 1.470; compare “newly come”, 10.434).

The last clause points to a story that Homer does not tell, and Servius supplies it. Rhesus, king of Thrace, the son of Mars as some say, or of the Hebrus, or of the Strymon and the Muse Euterpe, came to help Troy; because the gates were already shut he pitched his tents on the shore; betrayed by the Trojan Dolon, who had been sent out as a spy, he was killed by Diomedes and Ulysses, who had come out to spy themselves; and they took away the horses on which the fate of Troy hung, for if the horses had grazed on Trojan pasture or drunk from the Xanthus, Troy could not have fallen (on 1.469). In Homer the horses matter for their beauty and their speed (10.436–437, 10.547). In the *Rhesus* it is the king himself who is dangerous: if he lives through the night, Athena says, neither Achilles nor Ajax will keep him from sacking the ships (*Rhesus* 600–604; see [[euripides-rhesus]]).

**Diomedes' horses.** At the banquet that ends the book, Dido keeps Aeneas talking, asking about Priam and Hector, now about the armor of the son of Dawn, “now what the horses of Diomedes were like, now how great Achilles” (*Aeneid* 1.750–752). Diomedes took two famous teams in the Iliad, the horses of Aeneas himself (5.319–327) and the horses of Rhesus. Servius will not have Dido ask Aeneas about the first, which would not suit, and takes her to mean the second. She asks, he says, whether they were as fierce as the man-eating mares of the Thracian king Diomedes, which Hercules carried off and from which they were said to descend, or else whether they were truly so divine as to be counted among the fates of Troy (on 1.752).

**Dolon's son.** In the last book Turnus, raging through the battle, comes on “Eumedes, the offspring of ancient Dolon, renowned in war, who bore his grandfather's name and his father's spirit and hands; his father once, to go as a spy to the Danaans' camp, dared to ask the chariot of the son of Peleus as his price, and the son of Tydeus paid him another price for such daring, and he no longer aspires to Achilles' horses” (*Aeneid* 12.346–352). Turnus brings him down with a javelin, plants a foot on his neck, kills him with his own sword, and mocks him: let the Trojan lie and measure out the land he came to win by war; “these are the rewards” of those who try Turnus with the sword (*Aeneid* 12.353–361). Virgil takes the names from Homer, where Eumedes is Dolon's father (10.314; see [[eumedes]]), and the price from Dolon's bargain with Hector (10.319–323; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]).

Servius tells Dolon's end with a difference. Dolon, he says, was forced by torture to reveal the Trojans' plans and the arrival of Rhesus with the fated horses, and Diomedes and Ulysses left him bound to a tree and killed him on their way back (on 12.347). In Homer Dolon tells everything unforced, and himself proposes that they bind him and leave him until they return, “or bind me with a pitiless bond and leave me here” (10.443); Diomedes kills him at once (10.446–457; see [[dolon]]).
