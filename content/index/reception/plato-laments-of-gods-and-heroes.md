**Plato.** In the third book of the *Republic*, after the verses on Hades (see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]), Socrates turns to “the wailings and the laments of famous men” (387d). A good man, he argues, will not think death a terrible thing for a good man who is his comrade, and so will not lament for him as though he had suffered something terrible. Such a man is the most sufficient to himself for living well and has least need of anyone else, so that the loss of a son or a brother or of money is least terrible to him, and he will bear it most gently (387d–e). The dirges of famous men must therefore be taken out of poetry and given to women, and not to serious women at that, and to the worse sort of men, so that the guardians may scorn to act like them (387e–388a). Homer and the other poets are asked not to show Achilles, the son of a goddess, lying now on his side, now on his back, now face down, then rising and wandering distraught along the shore of the sea (24.10–12), or pouring the sooty dust over his head with both hands (18.23–24), nor Priam, near to the gods in birth, rolling in the dung and calling on each man by name (22.414–415; *Republic* 388a–b). Still less should they make the gods lament, as Thetis laments for her son (18.54), “ah me, wretched me, ah me, unhappy mother of the best of men” (388b–c); and if gods at all, then at least not the greatest of the gods, imitated so unlike himself as to say of Hector (22.168–169), “Ah, I see with my eyes a man dear to me chased around the city, and my heart grieves” (388c), and of Sarpedon:

> αἲ αἲ ἐγών, ὅ τέ μοι Σαρπηδόνα φίλτατον ἀνδρῶν
> μοῖρʼ ὑπὸ Πατρόκλοιο Μενοιτιάδαο δαμῆναι.

“Alas, alas, that Sarpedon, dearest of men to me, is fated to be brought down by Patroclus, son of Menoetius” (388c–d). For if the young heard such things in earnest and did not laugh at them as unworthily said, hardly would any of them, being only a man, think such words or deeds beneath himself and rebuke himself for them; without shame or endurance he would sing many dirges and laments over small sufferings (388d).

**Plato's Homer.** Plato's text begins αἲ αἲ ἐγών, “alas, alas”, where Homer's Zeus says ὤ μοι ἐγών, “ah me” (16.433), and has Hector chased περὶ ἄστυ, around the city, where the Iliad has περὶ τεῖχος, around the wall (22.168). He quotes the first two verses of Zeus' speech and stops at the lament. In the Iliad the lament opens a question: Zeus' heart is divided whether to snatch Sarpedon alive out of the battle and set him down in Lycia, or let him fall (16.435–438). Hera answers that the other gods will not approve (see [[not-all-the-gods-approve]]), and turns Zeus' grief into her argument: “But if he is dear to you, and your heart grieves for him” (16.450), let him leave Sarpedon to die and send Death and Sleep to carry him home (16.450–457; see [[sleep]] and [[death]]). Her words are those that Zeus himself speaks of Hector, the other passage that Plato quotes (22.169). Zeus does not disobey, and sheds bloody raindrops on the earth, “honoring his dear son” (16.459–460; see [[zeus]] and [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]). Homer does not say that he wept.

**Proclus.** In his essays on the *Republic* Proclus gives a chapter to the question, “What are the reasons for which poetry ascribes laments to the heroes and to the gods, even to the best of the heroes and to the greatest of the gods?” He states the charge in full. In Plato, Socrates stayed unmoved by the tears of his friends, and Apollodorus, who wept without ceasing, earned his master's rebuke (*Phaedo* 117d–e); Homer hands down heroes who mourn their own without restraint, Priam rolling in the dung and Achilles lying now on his back, now on his face, and pouring dust on his head like a child. If such suffering suits men, who are mortal by nature, it does not suit the gods, for the divine is set far from pleasure and pain; Thetis should not weep, and above all the greatest of the gods should not lament Hector pursued and Sarpedon his son, saying “ah me” over both (I 122–123 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). Proclus gives the lament in Homer's words, ὤμοι ἐγών, not in Plato's.

His answer has two parts. The heroes live the life of action. Pleasures and pains have no place in philosophers, who purify themselves and strip away all that is mortal; but to men who turn about in war and act through the passions, pleasures and pains belong, and without strong desires there would be no vigor in their deeds. Priam and Achilles were neither philosophers nor guardians, and it is no wonder if they lament their own, for the loss of friends and children and the destruction of cities seem to warriors a great share of misery (I 124 Kroll). The gods weep in another way, long accepted by the makers of myths, who show through tears the providence of the gods for the mortal things that come to be and perish. “Your tears are the much-enduring race of men”, says a poet hymning the Sun, and the mysteries hand down sacred laments as a symbol of the providence that comes down to us from the higher powers. So Thetis and Zeus are said to lament those dearest to them when they are in the last extremity, not because they suffer as men do, but because a particular care and gift proceeds from them to particular things. When the order of the whole runs with that care, the god's saving work is unhindered; when it does not, and the particular thing, as a part of the world that has come to be, undergoes its proper destruction, the myths say that those who cared for it lament (I 124–125 Kroll). In the Iliad the case against saving Sarpedon is Hera's, and it rests on the order among the gods: if Zeus sends his son home alive, other gods will wish to save theirs (16.445–449).
