In the second book of the *Republic* Socrates lays down “outlines for speaking about the gods” that the poets of his city must follow. The first is that a god is good, and therefore the cause not of all things but only of good things; for evils some other cause must be found (379a–c). Homer fails the test at once. Socrates rejects the two jars of Zeus, one of good gifts and one of evil (Iliad 24.527–532), and a line that is not in our text, that “Zeus is the dispenser for us of good and of evil” (379d–e). The nearest Homeric line is the soldiers' comment in Book 4, “Zeus, who is the dispenser of war for men” (4.84), which is repeated in Book 19 (19.224).

Then comes Book 4 itself: “the confounding of the oaths and the truce, which Pandarus confounded, if anyone says that it came about through Athena and Zeus, we will not approve” (379e). Nor may the young hear the line of Aeschylus that “god plants a cause in mortals when he wishes to ruin a house utterly” (380a).

The Iliad tells the story as Plato says the poets must not. Hera asks Zeus to send Athena and “contrive that the Trojans, against the exultant Achaeans, be the first to do harm in defiance of the oaths” (4.66–67). Zeus sends her, and she persuades the archer (4.68–104). Yet the poem also holds Pandarus and the Trojans to account. The narrator calls him a fool (ἄφρων, 4.104). Agamemnon says that the Trojans “trampled on the trusty oaths” and that Zeus will punish them “in resentment at this deceit” (4.157, 4.168), and in Book 5 Athena guides the spear that kills Pandarus (5.290–296). Homer lets the god's plan and the man's choice stand side by side. Plato demands a choice between them.

Plato's word for the breach, σύγχυσις (“confounding”), comes from the verb that Homer uses: “the oaths have been confounded by the Trojans”, says Idomeneus (σύν γʼ ὅρκιʼ ἔχευαν, 4.269–270). The same noun is the traditional title of the first part of Book 4 (see [[titles-of-book-4]]).
