The poem survives on papyri from Oxyrhynchus, damaged at the start and broken off at the end. It is unusual among Sappho's poems. It tells a story, and its language is full of the words and forms of epic.

A herald comes running to Troy. He is Idaeus, the swift messenger, as Priam's herald is in the Iliad (see [[idaeus]]), and he brings news that means “undying fame” (κλέος ἄφθιτον) for the city and “the rest of Asia”. Hector and his companions are bringing a quick-glancing girl from holy Thebe and from Placia, the delicate Andromache, in ships over the salt sea, and with her many golden bracelets and purple robes, embroidered trinkets, countless silver cups and ivory. Hector's dear father leaps up, and the news runs through the city. The Trojans yoke mules to the carts, and the women and girls climb in; the daughters of Priam ride apart; the men harness horses to the chariots. Pipe and lyre sound with the clatter of castanets, the girls sing a holy song, and the echo reaches the sky. Bowls and cups stand everywhere, myrrh and cassia and frankincense are mingled, the older women raise the cry, and the men sing a high and lovely song, calling on Paean, the far-shooter, lord of the lyre. They sing of Hector and Andromache, like the gods.

The Iliad does not tell the wedding. It recalls it once, at the worst moment. When Andromache sees Hector dragged behind Achilles' chariot, she faints, and flings from her head the headdress “that golden Aphrodite gave her on the day when Hector of the gleaming helmet led her from the house of Eetion, when he had given countless bride-gifts” (22.468–472). Sappho's procession, with its gifts, its music and its wedding song, is like the wedding on the shield of Achilles, where brides are led from their chambers through the city by torchlight to the sound of pipes and lyres (18.491–496). Her Andromache arrives with riches, as the Iliad's epithet πολύδωρος, “richly dowered”, may imply (6.394; see [[richly-dowered]]), though in the Iliad it is Hector who gives the gifts. Her “undying fame” is the phrase that Achilles uses of the fame he will win by dying at Troy (9.413).

The poem ends, as far as it survives, with the hymn to the bride and groom. Its listeners knew the rest. The Iliad's Andromache tells Hector that Achilles has killed her father and her seven brothers, and Hector foresees her led away a slave (6.413–428, 6.454–463).
