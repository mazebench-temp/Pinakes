summary: Book 24 of the Iliad is traditionally called Ἕκτορος λύτρα, “the Ransom of Hector”. The noun is later Greek, the prose word for Homer's ἄποινα, but its verb runs through the book, and Priam comes “to ransom” his son in a form of it that Homer uses nowhere else. Aelian names “the Ransom” among the parts of the Iliad once sung on their own, and Aristotle, naming the eagle of the book, places it “in the going out of Priam”. The title covers the middle of the book and passes over the dragging with which it begins and the laments and the funeral with which the poem ends.

The traditional title of *Iliad* 24 is Ἕκτορος λύτρα, “the Ransom of Hector”, or more literally “the price of Hector's release”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter, this one is Ω, omega, the last letter for the last book (see [[title-of-book-5]]). Its story is told under [[the-ransom-of-hector]], the ransom as a word under [[apoina]] and [[boundless-ransom]], and the rite of the suppliant under [[supplication]].

**The words.** λύτρα is the noun of λύω, “loose, release”: the price paid for releasing a captive or a body. Homer does not have it. His word for a ransom is ἄποινα, and Priam brings ἀπερείσιʼ ἄποινα, “a boundless ransom” (24.502), as Chryses did in the first book (1.13). But the verb of the title runs through the book. Zeus will have Achilles “receive gifts from Priam and release Hector” (λύσῃ, 24.76), and Iris tells Priam to go “to ransom his dear son” (λύσασθαι, 24.118); the middle infinitive λύσασθαι, of the one who pays, occurs five times in this book and nowhere else in Homer (24.118, 24.146, 24.175, 24.195, 24.237). Achilles fears “delay in the ransoming of the body” (λύσιος, 24.655), the only use of the noun λύσις in the Iliad, and he tells Priam, “Your son is released to you, old man, as you asked” (λέλυται, 24.599). The first word of the book is the same verb in its plainest sense: λῦτο δʼ ἀγών, “The gathering broke up” (24.1). Later prose put λύτρα where Homer has ἄποινα. Plato's Socrates, retelling the opening of the poem without meter, says that Chryses came “bringing ransom for his daughter” (τῆς τε θυγατρὸς λύτρα φέρων, *Republic* 3.393d; see [[plato-chryses-diegesis]]), and the Suda glosses ἄποινα as λύτρα (α 3716).

**The older names.** Aelian, writing that the ancients sang the poems of Homer in separate parts, gives for the Iliad “the Battle at the Ships, and a Doloneia, and the Aristeia of Agamemnon, and the Catalogue of Ships, and the Patrocleia, and the Ransom, and the Games for Patroclus, and the Obliteration of the Oaths” (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[aelian]] and [[title-of-book-16]]). His Λύτρα is the title's noun without Hector's name; for the Odyssey he names a Νίπτρα, “the Washing”, in the same form. Aristotle, describing the kinds of eagle, names one the plangos, “also called duck-killer and morphnos, which Homer too mentions in the going out of Priam” (ἐν τῇ τοῦ Πριάμου ἐξόδῳ, *History of Animals* 9.32). This is the bird that Zeus sends when Priam prays before he sets out, “the dark hunter, which men also call the dusky eagle” (μόρφνον θηρητῆρʼ, 24.316), and then Priam “drove out from the doorway and the loud-resounding colonnade” (24.315–323; see [[eagle]]). Aristotle names the episode for the journey, as Plato named the chariot race of *Iliad* 23 (see [[title-of-book-23]]). The title was also given to tragedies. Aeschylus' play on the subject is known as the *Phrygians*, or the *Ransom of Hector* (see [[aeschylus-phrygians]]), and the Suda lists a Ἕκτορος λύτρα among the plays of the tragic poet Timesitheus (τ 613). Where the name was first given to the book is not known.

**What the title covers.** The ransom fills the middle of the book. Zeus decides that Achilles shall take gifts and release the body, and Thetis brings her son the message (24.64–142; see [[zeus]] and [[thetis]]); Iris brings it to Priam, and he chooses the ransom from his chests over Hecuba's protest: twelve robes, twelve cloaks, twelve rugs, twelve mantles and twelve tunics, ten talents of gold, two tripods, four cauldrons and the cup that the Thracians gave him (24.143–237). Hermes leads him through the camp (24.322–467; see [[hermes]]), and in the hut Priam clasps Achilles' knees and asks for his son (24.468–551; see [[priam]] and [[achilles]]). Achilles' men take the ransom from the wagon, leaving two mantles and a tunic to wrap the body, and Achilles asks Patroclus not to be angry, “since the ransom he gave me is not unseemly” (24.552–595; see [[plato-the-price-of-hectors-body]]). The meal and the truce follow (24.596–676), and Hermes, waking Priam before dawn, puts a price on the old man himself: “but for you, alive, three times as much ransom would be given” (24.686).

**What the title leaves out.** The book begins where *Iliad* 23 ends. “The gathering broke up” is the end of the games, and Walter Leaf notes that if “but Achilles” (24.3) once followed 23.257, as has been suggested, the first three lines belong to the games (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.1). Then come Achilles' sleepless nights and the dragging of Hector three times around the mound of Patroclus, until Apollo covers the body with the aegis (24.3–21; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]), and the quarrel of the gods over the body (24.22–76; see [[the-judgment-of-paris]]). At the end the title passes over Cassandra's cry from Pergamus, the laments of Andromache, Hecuba and Helen, and the funeral (24.697–804; see [[the-laments-for-hector]] and [[the-funeral-of-hector]]). The poem's last line names the funeral, not the ransom: “So they held the funeral of Hector, tamer of horses” (24.804; see [[the-last-line-of-the-iliad]]).

**The older readers.** The book had readers from its parts. Pindar gives Hieron a saying of the men of old, that the immortals deal out two woes for every good, and passes on to the happiness of Peleus and the early death of his only son, as Achilles does in consoling Priam (*Pythian* 3.80–103; see [[pindar-the-jars-of-zeus]]). Plato would not have the young hear of the jars (*Republic* 2.379d–e; see [[plato-broken-oaths]]), of Achilles lying now on his side, now on his back, now face down (3.388a; see [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]]), of a corpse given back for a price (3.390e–391a; see [[plato-the-price-of-hectors-body]]), or of the draggings around the tomb of Patroclus (3.391b; see [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]]); in the *Ion* the simile of the lead weight is a passage for a fisherman to judge (538c–d; see [[plato-the-fishermans-lead]]). Proclus answered him on the jars, the ransom and the dragging (*On the Republic* I 96–100, 143–145, 150–151 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). Aristotle quotes Apollo's line on the senseless earth for the end of anger, and Priam's words that Hector seemed the son not of a mortal but of a god for a virtue above the human (*Rhetoric* 2.3, 1380b28–30; *Nicomachean Ethics* 7.1, 1145a18–22; see [[the-senseless-earth]] and [[aristotle-heroic-virtue]]). The story of Niobe had its own readers, Aeschylus and Sophocles, Ovid, and Pausanias, who went up Sipylus to see her (see [[niobe-play]], [[ovid-niobe]] and [[the-weeping-rock-of-sipylus]]). The death of Astyanax that Andromache foresees was told in the *Little Iliad* and in Euripides' *Trojan Women* (see [[little-iliad]] and [[euripides-astyanax]]). Among the Romans, Cicero took the body that Achilles gave back for proof that the dead feel nothing (see [[cicero-the-body-of-hector]]); Virgil set the ransom among the pictures on the walls of Carthage and gave Priam, dying, Achilles' respect for the suppliant to throw in the face of Achilles' son (see [[virgils-hector]] and [[virgils-priam]]); and Horace sang of Mercury guiding rich Priam through the Greek camp (see [[horace-mercury-and-priam]]).
