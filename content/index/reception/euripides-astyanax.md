The *Trojan Women* (415 BCE) is set in the Greek camp after the sack, while the women of Troy are shared out among the victors. Andromache comes in on a Greek cart among the spoils, with Astyanax at her breast, and beside her the bronze arms of Hector, which the son of Achilles will hang in the temples of Phthia (568–576). She has been given to Neoptolemus. Her good name has ruined her, she says. She did all that is thought fitting in a wife: she stayed in the house, since a woman who goes out gets talked about, kept her tongue quiet and her eyes gentle before her husband, and knew when to prevail and when to give way. The report of it reached the Greek camp, and the son of Achilles wanted her for himself (643–660). The Iliad's Andromache ran to the tower, and Hector sent her home to her loom (6.386–389, 6.490–493).

Then the herald Talthybius comes, unwilling to speak. The Greeks have decided that the child is not to live. Odysseus prevailed in the assembly, saying that the son of the best of fathers must not be reared, and the boy is to be thrown from the towers of Troy (709–725). Andromache says goodbye: “Dearest, too highly honored child, you will die at the hands of enemies, leaving your wretched mother; your father's nobility will kill you, which brings safety to others” (740–743). Hector will not come, rising from the earth with his famous spear, to save him (752–753). In the Iliad she told Hector that his own might would destroy him (6.407). Here his greatness destroys his son. The Iliad had foreseen the death: in her lament over Hector, Andromache fears that some Achaean will take the boy by the arm and hurl him from the tower, in anger for a brother, a father or a son whom Hector killed (24.734–738; see [[astyanax]]).

The body comes back on Hector's shield. Neoptolemus has sailed, taking Andromache, and she has asked that the child be buried in his father's bronze-backed shield, “the terror of the Achaeans”, instead of a cedar coffin, and that the shield not go to Phthia to hang in the room where she must be a bride again. Talthybius has washed the body in the streams of the Scamander (1123–1155), the river for which Hector named his son (6.402). Hecuba has the shield set down, and asks the Greeks what they feared from a child (1156–1160). What would a poet write on his tomb? “The Argives once killed this child because they were afraid of him”, a shameful epitaph for Greece (1188–1191). Then she speaks to the shield. The mark of Hector's arm is on its grip, and on its rim the sweat that ran down from his forehead in the toil of battle (1194–1199).

In the Iliad the child shrank from his father's helmet, and his parents laughed, and Hector laid it aside to hold him (6.466–474). In Euripides the father's armor is the boy's coffin.
