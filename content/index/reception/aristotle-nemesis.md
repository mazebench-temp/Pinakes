**The verse.** Cebriones has turned Hector's chariot toward the part of the field where Telamonian Ajax is routing the Trojans (11.521–537; see [[cebriones]]). There Hector ranges through the ranks of other men with spear and sword and great stones, “but he avoided battle with Ajax, son of Telamon” (11.540–542). In the manuscripts the next verse is “And father Zeus, throned on high, roused Ajax to flight” (11.544), and the retreat of Ajax follows. Between the two, Monro and Allen print in square brackets a verse that no manuscript of the Iliad has: Ζεὺς γάρ οἱ νεμέσασχʼ, ὅτʼ ἀμείνονι φωτὶ μάχοιτο, “For Zeus was indignant with him whenever he fought a better man” (11.543). It is known only from Aristotle's quotation. Because the edition prints it, it has a number of its own, and the translation keeps the brackets (see the translation's note on 11.543).

**Aristotle.** The second book of the *Rhetoric* treats the emotions an orator must know how to rouse and allay. After pity comes what “they call νεμεσᾶν”, indignation, which Aristotle sets opposite to pity: pity is pain at undeserved misfortune, indignation pain at undeserved good fortune, and both belong to a good character. What happens beyond a man's desert is unjust, “for what comes contrary to desert is unjust; that is why we attribute indignation to the gods as well” (*Rhetoric* 2.9, 1386b13–15; see [[pity]]). It is not felt at justice or courage, but at wealth and power and the like, and more at new wealth than at old (*Rhetoric* 2.9, 1387a8–24). Each good, he goes on, suits a certain kind of man: fine arms suit the brave man, not the just, and distinguished marriages the well-born, not the newly rich. So it is a cause for indignation when a good man gets what does not suit him, and when the inferior contends with the superior, above all in the same field:

{{quote:8d9fe5f4-5180-5f5b-8b05-ee705609d454}}

He introduces the verses with no more than “whence this too has been said” (*Rhetoric* 2.9, 1387a33), and does not name Homer.

In Aristotle's reading Hector is the lesser man and Ajax the greater, and the field they share is war. Zeus feels the indignation that Aristotle, at the start of the chapter, said men ascribe to the gods. The verses are the only quotation of the Iliad in the chapter; for his account of anger, built on Achilles, see [[aristotle-anger]].

**The diction.** The verse is made of Homeric language. νεμέσασχʼ is νεμέσασκε, an iterative form of νεμεσάω (“be indignant with”, with the dative οἱ), elided before ὅτʼ, whose rough breathing turns its κ to χ; with the optative μάχοιτο it says what happened whenever Hector fought such a man (see [[nemesis]]). Gods are indignant elsewhere in the poem: Apollo when the Argives push forward (4.507), Hera at Hector's boast (8.198), Poseidon with Zeus himself (13.16). “To fight a better man” is Agamemnon's warning to Menelaus when he holds him back from the duel with Hector in Book 7: “and do not wish, out of strife, to fight a man better than you, / Hector, son of Priam” (μηδʼ ἔθελʼ ἐξ ἔριδος σεῦ ἀμείνονι φωτὶ μάχεσθαι, 7.111–112). There Hector is the better man; in Aristotle's verse it is Ajax. Ajax had the better of their own duel in Book 7, piercing Hector's shield and corselet and laying him on his back with a stone until Apollo set him on his feet (7.249–272), and in Book 17 Glaucus taunts Hector that he did not dare to stand against Ajax, “since he is better than you” (ἐπεὶ σέο φέρτερός ἐστι, 17.168).

**In the passage.** With the verse, Zeus is named in two successive lines, first as the reason Hector keeps away from Ajax, then as the power that drives Ajax back (11.543–544). Without it, the narrative passes from Hector's avoidance straight to Zeus' intervention, and the reason is left unsaid. How Aristotle came by the verse, his quotation does not tell. Monro and Allen give it the same treatment as the four verses of Phoenix that only Plutarch preserves (9.458–461; see [[phoenix-patricide-lines]]): printed, numbered and bracketed.
