It stages the night of Book 10 in Hector's camp, with Dolon disguised as a wolf, Rhesus the son of a Muse and the river Strymon, and Athena guiding Odysseus and Diomedes to kill him; its authorship was doubted in antiquity and still is.

**The play.** The *Rhesus* tells the night of Book 10 from the Trojan side. The chorus are Trojan sentries, who wake Hector because the Achaean camp is ablaze with fires (1–51). Aeneas persuades him to send a spy before attacking, and Dolon volunteers (87–223). Rhesus arrives with his Thracians, and Hector receives him coldly for coming so late in the war (264–453). Toward dawn Odysseus and Diomedes, who have already killed Dolon, come looking for Hector, and Athena turns them against Rhesus (565–626). His wounded charioteer brings the news of the slaughter (728–803), and at the end his mother, a Muse, carries in his body and laments him (890–982).

**What it takes from Book 10.** The frame is Homer's: a council in the night, a spy who goes out for the price of Achilles' horses (*Rhesus* 182; 10.322–323), a newly come king camped apart from the rest (518–520, 613–615; “apart, newly come, the farthest out of all”, 10.434), his gold and his white horses, “brighter than snow” (304), which shine in the dark “like the wing of a river swan” (616–618; see [[the-horses-of-rhesus]]). Athena guides the two Achaeans and warns them to leave before the enemy is on them (595–626, 668–674; 10.507–511). When Athena points out the horses, Odysseus offers Diomedes the choice that he offers in Homer: kill the Thracians, or leave the killing to him and see to the horses himself (622–626; 10.479–481). The Thracians sleep without a watch, worn out from the march (762–769; 10.471–475), and the king dies in his sleep.

**What it changes.** Much is new.

- *Dolon.* Hector offers him any reward but his own royal power. Dolon will not marry into Priam's house and has gold enough at home; he asks for Achilles' horses, which Hector says he loves himself, and Hector promises them without an oath: “I will not deceive you” (161–194). In Homer Hector swears by Zeus, and the narrator calls the oath false (10.328–332). Dolon will go out in a wolf's skin with the beast's jaws over his head, walking on all fours like a wolf among the ditches and the ships, “this is the trick I have contrived” (208–215; see [[dolon]] and [[wolves]]). His death happens offstage; the two Achaeans arrive with his spoils and the army's watchword learned from him (572–573, 591–593), and Hector fears too late that Odysseus has met him (863–865).
- *The raiders' aim.* Odysseus and Diomedes come to kill Hector, not to spy. They find his bed empty, and Athena tells them that Hector is to die by another hand (574–607). She then deceives Paris, who comes to warn Hector, by taking the shape of Aphrodite (627–667), and Odysseus slips through the sentries with the watchword (675–691).
- *Rhesus.* He is the son of the river Strymon and of a Muse, who conceived him when the Muses went to Mount Pangaeum for their contest with Thamyris (279, 346–354, 919–931; see [[thamyris]]). In Homer he is “the son of Eioneus” (10.435; see [[eioneus-father-of-rhesus]]). He comes late because of a war with the Scythians, and boasts that one day of his will end the war (426–453).
- *The dream.* Homer gives Rhesus an “evil dream” as he dies (10.496–497; see [[the-evil-dream]]). In the play the dream is his charioteer's: he saw wolves mounted on the horses' backs, lashing them with their tails, and woke to the gasping of the dying and his master's blood (780–791).
- *The aftermath.* The charioteer accuses Hector of killing Rhesus for the horses (833–855). Homer's Hippocoon, whom Apollo wakes, has no part (10.515–522). The Muse blames Athena, not Odysseus and Diomedes (938–940), and foretells that her son will not go down to Hades but will live on, a man-god (ἀνθρωποδαίμων), hidden in a cave of the silver-veined earth (962–973).

The play also remembers Odysseus' other exploits of stealth, the theft of Athena's image from her shrine by night and his entry into Troy disguised as a beggar (501–507, 710–719; compare *Odyssey* 4.242–264; see [[odysseus-and-diomedes]]). Its sentries keep “the fourfold watch of the night” (τετράμοιρον νυκτὸς φυλακήν, 5), where Homer's Odysseus reckons the night in three portions (10.251–253; see [[aristotle-portions-of-the-night]]).

**The authorship.** The play comes down among Euripides' works, but an ancient introduction to it, as modern editors report it, says that some thought it spurious and that it showed rather the manner of Sophocles, though the records of the Athenian performances list a *Rhesus* by Euripides; it also knew of two other prologues, one of them prosaic and perhaps made up by actors. The critic Crates is said to have excused what he took for an error of astronomy in the play by calling it a work of Euripides' youth (so Gilbert Murray, in the introduction to his translation, 1913). Modern scholars still divide. William Ritchie defended the play as Euripides' (*The Authenticity of the Rhesus of Euripides*, 1964); many others, among them the commentators Vayos Liapis (2012) and Almut Fries (2014), take it for the work of a later poet of the fourth century BCE. Line numbers here follow Murray's Oxford text.
