At nightfall the Trojans camp on the plain and bring cattle, sheep, wine and bread from the city (8.543–547). In the manuscripts the next verse says only that “the winds carried the savor from the plain up to heaven” (8.549), the savor of the meat they roast (κνίση; see [[sacrifice]]). Monro and Allen, whose text the Pinakes Greek follows, print four more verses around it in square brackets, in which the Trojans offer hecatombs and the gods refuse them. No manuscript of the Iliad has them.

**The source.** The verses come from the Second Alcibiades, a dialogue handed down among Plato's works but probably not his (see [[mist-from-the-eyes]]). Socrates tells a story he has heard. The Athenians, who kept losing to the Spartans though they offered more and finer sacrifices than any other Greeks, sent to ask Ammon why, and his prophet answered that Ammon would rather have the Spartans' reverent speech (εὐφημία) than all the offerings of the Greeks. The Spartans pray only for what is noble along with what is good; others pray for whatever comes, and the gods, hearing them blaspheme, do not accept their costly processions and sacrifices. You will find such things in Homer too, Socrates says, and he tells, partly in indirect speech and partly in verse, how the Trojans as they made camp (ἔπαυλιν ποιουμένους) offered perfect hecatombs, and the winds carried the sweet savor into heaven, but the blessed gods took no share of it, nor wished to, for sacred Ilios was very hateful to them, and Priam and his people. So their gifts did them no good. The gods are not to be led astray by gifts, like a base moneylender; they look to the soul, whether a man is holy and just (*Alcibiades II* 149c–150a).

**The verses.** Each of the four is made of Homeric phrases. The first is nearly Odysseus' memory of Aulis, where the Achaeans “were offering perfect hecatombs to the immortals” (ἕρδομεν ἀθανάτοισι τεληέσσας ἑκατόμβας, 2.306). The hatred of Ilios is the hatred of Hera, Poseidon and Athena in Book 24, “as sacred Ilios had first become hateful to them” (ὥς σφιν πρῶτον ἀπήχθετο Ἴλιος ἱρή, 24.27). The last verse is word for word the line in which Zeus names the city he loves best: of all cities under the sun, sacred Ilios was most honored in his heart, and Priam, and the people of Priam, “For my altar never lacked an equal share of the feast” (4.44–49; the verse also at 4.165 and 6.449). He says the same of Hector's offerings in Book 24 (24.66–70; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). The bracketed verses deny the gods their share of the Trojans' feast in the words Zeus uses to affirm it.

**Editors and translators.** Whether the author of the dialogue found the verses in a text of Homer, or made them from Homeric phrases for his argument, cannot be known. They serve that argument exactly. Monro and Allen print them in brackets, and the translation keeps the brackets. Pope translated them: his Iliad (1715–1720) has the hecatombs burning on the shore, an “Ungrateful offering to the immortal powers!” Tennyson's blank-verse specimen of the passage (1863) has the savor rolled into heaven but no hecatombs and no refusal, and he gave it the reference “Iliad 8. 542–561”: his numbering counts the book without the four verses, so that its last line, the 565th in Monro and Allen, is the 561st (see [[the-watch-fires-in-english]]).
