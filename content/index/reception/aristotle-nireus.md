Book 3 of the *Rhetoric* discusses style, and chapter 12 the style suited to speeches meant for delivery. There, Aristotle says, a string of words without connectives makes one action seem like many, as if much had been done. Homer uses repetition to the same effect. Nireus brings only three ships, the smallest contingent in the Catalogue, yet three successive lines begin with his name: "Nireus brought three balanced ships from Syme, Nireus, son of Aglaea and of lord Charopus, Nireus, the most handsome man who came to Ilios" (2.671–673).

Aristotle explains the trick. When much is said about a man, his name is necessarily repeated; so when his name is repeated, much seems to be said. By this fallacy Homer "magnified him, though he mentioned him only once, and made him memorable, though he never speaks of him again."

The observation is exact. Nireus does not reappear in the Iliad, and the entry ends by taking back what it gave: "but he was a weakling, and few people followed him" (2.675). The triple name is one of the best-known examples of anaphora in Greek poetry.
