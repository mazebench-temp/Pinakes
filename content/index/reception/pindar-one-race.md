At the height of his day Diomedes attacks Aeneas, though he knows that Apollo himself is holding his hands over him. Three times he springs at him, three times the god beats back his shield, and at the fourth Apollo shouts at him: “Take thought, son of Tydeus, and give way, and do not wish to think yourself equal to the gods, since never alike is the race of immortal gods and of men who walk on the earth” (5.440–442). The word for “race” is φῦλον, a kind or tribe of beings. The gods are one kind and men another, and the rest of the book shows the difference. The gods bleed ichor and cannot die; men walk on the earth, and the next book compares their generations to leaves (6.146–149). See [[gods-and-mortals]].

Pindar's sixth Nemean ode, for a boy wrestler from Aegina, opens with words that read like a reply: “One is the race of men, one of gods; from one mother we both draw breath. But power wholly divided keeps us apart: the one is nothing, while the bronze heaven stays a secure seat forever. And yet we are somehow like the immortals, in greatness of mind or in our nature, though we do not know, by day or by night, toward what goal fate has written that we must run” (*Nemean* 6.1–7).

The first words are disputed. They may mean that men and gods are a single race, or that there is one race of men and another of gods, both born of the same mother, Earth. Either way, Pindar begins where Apollo's warning ends. He grants the difference of power, and still claims a likeness of mind and nature. His “bronze heaven” is Homer's too: the dust of the battle in Book 5 rises “into the brazen sky” (5.504), and the Iliad calls heaven χάλκεος, “of bronze”, elsewhere (17.425).
