**Callisthenes.** Plutarch calls him a sophist. He was the son of Hero, a niece of Aristotle, and had been brought up in Aristotle's house because of the kinship (*Alexander* 55.4); he is not the Athenian general of Aristotle's *Rhetoric* (see [[callisthenes-the-general]]). At Alexander's court, Plutarch says, he annoyed the other sophists and the flatterers. The young sought him out for his eloquence, the older men liked his orderly, dignified and self-sufficient way of life, and he was believed when he said that he had gone up to Alexander in the hope of bringing his fellow citizens home and resettling his native city. Envied for his fame, he gave his slanderers grounds of his own: he refused most invitations, and in company his gravity and silence seemed to show that he did not approve of what was done (*Alexander* 53.1–2).

**The speech.** At a dinner, Plutarch goes on, Callisthenes was asked to praise the Macedonians over the cup, and spoke so fluently that the guests rose to applaud him and threw their garlands at him. Alexander said, after Euripides, that it was no great task to speak well on a good theme, and bade him show his power by accusing the Macedonians, so that they might learn their faults and be better. Callisthenes turned to the other side and spoke out freely against them, saying that the strife among the Greeks had made Philip great; the Macedonians conceived a bitter hatred for him, and Alexander said that Callisthenes had given proof not of his eloquence but of his ill will toward the Macedonians (*Alexander* 53.3–4).

**The verse.** Hermippus says, according to Plutarch, that Stroebus, Callisthenes' reader, told these things to Aristotle, and that Callisthenes, seeing that the king had turned against him, said two or three times to himself as he went away, κάτθανε καὶ Πάτροκλος, ὅπερ σέο πολλὸν ἀμείνων, “Patroclus died too, who was far better than you”. So Aristotle, Plutarch says, seems to have spoken well when he said that Callisthenes was able and great in speech but had no sense (*Alexander* 54.1). Plutarch goes on to praise him for refusing to do obeisance to Alexander, and for saying openly what the best and oldest of the Macedonians resented in secret: he spared the Greeks a great disgrace, and Alexander a greater, but he ruined himself, since he seemed to have forced the king rather than persuaded him (54.2). When the pages' plot under Hermolaus was discovered, his enemies charged him with having urged them on, though none of the pages named him even under torture. Some said that Alexander had him hanged, others that he fell sick and died in chains; Chares, Plutarch reports, said that he was kept in chains for seven months, to be tried before the council in Aristotle's presence, and died, grown very fat and lousy, at the time when Alexander was wounded in India (55.1–5).

**Achilles' verse.** In the Iliad the line is Achilles' answer to Lycaon, son of Priam, who has clasped his knees and begged for his life: “No, friend, you too must die; why do you lament like this? / Patroclus died too, who was far better than you” (21.106–107; see [[patroclus-died-too]] and [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). The killer says it to his victim, and goes on to his own death: “yet over me too hang death and mighty fate” (21.110). Callisthenes says it to himself, taking Lycaon's part: he too must die, as better men have. Plutarch's text of the verse is ours. Walter Leaf, who calls the line famous, reports the story as a warning to Alexander in the height of his glory (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.107); in Plutarch's Greek Callisthenes speaks the verse to himself (πρὸς αὑτόν), as he goes away. Lucretius too gives the thought to the one who must die, to say to himself (see [[lucretius-on-the-great-dead]]).
