Zeuxis of Heraclea was one of the most celebrated painters of the late fifth century BCE. Cicero tells the story at the opening of the second book of *On Invention*. The people of Croton, then rich and flourishing, hired Zeuxis to adorn their temple of Juno (Hera) with paintings, and he said he wished to paint an image of Helen, so that a silent picture might hold the utmost beauty of a woman. They showed him their handsome boys exercising in the palaestra and told him their sisters were as fine; he asked to see the most beautiful girls, and chose five of them. He did not think he could find everything he sought for beauty in a single body, "since nature has made nothing perfect in every part" (2.1–3). Cicero tells it to explain his own method: he had not followed a single teacher of rhetoric, but taken the best from many.

Pliny tells of the five models too, but makes the painting a commission of the people of Acragas, for a temple of Juno Lacinia (*Natural History* 35.64). Valerius Maximus, among examples of self-confidence, adds that when Zeuxis had painted Helen he did not wait to hear what people would think of it, but at once wrote beneath it the three lines of the Iliad in which the old men of Troy say there is no blame in suffering so long for such a woman, for she is terribly like the immortal goddesses to look on (3.7 ext. 3; Iliad 3.156–158).

The lines were a bold caption. Homer never describes Helen's beauty; he shows what it does to the old men who see her. The painter had to show the beauty itself, and by borrowing their verdict he claimed to have painted what they saw. On the principle, see [[lessing-helen]].
