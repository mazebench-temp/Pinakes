summary: In the Iliad Nestor has outlived two generations of men and is king among the third; in the Odyssey Telemachus says that he has been king for three generations. Later writers took up the number. Cicero's Cato lets him praise himself because he saw the third generation of men; Horace's old man, who lived three ages, did not weep for Antilochus all his years; and Ovid's Nestor, reckoning a generation as a hundred years, has lived two hundred and is living his third.

**Homer.** The Iliad introduces Nestor with his age: “In his lifetime two generations of mortal men / had already passed away, who had been born and raised with him long before / in sacred Pylos, and now he was king among the third” (1.250–252; see [[nestor]] and [[genee]]). In the Odyssey Telemachus gives the same reason for putting his question to Nestor, who “knows judgments and wisdom” beyond all others: “for they say he has been king for three generations of men” (*Odyssey* 3.244–245). The verb, ἀνάξασθαι, is the only middle of ἀνάσσω in Homer, and Merry suspects the line. If it is genuine, he takes γένεʼ of time, “has been king during three generations of men”, with τρίς standing for τρία; perhaps, he suggests, it was added to explain the words that follow, “he appears to me like an immortal to look upon”, which referred only to the old king's bearing but were taken to mean a life beyond the mortal span (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.245; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 3.244–245). In *Odyssey* 3 Nestor has outlived his son, who lies at Troy with Ajax, Achilles and Patroclus: “and there my own dear son, at once strong and noble, / Antilochus” (*Odyssey* 3.111–112; see [[antilochus]]).

**How long a generation.** A generation was reckoned in more ways than one, Merry observes: the Greeks made it something over thirty years, as Herodotus does, “for three generations of men are a hundred years” (Herodotus 2.142.2), and the Latins a hundred, as Ovid's Nestor shows (below). Nestor, he adds, was called in later times τριγέρων and *trisaeclisenex*, “thrice-old” (on 3.245).

**Cicero.** In *On Old Age* Cato, about to speak of his own strength, remarks that talking of oneself is the habit of old men and is allowed to their years, and points to Nestor: “Do you see how in Homer Nestor very often proclaims his own virtues? For he was seeing the third generation of men (*tertiam enim aetatem hominum videbat*), and he had no need to fear that in telling the truth about himself he would seem too arrogant or too talkative” (*On Old Age* 31). The sweetness of his speech, Cato goes on, needed no strength of body (see [[cicero-nestor-honey]] and [[cicero]]).

**Horace.** Consoling the poet Valgius, who mourns without end for the boy Mystes, Horace sets Nestor among the mourners who stopped: “But the old man who had lived out three ages did not weep for lovable Antilochus all his years, nor did his parents or his Phrygian sisters weep for ever for young Troilus” (*At non ter aevo functus amabilem / ploravit omnis Antilochum senex / annos*, *Odes* 2.9.13–17; see [[horace]]). Merry, who quotes the phrase, compares *ter aevo functus* with the τρίς of Telemachus' line (on 3.245). In Homer the old man names his son among the dead at Troy in a verse and a half and goes on with his tale (*Odyssey* 3.111–112).

**Ovid.** In the *Metamorphoses* Achilles, victor over Cycnus, sacrifices a cow to Pallas, and at the feast that follows the Greek chiefs talk through the night of battles and of Cycnus, whom no weapon could wound. Achilles asks Nestor, “eloquent old man, the wisdom of our age” (*o facunde senex, aevi prudentia nostri*), to tell them of Caeneus (*Metamorphoses* 12.150–181). The old man answers that slow old age is in his way and that much he saw in his first years has escaped him, but he remembers more: “if long old age could make anyone the witness of many deeds, I have lived two hundred years; now my third age is being lived” (*vixi / annos bis centum; nunc tertia vivitur aetas*, *Metamorphoses* 12.182–188; see [[ovid]], [[caeneus]] and [[nestor-and-the-centaurs]]).
