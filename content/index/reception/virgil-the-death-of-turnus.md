summary: The last scenes of the Aeneid retell the end of Hector. Jupiter weighs the fates of Aeneas and Turnus; Turnus runs five circles around the field, hunted like a stag by a hound, while the Trojans stand in a ring and the prize is his life; a goddess gives him back his sword; he is held as in a dream; and he dies begging for his body, killed for the belt he stripped from Pallas, as Hector died in the armor he stripped from Patroclus.

The *Aeneid* ends with a single combat. Aeneas and Turnus, the Rutulian prince who had hoped to marry Lavinia, meet at last between the armies, and Virgil builds the fight on the death of Hector, scene by scene, from the scales of Jupiter to the soul's flight.

**The scales.** As the two men clash, “Jupiter himself holds up two scales, their beam made level, and sets in them the different fates of the two” (*Aeneid* 12.725–727). Homer weighs two men only once, Achilles and Hector, when the chase comes for the fourth time to the springs (22.208–213; see [[the-weighing-of-souls]] and [[the-scales-of-zeus]]).

**The chase.** Turnus' sword breaks on the armor that Vulcan made for Aeneas; in his haste he had taken his charioteer's sword instead of his father's, and the mortal blade shatters like brittle ice (*Aeneid* 12.728–741). He runs. Now this way, now that, he weaves uncertain circles, for the Trojans have shut him in on every side with a dense ring, and a wide marsh and the high walls hem him in (12.742–745). Hector too runs between the plain and the wall, turned back from the gates each time he makes for them (22.194–198). Aeneas, lamed by an arrow wound, presses after him foot to foot, like a hound that has caught a stag penned by a river or by the fright of crimson feathers: the stag flees and doubles back a thousand ways, and the Umbrian hound hangs on him with gaping jaws, “now, now he holds him, and as if holding him snaps his jaws, and is mocked by an empty bite” (12.746–755). In Homer the dog that chases the fawn of a deer through the glens finds it at last, however it cowers, and Hector does not escape the swift-footed son of Peleus (22.189–193; see [[the-dog-and-the-fawn]]). Turnus calls on each of the Rutulians by name for his own sword, and Aeneas threatens death to anyone who comes near, and destruction to their city (12.758–762); Achilles shakes his head at his own men and will not let them throw (22.205–207). Aristotle's objection to the scene, that on the stage it would be ridiculous, “with the men standing still and not pursuing, and Achilles waving them back” (*Poetics* 1460a14–17; see [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]), could be made of Virgil's ring of Trojans too. Then Virgil all but translates Homer: “five circles they complete in their running, and unweave as many, this way and that; for it is no light prize, no prize of sport, that they seek, but they contend for the life and blood of Turnus” (*neque enim levia aut ludicra petuntur / praemia, sed Turni de vita et sanguine certant*, 12.763–765). Homer's runners strove for no beast for sacrifice or ox-hide, “but they ran for the life of Hector, tamer of horses”, three times around the city (22.159–165).

**The weapons.** Aeneas' spear has stuck in the root of a wild olive sacred to Faunus, and Turnus prays to Faunus and to the Earth to hold it fast. Turnus' sister, the nymph Juturna, in the likeness of his charioteer Metiscus, runs up and gives him back his sword, and Venus, indignant, tears the spear from the root (*Aeneid* 12.766–787). In Homer only one of the two is armed again by a god: Achilles' spear sticks fast in the ground, and Athena snatches it up and gives it back to him unseen by Hector, while Hector, his own spear thrown, calls for another from a brother who is not there (22.273–277, 22.291–299; see [[deiphobus]]).

**The gods withdraw.** Jupiter sends a Dira, who takes the shape of the small bird that sits at night on tombs and deserted roofs and beats at Turnus' face and shield; his limbs grow numb, and his voice sticks in his throat. Juturna knows the sign, laments that she cannot die with her brother, and hides herself in the deep river (*Aeneid* 12.843–886). When Aeneas taunts him, Turnus answers that Aeneas' words do not frighten him: “the gods frighten me, and Jupiter my enemy” (*di me terrent et Iuppiter hostis*, 12.894–895). After the scales Apollo leaves Hector (22.213), and when Deiphobus is not there Hector knows that “the gods have called me to my death” (22.297; see [[apollo]]).

**The dream.** Turnus heaves a boundary stone, and it falls short. Then the dream: “as in dreams, when at night languid rest has weighed down our eyes, we seem to wish in vain to stretch out eager running, and in the midst of our efforts we sink down helpless; the tongue has no power, the familiar strength fails the body, and no voice or words follow: so to Turnus, wherever he sought a way by his courage, the dread goddess denies success” (*Aeneid* 12.908–914). Homer's dream is of the chase itself, in which neither man can do what he strives for: “As in a dream a man cannot pursue one who flees; / the one cannot escape, nor the other pursue” (22.199–201). Virgil gives the dream to the fleeing man alone (see [[as-in-a-dream]]).

**The plea.** Aeneas' spear goes through Turnus' thigh, and he falls on his bent knee. A suppliant, he stretches out his right hand: he has deserved it, and does not beg off; but if any care for a father can touch Aeneas, who had a father, Anchises, let him pity the old age of Daunus, “and give me, or if you prefer, my body robbed of the light, back to my people” (*Aeneid* 12.919–938). Hector, dying, begs by Achilles' parents that his body be given back to be burned (22.338–343), and Priam means to remind Achilles of his father Peleus (22.418–422; see [[priam]]). Achilles calls Hector a dog and refuses (22.344–354). Aeneas stands rolling his eyes and checks his hand, and the words were beginning to bend him (12.938–941).

**The belt.** Then he sees on Turnus' shoulder the belt of the young Pallas, whom Turnus had killed and whose belt he wore as an enemy's trophy, and he burns with fury: “Are you, dressed in the spoils of my own, to be snatched from me? Pallas sacrifices you with this wound, Pallas” (*Aeneid* 12.941–949). Hector dies in the armor of Achilles, which he had stripped from Patroclus, and it leaves bare only his throat (22.322–325); the first words that Achilles speaks over him are of Patroclus (22.331–336; see [[patroclus]]).

**The end.** Aeneas buries the sword in Turnus' breast, “and his limbs go slack with cold, and his life with a groan flees indignant to the shades below” (*vitaque cum gemitu fugit indignata sub umbras*, *Aeneid* 12.950–952). Hector's soul flies from his limbs to Hades, “lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth” (22.361–363; see [[psyche]]). The *Aeneid* stops there. The Iliad goes on for two more books, to the funeral of Patroclus and the ransom of Hector's body.
