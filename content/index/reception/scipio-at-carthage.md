In the spring of 146 BCE a Roman army under Scipio Aemilianus took Carthage after a long siege, and the city was burned. The Greek historian [[Polybius]], Scipio's friend and teacher, stood beside him, and told the scene in his *Histories*. That part of his work survives only in fragments, and the fullest account is Appian's, who says that Polybius heard the words himself.

Scipio, looking at the city in the last throes of its destruction, is said to have wept for his enemies. He reflected that cities, peoples and empires must all fall, like men: so it had been with Ilion, once a prosperous city, with the empires of the Assyrians, the Medes and the Persians, and lately with Macedon. Then, “either deliberately, or because the verses escaped him”, he spoke these two lines. Polybius asked him what he meant, and Scipio named his own country without concealment: he feared for it when he thought of the fortunes of men (Appian, *Punica* 132). In a fragment of Polybius' own narrative Scipio takes his hand and says that the moment is a glorious one, but that he fears someone will one day give the same order about his own country (38.21).

The lines occur twice in the Iliad. Here Agamemnon speaks them over his wounded brother, sure that Zeus will punish the Trojans for breaking the oaths (4.158–168). In Book 6 Hector speaks them to Andromache about his own city, and says that he grieves less for Troy than for her, when she is led away a captive (6.447–465). Both speeches introduce them with the same line, “For I know this well in my mind and in my heart” (4.163, 6.447). Scipio was the conqueror, as Agamemnon hoped to be, but he spoke the lines as Hector did, of his own city.
