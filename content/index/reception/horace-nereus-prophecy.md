The ode begins at sea. The treacherous shepherd is carrying off Helen, his hostess, in ships of Ida, when Nereus stills the winds to sing him his fate: Greece will come with a great army, sworn to break his marriage and Priam's ancient kingdom (1.15.1–8). Then:

> In vain, fierce in the protection of Venus,
> you will comb your hair, and play on the unwarlike lyre
> songs that please women; in vain in your bedroom
> you will avoid the heavy spears …
> yet, alas, late, you will befoul your adulterous hair with dust.
> (1.15.13–20)

The stanza is built from Hector's rebuke in Iliad 3. Your lyre would be no help to you, Hector tells Paris, nor the gifts of Aphrodite, your hair and your looks, when you lay mingled with the dust (3.54–55). Horace keeps every item: the protection of Venus for Aphrodite's gifts, the hair, the lyre, the dust. The bedroom where Paris hides from the spears is the chamber where Aphrodite sets him down after the duel (3.382, 3.391). Nereus goes on to name the Greeks who will hunt him, Odysseus, Nestor, Teucer, Sthenelus, Meriones and Diomedes, and says that Paris will flee Diomedes like a deer that has seen a wolf, "not what you promised your lady" (1.15.21–32). Helen, in Book 3, reminds Paris that he used to boast that he was a better man than Menelaus (3.430–431).

Horace moves the rebuke to the beginning of the story, and turns it from reproach into prophecy.
