**Homer.** Zeus rouses Hector against the ships, for he wishes to give him glory and is waiting to see a ship burn. “He raged as when spear-brandishing Ares, or deadly fire, / rages in the mountains, in the thickets of the deep forest; / and foam formed around his mouth” (15.605–607), and his eyes blazed under his brows, and his helmet shook around his temples. Zeus was honoring him, the narrator adds, because “Short-lived he was to be” (15.612): Athena was already rousing his fated day under the strength of Achilles (see [[hector]], [[forest-fire]] and [[short-lived]]).

**Longinus.** The ninth chapter of *On the Sublime* finds the first source of sublimity in greatness of mind, and most of its examples are Homer's (see [[longinus]]). After Homer's gods and the opening of the lawgiver of the Jews, the author offers “one more example from the poet, from the human sphere”, to show how Homer is accustomed to step with his heroes into their greatness. A sudden mist and helpless night hold the battle of the Greeks, and Ajax, at a loss, prays to Zeus to rescue the sons of the Achaeans from the mist, to make the sky clear and let them see with their eyes, “and in the light destroy us” (17.645–647). That, says the author, is the very feeling of Ajax. He does not pray to live, which would be too humble a request for a hero; unable in the useless darkness to put his courage to any noble use, he asks for light as soon as may be, sure to find a burial worthy of his valor, even if Zeus should be ranged against him (*On the Sublime* 9.10). “But here Homer blows with a fair wind along with the battles, and he himself suffers nothing other than this: he ‘rages, as when spear-brandishing Ares or deadly fire rages in the mountains, in the thickets of the deep forest, and foam forms around his mouth’” (*On the Sublime* 9.11). Yet the Odyssey, he goes on, shows that it is the mark of a great nature in its decline to grow fond of storytelling in old age. The Iliad, written at the height of the poet's inspiration, is dramatic and full of conflict throughout, the Odyssey mostly narrative, and in the Odyssey one might liken Homer to the setting sun, whose greatness remains without its force (*On the Sublime* 9.11–13).

**The transfer.** In Homer the simile is Hector's, and it comes at the height of his day at the ships. Longinus gives it to the poet, with small changes that make it his: μαίνεται, “rages”, for μαίνετο δʼ, “he raged”, and γίγνεται, “forms”, for γίγνετο, so that the fury is the poet's and present, and ἐνί for ἐν. He stops at the foam on the lips, before the blazing eyes and the lines on Hector's short life. The poet who rages with the fighting is the poet of the Iliad, a poem that Longinus calls ἐναγώνιον, “full of conflict”; the Odyssey, for Longinus, is the work of the same mind grown old.
