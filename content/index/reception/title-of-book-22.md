summary: Book 22 is traditionally called Ἕκτορος ἀναίρεσις, “the Death of Hector”. The noun is later Greek; Homer uses its verb for taking up a spear or a prize, and the title of Book 7 uses the noun for the taking up of the dead. The older readers named other parts of the book, Aristotle the pursuit of Hector and Plato the draggings and the laments. The killing is told in little more than a hundred lines, and the title passes over the pleas at the gate, the chase, the dragging and the laments.

The traditional title of Book 22 is Ἕκτορος ἀναίρεσις, “the Death of Hector”, or more literally “the slaying of Hector”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter, this one is Χ, chi (see [[title-of-book-5]]). The killing is told under [[the-death-of-hector]], the chase that comes before it under [[the-pursuit-of-hector]], and the dragging that follows under [[the-dragging-of-hector]].

**The word.** ἀναίρεσις is the noun of ἀναιρέω, “take up”. Homer has the verb, but never of killing. Men take up the barley for the sacrifice (1.449, 2.410), Meriones takes up a bronze spear from the hut (13.296), the little girl of Achilles' simile begs her mother to pick her up (16.7–10), and at the games men take up their prizes (ἀέθλια … ἀνελέσθαι, 23.823; compare 23.736). The race of this book is run for no such prize, “but they ran for the life of Hector, tamer of horses” (22.159–161). In classical prose the noun names the taking up of the dead from the field, and it has that sense in the second title of Book 7, νεκρῶν ἀναίρεσις, “the taking up of the dead” (see [[title-of-book-7]]). Plato's Socrates uses it so when he would have his soldiers give up stripping the dead of anything but their arms, and hindering the taking up of the dead (τὰς τῶν ἀναιρέσεων διακωλύσεις): to think the body of the dead man an enemy, when the enemy has flown away and left behind that with which he fought, is the mark of a small mind, no different from dogs that are angry with the stones thrown at them and do not touch the thrower (*Republic* 5.469c–e). In this book Achilles refuses Hector's body to his parents, and the Achaeans stab it where it lies (22.344–354, 22.369–375).

Later prose uses the verb for killing. Aristotle speaks of killing the sons of those whose fathers one has killed (τὸ τοὺς υἱοὺς ἀναιρεῖν, *Rhetoric* 1376a5–7), and Strabo, paraphrasing the Catalogue, where Achilles “struck down” Mynes and Epistrophus (κὰδ … ἔβαλεν, 2.692), writes that he killed them (ἀνεῖλεν, *Geography* 13.1.61; see [[strabo-on-the-city-of-mynes]]). Proclus uses it of the death that Hector foretells in this book: Achilles “is said to be killed” (ἀναιρεῖσθαι λέγεται) by a power of Apollo's line (*On the Republic* I 148 Kroll, on 22.359–360). The title takes the noun in this later sense. The poem's words are δαμάζω, “bring down”, and the reduplicated aorist ἔπεφνον, “killed”. Achilles tells the Achaeans that “the gods have granted us to bring down this man” (22.379), and bids the young men go back to the ships singing the paean, “We have won great glory; we have killed brilliant Hector” (22.393); in the house Andromache does not yet know that “gray-eyed Athena had brought him down at the hands of Achilles” (22.446).

**The older names.** The episode had names before the book had its title, and they name other parts of it. Aristotle twice calls it “the pursuit of Hector” (ἡ τοῦ Ἕκτορος δίωξις). The scenes of the pursuit would seem ridiculous on the stage, “with the men standing still and not pursuing, and Achilles waving them back”, but in epic they go unnoticed; and the pursuit is his example of an impossibility that is correct if it makes that or another part of the poem more astonishing (*Poetics* 24, 1460a14–17; 25, 1460b23–26; see [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]). His ἀνανεύων, “waving them back”, is Homer's verb: “And brilliant Achilles shook his head at his men” (ἀνένευε, 22.205). Plato's Socrates, asking the rhapsode Ion whether he is in his right mind when he most astonishes his audience, names among the passages he sings “Achilles rushing at Hector” (Ἀχιλλέα ἐπὶ τὸν Ἕκτορα ὁρμῶντα) and “something of the pitiful things about Andromache or Hecuba or Priam”; Ion answers that when he speaks of something pitiful his eyes fill with tears (*Ion* 535b–c). In the *Republic* Socrates speaks of “the draggings of Hector around the tomb of Patroclus” (τὰς Ἕκτορος ἕλξεις), which are the draggings of Book 24 (24.14–18; *Republic* 3.391b; see [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]]). Where the name was first given to the book is not known (for the parts of the poem once sung under their own names, see [[title-of-book-16]]).

**What the title covers.** The killing is told in 22.248–366, after Athena has brought Hector to stand (22.226–247). Hector, deceived by Athena in the likeness of his brother Deiphobus, stands and offers an agreement, that the winner give back the loser's body; Achilles refuses, since there are no trusty oaths between lions and men (22.226–272; see [[deiphobus]] and [[athena]]). Achilles' spear misses, and Athena gives it back to him unseen by Hector; Hector's glances off the shield, and when he calls for another spear Deiphobus is nowhere near, and he knows that the gods have called him to his death (22.273–305). He charges with his sword, and Achilles drives the spear through his throat, the one place that the armor he had stripped from Patroclus left bare (22.306–329). The dying man begs that his body be given back for burning, and Achilles refuses any ransom; Hector foretells that Paris and Phoebus Apollo will destroy Achilles at the Scaean gates, and his soul goes to Hades, “lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth” (22.330–363; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). “Lie dead”, Achilles answers (22.365).

**What the title leaves out.** The rest of the book belongs to the chase and to the city. It begins with the Trojans fled into Ilios “like fawns” and with Apollo's mockery of Achilles (22.1–24). Priam and Hecuba beg Hector to come inside the wall (22.25–91), and he debates with himself whether to go in, or to offer Achilles Helen and the treasure of Troy (22.92–130; see [[pondering-two-ways]]). The chase takes the two men three times around the city, past the lookout, the fig tree and the springs of the Scamander, while the gods watch, until Zeus weighs their fates in his golden scales (22.131–213; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). After the killing the Achaeans stab the body, Achilles drags it behind his chariot to the ships, and Priam, Hecuba and Andromache lament (22.367–515; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]] and [[lament]]).

**The older readers.** Plato quotes the book four times in the third book of the *Republic*. He would strike out of poetry Priam rolling in the dung and Zeus' grief for Hector pursued around the city (*Republic* 3.388b–c, quoting 22.414–415 and 22.168–169; see [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]]), Achilles' threat against Apollo (*Republic* 3.391a, quoting 22.15 and 22.20; see [[plato-achilles-and-the-river]]), and the soul that flies to Hades lamenting its fate (*Republic* 3.386d, quoting 16.856–857 = 22.362–363; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]). His *Cratylus* finds Homer's reason for the name Astyanax in a verse close to Andromache's in this book (*Cratylus* 392d–e, on 22.506–507; see [[plato-astyanax]]). Proclus answered the charge about Apollo by distinguishing the god from the daemon who guarded Hector (*On the Republic* I 147–148 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). Aristotle took Hector's fear of Polydamas' reproach for the courage of citizens (*Nicomachean Ethics* 3.8, quoting 22.100; see [[aristotle-civic-courage]]), and Cicero applied the same speech to himself (*Letters to Atticus* 2.5.1, quoting 22.105 and 22.100; see [[cicero-trojans]]); in the *Tusculan Disputations* he took the dragging for proof that the dead feel nothing (*Tusculan Disputations* 1.105; see [[cicero-the-body-of-hector]]). Strabo, after Demetrius of Scepsis, argued from the chase that Homer's Ilium did not stand where the Ilium of his own day stood (*Geography* 13.1.37; see [[strabo-on-the-chase-around-troy]]). Euripides' Andromache saw Hector dragged around the walls (*Andromache* 107–108, 399–400; see [[euripides-andromache]]), and Virgil's Aeneas sees him in the pictures at Carthage, dragged three times around the walls of Ilium and sold for gold (*Aeneid* 1.483–484; see [[virgils-hector]]). The end of the *Aeneid*, where Aeneas chases Turnus and kills him, is built on this book (see [[virgil-the-death-of-turnus]]).
