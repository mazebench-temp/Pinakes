The poem brings in its Trojan spy in four lines: the son of a herald, rich in gold and bronze, “ill-favored to look at, but swift-footed”, the only son among five sisters (10.314–317; see [[dolon]]). The Greek of the third line is εἶδος μὲν ἔην κακός, ἀλλὰ ποδώκης, “bad in εἶδος, but swift of foot” (on εἶδος, “look, form”, see [[eidos]]).

Chapter 25 of the *Poetics* is a handbook for answering critics of Homer, and one group of answers looks to the poet's language. Right after the mules of 1.50, which may be guards (see [[aristotle-mules]]), Aristotle turns to this verse: “And Dolon, ‘who was bad in εἶδος’: not that his body was ill-proportioned, but that his face was ugly; for the Cretans call good-looking in the face (εὐπρόσωπον) εὐειδές” (1461a12–14). The answer treats εἶδος as a word with a sense known from a dialect, as he treats οὐρῆας, and a Cretan usage gives it: if εὐειδής, “of good εἶδος”, can mean “fair-faced”, then εἶδος κακός can mean “ugly in the face”. The objection is not stated. It was presumably that a misshapen man could not be the fast runner the same verse makes him.

Aristotle quotes the line with ὅς ῥʼ ἦ τοι, where our manuscripts have ὃς δή τοι; the difference does not touch his point.

In Homer εἶδος is usually the whole appearance. Hector calls Paris εἶδος ἄριστε, “best in looks” (3.39), and nothing limits that to his face. The Iliad's other ugly man is ugly in body: Thersites is bandy-legged, lame in one foot, his shoulders hunched over his chest, his head pointed (2.216–219; see [[thersites]]). Aristotle's reading gives Dolon the opposite fault, an ugly face on a body that can run, and it removes the contradiction the critics found. The translation's “ill-favored to look at” leaves the question open (see the translation's note on 10.316). Either way the line pairs a poor look with a swift foot, and ποδώκης is the epithet of the man whose horses Dolon will ask for (10.322–323; see [[swift-footed]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]).
