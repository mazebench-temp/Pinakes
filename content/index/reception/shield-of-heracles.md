summary: A short epic handed down under Hesiod's name tells how Heracles killed Cycnus, son of Ares, and describes at length the shield that Hephaestus made for him. Its shield has the plan of the shield of Achilles, with the powers of battle, a dance of the gods, a city at war and a city at peace, the work of the fields, and Oceanus around the rim. Four of its verses, on Strife, Tumult and Doom in the battle, stand almost word for word in Iliad 18.

**The poem.** The *Shield* (Ἀσπίς), a poem of 480 verses, is handed down among the works of Hesiod (see [[hesiod]]). It opens with “Or like her who” (ἢ οἵη), and tells first of Alcmene and the birth of Heracles and Iphicles (1–56). Then Heracles and his charioteer Iolaus meet Cycnus, son of Ares, and Ares himself in the precinct of Apollo (57–121). Heracles arms, with greaves that are the gifts of Hephaestus and a golden corselet from Athena (122–138), and takes up his shield (139–320). He kills Cycnus with a spear through the neck, and wounds Ares in the thigh, so that Rout and Terror carry the god back to Olympus (321–466). Ceyx buries Cycnus, and the river Anaurus washes away his tomb at Apollo's command, because Cycnus used to rob the hecatombs brought to Pytho (467–480; see [[heracles]]).

**The shield.** The description runs to some 180 verses, longer than Homer's 130 (18.478–608), and it is laid out in the same way. Rout (Φόβος) stands in the middle, with Strife hovering above his brow; the powers of battle are around him, then snakes, boars and lions, the Lapiths and the Centaurs, and Ares and Athena (144–200). The gods' holy dance follows, with Apollo playing the lyre and the Muses leading the song (201–206), then a harbor with dolphins and a fisherman, and Perseus fleeing the Gorgons (207–237). A walled city at war has women crying out on its towers and old men praying at its gates, while the Dooms fight over the fallen (237–270). Beside it is a city at peace with seven golden gates, where a bride is brought to her husband with torches, pipes and lyres and dancing (270–285). Before the city are horsemen, plowmen, reapers and the vintage, then boxers, wrestlers, hunters and a chariot race for a golden tripod (285–313). Oceanus flows around the rim, with swans and fish (314–317): “a wonder to see even for loud-thundering Zeus, by whose counsels Hephaestus made the shield, great and heavy” (318–319).

**The shared verses.** The Hesiodic battle has the same three powers as the city at war on the shield of Achilles, Strife, Tumult and deadly Doom, in the same four verses (18.535–538 = *Shield* 156–159). The *Shield* has ἐθύνεον, “rushed”, where the Iliad has ὁμίλεον, “were in the throng”, and adds a verse of its own: Doom glares terribly and roars with a clashing din (*Shield* 160). Walter Leaf reports that the Iliad's four verses have sometimes been thought copied from the *Shield*; he answers that they are not alien to epic thought, and compares Strife in Books 4 and 11 (4.440, 11.3; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.535; see [[strife]] and [[ker-doom]]).

**Other likenesses.** The wedding in the city at peace has the half-verse of the Iliad's, πολὺς δʼ ὑμέναιος ὀρώρει, “and the bridal song rose loud” (*Shield* 274; 18.493). The vintage has a golden vineyard, “the glorious work of wise Hephaestus”, propped on silver poles, ἀργυρέῃσι κάμαξι (*Shield* 296–300; κάμαξι … ἀργυρέῃσιν, 18.563); its grapes are white and black (*Shield* 294), where Homer's are black (18.562). The reapers stand in “deep grain” (βαθὺ λήιον, *Shield* 288); Leaf takes the line for an imitation of this passage, and for evidence of the reading βαθυλήϊον, “deep with grain”, where the printed text of 18.550 has βασιλήϊον, “a king's domain” (on 18.550). Oceanus flows around the rim of both shields: ἀμφὶ δʼ ἴτυν ῥέεν Ὠκεανός, “and around the rim flowed Oceanus” (*Shield* 314; 18.607–608; see [[oceanus]]). Hephaestus makes both shields “great and heavy”, σάκος μέγα τε στιβαρόν τε (*Shield* 319; 18.478, 18.609). And both poets say that the figures are like the living: Homer's soldiers fight “like living mortals” (18.539), and on the Hesiodic shield the Lapiths and Centaurs fight “as if they were alive”, and the women on the towers are “like living women, the works of glorious Hephaestus” (*Shield* 189, 244).

**The difference.** Homer's shield is made in the poem, at Thetis' request, and shown as Hephaestus makes it, scene by scene (see [[the-making-of-the-arms]]); the Hesiodic shield is already made, and is described as Heracles takes it up. The Hesiodic shield sounds in use: the teeth of its snakes clash whenever Heracles fights (164–165), and the shield rings loud as the Gorgons run across it (231–233). Homer's wonder is of another kind: the plowed land grows dark behind the plowmen, “though it was made of gold; that was a wonder beyond all” (18.548–549). In the fifth book of the *Deipnosophistae* Athenaeus cites the *Shield*'s Muses who “led the song” (205–206) to argue that on Achilles' shield it is the singer, not the tumblers, who leads the dance (5.180c–181c; see [[the-singer-on-the-shield]]).
