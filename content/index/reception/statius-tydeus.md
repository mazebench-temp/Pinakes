Agamemnon tells the story of Tydeus at Thebes to shame his son (4.370–400). Tydeus came to Mycenae with Polynices to raise an army against Thebes; Zeus turned the Mycenaeans back with ill-omened signs. On the march, at the river Asopus, the Achaeans sent Tydeus ahead on an embassy. He found the Cadmeians feasting in the house of Eteocles, challenged them to contests and won them all, with Athena's help. On his way back fifty young men lay in ambush for him, led by Maeon son of Haemon and Polyphontes son of Autophonus. He killed them all but Maeon, whom he sent home, “obeying the portents of the gods” (4.398).

The Roman poet [[Statius]] made the war of the Seven the subject of his *Thebaid*, an epic in twelve books, and told Agamemnon's story at length. In *Thebaid* 2, Tydeus goes to Thebes as the envoy of Polynices, to claim the throne that Eteocles was to give up to his brother after a year. Eteocles refuses, and Tydeus leaves with open threats. Eteocles sends fifty men to ambush him on his road. Tydeus kills them all except Maeon, whom he sends back to report to the king. In *Thebaid* 3, Maeon tells Eteocles what has happened, reproaches him to his face, and kills himself with his own sword.

The Iliad never tells how Tydeus died; Diomedes says that the heaped earth covers him at Thebes (14.114). And the poem keeps him in Athena's favor: “such a helper was Athena to him” (4.390). Statius tells a story that was older than his poem, and is also told by Apollodorus (3.6.8). In *Thebaid* 8, Tydeus is mortally wounded by the Theban Melanippus. He kills him, has his head brought to him, and gnaws it; Minerva, who was bringing him immortality from Jupiter, sees him and turns away. Dante remembered the scene: in the ice at the bottom of Hell, Count Ugolino gnaws the skull of Archbishop Ruggieri as Tydeus gnawed the temples of Melanippus (*Inferno* 32.130–132).
