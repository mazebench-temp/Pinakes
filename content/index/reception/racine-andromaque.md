Racine's first preface (1668) begins by quoting Virgil's lines on Aeneas' arrival at Buthrotum, where he found Andromache the wife of Helenus, and says that they hold “in a few lines the whole subject” of the tragedy (see [[virgils-andromache]]). The play takes place in Epirus at the court of Pyrrhus, the son of Achilles, who holds Andromaque and her son captive and loves her. He is promised to Hermione, who loves him. Oreste, who loves Hermione, comes as the envoy of the Greeks to demand the death of Hector's son. Pyrrhus offers Andromaque a choice: marry him and he will protect the boy against all Greece, or refuse and he will give him up. She resolves to marry him, bind him by oath to protect her son, and kill herself before she can be his wife. Hermione, jealous, drives Oreste to have Pyrrhus killed at the altar, then turns on Oreste and kills herself on Pyrrhus' body, and Oreste goes mad. Andromaque lives.

In the second preface (1676) Racine explains the change he made to Euripides. Most people who had heard of Andromache knew her only as the widow of Hector and the mother of Astyanax, and would not believe that she could love another husband or another son. So he gave her no son by Pyrrhus, and let Astyanax live longer than he did. The French, he adds, could hardly object: Ronsard had made Astyanax the hero of his *Franciade*, and the old chronicles saved the boy from the ruin of Troy to make him the founder of the French monarchy.

His Andromaque lives by her memories of Troy, and the dearest is the scene at the gates. Refusing to forget, she tells her confidante Céphise how Hector, on the day his courage sent him to seek Achilles, “or rather death”, asked for his son and took him in his arms, and said to her, wiping away her tears:

> J'ignore quel succès le sort garde à mes armes;
> Je te laisse mon fils pour gage de ma foi:
> S'il me perd, je prétends qu'il me retrouve en toi.

“I do not know what success fate holds for my arms; I leave you my son as the pledge of my faith: if he loses me, I mean him to find me again in you” (act 3, scene 8). Racine joins the farewell of Book 6 to the day of Hector's death in Book 22. In Homer, Hector prays for his son to the gods and gives him back to his wife, and pities her as she laughs through her tears (6.476–485).
