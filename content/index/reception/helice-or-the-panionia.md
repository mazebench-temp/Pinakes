summary: Hippodamas dies bellowing like a bull dragged “around the Heliconian lord”. Strabo reports that some took the simile for the Ionians' sacrifice to Heliconian Poseidon at the Panionia, in the territory of Priene, where the bull's bellowing was the good omen, and inferred that the poet lived after the Ionian migration; others referred it to Helice in Achaea, the old seat of the cult, which the sea swallowed two years before Leuctra. Herodotus names the Panionium on Mycale as sacred to Poseidon Heliconius.

**The simile.** Achilles stabs Hippodamas in the back as he leaps down from his chariot and flees, “And he gasped out his life and bellowed, as when a bull / bellows as he is dragged around the Heliconian lord / by young men dragging him; and the earth-shaker is glad of them” (20.403–405; see [[hippodamas]] and [[the-bull-of-the-heliconian-lord]]). The lord is Poseidon, the earth-shaker of the same sentence, and the bull is his victim. Walter Leaf takes ἀμφί literally, “dragged round” the altar of the god (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.404). Ἑλικώνιος, “Heliconian”, is found only here in Homer (see [[heliconian-poseidon]]). Helice itself is named twice: in the Catalogue, among Agamemnon's towns (2.575), and by Hera, who reminds Poseidon that the Danaans “bring you gifts to Helice and Aegae, / many and pleasing” (8.203–204; see [[helice]] and [[aegae]]).

**Strabo.** Strabo tells the story in his account of Achaea. The sea, raised by an earthquake, flooded Helice and the temple of Heliconian Poseidon, whom the Ionians still honor and to whom they sacrifice the Panionia. Homer, as some suppose, recalls this sacrifice in the verses on Hippodamas (20.403–404). From them they infer that the poet is later than the Ionian migration, since he recalls the Panionian sacrifice that the Ionians offer to Heliconian Poseidon in the territory of Priene; the Prienians themselves are said to come from Helice, and for this sacrifice they appoint a young man of Priene to oversee the rites. They take their proof still more from what is said of the bull, for the Ionians believe the sacrifice auspicious when the bull bellows as it is sacrificed. Those who argue against them transfer these proofs, the bull and the sacrifice, to Helice, as if the custom had been kept there and the poet were comparing what was done there (*Geography* 8.7.2; see [[strabo]] and [[ionians]]).

**Helice drowned.** The town was drowned two years before the battle of Leuctra. Eratosthenes, Strabo says, saw the place himself, and the ferrymen told him that a bronze Poseidon stood upright in the strait, holding a hippocamp in his hand, a danger to men fishing with nets. Heraclides said that the disaster happened in his own time, at night. The city lay twelve stades from the sea, and the whole district was covered together with it, so that two thousand men sent by the Achaeans could not recover the dead. It came, he said, from the wrath of Poseidon (κατὰ μῆνιν Ποσειδῶνος). The Ionians driven out of Helice had sent to ask its people for the image of Poseidon, or failing that for a copy of the shrine; they refused, and refused again when the Achaean league voted for the Ionians, and the disaster came the next winter. Afterwards the Achaeans gave the Ionians their copy (*Geography* 8.7.2).

**Herodotus.** When the Ionians lived in the Peloponnese, Herodotus says, they had twelve divisions, the twelve that the Achaeans who drove them out held in his day; among them is Helice, “to which the Ionians fled when they were beaten in battle by the Achaeans” (*Histories* 1.145). The Panionium, he writes, is a sacred place on Mycale, facing north, set apart in common by the Ionians for Poseidon Heliconius. Mycale is a headland of the mainland that runs out westward opposite Samos, and there the Ionians gathered from their cities used to hold a festival that they called the Panionia (1.148.1; see [[mycale]] and [[herodotus]]).

**The omen.** Walter Leaf quotes a note in the A scholia that bears out the Ionians' belief: when men sacrifice, if the oxen bellow, the god is thought to accept the sacrifice, and if they are silent, they are grieved, believing him angry. He derives Ἑλικώνιος from Helice, a seat of Poseidon's worship, and adds that the most famous cult of Heliconian Poseidon was the Panionian festival near Priene: if that is meant, the passage must be late (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.404). The *Homeric Hymn to Poseidon* calls the god the one “who holds Helicon and broad Aegae” (ὅς θʼ Ἑλικῶνα καὶ εὐρείας ἔχει Αἰγάς, *Homeric Hymn* 22.3), and Leaf infers from it that Helicon was another form of Helice, not the Boeotian mountain (on 20.404). The translation keeps the adjective, “the Heliconian lord” (see the translation's note on 20.404).
