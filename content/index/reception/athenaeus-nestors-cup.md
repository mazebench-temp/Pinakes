The eleventh book of Athenaeus' *Deipnosophistae* (*The Learned Banqueters*) is a catalogue of drinking cups in alphabetical order, and under N comes the Νεστορίς, “the Nestoris”. It opens with Homer's six verses on the cup (11.632–637) and then follows, at length and often in his own words, Asclepiades of Myrlea and his treatise *On the Nestoris* (*Deipnosophistae* 11.487f–494b; see [[the-cup-of-nestor]]). Asclepiades was a grammarian who taught in Turdetania, in southern Spain, and wrote on the peoples there (Strabo, *Geography* 3.4.3).

**The questions.** The verses, it says, raise several problems: what “studded with golden nails” means; why the cup has four handles, when other cups have two; how doves can feed around each handle; how a cup can have two bases; and, peculiar to this passage, why the others moved the cup with toil while “Nestor, the old man, lifted it without toil” (488a–b).

**Nails, handles and bases.** Some took the golden nails for studs driven into a silver cup from outside, as in inlay work, and compared Achilles' scepter, “studded with golden nails” (1.246), and Agamemnon's sword with its gleaming golden studs (11.29–30). The engraver Apelles, Asclepiades says, showed him on Corinthian work how such nails were set: slight bosses raised with the chisel, like nail heads. The four handles were explained either as two above and two small ones on the belly, as on Corinthian water jars, or, as Apelles showed, as two handles each split into two rods from a single root at the base and joined again at the rim. The two bases were a second foot added beneath the first, narrow at the top and broad below, as on the kantharos and other cups of his own day (488b–489a). Dionysius the Thracian, the grammarian, is said to have had a Nestoris made in Rhodes from silver his pupils gave him; as Promathidas of Heraclea described it, it was a cup with its handles side by side and doves on them, and two small rods laid lengthwise beneath it for the two bases. A cup of that kind, the text adds, could still be seen at Capua in Campania, dedicated to Artemis, which the people there said had been Nestor's: it was of silver, with Homer's verses inlaid in gold letters (489a–c).

**The Pleiades.** Asclepiades' own reading is cosmic. The ancients, believing the world to be a sphere, made tables, tripods and cups round in its image, and Nestor's cup, more than any other, bears the sky: the golden nails on the silver are stars, fixed like nails, gold for their fire against the silver of the heavens. The doves are not birds at all. πελειάδες, he argues, are the Pleiades, whose rising and setting mark the harvest and the plowing (he quotes Hesiod, *Works and Days* 383–384, and Aratus); the poet engraved the signs of the ripening crops on the cup of the wisest of men because a cup too receives nourishment, the other kind. He reads the Πέλειαι that bring ambrosia to Zeus in the Odyssey the same way (*Odyssey* 12.62–63), crediting the idea to the poet Moero of Byzantium, and gathers poets who call the Pleiades “doves” (489c–491d). Two doves, then, stand at each handle, four in all; and δύω δʼ ὑπὸ πυθμένες ἦσαν is to be read neither as “and below were two bases” nor, as Dionysius the Thracian divided it, as two words, but as one, ὑποπυθμένες, “two beneath the base”, so that two more Pleiades hold the cup up from below: six in all, as six are seen in the sky, though the Pleiades are called seven (491d–492d). And since the κυκεών, wine mixed with cheese and barley, was made in this cup, it took both kinds of food, the dry and the wet (492d–f, quoting 11.624–641; see [[hecamede]]).

**Nestor's strength.** On the lifting, the discussion first rejects a reading that confines the comparison to Machaon and Nestor: ἄλλος means any other man, and the cup was so large and heavy that anyone would have moved it only with difficulty, while Nestor, who liked his wine, lifted it easily from long habit (493a–c). Then it quotes Sosibius, called “the solver” (ὁ λυτικός). Critics blamed the poet for making the old man stronger than Diomedes and Ajax, and Achilles too, and Sosibius “acquits” him by transposition: take ὁ γέρων, “the old man”, out of the second verse and put it in the first, “another old man would move it with toil … but Nestor lifted it without toil”. So Nestor is the only old man who could lift it (493c–e). King Ptolemy Philadelphus, the story goes, repaid Sosibius in kind. He had the treasurers tell Sosibius that his stipend had already been paid, and when Sosibius complained, the king showed him the register: from the names Soter, Sosigenes, Bion and Apollonius, taking a syllable from each, Sosibius could find himself paid “by your own methods”, caught “not by others, but by your own feathers, as the wonderful Aeschylus says” (493e–494b; for the Aeschylean eagle see [[aeschylus-myrmidons]]).

**Homer.** Nothing in the Iliad points to the stars, and the doves feed (νεμέθοντο, “were feeding”) as birds do (11.634–635; see [[doves]]). The weight is Homer's own contrast between the old man's strength at the table and the weakness he admits a few lines later, “my strength / is not such as it was before in my supple limbs” (11.668–669; see [[the-tithonus-verse]]). Some of Asclepiades' questions are still open: how the doves and the two bases were set is debated (see the translation's note on 11.624–641).
