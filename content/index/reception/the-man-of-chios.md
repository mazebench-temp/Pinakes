Diomedes asks Glaucus who he is, and Glaucus answers first that birth matters little: the generations of men are like the leaves (see [[the-generations-of-leaves]] and [[genee]]). Then he tells his lineage anyway, at length. The first line became one of the most quoted in Greek.

**Mimnermus.** The elegist Mimnermus, writing in the later seventh century BCE, gave the leaves a new subject (fr. 2 West). “We, like the leaves that the flowery season of spring brings forth, when they grow quickly in the rays of the sun, like them for a span of time delight in the flowers of youth.” But beside us stand the black Keres, one holding the end of grievous old age, the other of death. His “season of spring” (ὥρη ἔαρος) is Glaucus' (ἔαρος … ὥρη, 6.148), and his leaves grow with flowers, as they do in a simile of Book 2 (2.468; see [[leaves-and-flowers]]). His two Keres are like the two fates that Achilles says he carries (διχθαδίας κῆρας, 9.411), but they offer no choice. Homer's leaves are the succession of the generations; Mimnermus' are the shortness of a single youth.

**Simonides.** An elegy quotes the line word for word and names its author, if only by his island:

> One thing the man of Chios said most beautifully:
> “As is the generation of leaves, so is that of men.”
> Few mortals who have heard it with their ears
> have laid it up in their hearts; for hope is with every man,
> hope that grows in the breasts of the young.

The poem goes on (fr. 20 West). While a man has the lovely flower of youth, his heart is light and he plans many things that will never be done; he does not expect to grow old or to die. Fools, not to know how short the time of youth and life is for mortals. Learn this, the poet says, and indulge your soul with good things to the end. The lines are preserved in the anthology of Stobaeus, who gives them to Simonides. Some older editors assigned them to Semonides of Amorgos. M. L. West printed them as Simonides fr. 19–20 in the second edition of his *Iambi et Elegi Graeci* (1992), and they are now usually cited so.

The man of Chios is Homer. In the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* the singer asks the girls of Delos, if anyone should ask them who is the sweetest of singers, to answer “a blind man, and he lives in rocky Chios” (169–173). Thucydides quotes the passage as Homer's own (3.104), and the Homeridae, the reciters of Homer, were said to be a family of Chios named after the poet (Suda ο 248). The couplet is one of the earliest surviving quotations of a Homeric line with its author named.

**Aristophanes.** In the parabasis of the *Birds* (414 BCE), the chorus addresses mankind in the grand style: “Come now, you men, dim-living by nature, like the generation of leaves (φύλλων γενεᾷ προσόμοιοι), feeble, figures molded of clay, shadowy tribes without strength, wingless creatures of a day” (*Birds* 685–687). The birds, who go on to claim that they are older than the gods, borrow Homer's comparison to show how small men are.

**Marcus Aurelius.** The emperor wrote in his notebook that a man bitten by true doctrines needs only the briefest and most commonplace saying to free him from grief and fear, such as “the wind scatters the leaves on the ground … so is the generation of men”. Your children too are little leaves, he goes on, and so are those who praise you and those who curse you, and those who will hand on your fame after you: all of them come on “in the season of spring”, then the wind throws them down, and the forest puts forth others in their place (*Meditations* 10.34). Glaucus used the lines to set aside a question about his birth; the Stoic uses them against the fear of death.

The same comparison appears in the Wisdom of Ben Sira: “As of the green leaves on a thick tree, some fall, and some grow; so is the generation of flesh and blood, one cometh to an end, and another is born” (Sirach 14.18). It need not come from Homer.
