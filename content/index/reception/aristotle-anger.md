Aristotle defines anger (ὀργή) as “a longing, accompanied by pain, for revenge [that is apparent], because of an apparent slight against oneself or ⟨something⟩ of one's own, when the slight is not warranted” (*Rhetoric* 2.2, 1378a30–33; see [[orge]]). The slight (ὀλιγωρία) has three kinds, contempt, spite and outrage (ὕβρις); dishonor belongs to outrage, and dishonor is his reason for Achilles' anger:

{{quote:89408144-fc7c-5f8d-ba4f-38ddfae4f82d}}

The first verse is Achilles' complaint to Thetis (1.356), the second his answer to Ajax in the embassy, “as if I were some dishonored vagrant” (9.648; see [[like-a-dishonored-vagrant]]). See [[oligoria]] and [[hybris]].

In the same chapter Aristotle quotes Iliad 18.109–110 on the pleasure of anger, “far sweeter than dripping honey” (1378b4–7; see [[sweeter-than-dripping-honey]]), and, for the anger of the great at those beneath them, Odysseus' “Great is the spirit of kings whom Zeus has nurtured” (2.196) beside Calchas' king who “keeps a grudge thereafter” (1.82):

{{quote:576c2174-b267-59ad-964d-5133c0c4b3a7}}

His theory of anger is, in large part, a reading of Achilles. See [[aristotle]], [[time]], [[menis]] and [[a-kings-grudge]].
