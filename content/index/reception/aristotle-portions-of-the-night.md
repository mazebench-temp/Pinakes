**The problem.** Diomedes has chosen Odysseus for the raid, and Odysseus cuts the praise short: it is time to go, for the stars have moved on, and the night has three portions (μοῖραι), of which two are gone (10.248–253; see [[moira]] and [[do-not-praise-me-too-much]]). The Odyssey marks the night's third part in the same way, by the stars: “when it was the third part of the night, and the stars had moved on” (ἦμος δὲ τρίχα νυκτὸς ἔην, μετὰ δʼ ἄστρα βεβήκει, *Odyssey* 12.312, 14.483). The difficulty is in πλέων, “more”. If more than two portions of the night have passed, the third cannot remain entire.

**Aristotle.** Among the answers that look to the poet's language, Aristotle lists ambiguity (ἀμφιβολία), and this verse is his example: “παρῴχηκεν δὲ πλέω νύξ: for πλέω is ambiguous” (1461a25–26). The comparative can mean “more than” something, and then the arithmetic fails; or it can mean “the greater part”, and then the next words say what that greater part is, two of the three portions. Read the second way, the verses say that the greater part of the night has gone, namely two portions of it, and that one portion remains. The translation takes them so (see the translation's note on 10.251–253).

Aristotle's quotation differs from Monro and Allen's text in two words, παρῴχηκεν for παροίχωκεν and πλέω for πλέων. According to Walter Leaf, παρῴχηκεν is also the reading of almost all the manuscripts of the Iliad, Aristarchus probably read παροίχωκεν, and the manuscripts of the *Poetics* are divided between πλέω and πλέον (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 10.252).

**Other ancient answers.** Leaf reports from the scholia that the Alexandrian editors, Zenodotus, Aristophanes and Aristarchus, rejected the verse that follows (10.253), presumably because they could make no sense of it, and that Porphyry gathered a long list of solutions. Aristotle, by Porphyry's account, kept the verse and explained it; Chrysippus took it to mean that more than two portions were gone and only the third was left to act in (Leaf on 10.252–253). The *Rhesus* divides the night otherwise: its chorus of Trojan sentries keeps “the fourfold watch of the night” (τετράμοιρον νυκτὸς φυλακήν, *Rhesus* 5; see [[euripides-rhesus]]).

**In the poem.** The exact count matters less than the urgency. The night is “far spent, and dawn is near” (10.251), and the raid fills its last part: the two set out, take Dolon, kill Rhesus and come back to bathe and eat before Day 26 dawns at the opening of Book 11 (see [[war-and-night]]).
