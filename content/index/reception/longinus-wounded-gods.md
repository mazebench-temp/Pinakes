After the leap of the gods' horses (9.5; see [[longinus-horses-of-the-gods]]), the author of *On the Sublime* turns to the battle of the gods in Books 20 and 21. The great heaven blares like a trumpet (21.388). Hades leaps from his throne and cries out in fear that Poseidon will break open the earth and lay bare the houses of the dead, which even the gods hate (20.61–65). The earth is torn open, Tartarus laid bare, the whole world overturned, and heaven and Hades, mortal and immortal, all fight together and share the danger (9.6).

Then he checks himself. These things are terrifying, he says, but unless they are taken as allegory they are altogether impious, and they do not keep what is fitting. Homer, it seems to him, by telling of the wounds of the gods, their factions, their revenges, tears, bonds and all their passions, has made the men at Troy gods, as far as he could, and the gods men. For us in our misery, death is laid up as a harbor from our ills; of the gods, Homer made eternal not their nature but their misfortune (9.7). Far better are the passages that show the divine as pure and truly great, like the advance of Poseidon, when the long mountains and the forest tremble under his immortal feet (9.8; the quotation runs half of 13.18 into a verse of Book 20, 20.60, and goes on with 13.19 and 13.27–29; see [[longinus-poseidons-advance]]).

His list could be a summary of Book 5. Diomedes wounds Aphrodite and then Ares, and both go up to Olympus in pain (5.335–369, 5.855–869). Dione comforts her daughter with a catalogue of gods who “have endured cruel pains at the hands of men, bringing them on one another” (5.383–384): Ares bound by the sons of Aloeus and shut in a bronze jar for thirteen months, Hera shot in the breast by Heracles, Hades shot in the shoulder (5.385–402). The gods take sides, and Ares complains that they are forever suffering by one another's will when they do favors for men (5.872–874). Zeus answers that of all the gods on Olympus Ares is the most hateful to him (5.889–890).

Ares' complaint states the critic's point about death in the god's own words. But for his swift feet, he says, he would have suffered long among the grim heaps of the dead, or lived on strengthless from the blows of the bronze (5.885–887). A man so wounded dies. A god can only go on suffering. The book gives the reason as well: the gods have ichor for blood, and they are bloodless and immortal because they eat no bread and drink no wine (5.339–342; see [[ichor]]).

Plato had banned from his city “all the battles of the gods that Homer composed”, whether they were composed with hidden meanings or without (*Republic* 2.378d; see [[plato-hephaestus-hurled]]). The allegorists answered that such stories were figures of nature and of the soul (see [[heraclitus-allegorist]]). The author of *On the Sublime* stands between them. He admires the battle of the gods as sublime, and calls it impious if it is read as it is written.
