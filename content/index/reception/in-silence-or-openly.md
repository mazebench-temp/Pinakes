The lot has fallen to Ajax, and while he arms he asks the Achaeans to pray to Zeus for him. The request takes two lines, and the line after the passage carries it out in the same words: εὔχεσθε Διὶ Κρονίωνι ἄνακτι, “pray to lord Zeus, son of Cronus”, and εὔχοντο Διὶ Κρονίωνι ἄνακτι, “they prayed to lord Zeus, son of Cronus” (7.194, 7.200). Between them stand five lines in which Ajax changes his mind and boasts. They should pray silently, so that the Trojans do not hear, or else aloud, since they fear no one; no one will drive him back by force or by skill, “for I do not think that I was born and bred in Salamis so unskilled as that” (7.195–199).

**The athetesis.** Ancient editors marked the five lines as spurious, as the scholia report. The change of mind and the boast were judged unfitting for the speaker. Without them the command and its fulfillment follow each other directly, a common pattern in Homer, and the prayer that follows is spoken aloud: “and this is what a man would say, looking up at the wide sky” (7.201; see [[prayer]] and [[tis-speech]]). The objection is of the kind brought against Andromache's advice to Hector (6.433–439): the lines were measured by what befits the speaker (see [[andromaches-advice]]).

**The case for the lines.** Monro and Allen print them, and the translation keeps them. Silent prayer is not foreign to Homer: in the footrace at the funeral games Odysseus prays to Athena “in his heart” (ὃν κατὰ θυμόν, 23.769). The change of mind suits a man who has just seen his own lot leap from the helmet and rejoiced (7.189–192). He begins with caution and ends with defiance. His boast of skill (ἰδρείῃ, 7.198) is taken up in the duel, when Hector answers that he is no feeble boy or woman and lists what he knows of fighting: to swing the oxhide shield to the right and to the left, to charge among the chariots, to dance for Ares in close combat (7.235–241; see [[hector]]). The word returns only once more in the Iliad, for Hector's “skill in war” as he shields himself from Ajax's spears at the ships (ἰδρείῃ πολέμοιο, 16.359). And Salamis, where Ajax was “born and bred” (7.198–199), is named elsewhere in Homer only in the Catalogue, as the home of his twelve ships (2.557; see [[ajax]] and [[salamis]]).
