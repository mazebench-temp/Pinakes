**Homer.** The truce of Book 7 is asked by the Trojans and granted by the Achaeans. Priam sends the herald Idaeus at dawn to ask both sides “to cease from grim-sounding war until we have burned the dead” (7.375–377, 7.394–396). The Achaeans reject Paris' offer of the possessions, but Agamemnon grants the truce with a maxim: “for one should not grudge the corpses of the dead, once they have died, the quick comfort of fire” (7.409–410; see [[agamemnon]] and [[idaeus]]). Both armies go out for the dead and for wood. They meet on the plain, wash the blood from bodies they can hardly tell apart, and burn them, the Trojans in silence at Priam's command (7.417–432; see [[the-truce-for-the-dead]] and [[cremation]]).

**Virgil.** After the battle in which Pallas and Mezentius have fallen, envoys come from the Latin city carrying olive branches. They ask Aeneas to give back the bodies that lie on the plain and to let them be buried: there is no quarrel with the defeated and the dead (*Aeneid* 11.100–105). Aeneas answers, “You ask me for peace for the lifeless, killed by the fortune of war? I would gladly grant it to the living too” (11.110–111). The war, he says, should have been settled by Turnus meeting him in single combat, and the one to whom a god or his own right hand gave life would have lived (11.115–118). A truce of twelve days is agreed, and Trojans and Latins wander the wooded hills together unharmed, felling ash, pine, oak and cedar and carting the timber off on groaning wagons (11.133–138).

The funerals follow Homer's pattern and add Roman rites (11.182–212). At dawn Aeneas and Tarchon build pyres on the curving shore; the Trojans ride three times around the burning pyres with cries of mourning, throw spoils taken from the Latins into the flames, and sacrifice. The Latins bury many of their dead, carry others off to their fields and to the city, and burn the rest, “a huge heap of confused slaughter”, without count or honor; on the third day they rake together the deep ash and the mingled bones and heap warm earth over them. In Homer the dead are hard to tell apart (7.424) and lie under a single mound, “one for all alike” (7.336–337, 7.435–436), though Nestor had spoken of carrying their bones home to their children (7.334–335; see [[bones-for-the-children]]).

**The difference.** Homer's truce follows a drawn duel and a day of heavy losses on both sides, and neither army admits defeat (see [[title-of-book-7]]). Virgil's Latins are the beaten side, and they ask for their dead as the defeated side did in classical Greek practice. Priam forbids his people to wail (7.427); Virgil's Trojans mourn aloud. And where Homer's kings refuse a peace in the same scene (7.400–407), Aeneas offers one.
