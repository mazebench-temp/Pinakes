The *Myrmidons* took its name from its chorus, Achilles' own men. The play is lost; what survives is a few quotations in later writers and part of a speech on a papyrus. Together they show a drama of Achilles' wrath, from his withdrawal to the death of Patroclus.

**Achilles apart.** One fragment, in the marching rhythm of anapaests, addresses him: “These things you see, glorious Achilles, the spear-ravaged toils of the Danaans”, whom he has left while he sits within his hut (fr. 131 Nauck; the words for leaving and sitting are editors' restorations). In Aristophanes' *Frogs* the chorus greets Aeschylus himself with the same words (992). Another asks, “Achilles of Phthia, why, hearing the man-slaying cry of toil, do you not come to help?” (fr. 132). Euripides recites it in the *Frogs* to prove that Aeschylus is a bad maker of songs who “always makes the same thing” (1249–1250, 1264–1265). Aristophanes also mocks Aeschylus for seating a veiled figure, “some Achilles or Niobe”, who does not show his face and says nothing (911–913); which play's Achilles he meant is not said.

**The refusal.** A papyrus preserves part of a speech by Achilles (PSI 1211), damaged at the beginnings of the lines, so that some of the words below are editors' restorations. The Achaeans threaten to stone him. “They will stone my body”, he answers, but stoned to death the son of Peleus will do the Danaans no good at Troy; the Trojans could sit idle and win without a spear. He speaks of “a bad shepherd”, presumably Agamemnon. If by staying out of the battle he alone has caused so great a rout, as the allies say, then he is everything to the Achaean army, and he is not ashamed to say it. The broken lines that follow speak of betrayal, of a man's death, of an accuser, and at the end of what seem to be “reconciliations” (διαλλαγαί) and “a soothing gift” (μείλιγμα).

**Homer.** In the Iliad the army never turns on Achilles. It suffers in his absence, as he foretold (1.240–244), Agamemnon admits that he is worth many people (9.116–117), and Nestor asks for “kindly gifts and soothing words” (μειλιχίοισι, 9.113), the root of the papyrus' “soothing gift”. The envoys find him singing to the lyre in his hut, with Patroclus silent beside him (9.185–191), and he refuses them for his own grievance against Agamemnon: “His gifts are hateful to me” (9.378). Aeschylus keeps the withdrawal and the refusal and turns the army against the man it needs; how his play brought the gifts and the envoys on stage, and who spoke to Achilles in these scenes, the fragments do not say.

**Patroclus.** Later fragments mourn Patroclus. In lines usually given to Achilles over the body, the speaker reproaches the dead man, who did not revere “the pure sanctity of thighs” and was ungrateful for many kisses (frr. 135–136). Achilles tells Antilochus, who in the Iliad brings him the news (18.1–21), to lament him, the living man, rather than the dead, “for all I have is gone” (fr. 138). He tells the Libyan fable of the eagle shot with an arrow feathered with its own feathers, “not by others, but by our own feathers are we taken” (fr. 139); a voice calls for arms (fr. 140). Plato's Phaedrus objects that Aeschylus talks nonsense in making Achilles the lover of Patroclus, when Achilles was the more beautiful and the younger, as Homer says (*Symposium* 180a).
