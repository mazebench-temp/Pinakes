The first man Turnus kills there is named Antiphates, like the first of Leonteus' victims at the gate, and Turnus, shut inside, fails to do what Hector does: break the bars and let his army in.

**Homer.** Asius alone of the Trojans will not leave his chariot, and he drives for the gate on the left of the ships, which men are holding open for companions fleeing from the plain (12.110–126; see [[asius]]). In front of the high gates stand two Lapiths, Polypoetes, son of Peirithous, and Leonteus, “like oaks in the mountains, with lofty crowns, / that withstand the wind and the rain all their days” (12.131–133; see [[polypoetes]], [[leonteus]] and [[oaks-on-the-mountains]]). When the Trojans rush the wall the two dart out and fight before the gates like wild boars (12.141–153). Polypoetes kills Damasus, Pylon and Ormenus; Leonteus spears Hippomachus at the belt, and then, drawing his sword, “first he darted through the throng at Antiphates”, and after him brings down Menon, Iamenus and Orestes (12.182–194). Asius' attack on the gate fails. At the end of the book Hector breaks a gate with a stone: the hinges snap, the bars give way, the doors split apart, and he leaps in and calls the Trojans after him (12.445–471; see [[the-gates-of-the-wall]]).

**Virgil.** The ninth book of the Aeneid is a siege of a walled camp, as in the Iliad, but the Trojans are inside it. Aeneas, leaving to seek allies, has ordered them not to fight in the open but to keep to the camp and its walls (*Aeneid* 9.40–43). Two brothers, Pandarus and Bitias, sons of Alcanor of Ida, young men as tall as the firs and mountains of their country, open the gate that their leader's command has entrusted to them, and invite the enemy in. They stand inside it, on the right and on the left, before the towers, armed, their crested heads held high, like twin oaks (*consurgunt geminae quercus*) that rise beside the Po or the Adige and lift their unshorn heads to the sky, nodding their lofty crowns (*Aeneid* 9.672–682). The Rutulians rush the open gate and are killed on its threshold or turn and flee, and the Trojans gather and venture out (*Aeneid* 9.683–690).

Turnus hears of it and makes for the gate. The first to fall, to his javelin, is Antiphates, the bastard son of Sarpedon by a Theban mother; then Merops, Erymas, Aphidnus, and Bitias, whom a siege spear brings down that neither two bull's hides nor a corselet of double scales and gold could stop (*Aeneid* 9.691–716). Pandarus sees his brother dead, swings the gate shut with his shoulders, and shuts many of his own men outside. But in his madness (*demens*) he does not see that he has shut the Rutulian king inside the camp, like a tiger among helpless flocks (*Aeneid* 9.722–730). Turnus kills him (*Aeneid* 9.731–755). Then, the narrator says, if the thought had come to the victor at once to break the bars with his hand and let his comrades in at the gates, that would have been the last day of the war and of the Trojan people; but rage drives him on against the enemy inside, until, beaten back from the walls, he leaps in full armor into the Tiber (*Aeneid* 9.756–818).

**The model.** The correspondences are close.

- *Two men at a gate, two great trees.* Homer's Lapiths stand before the high gates like oaks in the mountains, rooted against wind and rain; Virgil's brothers stand at the open gate like twin oaks with lofty crowns. In Homer the gate is held open for the Achaeans' own fugitives, and the two stand in front of it; in Virgil the brothers open their gate to provoke the enemy, and stand inside it.
- *The first victim.* Leonteus' first victim with the sword is Antiphates: Ἀντιφάτην μὲν πρῶτον, “first … Antiphates” (12.191). Turnus' first victim at Virgil's gate has the same name and the same “first”: *et primum Antiphaten* (*Aeneid* 9.696). Virgil makes him a son of Sarpedon, the leader of the allies in Iliad 12.
- *The narrator's verdict.* Homer calls Asius a fool (νήπιος) as he drives his chariot at the gate, and his men fools as they shout that the Achaeans will no longer hold (12.113, 12.127; see [[nepios]]). Virgil calls Pandarus *demens*, “mad”, as he shuts the gate on Turnus.
- *The gate broken.* Homer's attack ends when Hector breaks the bars and hinges of a gate and the Trojans pour in (12.453–471). Virgil's ends when Turnus, already inside, fails to break the bars and let his army in, and the war goes on.

The sides are reversed. The Trojans, who in Homer storm the Achaean wall, defend their own wall in Virgil, and their gate fails them as the Lapiths' gate does not fail the Achaeans.
