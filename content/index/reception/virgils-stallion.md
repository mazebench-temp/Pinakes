summary: Homer's simile of the stalled horse that breaks its halter and gallops to the river, used of Paris (6.506–511) and again of Hector (15.263–268), was translated by Ennius and reused by Virgil for Turnus running out to battle. Virgil sets it, as Homer does, just after the women of the city have prayed to the warrior goddess on the citadel.

**Homer.** When Paris has armed at last, he runs down through the city like a stalled horse that breaks his halter and gallops across the plain, a horse used to bathing in the river, head high, mane streaming over his shoulders, making for the pasture of the herd (see [[the-stallion]] and [[paris]]). The same six lines return in Book 15, when Apollo has breathed strength into the wounded Hector and he runs back to the fight (15.263–268). Homer gives the simile to both brothers.

**Ennius.** The first Latin epic poet translated it in his *Annals*: a horse, stuffed full at the stalls, breaks its halter in its high spirits and carries itself over the plain and its glad meadows, chest held high, tossing its mane. The context of the lines is lost. They survive because Macrobius, discussing Virgil's debts, sets Homer's Greek, Ennius' Latin and Virgil's lines side by side (*Saturnalia* 6.3).

**Virgil.** In the eleventh book of the *Aeneid* the Trojans march on the Latin city. Queen Amata goes up to the temple of Pallas on the citadel with a crowd of mothers bearing gifts, and with her Lavinia, “the cause of so much evil”, her eyes cast down. The women fill the temple with incense and pray from the threshold: “Mighty in arms, ruler of war, Tritonian maiden, break with your hand the spear of the Phrygian pirate, and lay him face down on the ground, and throw him down under our high gates” (*Aeneid* 11.477–485). It is Theano's prayer, point by point:

{{quote:6d83ccd3-0ec9-5127-93d6-ddbc7cd049d1}}

The spear is broken, and the man thrown down headlong before the gates. The “Phrygian pirate” is Aeneas, cast as a second Paris, the stranger who has come to take a bride. Virgil does not say how the goddess answered.

Then Turnus arms, and runs down from the high citadel, golden and gleaming, exulting in spirit, in hope already grasping the enemy (11.486–491), “as when a horse, breaking its bonds, flees the stalls, free at last, and, master of the open plain, makes for the pastures and the herds of mares, or, used to bathing in the familiar river, darts out and neighs, neck held high, exulting, and his mane plays over his neck and shoulders” (11.492–497). Paris came down “from the height of Pergamus, gleaming in his armor like the shining sun, laughing aloud” (6.512–514). Virgil keeps the order of Homer's book, the women's prayer on the citadel and then the warrior running down like a horse set loose, and adds the mares and the words “free at last” (*tandem liber*). The prayer that Homer gave to the Trojan women is spoken against the Trojans, and the simile that Homer gave to Trojans is given to their enemy.
