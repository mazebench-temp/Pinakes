In *Republic* 3 Socrates says that the guardians must not be lovers of laughter, since violent laughter brings violent change. "If anyone represents men of worth as overcome by laughter, we must not accept it, much less if gods." He quotes these two lines as the example to reject.

The objection belongs to Plato's wider attack on Homer's gods, who quarrel, deceive, lust and laugh like men.

**Proclus.** Proclus answers it in his essays on the *Republic*. The theologians, he says, make Hephaestus the maker of the whole visible world, lame in both feet because the world he makes has no need of feet. The care of the gods for the world of the senses, in which they share his making, is called their play, and the makers of myths call it laughter: when the poet says that the gods laughed unquenchable laughter at Hephaestus moving through the halls, he shows them as his fellow makers, who complete his art, and the laughter is unquenchable because their gifts never fail. The myths make the gods weep at times but laugh without end, since their tears are signs of their care for mortal things, which are and then are not, and their laughter of their work on the whole, which is always the same (*On the Republic* I 126–128 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). The same laughter rises at the trapped Ares and Aphrodite in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 8.326–327; see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite-in-later-writers]]).
