The traditional title of *Odyssey* 3 is Τὰ ἐν Πύλῳ, “the events at Pylos”; this library calls the book “At Pylos”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter of the alphabet, this one is γ, gamma (see [[title-of-book-5]]). Its story is told under [[the-journey-of-telemachus]], its host under [[nestor]], and the place under [[pylos]].

**Aelian's list.** The name is old. Aelian, writing that the ancients sang the poems of Homer divided into parts, gives as examples the names they used, first for the Iliad, from the Battle at the Ships to the Obliteration of the Oaths (see [[title-of-book-10]] and [[titles-of-book-4]]), and then “for the other”: “the events at Pylos (Τὰ ἐν Πύλῳ), and the events at Lacedaemon, and the cave of Calypso, and the events about the raft, and the tales to Alcinous, and the Cyclopeia, and the Nekyia, and the events with Circe, and the Washing, and the killing of the suitors, and the events in the country, and the events at Laertes' farm” (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[aelian]]). The events at Pylos come first. Aelian does not claim that his list is complete, but it has no name for the first two books, the council of the gods, Athena's visit to Telemachus and the assembly of the Ithacans (see [[odyssey]]).

**Named for a place.** Six of Aelian's twelve names for the Odyssey are of one form, τά, “the events”, with a place, a person or a thing: at Pylos, at Lacedaemon, about the raft, with Circe, in the country, at Laertes' farm. None of his names for the Iliad is of that form. Those name a battle, a man's lay (Δολώνεια, Πατρόκλεια), a hero's day of prowess, a catalogue, a ransom, games and the oaths, and the Odyssey's Κυκλώπεια and Νίπτρα are of the same kinds. Five of the Odyssey's twelve name a place, counting the cave of Calypso, and the first two follow Telemachus from Nestor's house to Menelaus', as Athena planned in the first book: “First go to Pylos and ask brilliant Nestor, / and from there to Sparta, to fair-haired Menelaus” (*Odyssey* 1.284–285; see [[athena-visits-telemachus]]).

**What the title covers.** The book is at Pylos from its first lines almost to its last. At sunrise Telemachus and the goddess, in the likeness of Mentor, “came to Pylos, the well-built citadel of Neleus” (*Odyssey* 3.4), where the people were sacrificing to Poseidon on the shore (see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]). Then come the welcome and the feast, Nestor's question to the strangers, his two long tales, Athena's departure in the likeness of a sea eagle, the night in his house, the sacrifice of the heifer to Athena the next morning, and Polycaste's bath (*Odyssey* 3.31–469; see [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]] and [[polycaste]]). Telemachus and Peisistratus then leave “the steep citadel of Pylos” (Πύλου αἰπὺ πτολίεθρον, *Odyssey* 3.485), and the last lines take them to the house of Diocles at Pherae and on across the plain toward Lacedaemon (*Odyssey* 3.486–497; see [[pherae-in-messenia]]). There Aelian's second part begins: “And they came to hollow Lacedaemon with its many ravines” (*Odyssey* 4.1).

**Told at Pylos.** Much of the book is speech, and Nestor's tales go far from Pylos: to the dead at Troy, to the quarrel of the sons of Atreus, to the voyages home by Tenedos, Lesbos and Geraestus, to Agamemnon's death at Mycenae and to Menelaus' wanderings from Sunium to Crete and Egypt (*Odyssey* 3.103–200, 3.254–312; see [[the-departure-of-the-greeks]], [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]], [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]] and [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). The title gives the setting of the tales, not their matter.

**Telemachus' own summary.** When he comes home, Telemachus tells his mother the events at Pylos in a few verses: “We went to Pylos and to Nestor, shepherd of the people”, who received him in his high house and cared for him like a father for a son come home after long absence, but had heard nothing of Odysseus, alive or dead, and sent him on to Menelaus with horses and a chariot (*Odyssey* 17.109–117, a plain rendering).

**Which Pylos.** The place that gives the book its name was disputed in antiquity. Three towns of the western Peloponnese claimed to be Nestor's Pylos, and Strabo argued, in part from this book, for the one in Triphylia (see [[strabo-on-nestors-pylos]] and [[pylos]]).
