{{quote:a07873ef-41d8-55e7-8356-abd8df4d97d6}}

In 44 BCE, after the murder of Caesar, Brutus resolved to leave Italy, and went down through Lucania to Elea on the coast. There his wife Porcia, the daughter of Cato, was to leave him and go back to Rome. She tried to hide her grief, Plutarch says, but a painting betrayed her. It showed a scene from Greek story: Andromache seeing Hector off, taking their little son from his arms, with her eyes on her husband. Porcia looked at it and wept, and came back to it many times a day.

Then Acilius, one of Brutus' friends, recited Andromache's words to Hector: “Hector, you are father to me, and lady mother, and brother, and you are my husband in his prime” (6.429–430). Brutus smiled and said that he, at least, had no mind to answer Porcia as Hector did, and tell her to see to “the loom and the distaff, and tell your handmaids” (6.491). Her body, he said, was not made for deeds like a man's, but in spirit she was as brave for her country as they were. Plutarch had the story from Porcia's son Bibulus (*Brutus* 23). He had already told how Porcia, to prove to Brutus that she could keep his secret, cut her own thigh with a knife and bore the wound in silence (*Brutus* 13).

{{quote:ade46910-70ac-5ff4-9dab-c671725d7477}}

The painting showed the moment when Hector, after his prayer, placed his son in his wife's arms, and she took him to her fragrant bosom, laughing through her tears (6.482–484). It is known only from this story. Acilius found Porcia's grief in Andromache's words. Brutus took the rest of the scene, Hector's answer, which gives war to the men and the loom to the women (see [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]]), and declined it for his wife.

The parting has been painted many times since. Giorgio de Chirico's *Hector and Andromache* (1917) shows the pair as two faceless mannequins in an embrace.
