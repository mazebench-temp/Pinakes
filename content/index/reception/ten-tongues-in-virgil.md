Before the Catalogue the poet asks the Muses for help. He will name the leaders and the ships, but not the rank and file. That he could not do "not even if I had ten tongues and ten mouths, a voice that could not break, and a heart of bronze within me" (2.489–490), unless the Muses reminded him. The image measures the task against the singer's body: even a superhuman voice would fail without the Muses' memory.

Virgil used the figure twice and multiplied it. In the *Georgics*, turning to Maecenas, he disclaims any wish to take in every kind of tree and vine in his verses, "not if I had a hundred tongues and a hundred mouths, a voice of iron" (2.42–44). In the *Aeneid* the Sibyl uses the same words to tell Aeneas that she could not list all the forms of crime and all the names of punishment in Tartarus (6.625–627). Homer's "voice that could not break" becomes a voice of iron (*ferrea vox*), and what exceeds the power of speech is no longer an army but the variety of nature, and then of sin.

By the next century the figure was a convention. Persius opens his fifth satire with a jibe at it: poets have this habit, to call for a hundred voices, and to wish for a hundred mouths and a hundred tongues for their songs (5.1–2).
