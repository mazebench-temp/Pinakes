Phoenix tells Achilles why he left home. At his mother's urging he lay with his father's concubine, and his father Amyntor cursed him, that he would never set on his knees a son born of Phoenix; and the gods, Zeus of the underworld and Persephone, fulfilled the curse (9.447–457; see [[amyntor]] and [[the-flight-of-phoenix]]). In the manuscripts the next verse is 9.462: “Then the heart in my breast could no longer at all be held to staying in the halls while my father was angry.” Monro and Allen, whose text the Pinakes Greek follows, print four more verses between the curse and the flight, in square brackets. In them Phoenix planned to kill his father, and one of the immortals checked his anger by putting in his heart the talk of the people, so that he would not be called a father-killer among the Achaeans. No manuscript of the Iliad has them.

**The source.** The verses come from Plutarch's essay *How the Young Man Should Study Poetry*, where he teaches the young reader to tell, within one character, what the poet shows as done well and what as done badly. Achilles does badly when he goes for his sword and well when he pushes it back into the sheath at Athena's word; Agamemnon is laughable in the assembly but kingly when he gives up Chryseis (26C–E, on Book 1). Then Phoenix, “cursed by his father because of the concubine”, says the four verses. “Now Aristarchus removed these verses out of fear (ὁ μὲν οὖν Ἀρίσταρχος ἐξεῖλε ταῦτα τὰ ἔπη φοβηθείς); but they are right for the occasion, since Phoenix is teaching Achilles what anger is, and what men dare through passion when they do not use reason or listen to those who would soothe them.” Homer also brings in Meleager, Plutarch goes on, angry with his fellow citizens and then softened, blaming the passions and praising the man who resists and masters them and changes his mind (26F–27A; see [[meleager]]).

**Aristarchus.** Plutarch does not say what Aristarchus feared. The plain offense is a son's plan to kill his father, told by the man whom Peleus sent to teach Achilles; the Alexandrian critics often judged a verse by what befitted its speaker (see [[andromaches-advice]]). Plutarch's verb, ἐξεῖλε, “took out”, says more than the usual reports of Aristarchus' athetesis, which marked a verse as spurious and left it in the text (see [[aristarchus]]). The four verses are not in the manuscripts, and how Plutarch knew them, from a copy of the poem or from a report of Aristarchus' work, is not known.

**The verses.** Their language is Homer's. “Then I planned” begins as Odysseus' plan to stab the sleeping Cyclops, before another thought held him back (τὸν μὲν ἐγὼ βούλευσα, *Odyssey* 9.299–302); “to kill with the sharp bronze” is the suitors' plan for Telemachus (*Odyssey* 4.700); the suitors fear “the talk of men and women” (φάτιν, *Odyssey* 21.323), and Helen imagines her brothers kept from the war by fear of “the shame and the many reproaches” that are hers (ὀνείδεα πόλλʼ, 3.242). Only πατροφόνος, “father-killer”, occurs nowhere else in Homer; the *Odyssey* calls Aegisthus πατροφονεύς, the killer of Orestes' father (*Odyssey* 1.299, 3.197, 3.307).

**In the speech.** Without the verses, Phoenix goes straight from the curse to his longing to leave, and his kinsmen guard him for nine nights before he escapes (9.462–477). With them, his story becomes the lesson Plutarch found in it. Anger at a father, like Achilles' anger at his king, comes to the edge of killing and is checked by a god, here not by Athena's hand on his hair (1.197) but by the thought of what men would say. The rest of his speech pleads for the same restraint: “So, Achilles, tame your great spirit” (9.496), and the story of Meleager shows what the refusal costs (see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). Monro and Allen print the verses in brackets, and the translation keeps the brackets.
