The traditional title of Book 8 is Κόλος μάχη, “the curtailed battle”, usually rendered “the interrupted battle”. The titles of Books 5, 6 and 7 are built on nouns of later Greek, ἀριστεία, ὁμιλία and μονομαχία (see [[title-of-book-5]], [[title-of-book-6]] and [[title-of-book-7]]). This one is made of Homer's own words. μάχη, “battle”, is everywhere in the poem; in this book the thunder of Zeus gives the Trojans “a sign of the victory that turns the battle” (μάχης ἑτεραλκέα νίκην, 8.171).

**κόλος.** The adjective means “docked, cut short”, and the Iliad has it once. In the fight at the ships Hector strikes Ajax's ash spear with his sword at the socket behind the point and shears the point away, and Telamonian Ajax is left brandishing a docked spear (κόλον δόρυ) while the bronze point rings on the ground far behind him. He knows the work of the gods: Zeus is cutting short his plans of battle (μάχης ἐπὶ μήδεα κεῖρε) and wills victory for the Trojans, and he draws back out of range (16.114–122). The verb of the same family, κολούω, “dock, curtail”, is Hector's word for Achilles' boasts: he will fulfill one word and cut the other short halfway (τὸ δὲ καὶ μεσσηγὺ κολούει, 20.369–370).

**The battle cut short.** The title fits the book. Its battle begins evenly: “As long as it was morning and the sacred day was growing, / so long the missiles of both sides struck home, and the people fell” (8.66–67). At noon Zeus weighs the fates of the two armies, and the Achaeans' day sinks (8.68–77; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). From then on every attempt to turn the day is stopped short. Zeus' thunderbolt turns back Diomedes and Nestor (8.130–171). The Achaeans rally at the sign of the eagle and Teucer's arrows, until Hector fells him with a stone and “again the Olympian roused might in the Trojans” (8.245–335). Iris turns back Hera and Athena at the gates of Olympus (8.397–437). Then the sun sets before Hector can reach the ships: “For the Trojans the light went down against their will, but to the Achaeans / welcome, thrice prayed for, came the murky night” (8.487–488). Hector tells his army that darkness came first and saved the Argives, “But now let us yield to black night” (8.497–502). The words recall the heralds who parted him from Ajax at the end of their duel, “it is good to yield to night” (ἀγαθὸν καὶ νυκτὶ πιθέσθαι, 7.282, 7.293; see [[yield-to-night]] and [[war-and-night]]). The fighting resumes only at the dawn of Book 11, after a night in which the Achaeans send an embassy to Achilles and spies into the Trojan camp.
