Hades is the third of Dione's examples of gods who have suffered at the hands of men. Heracles, “the same man” who shot Hera in the breast, shot him too, ἐν Πύλῳ ἐν νεκύεσσι (5.392–397). Monro and Allen print Πύλῳ with a capital, and the translation follows them: “in Pylos among the dead”. The capital is an editor's decision, since ancient texts did not distinguish capital letters from small ones. Read ἐν πύλῳ, the words mean “at the gate among the dead”, the gate of the underworld. Both readings are ancient, and each points to a different exploit of Heracles.

**Pylos.** The Iliad knows of Heracles' war on Pylos. Nestor remembers how “the might of Heracles” came in years gone by and crushed the Pylians, killing all their best men; of the twelve sons of Neleus, Nestor alone survived (11.689–693). The two other times the Iliad has ἐν Πύλῳ, it means the town: the men of Nestor's generation were born and raised with him “in sacred Pylos” (1.252), and there the Pylians were crushed (11.689). On this reading, “among the dead” means among the bodies of the fallen. Ares speaks of the battlefield in the same way, when he tells Zeus that but for his swift feet he would have suffered “among the grim heaps of the dead” (5.885–886). Later writers knew a battle at Pylos in which gods fought against Heracles. Pindar says that Poseidon stood against him at Pylos, and Apollo with his silver bow, and that Hades did not keep still the staff with which he leads the bodies of the dying down to his hollow street; then he breaks off, because to revile the gods is a hateful skill (*Olympian* 9.29–41). Apollodorus says that in the war on Pylos Heracles wounded Hades, who was helping the Pylians (*Library* 2.7.3), and Pausanias explains the worship of Hades at Elis by the same story (6.25.2–3).

**The gate.** The other reading keeps Hades at home. In the Iliad he is πυλάρτης, “the gatekeeper” (8.367, 13.415), and the dead pass “the gates of Hades” (5.646, 9.312, 23.71). Heracles went down to his house. Athena recalls that she saved him when Eurystheus sent him to the house of Hades to fetch the hound from Erebus (8.366–369; see [[heracles-and-cerberus]]), and in the Odyssey the ghost of Heracles tells Odysseus that he brought the dog up out of Hades (*Odyssey* 11.623–626). A wound given “at the gate, among the dead” would belong to that descent. Against this reading, the masculine πύλος, “gate”, is found nowhere else. Homer's word is the feminine πύλαι, “gates”, which he uses only in the plural.

On either reading, the arrow is lodged in the god's massive shoulder, and Hades goes up to Olympus in pain. Paeëon heals him with drugs, “for he was not made to be mortal” (5.398–402), and he heals Ares with the same words at the end of the book (5.900–901; see [[paeeon]]). Dione's lesson is the same whichever exploit she has in mind. See [[hades]], [[heracles]], [[pylos]] and [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]].
