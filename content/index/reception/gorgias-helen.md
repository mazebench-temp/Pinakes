Gorgias of Leontini wrote the *Encomium of Helen* as a display piece, and says so in its last sentence: he wished to write "an encomium of Helen, and a plaything of my own" (§21). The poets and their hearers, he says, speak of Helen with one voice, and her name has become a byword for disaster (§2). He will take the blame from her by reasoning.

Whatever happened, she went to Troy for one of four reasons: "by the wishes of Fortune, the plans of the gods and the decrees of Necessity; or seized by force; or persuaded by words; or captured by love" (§6). If a god was the cause, the god is to blame, for a human being cannot resist a god. If force, the man who used it did the wrong, and she suffered it (§7). If words, speech is "a great lord", which works on the soul as drugs work on the body (§§8–14). If love, love is either a god, whom no one can resist, or a sickness of the soul, a misfortune and not a crime (§§15–19). In every case, he concludes, she escapes the charge (αἰτία, §20).

Each of these excuses can be found in Book 3. Priam tells Helen that she is not to blame (αἰτίη) in his eyes: it is the gods who are to blame ([[aitios|αἴτιοι]], 3.164). Aphrodite, when Helen refuses to go to Paris' bed, threatens her, and Helen goes, afraid and silent (3.413–420). Paris remembers how he "snatched" her away (ἁρπάξας, 3.444), the word Gorgias uses for force (βίᾳ ἁρπασθεῖσα). And desire ([[eros|ἔρος]]) seizes Paris as it never has before (3.442). But Book 3 also gives Helen a voice that Gorgias leaves out. She blames herself, calls herself "dog-faced" (3.180) and "hateful" (3.404), and wishes she had died before she left home (3.173–175).

Isocrates, in his own *Helen*, praised the author of an earlier speech on her for his choice of subject, but said he had written a defense of her actions instead of a praise (14–15). For the same debate on the tragic stage, see [[euripides-trojan-women]].
