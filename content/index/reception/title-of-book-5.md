The traditional title of Book 5 is Διομήδους ἀριστεία, “the aristeia of Diomedes”. The noun ἀριστεία means excellence or prowess, above all in war. It is formed from the verb ἀριστεύω, “be the best”, and so from ἄριστος, “best” (see [[aristos]]). The noun is not Homeric, but the book gives Diomedes the adjective three times. Pandarus shouts that “the best of the Achaeans is hit” (5.103). Dione pictures Aegialeia “longing for her wedded husband, the best of the Achaeans” (5.414). And when Athena mounts his chariot the oaken axle groans, “for it carried a dread goddess and the best of men” (5.839). See [[best-of-the-achaeans]].

The name is old. Herodotus, arguing that Homer knew of Alexander's wanderings on the voyage home with Helen, says that the poet mentions them “in the Aristeia of Diomedes” (ἐν Διομήδεος ἀριστείῃ), and quotes four lines on the embroidered robes that Alexander brought from Sidon, on the voyage on which he brought Helen home (Herodotus 2.116.3; see [[helen-in-egypt]]). The lines are 6.289–292. In our text they belong to Book 6, where Hecuba goes down to her storeroom to choose one of those robes as a gift for Athena.

So the episode that Herodotus knew by this name did not stop where our Book 5 stops. Book 6 goes on with Diomedes' day. The gods have left the battle (6.1), and Diomedes kills Axylus of Arisbe, a friend to every traveler, and his charioteer (6.12–19). The seer Helenus sends Hector into the city because the Trojan women must pray to Athena to keep “the son of Tydeus” away from Ilios: he is the strongest of the Achaeans, Helenus says, more feared than Achilles ever was (6.96–101). Diomedes meets Glaucus between the armies, and the two exchange armor (6.119–236). Then the priestess [[Theano]] lays Hecuba's robe on the knees of Athena and prays to her to break Diomedes' spear, and Athena refuses (6.286–311). An episode named for Diomedes could have run at least that far.

The division of the Iliad into twenty-four books, each named by a letter of the alphabet (this one is Ε), is usually credited to the scholars of Alexandria, though some think it older. Herodotus shows that the episodes already had names, and that their bounds were not those of the later books (see [[the-boeotia]] and [[titles-of-book-4]]). The modern name for the type-scene of a hero's great day, the aristeia, comes from these ancient titles (see [[the-aristeia]] and [[the-aristeia-of-diomedes]]).
