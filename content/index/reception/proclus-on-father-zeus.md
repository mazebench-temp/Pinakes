summary: Plato's Timaeus makes Oceanus and Tethys the parents of Cronus and Rhea, while the Orphic theogony makes them their brother and sister. Proclus answers that among the gods a brother may also be called father for his higher rank, and offers Homer as proof: in Agamemnon's story Hera, Zeus' sister, calls him “Father Zeus”, and Poseidon, his brother, does the same.

**Homer.** Hera, having brought Eurystheus to birth, goes to tell Zeus herself, and addresses him as “Father Zeus of the gleaming thunderbolt” (19.121; see [[the-birth-of-heracles]] and [[hera]]). She is his sister as well as his wife: she tells him in *Iliad* 4 that crooked-counseling Cronus fathered her, “the most senior, / on both counts, by birth and because I am called / your wife” (4.59–61). Poseidon, too, is his brother, “For we are three brothers, born of Cronus, whom Rhea bore, / Zeus and I, and the third is Hades” (15.187–188), and he too opens a complaint with “Father Zeus, is there any mortal on the boundless earth” (7.446; see [[poseidon]]). Zeus is “the father of men and gods” to all (1.544; see [[father-zeus]] and [[zeus]]).

**Proclus.** In the *Timaeus* Timaeus follows the old genealogies: Oceanus and Tethys were the children of Earth and Heaven, and from them came Phorcys, Cronus and Rhea (*Timaeus* 40e; see [[plato]]). Proclus sets this beside the Orphic theogony, in which Earth, unknown to Heaven, bore seven daughters and seven sons, Tethys and Rhea among the daughters and Oceanus and Cronus among the sons, and asks how the *Timaeus* can bring Cronus and Rhea out of Oceanus and Tethys. His first answer is that it is no wonder if the same gods are called the brothers of some and, for their higher rank, their fathers, since those that come forth first bring forth those that follow them together with their own causes. So all the sons of Cronus are brothers, born of one generative source, “yet Zeus is called father both by Hera and by Poseidon in the divine poet”, and he quotes Hera's verse and Poseidon's. It is no wonder, then, that Oceanus and Tethys should be called both brother and sister and parents of Cronus and Rhea, keeping a father's rank among brothers. His second answer is that of the two sevens of Titans Oceanus alone stays with his father and does not leave his kingdom, while the others plot against him; and he quotes Orphic lines in which Oceanus remains in his halls, pondering which way to turn his mind, whether to join Cronus and his brothers against his father or to stay at home in peace (*On the Timaeus* III 184–185 Diehl; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]] and [[oceanus]]).

**Father and brother.** Homer's Hera uses the title in a speech of triumph: she has just won the oath and the birth, and calls on the father whose own word now binds him (19.121–124). Proclus takes it as a mark of rank among equals. The Iliad gives Zeus that rank in his own message to Poseidon, that he is “far stronger than he in strength, / and the elder by birth” (15.165–166), and Iris reminds Poseidon that “the Erinyes always follow the elder” (15.204; see [[the-erinyes-follow-the-elder]] and [[poseidon]]).
