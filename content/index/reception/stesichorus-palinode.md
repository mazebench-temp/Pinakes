In the *Phaedrus*, Socrates has just made a speech against love, and fears he has offended the god Eros. For those who sin in telling stories, he says, there is an ancient purification, "which Homer did not perceive, but Stesichorus did". Stesichorus was deprived of his eyes for speaking ill of Helen, and, unlike Homer, he knew the cause, being a man of the Muses. At once he composed:

> That story is not true.
> You did not go on the well-benched ships,
> you did not come to the citadel of Troy.

When he had finished the whole of the so-called palinode, "song sung again", he saw at once (243a–b). The remark about Homer is a joke. Tradition made him blind, and Socrates implies that he too had paid for his Helen, without knowing it.

The *Republic* shows what the palinode put in her place. The pleasures most men pursue, Socrates says, are phantoms of true pleasure, and men fight over them as the men at Troy, according to Stesichorus, fought over the phantom (εἴδωλον) of Helen, not knowing the truth (9.586c).

Other writers knew the story. Isocrates tells the blinding and the cure as proof of Helen's power (*Helen* 64). Pausanias records a tale from Croton: a man named Leonymus, visiting the White Island off the mouth of the Danube, found Helen living there as the wife of Achilles, and she sent him to tell Stesichorus that his blindness came from her anger (3.19.11–13). A papyrus commentary reports that there were two palinodes, one finding fault with Homer for putting Helen at Troy instead of her phantom, the other with Hesiod (Stesichorus fr. 193 PMG).

Book 3 is where the Iliad's Helen first appears in person: at her loom, on the tower by the Scaean gates, in Paris' chamber. She speaks of her coming to Troy as her own act. She followed Priam's son, and wishes that death had pleased her first (3.173–175). The palinode clears her by taking her out of the story. The Iliad already knows a battle over a phantom: Apollo makes an εἴδωλον of Aeneas, and Trojans and Achaeans hack at each other's shields around it (5.449–453). Herodotus and Euripides sent the real Helen to Egypt: see [[helen-in-egypt]].
