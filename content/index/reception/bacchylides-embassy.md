Bacchylides of Ceos, a contemporary and rival of Pindar, wrote choral songs in the first half of the fifth century BCE. His poems were lost until a papyrus containing many of them was published in 1897. Poem 15, one of his dithyrambs, is titled *The Sons of Antenor, or The Demand for Helen's Return*. Its opening is damaged. Theano, Antenor's wife and priestess of Athena, receives the envoys, Odysseus son of Laertes and Menelaus son of Atreus; they are brought before Priam and his sons and the assembled Trojans; and Menelaus speaks.

His speech is a warning. Zeus, who rules on high and sees all things, "is not to blame (αἴτιος) for mortals' great sorrows". Straight Justice, the companion of holy Good Order and wise Themis, is there for all men to reach. But Insolence (Ὕβρις), who flourishes on shifty gains and folly, gives a man another's wealth and power only to send him to deep ruin: she destroyed the arrogant Giants, sons of Earth. The preserved text ends there.

In Iliad 3 Antenor remembers receiving the two envoys as guests in his house, and the speeches they made to the Trojans: Menelaus spoke few words, but very clearly (3.205–215). Bacchylides gives Menelaus the moral speech the Iliad leaves out. Its first claim answers another voice of Book 3. Priam had told Helen that the gods, not she, were to blame (θεοί νύ μοι [[aitios|αἴτιοί]] εἰσιν, 3.164); Menelaus tells the Trojans that Zeus is not to blame, and that the fault is theirs (see [[who-is-to-blame]]). The Odyssey gives the same complaint to Zeus himself: mortals blame the gods, but bring sorrows on themselves by their own recklessness (Odyssey 1.32–34).
