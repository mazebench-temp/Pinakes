The traditional title of Book 10 is Δολώνεια, “the Doloneia”, the lay of [[dolon|Dolon]]. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter, this one is Κ, kappa (see [[title-of-book-5]]). The name is formed like Πατρόκλεια, the old title of Book 16, and like Ὀδύσσεια itself, “the song of Odysseus”: the hero's name with the ending ‑εια. The book's story is told under [[the-doloneia]].

**Aelian's list.** The name is old. Aelian, collecting curiosities in the third century CE, writes that the ancients once sang the poems of Homer divided up, and gives the names they used: “The battle at the ships, and a Doloneia (Δολώνειάν τινα), and the Aristeia of Agamemnon, and the Catalogue of Ships, and the Patrocleia, and the Ransom, and the Games for Patroclus, and the Obliteration of the Oaths”. A list of episodes of the Odyssey follows, among them the Cyclopeia (Κυκλώπεια), another name of the same shape. Late, he adds, Lycurgus of Sparta first brought Homer's poetry to Greece as a whole, from Ionia, and afterward Peisistratus collected it and produced the Iliad and the Odyssey (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14). Several of his names have their own entries here (see [[the-boeotia]] and [[titles-of-book-4]]). What Aelian says about separate performance bears on the old question whether this book belonged to the Iliad from the first (see [[the-doloneia-question]]).

**Named for the victim.** The title passes over the two Achaeans who go out, and the Thracian king they kill, and names the Trojan spy whom they catch, question and kill (10.314–468). Πατρόκλεια likewise names its book for the man who dies in it. Dolon fills the middle of the book, from Hector's council to the spoils hung on the tamarisk, and the Trojan half of the night turns on him. Later readers heard δόλος, “trick”, in it. The *Rhesus* has Hector call him “well named” (ἐπώνυμος, *Rhesus* 158), and Ovid's Penelope says that Rhesus was betrayed by sleep and Dolon by a trick, *dolo* (*Heroides* 1.40; see [[euripides-rhesus]] and [[ovid-dolon-and-rhesus]]).

**The night-rousing.** A second name appears in Strabo. Reporting how Crates made Phoenix a Phocian, he speaks of the helmet “which Odysseus used in the night-rousing” (κατὰ τὴν νυκτεγερσίαν, *Geography* 9.5.18; see [[hellas-or-ormenium]] and [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]]). The noun is made from νύξ, “night”, and ἐγείρω, “wake, rouse”, and the verb runs through the book as through no other in the Iliad. It occurs eleven times: Agamemnon tells Menelaus to bid the men stay awake (ἐγρήγορθαι, 10.67); Nestor proposes to wake the others (10.108, 10.146), learns that Menelaus woke before his brother (10.124), and rouses Odysseus and then Diomedes, “Wake up, son of Tydeus!” (10.138, 10.157–159); Diomedes asks whether there are no younger men to wake the kings (10.166); the leaders of the sentries are found awake (10.182), and so are the Trojans at their fires (10.419). The last instance is Athena's warning that some other god may wake the Trojans (10.511). The book opens with a king who cannot sleep and ends before dawn with the two raiders at their meal (see [[hypnos]] and [[war-and-night]]).
