Dione warns in Book 5 that the man who fights the immortals does not live long, and does not come home to his children (5.406–409; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]). The Odyssey lets Diomedes come home: Nestor says that on the fourth day of the voyage his companions beached their ships in Argos (*Odyssey* 3.180–182). Later legend gave him a harder end, and some tellers made Aphrodite its cause.

**Italy.** Diomedes left Argos and settled in Daunia, in southern Italy. Strabo reports that Argyrippa, later called Arpi, and Canusium were said to be his foundations; that old offerings in the temple of Athena at Luceria were shown as signs of his rule; and that off the coast lay two islands of Diomedes, one inhabited and the other deserted. On the deserted one, some said, Diomedes vanished and his companions were turned into birds. The birds are still there, tame, living a kind of human life in the order of their feeding and in their gentleness to decent men, and fleeing the wicked and criminal (*Geography* 6.3.9). Strabo counts four accounts of his end: that he was called home and died there; that he stayed in Italy until his death; the story of his vanishing on the island; and the story of the Heneti at the head of the Adriatic, who spoke of his apotheosis (6.3.9). Among the Heneti a white horse was sacrificed to him, and two groves were shown, one of Argive Hera and one of Aetolian Artemis (5.1.9).

The collection of marvels handed down under Aristotle's name tells of a remarkable sanctuary of Diomedes on the island of Diomedeia in the Adriatic. Around it sit great birds with large, hard beaks. If Greeks come ashore, the birds stay quiet; if any of the barbarians who live nearby, they fly up, dive at their heads and kill them with their beaks. They are said to have come from the companions of Diomedes, who were shipwrecked off the island when Diomedes was treacherously killed by the king of the place ([Aristotle], *On Marvellous Things Heard* 79).

**Virgil.** Virgil's Diomedes names the birds among the punishments that followed his wounding of Venus: his lost companions have taken to the sky on wings, haunt the rivers as birds, and fill the rocks with tearful cries (*Aeneid* 11.271–274; see [[virgils-diomedes]]).

**Ovid.** Ovid tells how it happened. When the envoy of the Latins comes to ask for help, Diomedes explains why he cannot give it. Venus had not forgotten her wound, and the Greeks' homecoming was full of storms and suffering. When one of his companions, Acmon of Pleuron, mocked the goddess and dared her to do her worst, his voice thinned, feathers covered him, and he and others of the company became white birds, “if not swans, very close to white swans” (*Metamorphoses* 14.457–511).

Not every story ended in loss. Pindar, praising the glories of Argos, says that the gray-eyed goddess once made Diomedes an immortal god (*Nemean* 10.7). The goddess who set him against the gods in Book 5 (5.405) gave him in the end the opposite of the short life that Dione foretold for such a man.
