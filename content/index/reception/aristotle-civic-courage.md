**Homer.** Zeus' thunderbolt has fallen before Diomedes' horses, and Nestor tells him to turn and flee: Zeus is giving glory to Hector today, and “No man can hold back the mind of Zeus” (8.143). Diomedes agrees, but one thing grieves him. One day Hector will boast among the Trojans that the son of Tydeus fled before him to the ships, “then may the wide earth gape for me” (8.150). Nestor answers that the Trojans will not believe it, nor the Trojan women “whose husbands in their prime you have flung in the dust” (8.156), and turns the horses. Hector shouts his taunts after them, and three times Diomedes ponders turning to fight, and three times Zeus thunders from Ida (8.157–171; see [[diomedes]] and [[nestor]]).

**Aristotle.** In the third book of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, after defining courage, Aristotle lists five states that are called courage but are not courage in the strict sense. The first, and the most like the real thing, is the courage of citizens (ἡ πολιτική). Citizens face dangers because of the penalties of the laws, the reproaches and the honors, and so the bravest peoples seem to be those among whom cowards are dishonored and the brave are honored. “Homer too portrays such men,” he says, “as Diomedes and Hector.” He quotes Hector's words outside the walls in Book 22, “Polydamas will be the first to lay reproach on me” (22.100), and Diomedes' words here: “Hector will say one day, speaking among the Trojans, ‘The son of Tydeus …’” (8.148–149). This courage, he goes on, is most like true courage because it comes from virtue: from shame (διʼ αἰδῶ), from desire of something noble, that is, honor, and from avoidance of reproach, which is base (1116a17–29).

Below it he ranks those whom their commanders compel. They act from fear, not shame, and shun what is painful, not what is base. His example is again a Homeric threat, which he gives to Hector: whomever I see cowering apart from the battle, he will not be sure to escape the dogs (1116a29–b3). The words are close to Agamemnon's in his speech to the army in Book 2 (2.391–393), and Hector threatens his own men in much the same way in Book 15 (15.348–351). Zeus' threat to the gods at the start of Book 8 begins in the same way, “Whomever I see” (8.10).

**Shame.** The two examples are two sides of one exchange. Diomedes fears what Hector will say; Hector, in Book 22, fears what Polydamas will say. The αἰδώς that Agamemnon asks of the army in Book 5, when he tells them to “feel shame before one another in the fierce battles” (5.530), is the same feeling, shared by a whole army (see [[aidos]] and [[shame-saves-more]]). In Book 8 Diomedes' shame does not decide what he does. Zeus' thunder decides it, and Nestor turns the horses.
