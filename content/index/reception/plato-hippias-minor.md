**Hippias' proof.** After a display on Homer, Hippias of Elis tells Socrates that Homer made Achilles the best of those who came to Troy, Nestor the wisest, and Odysseus the most versatile (πολυτροπώτατον). Is Achilles not versatile too? Not at all: he is “most simple and most true” (ἁπλούστατος καὶ ἀληθέστατος), as Homer shows “in the Prayers” (ἐν Λιταῖς), and Hippias recites Achilles' opening words. In them Homer shows Achilles true and simple, Odysseus versatile and false, “for he makes Achilles speak these verses at Odysseus” (364b–365b). Hippias takes “that man” who hides one thing and says another to be Odysseus; the poem does not say whom Achilles means (see [[hateful-as-the-gates-of-hades]] and [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]). The title by which he cites the book is discussed under [[title-of-book-9]].

**Plato's text.** Hippias' verses are not quite ours. For 9.310 he has ὥσπερ δὴ κρανέω τε καὶ ὡς τελέεσθαι ὀίω, “as I will bring it to pass and as I think it will be fulfilled”. He leaves out 9.311, and he ends with a verse that begins like 9.314 and ends like our 9.310, αὐτὰρ ἐγὼν ἐρέω, ὡς καὶ τετελεσμένον ἔσται, “but I will speak as it will in fact be accomplished”. Our 9.314 is a verse that Nestor speaks earlier in the book, at 9.103. Whether the differences come from Plato's text of Homer or from his memory cannot be told.

**Socrates' answer.** Leaving Homer aside, “since one cannot ask him what he meant”, Socrates leads Hippias to agree that the man best able to tell the truth about numbers is also best able to lie about them: the true man and the false are the same (365c–369b). Then he turns back to the poem. Odysseus is nowhere seen lying, but Achilles is. Soon after his words against the man who hides one thing and says another, he tells Odysseus that he will sail home at dawn and reach Phthia on the third day (9.357–363), as he had told Agamemnon in Book 1, “Now I will go to Phthia” (1.169–171); yet he never prepares to launch his ships, “very nobly disregarding the truth” (369e–370e). Hippias replies that Achilles lies unwillingly, compelled by the army's misfortune to stay, while Odysseus lies willingly and by design. Socrates answers that Achilles was the cleverer deceiver, who told Ajax something else to Odysseus' face without Odysseus noticing:

{{quote:c183e4a7-f4b4-5dfe-9c2a-a3c083aef940}}

Did the pupil of Thetis and of the wise Chiron forget so soon? Hippias says that he spoke otherwise to Ajax “won over by goodwill” (ὑπὸ εὐνοίας ἀναπεισθείς). Then, says Socrates, by what they agreed before, Odysseus, who lies willingly, is the better man (371a–e). Hippias refuses the conclusion, and the dialogue ends with Socrates arguing that the man who does wrong willingly, if there is one, can only be the good man, and confessing that he wanders back and forth on the question (372d–376c).

**Aristotle.** In the *Metaphysics* Aristotle names the dialogue to reject its argument. “The argument in the Hippias”, he says, misleads in showing that the same man is false and true: it calls false the man who is able to lie, and it proves that the man who does wrong willingly is better from the example of a man who limps on purpose, meaning a man who imitates a limp (5.29, 1025a6–13).

**Homer.** The poem gives Achilles three answers, not two: to Phoenix he says that at dawn they will consider whether to go or stay (9.618–619), and Odysseus reports only the first (9.677–692; see [[achilles]] and [[message-repeated]]). Achilles gives the reason for the change himself. All that Ajax has said seems spoken after his own heart, “but my heart swells with anger whenever I remember” the insult (9.645–648). Hippias calls the change goodwill and Socrates craft; the poem shows a man moved and still angry (see [[truth-and-deception]]).
