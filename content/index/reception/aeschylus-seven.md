Aeschylus' *Seven against Thebes* (467 BCE) is set inside the besieged city. A scout reports which Argive champion stands at each of the seven gates, and Eteocles, Oedipus' son, sets a Theban against each one.

The first is Tydeus, at the Proetid gate. The seer, the son of Oecles (Amphiaraus), will not let the army cross the river Ismenus, because the sacrifices are not favorable. Tydeus, raging and eager for battle, shouts like a serpent at noon, and reviles the seer for cowardice. On his shield is the night sky, blazing with stars, with the full moon in the middle (*Seven against Thebes* 375–396). Eteocles answers that the night on the shield may prove a prophecy against its bearer, when the night of death falls on his eyes, and sends Melanippus against him (397–416). At the next gate Capaneus boasts that he will sack the city whether the god wills it or not; his shield shows a naked man with a torch, and the words “I will burn the city” (422–436). Later the seer himself, at the sixth gate, denounces Tydeus as a murderer, a troubler of the city and Argos' chief teacher of evils, and rebukes Polynices for leading a foreign army against his own country (568–596).

In the Iliad Tydeus is remembered by a king who never saw him, as a model for his son (4.370–400). Agamemnon's story has the gods on Tydeus' side. Athena helps him win every contest among the Cadmeians, and when he kills the men who lay in ambush for him he spares one, “obeying the portents of the gods” (4.390, 4.398). But the same story begins with a warning: when Tydeus and Polynices came to Mycenae for allies, Zeus turned the Mycenaeans back with ill-omened signs (4.381). Sthenelus, the son of Capaneus, draws the conclusion: the sons took Thebes “trusting in the portents of the gods and the help of Zeus”, while their fathers “perished through their own recklessness” (4.408–409). Aeschylus puts that recklessness on the stage: a Tydeus who scorns the seer's reading of the sacrifices, a Capaneus who defies Zeus.
