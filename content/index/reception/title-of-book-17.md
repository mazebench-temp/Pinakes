summary: Book 17 of the Iliad is traditionally called Μενελάου ἀριστεία, “the Aristeia of Menelaus”. Menelaus is the first to stand over the body of Patroclus and one of the two who carry it away, but he kills only two men, twice leaves the body, and the god who taunts Hector with him calls him “a soft spearman”. The title passes over Ajax, Hector in Achilles' armor, the mist and the weeping horses; Aelian's list of the parts once sung on their own has no such name, and the older readers remembered the book for the soft spearman, Ajax's prayer and Euphorbus.

The traditional title of *Iliad* 17 is Μενελάου ἀριστεία, “the Aristeia of Menelaus”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter, this one is Ρ, rho (see [[title-of-book-5]]). The book's story is told under [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]], and Menelaus' part in it under [[menelaus]].

**The name.** The title is built like Διομήδους ἀριστεία and Ἀγαμέμνονος ἀριστεία, the titles of *Iliad* 5 and 11 (see [[title-of-book-5]] and [[title-of-book-11]]). Its noun is not Homeric: ἀριστεία, “prowess”, is formed from the verb ἀριστεύω, “be the best”, and so from ἄριστος, “best” (see [[aristos]] and [[the-aristeia]]). In this book the adjective goes to Menelaus' victim, not to him. Apollo, rousing Hector, reports that “warlike Menelaus, the son of Atreus, / standing over Patroclus, has killed the best of the Trojans” (17.79–80), and the simile that follows the killing gives the lion “the cow that is the best” of the herd (17.62; see [[lion-among-cattle]]). The verb of the title is used in the book only once, of the Paeonian Apisaon, who as Lycomedes kills him is said to be the next after Asteropaeus: “and after Asteropaeus he was the best in the fighting” (17.351; see [[apisaon-son-of-hippasus]]). And the best of the Achaeans, in the book's own words, is the dead man. Menelaus tells Antilochus that “the best of the Achaeans has been slain, / Patroclus” (17.689–690; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]).

**Aelian's list.** Aelian, writing that the ancients once sang the poems of Homer in separate parts, names a Patrocleia among them, and nothing that answers to this book (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[aelian]] and [[title-of-book-16]]). Where the Patrocleia ended in performance is not known. *Iliad* 17 takes up the story at the moment Book 16 leaves it, with Patroclus dead on the field, and it is the fight for his body from its first verse to its last.

**What the title covers.** Menelaus opens the book and helps to close it. “Nor did it escape the son of Atreus, Menelaus, dear to Ares, / that Patroclus had been brought down” (17.1–2): he is the first to bestride the body and kills Euphorbus over it, and at the end he and Meriones lift it and carry it toward the ships (17.1–60, 17.717–746). Between, the book gives him the parts of an aristeia in a lower key (see [[the-aristeia]]). Athena gave Diomedes “might and daring” at the start of his day (5.1–2); Menelaus prays to her without knowing her, and she puts strength in his shoulders and knees and in his breast “the daring of a fly” (17.561–573; see [[tharsos]] and [[flies]]). He kills two men, Euphorbus and Podes (17.43–60, 17.575–581; see [[euphorbus]] and [[podes]]), and twice he leaves the body, each time going like a lion from a farmyard (17.106–113, 17.657–667). Much of his work is calling others: Ajax, the leaders of the Argives, and Antilochus with the news for Achilles (17.114–122, 17.246–255, 17.651–701). The narrator turns to him twice as “you” (17.679, 17.702; see [[apostrophe]]).

**A soft spearman.** The god who reports his victory also belittles him. Apollo, in the likeness of Phaenops, asks Hector which of the Achaeans would still fear him, now that he has shrunk back before “Menelaus, who before now at least / was a soft spearman” (17.587–588). μαλθακὸς αἰχμητής is found only here in Homer. Walter Leaf reports that Aristonicus remarked that the words are put in the mouth of an enemy and are not the poet's own, since the poet elsewhere calls Menelaus ἀρηΐφιλος, “dear to Ares”; Leaf takes the remark to be aimed at Plato's *Symposium*, and adds that there is often something disparaging in the way Menelaus is spoken of, “though in action he always proves better than his reputation” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.588). The taunt carries its own answer: in the same speech Apollo tells Hector that Menelaus has gone off alone with a body from among the Trojans, and that the man he has killed is Podes, Hector's trusted companion (17.588–590).

**What the title leaves out.** The fight for the body is the work of many. Ajax is its bulwark. He comes up with his shield like a tower, stands over Patroclus like a lion over its young, kills Hippothous and Phorcys, and keeps the Achaeans packed around the body, and the narrator sets him above all the Danaans in looks and in deeds after Achilles (17.128–139, 17.274–318, 17.356–365; see [[ajax]]). Hector puts on the armor of Achilles while Zeus foretells his death (17.183–214; see [[exchange-of-armor]]). Zeus covers the fighters around the body in mist, and lifts it at Ajax's prayer (17.268–273, 17.366–377, 17.626–650). The horses of Achilles weep for their charioteer, and Zeus pities them (17.426–458; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Automedon fights from their chariot and kills Aretus (17.459–542; see [[automedon]]). Achilles, by the ships, still does not know that Patroclus is dead (17.401–411; see [[achilles]]). And the book ends not with Menelaus but with the two Ajaxes, who hold back the Trojans as a wooded ridge holds back the floods while the young men of the Achaeans flee screaming before Aeneas and Hector (17.746–761; see [[the-two-ajaxes]]).

**The older readers.** The ancient readers who use the book do not name it, and they remember Menelaus less for his prowess than for Apollo's taunt. Plato's Socrates takes “a soft spearman” for Homer's own judgment: Homer made Agamemnon an exceptionally good warrior and Menelaus a soft spearman, and then had him come unbidden to his better's feast (*Symposium* 174b–c; see [[plato-menelaus-uninvited]]). In the *Republic* the phrase becomes a type: the man who lets soft and mournful music pour into his soul through his ears goes on until he has melted his spirit and cut the sinews out of his soul, and made himself “a soft spearman” (*Republic* 3.411a–b; see [[plato]]). The book's other famous passage is Ajax's prayer in the mist, which *On the Sublime* reads as the wish of a hero who would rather die in the light than live idle in the dark (*On the Sublime* 9.10; see [[longinus-ajaxs-prayer]]). Pythagoras claimed that his soul had once been Euphorbus', the man Menelaus kills at the start of the book (Diogenes Laertius 8.4–5, after Heraclides of Pontus; see [[euphorbus]] and [[pythagoras]]). Strabo uses Hippothous' death “far from deep-soiled Larisa” to decide which Larisa the Pelasgians came from (*Geography* 13.3.2; see [[larisa]]); a note collected among the fragments of Aristotle's *Homeric Problems*, and Proclus after it, take Hector's design on the body as Achilles' excuse for dragging Hector (see [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]]); Plutarch draws lessons from Glaucus' rebuke of Hector and Hector's answer, and from Patroclus, who “knew how to be kind to all” (*How the Young Man Should Study Poetry* 31f–32b, 34f; see [[plutarch]]); and Virgil's war-horse weeps for Pallas as the horses of Achilles weep for Patroclus (*Aeneid* 11.89–90; see [[virgils-weeping-horse]]).
