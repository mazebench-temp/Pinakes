summary: Rhesus dies as “an evil dream stood over his head that night, the son of Oeneus' son”: either Diomedes himself is the dream, or the king was dreaming as he died. The Alexandrian editors rejected the verse that names Diomedes, and Odysseus' count of the dead, which makes Dolon the thirteenth after the narrator has made Rhesus the thirteenth, troubled ancient readers too.

**Two readings.** Diomedes has killed twelve sleeping Thracians (10.488). The king is the thirteenth, and he dies “as he lay gasping” (10.495–496). The poet gives the reason: “for an evil dream stood over his head / that night, the son of Oeneus' son, by the design of Athena” (10.496–497). Standing over a sleeper's head is what dreams and phantoms do in Homer (2.20, 2.59, 23.68; *Odyssey* 4.803, 6.21). With the second verse, the dream is most simply Diomedes himself, the grandson of Oeneus, standing over the sleeping king: the phrase is a grim irony, and Athena's design is the whole raid. The verses can also be read of a real dream, in which Rhesus saw Diomedes standing over him as he died; and without the second verse the dream is simply a nightmare that does not wake him. The translation renders both verses and takes the first reading (see the translation's note on 10.494–497 and [[onar]]). Οἰνεΐδαο πάϊς names Diomedes by his grandfather only here; the patronymic standing alone is his father Tydeus, “the son of Oeneus” (Οἰνεΐδαο, 5.813; see [[oeneus]] and [[diomedes]]).

**The Alexandrian editors.** According to Walter Leaf, who reports the scholia, Aristarchus rejected the second verse (10.497), and Zenodotus and Aristophanes did not have it in their texts (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 10.496). Leaf agreed with them. He took the passage to mean that Rhesus was breathing heavily under a real and ominous dream that failed to save him, and thought that a rhapsode who read the “evil dream” as a bitter name for Diomedes added the verse to make his reading plain. He objected that the accusative τὴν νύκτα means “all night long”, where the sense wants “in the night”, and he cited the observation that in Homer only people known to the sleeper appear in dreams. Monro and Allen print the verse without brackets.

**The tragedy.** The *Rhesus* gives the dream to another sleeper. Rhesus' wounded charioteer tells how he dreamed that wolves had mounted the horses he drove for his master and were lashing them with their tails, and woke to the groans of the dying and his master's warm blood (*Rhesus* 780–791; see [[euripides-rhesus]]). Leaf put the change down to the needs of the stage, since the dead king could not tell his dream (on 10.496). The wolves suit a play whose Dolon goes out disguised as a wolf (*Rhesus* 208–215).

**The thirteenth.** Odysseus' report to Nestor counts differently from the narrator. The narrator makes Rhesus the thirteenth man, after twelve (10.488, 10.495). Odysseus says that their lord was killed by Diomedes, “and beside him twelve companions, all of the best”, and then calls Dolon “A thirteenth, a spy” (10.559–561). The count holds if the king is set apart and the twelve and Dolon are counted together (see the translation's note on 10.559–561). Leaf reports a variant τετρακαιδέκατον, “fourteenth”, which Aristonicus mentions, and judged it a conjecture made to remove the difficulty (on 10.561).
