summary: Book 12 of the Odyssey is headed Σειρῆνες, Σκύλλα, Χάρυβδις, βόες Ἡλίου, “the Sirens, Scylla, Charybdis, the cattle of the Sun”, the four dangers of the last voyage in Odysseus' tale, named in the order in which Circe foretells them and the ship meets them, two of them in their later Attic forms. Strabo names the Sirenussae, the strait, Scylla and Charybdis in the same order, and Euripides and Propertius sum up the book by the same marvels. The heading leaves out the burial of Elpenor at the beginning and the wreck and the drift to Calypso at the end.

The title of *Odyssey* 12 in Merry's edition is Σειρῆνες, Σκύλλα, Χάρυβδις, βόες Ἡλίου, “the Sirens, Scylla, Charybdis, the cattle of the Sun” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, heading of Book 12); this library calls the book “The Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, and the Cattle of the Sun”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter of the alphabet, this one is μ, mu (see [[title-of-book-5]]). Its three adventures are told under [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]], [[odysseus-passes-scylla]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]], its creatures under [[sirens]], [[scylla]] and [[charybdis]], and the god of the herds under [[helios]]. The burial with which it begins ends the story of [[the-death-of-elpenor]], and the book ends the tale that Odysseus began to the Phaeacians in *Odyssey* 9 (see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).

**The words of the title.** The heading lists four nominatives, three names and a phrase, and gives two of them in later forms than the poem's. Σειρῆνες is Homer's word: “but the Sirens bewitch him with their clear song” (*Odyssey* 12.44). The poem also speaks of them in the dual, “the voice of the two Sirens” (Σειρήνοιιν, *Odyssey* 12.52; again 12.167), and Merry concludes that Homer's Sirens are two (Merry on *Odyssey* 12.39; see the translation's note on 12.39, 12.52, 12.167, 12.185). Σκύλλα is the Attic form of Homer's Σκύλλη, “There within dwells Scylla, yelping terribly” (*Odyssey* 12.85), the form of the tragedians and of the prose writers: Aeschylus' Cassandra, looking for a name for the queen who will kill Agamemnon, asks whether she should call her “some Scylla dwelling in the rocks, the bane of sailors” (Σκύλλαν τινὰ / οἰκοῦσαν ἐν πέτραισι, ναυτίλων βλάβην, *Agamemnon* 1232–1234, a plain rendering; see [[scylla-and-charybdis-in-later-writers]]). Χάρυβδις is the poem's own nominative, “brilliant Charybdis” (δῖα Χάρυβδις, *Odyssey* 12.104, 12.235). βόες Ἡλίου puts into Attic the poem's Ἠελίοιο βόες, “the cattle of the Sun” (*Odyssey* 12.128), with the contracted genitive of ἥλιος for Homer's Ἠελίοιο. The cattle are named at the beginning of the poem, where the companions perish “through their own recklessness, / fools, who ate up the cattle of Hyperion the Sun” (*Odyssey* 1.7–8; see [[proem]]). Strabo uses the Attic phrase when he lists the plain marvels of Homer's wanderings, “Ocean and Hades and the cattle of the Sun”, ὠκεανὸν καὶ ᾄδην καὶ Ἡλίου βόας, and counts “the shape of Scylla”, μορφὴν Σκύλλης, among them (*Geography* 1.2.11, plain renderings; see [[strabo]]).

**In the order of the voyage.** Circe tells the dangers in this order: first the Sirens (*Odyssey* 12.39–54); then two ways, one past the overhanging rocks that the gods call the Wanderers and the other between the two crags of Scylla and Charybdis (*Odyssey* 12.55–126); and last the island of Thrinacia, where the cattle of the Sun graze (*Odyssey* 12.127–141; see [[thrinacia]]). The ship meets them in the same order, and Odysseus names three of the four in the verses that bring him to the island: “But when we had escaped the rocks and dread Charybdis / and Scylla, then at once we came to the flawless island of the god; / and there were the fine broad-browed cattle” (*Odyssey* 12.260–262). The heading leaves out the way he did not take, past the Wanderers, which only the Argo has sailed by (*Odyssey* 12.59–72; see [[the-wanderers]] and [[the-argo]]).

**What the title covers.** The four take unequal shares of the book's 453 lines. Circe foretells them all in her instructions, broken only by Odysseus' question about Scylla (*Odyssey* 12.37–141, 12.112–114). The passage of the Sirens fills fifty-nine lines, from Circe's departure at dawn to the moment when the companions take the wax from their ears (*Odyssey* 12.142–200), and the passage of Scylla and Charybdis as many again (*Odyssey* 12.201–259). The cattle of the Sun take the largest share: from the landing on Thrinacia to the thunderbolt that breaks the ship, their story runs to 160 lines (*Odyssey* 12.260–419), more than a third of the book. The heading names neither the beginning nor the end. The book opens with the return from the house of Hades to Aeaea, the burial that Elpenor's soul asked for in the book before, and Circe's welcome of men “twice dying, when other men die once” (*Odyssey* 12.1–27; 11.71–78; see [[elpenor]] and [[odysseus-and-circe]]). It closes with Odysseus alone on the keel and the mast, carried back to Charybdis, saved by the wild fig tree above her, and borne for nine days to Ogygia (*Odyssey* 12.420–449; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and [[ogygia]]). Charybdis, the third name, stands for two passages, one with the ship and one without it. There the tale stops, “But why do I tell you this tale? / For I already told it to you yesterday in your house, / to you and to your strong wife” (*Odyssey* 12.450–452), for on the evening of his arrival Odysseus had told Arete, when she asked who had given him his clothes, how he came from Calypso's island (*Odyssey* 7.237–297; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**The names in Strabo.** Strabo, approving Polybius' view that Odysseus' wanderings were set around Sicily and Italy, asks what poet or historian persuaded the Neapolitans to point to a tomb of Parthenope the Siren, and the people of Cumae, Dicaearchia and Vesuvius to speak of an oracle of the dead at Avernus and of Baius and Misenus as companions of Odysseus; “and so too the matters concerning the Sirenussae and those concerning the strait, and Scylla and Charybdis and Aeolus”, τὰ περὶ Σειρηνούσσας καὶ τὰ περὶ τὸν πορθμὸν καὶ Σκύλλαν καὶ Χάρυβδιν καὶ Αἰόλον, which should neither be examined too exactly nor be left without root or hearth (*Geography* 1.2.18, plain renderings). The list keeps the heading's order for its first three names, in the construction τὰ περί of the heading of the tenth book (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-10]]). Earlier, answering Eratosthenes, who held that Homer did not mean to set the wandering in known places, Strabo asks whether Aetna and Tyrrhenia were known but “the Scyllaeum and Charybdis and the Circaeum and the Sirenussae” were not (*Geography* 1.2.14, a plain rendering). In Strabo's geography the Sirens have become places, the Sirenussae by the headland of Surrentum, with three rocky islets that are called the Sirens, and Scylla has become the Scyllaeum, a high rock on the Italian side of the strait (*Geography* 1.2.12, 6.1.5; see [[the-sirens-in-later-writers]] and [[scylla-and-charybdis-in-later-writers]]).

**Later summaries.** Two later poets sum up the book by the same marvels. Euripides' Cassandra, foretelling the sufferings of Odysseus, names first “dread Charybdis”, who has made her dwelling in the narrow channel of the rock, then the raw-eating Cyclops, Circe who shapes men into swine, the shipwrecks and the longing for the lotus, and last “the holy cattle of the Sun, which will one day send forth flesh with a voice, a bitter cry for Odysseus” (*Trojan Women* 431–441, plain renderings; see [[euripides]]), the meat that lowed on the spits in this book (*Odyssey* 12.395–396). Propertius makes Postumus a second Ulysses, whom so many long delays did not harm: Ismarus taken, the blinded Polyphemus, Circe's deceits and the lotus, “Scylla, and Charybdis torn by alternate waters, the bullocks of Lampetie lowing on Ithacan spits (his daughter Lampetie had pastured them for Phoebus)”, the nights and days of swimming, the dark houses of the silent souls, and “the Sirens' waters, approached with a deaf crew” (*Scyllaque et alternas scissa Charybdis aquas, / Lampeties Ithacis veribus mugisse iuvencos / (paverat hos Phoebo filia Lampetie)*, … *Sirenum surdo remige adisse lacus*, *Elegies* 3.12.23–36, plain renderings; see [[propertius]] and [[lampetie]]). Merry sets the Greek and the Latin beside the lowing of the meat (Merry on *Odyssey* 12.395, the Latin after Ameis).

**Merry's headings.** Merry's titles for the four books of the tale are Ἀλκίνου ἀπόλογοι, Κυκλώπεια (Book 9); Τὰ περὶ Αἰόλου καὶ Λαιστρυγόνων καὶ Κίρκης (Book 10); Νέκυια (Book 11); and this book's four names (Merry, headings of Books 9–12; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-9]], [[the-title-of-odyssey-10]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-11]]). The heading of the tenth book names the persons of its adventures, a man, a people and a goddess; this one names dangers, and none of them is met face to face. The Sirens sing from their meadow as the ship rows past; Odysseus, armed on the deck, cannot catch sight of Scylla anywhere, and sees only the feet and hands of his companions as they are lifted on high (*Odyssey* 12.232–233, 12.247–249); Charybdis is a whirlpool; and the cattle belong to the Sun, whose words Odysseus learned from Calypso (*Odyssey* 12.389–390). Merry's edition ends with this book. Monro's, which begins with the next, heads it Ὀδυσσέως ἀπόπλους παρὰ Φαιάκων καὶ ἄφιξις εἰς Ἰθάκην, “the departure of Odysseus from the Phaeacians and his arrival in Ithaca” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, heading of Book 13; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-13]]).
