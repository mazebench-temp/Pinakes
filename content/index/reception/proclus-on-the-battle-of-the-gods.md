summary: Plato would not admit the battles of the gods that Homer composed, nor “the strife and judgment of the gods through Themis and Zeus”. Proclus answers that Homer's gods do not truly fight. Zeus' assembly through Themis turns the gods back to their maker before he sends them to care for the world, and the eleven gods of the battle, five on one side and six on the other, stand in their pairs for the oppositions of which the world is made, with Aphrodite to bring them into friendship.

**Plato's charge.** Among the stories that may not be told in his city, Plato's Socrates names all the battles of the gods that Homer composed, whether they were composed with hidden meanings or without (*Republic* 2.378b–d; see [[title-of-book-20]] and [[plato-hephaestus-hurled]]), and, a page later, “the strife and judgment of the gods through Themis and Zeus” (θεῶν ἔριν τε καὶ κρίσιν διὰ Θέμιτός τε καὶ Διός, 2.379e–380a; see [[plato-broken-oaths]]). He does not say which strife and which judgment he means.

**The assembly through Themis.** Proclus, defending Homer in his essays on the *Republic*, takes the strife to be the one “to which Zeus stirs up the multitudes of the gods through Themis”, and the judgment to be the judgment of Paris, which he explains in the next chapter as the choice of a life (*On the Republic* I 106–108 Kroll). Zeus, he says, is the unity of all the gods within the world, and his activity toward them is twofold: he turns them back to himself, and he moves them to care for what is below them. So the poem gives him two speeches to the gods. In the first he bids them all stand apart from the war, as in Book 8 (8.10–12); in the second he moves them to providence, so that each may act on the world after his own nature, “and help either side, whichever way each of you has a mind” (20.25). And because their going out into the world is not torn away from their maker's unity, Themis first turns them back to him: “And Zeus bade Themis call the gods to assembly” (20.4), so that in caring for the world they act by their father's will and by the judgment of Themis, whose child is Justice (I 106–107 Kroll; see [[themis-the-goddess]]).

He gives the same reading in his other commentaries. Themis goes everywhere, gathers the number of the gods and turns it toward the maker; when Zeus turns them to himself without her, he commands them to stay apart on Olympus and to turn neither to Greeks nor to barbarians, but through her he turns them to the care of what comes after them and bids them direct the war of the world, and Plato, in the one speech of the maker to the young gods in the *Timaeus*, has joined the two speeches that Homer gave to Zeus (*On the Timaeus* III 200–201 Diehl). The maker's “turn yourselves” to the making of living things is close to Zeus' “go, until you come among the Trojans and the Achaeans” (20.24), which rouses the gods to the war of becoming (III 227 Diehl). The gods gather “within the house of Zeus” (20.13), Zeus divides them there into two ranks and rouses them to care for what comes after, while he himself stays in his own place: “So spoke the son of Cronus, and roused relentless war” (20.31; *On the Cratylus* 99; see [[zeus]]).

**The pairs.** In the essay on the poets' myths Proclus asks how “this so-called Theomachy, which Homer composed”, should be understood (*On the Republic* I 87 Kroll). There is no faction among the gods, he says, as the poet himself shows when he says that the blessed gods take their delight on Olympus all their days (*Odyssey* 6.46). War among the gods is a figure. Other poets set the gods at war after the oppositions that divide the divine kinds at the very first principles of all things, as when the Titans are said to fight Dionysus and the Giants Zeus; Homer sets at war not the highest gods but the orders that go out from them furthest, closest to the things they govern and so drawn into their oppositions, their wounds and blows (I 89–94 Kroll). Zeus himself stays apart, and the gods who remain “within Zeus” do not fight. Those who share in the war are eleven, after the army of gods and daemons that follows Zeus in eleven divisions in the *Phaedrus* (246e–247a), five who lead the better column, bound by the pentad, and six who care for things of matter, by the hexad (I 94 Kroll): the five who go to the ships and the six who go to the Trojans (20.33–40).

Then he reads the pairs (20.67–74). Poseidon against Apollo stands for the seeming opposition of all the universal things beneath the moon, and that is why these two gods never actually fight. Hera against Artemis is the division of souls, rational and irrational, Hera the cause of the better, Artemis bringing the worse to birth and into the light. Athena against Ares is the providence of mind against the providence of necessity in all the war of becoming: she presides over the opposites with intelligence, he strengthens their natural powers and rouses them against one another. Hermes against Leto is the difference between the movements of knowledge and of life in souls, the one perfecting knowledge, the other life. Hephaestus against the river Xanthus orders the opposite principles of bodily matter, he gathering the powers of heat and dryness, the river those of cold and wet, from which all becoming is made. And since all oppositions must end in agreement, Aphrodite is there too, making friendship among the opposed, though she fights on the side of the worse (I 94–95 Kroll; see [[aphrodite]]). For more, Proclus refers his readers to his teacher's solutions of the Homeric problems (I 95 Kroll).

In the poem three of the pairs come to blows, all in Book 21. Hephaestus' fire overcomes the river (21.328–382); when the other gods fight, Athena fells Ares, and Hera takes Artemis' bow from her shoulders and beats her about the ears with it, but Apollo declines to fight his father's brother, and Hermes will not fight Leto (21.391–501). Walter Leaf sees no principle in the pairing except in Hephaestus and the river, fire against water (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.67).

**Achilles and the river.** Proclus also defends Achilles' fight with the river in Book 21. He did not disobey the god himself, but fought the visible water that hindered his attack on his enemies, or one of the local powers, with Athena and Poseidon on his side; for the poem tells now of men's battles with one another, now of the higher kinds, “as in the so-called Theomachy”, and now of heroes standing against daemonic natures (*On the Republic* I 148–149 Kroll; see [[scamander]]).
