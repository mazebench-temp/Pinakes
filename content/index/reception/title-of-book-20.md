summary: Book 20 is traditionally called Θεομαχία, “the Battle of the Gods”. Plato already speaks of the battles of the gods that Homer composed, On the Sublime calls the passage “the Theomachy”, and Proclus “the so-called Theomachy”. The traditional title of Book 20 is Θεομαχία, “the Battle of the Gods”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter, this one is Υ, upsilon (see [[title-of-book-5]]). The episode is told under [[the-battle-of-the-gods]].

**The words.** The noun is not Homeric. It joins θεός, “god”, and μάχη, “battle”, and the poem says the same thing in its own words. When Zeus has thundered and Poseidon has shaken the earth, “so great was the din that rose as the gods came together in strife” (θεῶν ἔριδι ξυνιόντων, 20.66), and when the pairs have been set against one another, “So gods went to meet gods” (θεοὶ ἄντα θεῶν, 20.75). Poseidon uses the narrator's words when he refuses to begin: “I would not wish that we should drive the gods together in strife” (θεοὺς ἔριδι ξυνελάσσαι, 20.134; see [[eris]]). The end of 20.66 is also a verse-end of Hesiod's, at the height of the war of the gods against the Titans: τόσσος δοῦπος ἔγεντο θεῶν ἔριδι ξυνιόντων, “so great a din arose as the gods came together in strife” (*Theogony* 705; see [[hesiod]] and [[titans]]). In tragedy the verb θεομαχέω means something else, to fight against a god, as Pentheus does in Euripides' *Bacchae* (45, 325, 1255).

**Plato and the critics.** The word is old, though not as a title. Plato's Socrates will not have it told in his city that gods make war on gods and plot against them and fight, and he will not admit “Hera's bonds at the hands of her son, and the hurling of Hephaestus by his father when he was about to defend his mother from a beating, and all the battles of the gods that Homer has composed” (θεομαχίας ὅσας Ὅμηρος πεποίηκεν), whether they were composed with hidden meanings or without, for the young cannot tell what is a hidden meaning and what is not (*Republic* 2.378b–d; see [[plato-hephaestus-hurled]]). The plural takes in every battle of the gods in Homer.

The author of *On the Sublime* uses the singular for this episode. “Prodigious too are the images in the Theomachy”, he writes (τὰ ἐπὶ τῆς θεομαχίας φαντάσματα), and quotes the trumpet of the great heaven from Book 21 and Hades' fear from this book, 21.388 and 20.61–65; a little later he finds “far better than the passages on the Theomachy” those that show the divine as undefiled and great (*On the Sublime* 9.6, 9.8; see [[longinus-wounded-gods]] and [[longinus-poseidons-advance]]). Proclus, answering Plato, speaks of “this so-called Theomachy, which Homer composed” (τὴν λεγομένην ταύτην θεομαχίαν, *On the Republic* I 87 Kroll), and in another essay of the poem's battles of the higher kinds, “as in the so-called Theomachy” (ἐν τῇ καλουμένῃ θεομαχίᾳ, I 149 Kroll; see [[proclus-on-the-battle-of-the-gods]]). Both critics mean the battle that this book prepares and the next one fights. Where the name was first given to the book is not known. Aelian's list of the parts of Homer once sung on their own has no such name (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[aelian]] and [[title-of-book-16]]).

**What the title covers.** The battle is prepared at length and then put off. Zeus bids Themis call the gods to assembly, and not one of the rivers stays away but Oceanus (20.4–12; see [[themis-the-goddess]]). He will sit in a fold of Olympus and watch, and he lets the others go down and help either side, for he fears that Achilles alone may sack the wall against fate (20.20–30; see [[zeus]] and [[watching-the-war]]). Hera, Athena, Poseidon, Hermes and Hephaestus go to the ships; Ares, Apollo, Artemis, Leto, Xanthus and Aphrodite go to the Trojans (20.32–40). Strife rises, Athena and Ares shout, Zeus thunders from above and Poseidon shakes the earth from below, until Aidoneus leaps from his throne in fear that his dwellings will be laid bare (20.47–66; see [[poseidon]] and [[hades]]). The gods are set in pairs, Apollo against Poseidon, Athena against Enyalius, Artemis against Hera, Hermes against Leto, the river against Hephaestus (20.67–74).

Then nothing happens. Poseidon tells Hera that he would not drive the gods together in strife, “since we are far the stronger”, and that they should go and sit “off the path, at a lookout, and war will be the concern of men” (20.133–137). His party sits on the heaped-up wall of divine Heracles, wrapped in an unbreakable cloud, and the others on the brows of Callicolone, “but to begin harsh war / both sides were loath, though Zeus, sitting on high, bade them” (20.144–155; see [[the-wall-of-heracles]] and [[callicolone]]). The gods come to blows only in the second half of Book 21. Hephaestus' fire overcomes the river (21.328–382); then strife falls on the other gods, the wide earth rings, the great heaven blares like a trumpet around them, and Zeus laughs on Olympus as he watches the gods come together in strife (21.385–390). Athena fells Ares with a stone, Apollo will not fight his father's brother for the sake of mortals, Hera takes Artemis' bow from her shoulders and beats her about the ears with it, and Hermes declines to fight Leto (21.391–501). Walter Leaf reports that some have proposed to omit 20.66–74, “so that the actual conflict of the gods will be postponed to the Theomachy in the next book”, and answers that line 75 shows that the whole introduction to the book hangs together (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.55). He takes the assembly of the gods to belong to “the Theomachy at the end of Φ” (on 20.18).

**What the title leaves out.** Apart from these scenes the book belongs to men. Apollo, in the likeness of Lycaon, sends Aeneas against Achilles (20.79–111). The two meet between the armies. Achilles taunts him, Aeneas answers with his lineage from Dardanus, son of Zeus, and the spears and the stone follow, until Poseidon, though he favors the Achaeans, pities Aeneas, pours a mist over Achilles' eyes and sends Aeneas flying to the edge of the battle, for he is fated to escape and to rule the Trojans with his children's children (20.156–352; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]] and [[the-rule-of-aeneas]]). Achilles and Hector rally their men, and Apollo sends Hector back into the crowd (20.353–380). Then Achilles kills Iphition, Demoleon, Hippodamas and Priam's youngest son, Polydorus; Hector comes against him and Apollo snatches him away in thick mist; and Achilles rages through the Trojans like fire through the deep valleys of a dry mountain, while his horses trample the dead like oxen treading out barley (20.381–503; see [[achilles]] and [[the-aristeia]]). In these scenes the gods do what they do in every book: they rouse, warn and rescue.

**The older readers.** The ancient readers went first to the gods. Plato's *Republic* strikes out Hades' dwellings, “terrible and dank, which even the gods hate”, among the verses that make death fearful (*Republic* 3.386d; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]), and his *Cratylus* asks why the river “whom the gods call Xanthus, and men Scamander” is more rightly called Xanthus (*Cratylus* 391e; see [[plato-chalcis-and-cymindis]]). *On the Sublime* quotes Hades' fear for its terror, and Proclus reads the pairs of gods as figures of the oppositions of which the world is made (see [[proclus-on-the-battle-of-the-gods]]). The rest of the book had readers of its own. Plato finds in Aeneas' genealogy the time when men lived on the slopes of Ida, before Ilios was built in the plain (*Laws* 3.681e–682b; see [[plato-the-slopes-of-ida]]). Strabo argues from Poseidon's prophecy that Homer keeps Aeneas in Troy, against the stories of his wanderings, and reports a reading that made it a prophecy of Rome (*Geography* 13.1.53; see [[strabo-on-the-rule-of-aeneas]]); Virgil turned it into Apollo's oracle to the Trojans at Delos (*Aeneid* 3.94–98; see [[virgil-the-house-of-aeneas]]). Aristotle explains how Ganymede can pour wine for Zeus, and in how many senses Aeneas' spear was stopped in the shield (*Poetics* 25, 1461a29–35; see [[aristotle-wine-of-the-gods]] and [[the-bronze-spear-stopped]]); Cicero would not listen to the story of Ganymede at all, as one of Homer's inventions that gave the gods what is human (*Tusculan Disputations* 1.65; see [[cicero]]). Strabo reports that the bull dragged around the Heliconian lord was taken as proof that the poet lived after the Ionian migration (*Geography* 8.7.2; see [[helice-or-the-panionia]]), and that some added the verse on Hyde to the Catalogue (*Geography* 13.4.6; see [[hyle-or-hyde]]).
