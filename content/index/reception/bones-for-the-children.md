**The lines.** Nestor's proposal on the evening of the duel is a single plan. The fighting is to stop at dawn; the Achaeans are to gather their dead on wagons drawn by oxen and mules and burn them near the ships, so that each man may take the bones home to the children of the dead; they are to heap a single tomb around the pyre, and against it build high towers with gates, and dig a ditch (7.327–343; see [[nestor]]). The bones do not go home. When the work is done, a chosen band raises the mound over the place of burning, “a single tomb, bringing earth from the plain, one for all alike” (7.435–436; see [[tomb]] and [[cremation]]).

**The objection.** Some ancient critics rejected the two lines. The plan to take the bones home sits badly with the single tomb heaped at once over the pyre, and elsewhere the Iliad leaves its dead at Troy. Agamemnon, fearing that Menelaus will die of his wound, says that “the earth will rot your bones as you lie in Troy” (4.174–175). The ghost of Patroclus asks that his bones and Achilles' lie together in one urn, and Achilles has a mound raised for him which the Achaeans are to make broad and high when he too has died (23.83–92, 23.243–248). In the Odyssey the bones of both lie under a great mound on the Hellespont, and Nestor tells Telemachus how the best of the Achaeans, Ajax, Achilles, Patroclus and his own son Antilochus, lie at Troy (*Odyssey* 24.76–84, 3.108–112).

**The Athenian custom.** Classical Athens did bring its war dead home. Thucydides describes the public funeral held in the first winter of the Peloponnesian War. The bones of the fallen were laid out in a tent, carried on wagons in cypress coffins, one for each tribe, with an empty bier for the missing, and buried in the public tomb outside the city; only the dead of Marathon, for their outstanding valor, had been buried where they fell (2.34). Aeschylus' chorus in the *Agamemnon* knows both ends of a war at Troy. Ares, “the gold-changer of bodies”, sends home to the families, in place of men, urns filled with ash, while others lie in graves around the wall of Troy, held by the enemy land (*Agamemnon* 437–455). Whether the two lines of Book 7 were added under the influence of such a custom, or keep an older practice that the poet mentions and then leaves aside, cannot be decided.

**Reading the lines with the rest.** They can also stand as they are. The mound is heaped “around the pyre” (ἀμφὶ πυρήν, 7.336; ἀμφʼ αὐτήν, 7.435), and a single tomb over the place of burning need not rule out bones gathered to be carried home. Nestor speaks of what will happen “whenever we return again to our native land” (7.335), a return that the Iliad does not reach. Agamemnon gives the truce on the plainest ground, that the dead are owed their fire (7.408–410; see [[the-truce-for-the-dead]]).
