Mynniscus of Chalcis acted for Aeschylus (see [[mynniscus]]); Callippides was the star of the next generation, famous for making audiences weep and, his critics said, for vulgar realism (see [[callippides]]). The ape was the proverbial mimic, and its name a common insult for a cheap imitator (see [[ape]]). The same opinion was held of the actor Pindarus (see [[pindarus-the-actor]]). Aristotle reports the jibe as part of the case against tragedy: as the later actors stand to the earlier, so the whole art of tragedy stands to epic, a vulgar art for inferior spectators. His answer is that the fault belongs to the performers, not to the art, and that Callippides was faulted not for movement as such but for imitating women who are not free (1462a5–13; see [[epic-and-tragedy]] and [[hypokrites]]).
