**Homer.** Poseidon yokes his bronze-footed, golden-maned horses at Aegae, puts on gold, takes his golden whip and drives over the waves. The sea beasts frolic around him out of their lairs, knowing their lord, the sea parts for joy, and the horses fly so fast that the bronze axle is not wetted (13.23–31; see [[poseidon]] and [[chariot-journey]]). He is going to war, in secret, against his brother's will (13.15–16, 13.351–357). Virgil uses the scene twice, and both times Neptune calms the sea instead of crossing it to fight.

**The storm.** In the first book of the *Aeneid* Juno has Aeolus let the winds loose on Aeneas' fleet. Neptune feels the sea in turmoil and, deeply stirred, lifts his calm head above the waves; he sees the Trojans overwhelmed, “nor did the tricks and the anger of Juno escape her brother” (*Aeneid* 1.124–130). He rebukes the winds, *Quos ego*, “Whom I —!”, breaks off, and sends them to tell their king that the rule of the sea and the trident were given by lot not to him but to Neptune (*Aeneid* 1.132–141). Then he calms the swollen waters, drives off the clouds and brings back the sun. Cymothoe and Triton push the ships off the rock, he lifts them with his trident, opens the sandbanks, *atque rotis summas levibus perlabitur undas*, “and glides over the tops of the waves on light wheels” (*Aeneid* 1.142–147). A simile follows, of a man of weight and merit who quiets a rioting crowd with his words; so the whole roar of the sea falls, and the father, looking out over the waters and carried under a clear sky, turns his horses and, flying, gives the reins to his willing chariot (*Aeneid* 1.148–156).

**The calm sea.** In the fifth book Venus asks Neptune to give her son safe passage to Italy. He promises it, at the price of one life, and then *iungit equos auro genitor*, “the father yokes his horses with gold”; he puts foaming bits on the wild creatures and lets the reins run free. In his sea-blue chariot he flies lightly over the surface of the water; the waves sink down, and under the thundering axle the swollen sea is laid level, and the clouds flee the sky. Then come the many shapes of his companions, *immania cete*, “huge sea beasts”, the aged band of Glaucus, Palaemon son of Ino, the swift Tritons and all the host of Phorcus, and on the left Thetis and Melite and the maiden Panopea, Nisaee, Spio, Thalia and Cymodoce (*Aeneid* 5.816–826).

**What Virgil kept.** The gold (13.24–26), the chariot flying over the surface of the sea (13.27–29), the axle (13.30) and the creatures of the sea who come out to their lord (13.27–28) are Homer's, and *cete* is Homer's word, κήτεα, in Latin letters. Homer's sea parts to let the god through; Virgil's lies down under his axle. Homer's frolicking beasts become a procession of named sea gods and Nereids, among them Thetis, whom the Iliad never shows in Poseidon's train. And the god's errand is reversed. Homer's Poseidon deceives his brother by helping the Achaeans in secret; Virgil's Neptune finds out his sister's deceit and undoes it. His claim to the sea, *mihi sorte datum*, “given by lot to me”, is the claim of Homer's Poseidon in Book 15, when he tells Iris that the three sons of Cronus divided the world by lot and that he obtained the gray sea to dwell in forever (15.187–193; see [[lots]]).
