summary: The Shield of Heracles, handed down under Hesiod's name, opens with the night on which Zeus and Amphitryon both lay with Alcmene, the begetting that Agamemnon's story of Heracles' birth passes over, and has Heracles blame Zeus for the folly that sent his twin brother to serve Eurystheus, in the words of Agamemnon's apology. It also shares with Book 19 the verses with which a hero puts on the gifts of Hephaestus, and the gnashing of teeth that Aristarchus thought unworthy of Achilles.

**The begetting.** Agamemnon's story begins on the day when mighty Heracles “was to be born to Alcmene in well-crowned Thebes” (19.99), and tells not the birth but the trick that made Eurystheus lord of the Argives (19.95–133; see [[the-birth-of-heracles]]). The *Shield of Heracles*, a short epic handed down among the works of Hesiod (see [[shield-of-heracles]] and [[hesiod]]), opens with what went before. It begins “Or like her who” (ἢ οἵη), in the manner of a catalogue of women, and tells of Alcmene, daughter of Electryon, who surpassed all women in beauty and stature and came to Thebes with Amphitryon after he had killed her father in a quarrel over cattle. He might not enter her bed until he had avenged her brothers on the Taphians and Teleboans, and the gods were witnesses (*Shield* 1–22). The father of gods and men wove another plan, to beget a defender against ruin for gods and for men who eat bread. He came down from Olympus by night, plotting a trick, and lay with her; and on the same night Amphitryon came home from his great task and lay with her too (27–47). “Yielding to a god and to a man far the best”, she bore twin sons in seven-gated Thebes, not alike in mind, the lesser, Iphicles, to Amphitryon, and the far greater, βίην Ἡρακληείην, “mighty Heracles”, to the son of Cronus (48–56; see [[alcmene]], [[amphitryon]] and [[heracles]]). The name in the accusative is the Iliad's phrase, as in Agamemnon's story (19.98; see [[might-of-heracles]]).

**The folly of Iphicles.** Before the fight with Cycnus, Heracles tells his charioteer Iolaus, the son of Iphicles, how Amphitryon came as a suppliant from Tiryns to “well-crowned Thebes” (ἐυστέφανον ποτὶ Θήβην, 80), and how the two brothers were born unlike in body and mind. Of his brother he says, τοῦ μὲν φρένας ἐξέλετο Ζεύς, “Zeus took away his wits”: Iphicles left his home and his parents to honor Eurystheus, the wretch, and surely groaned for it often afterward, bearing his own blind folly (ἄτη), which cannot be taken back, “but on me a god laid hard labors” (89–94). The half-verse is Agamemnon's of himself: “But since I was blinded, and Zeus took away my wits” (καί μευ φρένας ἐξέλετο Ζεύς, 19.137; see [[ate]] and [[phrenes]]). In Agamemnon's story it is Zeus who groans, whenever he sees his son doing unseemly work “under the labors that Eurystheus set him” (19.132–133; see [[eurystheus]]). The manuscripts of the *Shield* call Eurystheus ἀλιτήμενος, “sinful” (91); Walter Leaf reports that Bentley changed the word to ἠλιτόμηνος, “short of his months”, the Iliad's word for the child Hera brought out before his time (19.118; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.118). Leaf also notes that well-crowned is said of a city only once in Homer, of Thebes at 19.99, and that the *Shield* and the *Theogony* give the epithet to Thebes too (on 19.99; *Theogony* 978; see [[well-crowned]] and [[thebes]]).

**The arming.** When Heracles arms, he puts around his shins greaves of shining mountain-copper, “the glorious gifts of Hephaestus”, and then a golden corselet, the gift of Athena (122–127), in the verses of the Iliad's arming scenes: κνημῖδας … περὶ κνήμῃσιν ἔθηκεν, δεύτερον αὖ θώρηκα περὶ στήθεσσιν ἔδυνε. Achilles puts on the gifts of Hephaestus in this book in the same words: “First he put the greaves around his shins, / … / second, he put on the corselet around his chest” (19.369–371; see [[arming-scene]] and [[the-making-of-the-arms]]). Then Heracles takes his sword, his quiver, his spear and his helmet, and last the shield (128–139); Achilles takes sword, shield, helmet and spear (19.372–391).

**The teeth.** As Achilles arms, “there was a gnashing of his teeth, and his two eyes / shone like the blaze of fire” (19.365–366; see [[eyes-like-fire]]). Aristarchus athetized the four lines, finding the gnashing grotesque (see the translation's note on 19.365–368). The *Shield* has the same pictures on the shield of Heracles. In its middle is Fear, staring back with eyes that blaze with fire, and his mouth is full of white teeth (τοῦ καὶ ὀδόντων μὲν πλῆτο στόμα, 144–146). Around him are the heads of twelve terrible snakes, “and there was a gnashing of their teeth whenever the son of Amphitryon fought” (τῶν καὶ ὀδόντων μὲν καναχὴ πέλεν, εὖτε μάχοιτο, 161–165), the half-verse of 19.365, τοῦ καὶ ὀδόντων μὲν καναχὴ πέλε. In the *Shield* the gnashing and the fiery eyes belong to the terrors on the shield; in the Iliad to the man who carries it.
