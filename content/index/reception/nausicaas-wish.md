**The verses.** Odysseus has bathed apart from the girls, and Athena has made him taller and stouter and poured grace on his head and shoulders; he sits apart on the shore, “shining with beauty and grace; and the girl gazed at him” (*Odyssey* 6.229–237; see [[gold-poured-around-silver]]). Nausicaa speaks to her handmaids, not to him. The man who “seemed to me unsightly” is now “like the gods who hold the wide sky”, and she wishes: “Would that such a man might be called my husband, / living here, and that it might please him to stay here” (*Odyssey* 6.242–245). Then, in the verse with which she first told them to feed him, she tells them to give the stranger food and drink (*Odyssey* 6.246 = 6.209; see [[nausicaa]]).

**Aristarchus and Alcman.** The scholia report that Aristarchus athetized both verses, but hesitated over the first, “since Alcman too adapted it, bringing in maidens who say ‘Father Zeus, would that he were my husband’” (Ζεῦ πάτερ, αἲ γὰρ ἐμὸς πόσις εἴη; the scholia, in Merry's critical note on *Odyssey* 6.244–245; see [[aristarchus]]). For the scholium's μετέβαλε, “adapted”, Merry reports Dindorf's μετέλαβε, “took it over for his own”. The Spartan poet's maidens wish for a husband in Nausicaa's words, and the scholium's “since” makes the borrowing a witness that the first verse was old. Merry gives the judgment more simply: Aristarchus obelizes both verses, “but is not indisposed to let the former stand” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 6.244).

**Plutarch and Ephorus.** The verses, Merry says, “incurred the disapproval of some of the older critics”. Plutarch, in his essay on how the young man should listen to poetry, was offended by them, and Merry quotes his words, that her boldness and her licentiousness are to be blamed; he gives no more of the passage. A scholium reports the opposite judgment of the historian Ephorus, who praised the speech as coming from a soul well formed by nature for virtue; the scholiast himself took it rather as a mark of the luxurious life of the Phaeacians (Merry on *Odyssey* 6.244, quoting the scholium; see [[plutarch]] and [[phaeacians]]).

**Merry's judgment.** “Modern feeling”, Merry writes, “will doubtless side with Ephorus, regarding the words as expressing the thoughts of an innocent girl, to whom marriage was a certainty and not a mere possibility”. She has no hearers but her own handmaids, for Odysseus sits apart (*Odyssey* 6.236), and before men she is a different girl: even to her father she was ashamed to name her marriage (*Odyssey* 6.66; Merry on *Odyssey* 6.244). The book has spoken of her marriage from its first scene, when Athena told her in the dream that she would not be a maiden much longer, “for already the best men in the land are wooing you” (*Odyssey* 6.33–34; see [[marriage]]), and Odysseus has already called most blessed the man who will lead her home (*Odyssey* 6.158–159).

**The verses in the book.** The wish is answered within the book by its opposite. The gossip that Nausicaa fears on the road to the city puts the same thought in the mouth of a baser man: “He will be her own husband, surely” (*Odyssey* 6.277), and she would be indignant with a girl who mingled with men “before she came openly to her marriage” (*Odyssey* 6.288; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]]); the scholia report that those fourteen verses too were athetized as unfitting to the speaker (see the translation's note on 6.275–288). In the next book her father wishes the same, that such a man as Odysseus might have his daughter and be called his son-in-law, staying there (*Odyssey* 7.311–315, a plain rendering; see [[alcinous]] and [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]]). The marriage is never made. See also the translation's note on 6.244–245.
