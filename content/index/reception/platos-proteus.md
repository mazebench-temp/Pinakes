summary: Plato's Socrates three times makes Homer's Proteus the image of a speaker who slips away from the question. The brothers of the Euthydemus play “Proteus the Egyptian sophist”, and Socrates will hold them as Menelaus held him; Euthyphro is not to be let go, like Proteus, until he tells; Ion, like Proteus, takes every shape until he escapes as a general. In the Republic no poet may tell lies about Proteus, since a god keeps his own form, and Proclus answers that Proteus, an intellect full of forms, only seems to change to the souls that look at him.

**Homer.** Eidothea tells Menelaus that her father, “immortal Proteus of Egypt, who knows the depths / of the whole sea, the servant of Poseidon” (*Odyssey* 4.385–386), will try to escape “by becoming all the things that upon the earth / creep, and water, and wondrous fire”, and must be held fast until he speaks in his own shape (*Odyssey* 4.417–424). Seized among his seals, he becomes a bearded lion, a serpent, a leopard, a boar, flowing water and a tree, and only when he is weary does he question his captor and answer him (*Odyssey* 4.454–480; see [[proteus]] and [[eidothea]]). Plato took three things from the scene: the shapes, the hold, and the Egyptian.

**The Euthydemus.** The brothers Euthydemus and Dionysodorus have answered every question with a trick, and Ctesippus is ready to abuse them. Socrates calms him: the strangers' wisdom is marvelous, “but they are not willing to show it to us in earnest; they are imitating Proteus, the Egyptian sophist, and bewitching us (τὸν Πρωτέα μιμεῖσθον τὸν Αἰγύπτιον σοφιστὴν γοητεύοντε ἡμᾶς). So let us imitate Menelaus, and not let the two men go until they show us what they are in earnest about” (*Euthydemus* 288b–c, a plain rendering). They must be begged, coaxed and prayed to appear. The “Egyptian sophist” is Homer's Πρωτεὺς Αἰγύπτιος (*Odyssey* 4.385), and the sophist's tricks are his shapes.

**The Euthyphro.** At the end of the dialogue, with the definition of piety still not found, Socrates turns to Euthyphro: “you know it, if any man does, and you are not to be let go, like Proteus, until you tell” (οὐκ ἀφετέος εἶ ὥσπερ ὁ Πρωτεὺς πρὶν ἂν εἴπῃς, *Euthyphro* 15d). In Homer the hold is loosed only when the Old Man speaks: “then stop your force and set the old man free” (*Odyssey* 4.422). Euthyphro does not tell; he says he is in a hurry and goes (15e).

**The Ion.** Ion has promised to show what he knows about Homer, and ends by claiming to be the best general in Greece as well as the best rhapsode. Socrates: “you simply become every kind of thing, like Proteus, twisting up and down, until at last you slip away from me and appear as a general, so as not to show me how clever you are in your wisdom about Homer” (*Ion* 541e–542a, a plain rendering). The rhapsode's last shape is the one he has learned, he says, “from Homer” (541b).

**The Republic.** Among the rules for poets in an ideal city, Socrates lays down that a god, being the best, could change only for the worse, and so “remains always simply in his own shape” (*Republic* 2.381c). Let no poet tell, then, that gods in the likeness of strangers go about the cities, “nor let anyone tell lies about Proteus and Thetis” (μηδὲ Πρωτέως καὶ Θέτιδος καταψευδέσθω μηδείς, 381d). Nor may the gods, keeping their own form, make us think that they appear in all kinds of shapes, “deceiving and bewitching” us (381e), for no god would wish to lie, in word or by holding out a phantom (382a). The verb is the one Socrates used of the brothers' Proteus in the *Euthydemus*. The ban covers Athena in the likeness of Mentor as well (see [[athena-as-mentor-in-later-writers]]).

**Proclus' answer.** Proclus, defending Homer against the *Republic*, finds three ways in which the poets give changes to beings that do not change. In the first the change is in those who see the god: the simple appears manifold to them, though the gods neither change nor mean to deceive (*On the Republic* I 111–112 Kroll). The second is Proteus. A divine being of many powers, full of every kind of form, holds out different sights to those who look at it. Proteus is lower than the first gods, “immortal, but not yet a god”, the servant of Poseidon (ὑποδμώς, *Odyssey* 4.386), and not yet given a ruling rank, but “an angelic intellect in the lot of Poseidon”, holding in himself the forms of all things that come to be. Under him is Eidothea, a daemonic soul joined to him, who fastens her own thoughts on his forms; then a number of rational souls, whom the myth calls seals. He is said to count them because they are eternal, for the number of things that come to be and perish is unlimited (*Odyssey* 4.411–412). The souls that look at him attach themselves now to one of his forms, now to another, and take the passing of their own thoughts for a change in what they think, “and so he seems to those who hold him to become ‘all the things that upon the earth / creep, and water, and wondrous fire’” (*On the Republic* I 112–113 Kroll, quoting *Odyssey* 4.417–418; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). The third way, a god coming down through the orders below it, is the one Proclus finds in Athena likened to Mentor.
