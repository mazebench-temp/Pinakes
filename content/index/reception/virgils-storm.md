summary: The storm that opens the Aeneid follows Poseidon's storm in the fifth book of the Odyssey. The winds fall on the sea together and roll great waves, clouds snatch away the sky and night lies on the sea; Aeneas' limbs are loosened with cold, and he cries that those who died beneath the walls of Troy were three and four times blessed, as Odysseus does on his raft. Then a wave strikes from above, a helmsman is thrown out headlong, a ship is whirled round, and the survivors come ashore with great love of the land.

**Homer.** Poseidon, coming back from the Ethiopians, sees Odysseus on his raft within sight of Scheria and raises the storm. He gathers the clouds, takes his trident, rouses the blasts of all the winds and covers land and sea with clouds, “and night arose from the sky”; the East Wind, the South Wind, the West Wind and the North Wind clash together, “rolling up a great wave” (*Odyssey* 5.282–296; see [[poseidon]]). Odysseus' knees and heart are loosened, and he speaks to his own heart: Calypso's warning is coming true, Zeus has thrown the sea into confusion, and sheer destruction is sure (*Odyssey* 5.297–305). Then he calls the Danaans who died at Troy three times blessed, and four times, and wishes he had died on the day when the Trojans hurled their spears at him around the dead son of Peleus: he would have had his funeral rites, and the Achaeans would have spread his fame (*Odyssey* 5.306–312; see [[the-fight-over-the-body-of-achilles]]). As he speaks, a great wave strikes him from above and whirls the raft around; he falls far from it, the steering oar flies from his hands, the mast breaks, and the sail and the yard fall into the sea (*Odyssey* 5.313–318). A second wave scatters the beams of the raft (*Odyssey* 5.365–370).

**Virgil.** At Juno's request Aeolus strikes the hollow mountain with his spear, and the winds rush out where a gate is given and blow over the lands in a whirlwind (*Aeneid* 1.81–83; see [[virgil-juno-and-aeolus]]). “They fell upon the sea, and the East Wind and the South Wind together, and the South-West Wind thick with squalls, upheave it all from its lowest depths and roll vast waves to the shores” (*una Eurusque Notusque ruunt creberque procellis / Africus, et vastos volvunt ad litora fluctus*, *Aeneid* 1.84–86; plain renderings throughout). The shouting of men and the creaking of the rigging follow; “suddenly the clouds snatch away the sky and the day from the Trojans' eyes; black night lies on the sea” (*Aeneid* 1.87–89); the poles thunder, the upper air flashes with fire, and everything threatens the men with present death (*Aeneid* 1.90–91). “At once Aeneas' limbs are loosened with cold” (*Extemplo Aeneae solvuntur frigore membra*, *Aeneid* 1.92). He groans, stretches both his hands to the stars, and cries: “O three and four times blessed (*O terque quaterque beati*), whose lot it was to die before their fathers' eyes beneath the high walls of Troy!” He wishes he had fallen on the plains of Ilium at the hand of Diomedes, “where fierce Hector lies by the spear of Achilles, where huge Sarpedon lies, where the Simois rolls beneath its waves so many shields and helmets of men, and brave bodies, caught in its stream” (*Aeneid* 1.93–101; see [[virgils-diomedes]]). As he flings out these words, a squall howling from the north strikes the sail full on and lifts the waves to the stars; the oars break, the prow swings round and gives its side to the waves, and a sheer mountain of water follows (*Aeneid* 1.102–105). The South Wind drives three ships onto hidden rocks, the East Wind three onto the shoals of the Syrtes (*Aeneid* 1.108–112). Before Aeneas' eyes a huge sea strikes from above on the stern of the ship that carried the Lycians and faithful Orontes: the helmsman is shaken out and rolled headlong, and the wave whirls the ship three times round on the spot until the eddy swallows it (*Aeneid* 1.113–117). “Here and there men appear swimming in the vast gulf” (*Adparent rari nantes in gurgite vasto*), with arms, planks and the treasure of Troy on the waves, and the other ships take in the hostile water as their joints loosen (*Aeneid* 1.118–123), until Neptune calms the sea (*Aeneid* 1.124–156; see [[virgil-neptunes-chariot]]).

**What Virgil kept.** The winds fall on the sea together, and Virgil's Eurus and Notus are Homer's East and South Winds (Εὖρος, Νότος, *Odyssey* 5.295); his third is Africus, the South-West Wind, where Homer has the West Wind, and the North Wind comes in a few lines later, as Aquilo (*Aeneid* 1.85–86, 1.102). They roll vast waves, *volvunt fluctus*, as Homer's winds roll up a great wave, μέγα κῦμα κυλίνδων (*Odyssey* 5.296). Clouds and night cover the sea in both (*Odyssey* 5.293–294). The hero's collapse is the same, λύτο γούνατα, “his knees were loosened”, and *solvuntur membra*, “his limbs are loosened”, with Virgil's cold added (*Odyssey* 5.297; *Aeneid* 1.92). The cry keeps Homer's numbers, “three times … and four times”, τρὶς … καὶ τετράκις, *terque quaterque*; but Homer's blessed are the Danaans, the enemy, and Virgil's are the Trojans who died “before their fathers' eyes” beneath the walls. Odysseus wishes he had died in the fight around Achilles' body; Aeneas wishes for the death he nearly had at the hands of Diomedes, who crushed his hip with a stone in *Iliad* 5 (5.297–318; see [[aeneas]]). Odysseus thinks of the funeral and the fame that the Achaeans would have given him; Aeneas of the plain where Hector and Sarpedon lie, and of the Simois, the river where, in the Iliad, “many oxhide shields and helmets / had fallen in the dust” (12.22–23; see [[simoeis]]). The wave that strikes “from above”, κατʼ ἄκρης, *a vertice* (Merry sets the two side by side: Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 5.313), the man thrown from his craft and the craft whirled round come from the moment after Odysseus' cry (*Odyssey* 5.313–315; *Aeneid* 1.114–117), and Virgil's ships whose joints loosen in the sea recall Odysseus' resolve to stay on his raft “as long as the beams hold together in their fastenings” (*Odyssey* 5.361). The gods are rearranged. In Homer the sea god raises the storm in his own anger, and Athena binds up the other winds (*Odyssey* 5.382–387); in Virgil Juno has the keeper of the winds raise it, and the sea god, angry that it was raised without him, calms it.

**The landing.** The Trojans make for the nearest shore, in Libya, and Aeneas brings seven ships into a harbor sheltered by an island, under a cave of fresh water and seats of living rock, “the home of the nymphs”, where no cables hold the weary ships and no anchor (*Aeneid* 1.157–171). It has the features of the harbor of Phorcys in Ithaca, where ships stay without moorings and the cave of the nymphs called Naiads has its ever-flowing waters (*Odyssey* 13.96–112, plain renderings), and where the Phaeacians will set Odysseus ashore. “And with great love of the land the Trojans disembark, take possession of the longed-for sand, and lay their limbs dripping with brine upon the shore” (*magno telluris amore / egressi optata potiuntur Troes harena / et sale tabentis artus in litore ponunt*, *Aeneid* 1.171–173). Odysseus, swollen and spewing sea water, lies breathless at the river's mouth, and then, withdrawing from the river, kisses the grain-giving earth (*Odyssey* 5.453–463).
