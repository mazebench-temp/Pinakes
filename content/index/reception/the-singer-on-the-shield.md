**The text.** The dance on the Shield ends with a throng around the dancers, a singer, and two tumblers who whirl in the midst, “leading the song and dance” (18.603–606; see [[dance]]). The words for the singer, μετὰ δέ σφιν ἐμέλπετο θεῖος ἀοιδὸς / φορμίζων, “and among them a divine singer sang / to the lyre”, were inserted here by editors from Wolf on, on the authority of Athenaeus (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.604). The same words stand in the Odyssey, at the double wedding in Menelaus' palace: the neighbors and kinsmen feast, a divine singer sings to the lyre, and two tumblers whirl in the midst μολπῆς ἐξάρχοντος, “as he leads the song and dance” (*Odyssey* 4.15–19). The Iliad's participle is plural, ἐξάρχοντες, and belongs to the tumblers; the Odyssey's is singular, and belongs to the singer.

**Athenaeus.** In the fifth book of the *Deipnosophistae*, on the feasts of Homer, Athenaeus argues that the poet gave music and dancing at table to the suitors and the Phaeacians, not to Nestor or Menelaus. The lines in Menelaus' palace, he says, were added by Aristarchus and his school, who did not see that the wedding feast was over; they were “transferred from the Hoplopoiia, with the very error of diction”. For the tumblers did not lead; they danced while the singer led, since to lead off belongs to the lyre. So in the *Shield* of Hesiod the goddesses, the Muses, “led the song” (*Shield* 205–206; see [[shield-of-heracles]]), and Archilochus was “himself leading off the Lesbian paean to the pipe”. He mocks Diodorus, a follower of Aristophanes, who would write καθʼ αὑτούς with a rough breathing and so commit a solecism (*Deipnosophistae* 5.180c–f). The performers at Menelaus' sober feast, he repeats, are an interpolation, taken from the Cretan dance of which the poet speaks “in the Hoplopoiia” (*Deipnosophistae* 5.180f); and he quotes that dance with the singer in it (18.590–594, 18.603–606). Dancing and tumbling were native to the Cretans: so Aeneas calls the Cretan Meriones a dancer (16.617–618), and dance songs are called Cretan. Aristarchus, he charges, not only put lines into Menelaus' feast that did not belong there, but took the singer out of the Cretan dance, cutting the verses down so that the throng stands around the dance “delighting in it; and two tumblers among them …”, “so that ἐξάρχοντες becomes altogether incurable, since it can no longer be referred to the singer” (*Deipnosophistae* 5.180f–181c; see [[athenaeus]] and [[aristarchus]]).

**Lucian and Leaf.** Lucian, in his dialogue *On the Dance*, passes over what Homer made on the shield about Ariadne and the dance Daedalus fashioned for her, “as you have read it”, and “the two dancers whom the poet there calls tumblers, leading the dance” (*On the Dance* 13; see [[the-dancing-floor-of-ariadne]]). Leaf cites Lucian as a reader of ἐξάρχοντες. He reports that Ludwich showed Athenaeus' evidence to be worthless: it contradicts all that is known of Aristarchus' method and of the slight effect his judgments had on the text. The line may have stood in some of the old erratic copies that antiquarians like Athenaeus favored, but there is no ground for thinking it ever stood in a recognized text, and Didymus knows nothing of it. Those who print the singer must change ἐξάρχοντες to ἐξάρχοντος, a genitive absolute without a noun that Leaf finds very doubtful in Homer. As the text stands it is quite intelligible to him: the two tumblers are leaders, perhaps professional performers, to whose act the young men and women dance (on 18.604).

**The printed text.** The text printed here has the singer and ἐξάρχοντες both, so that the tumblers lead the song and dance; the English renders it so (see the translation's note on 18.604–606). On the Shield's dance and its Cretan character see [[the-dancing-floor-of-ariadne]] and [[pausanias-the-dance-of-ariadne]].
