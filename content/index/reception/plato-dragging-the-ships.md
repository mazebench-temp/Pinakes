**Homer.** The wall is down and the Trojans are fighting at the ships. Agamemnon proposes to drag the row of ships nearest the sea into the water, moor them out until night, and then launch all the rest: “For there is no blame in fleeing from evil, not even by night. / Better the man who flees and escapes evil than the man who is caught” (14.74–81; see [[agamemnon]], [[ships]] and [[no-blame-in-fleeing]]). Odysseus answers with a rebuke. No man who knew how to speak fitting words and bore a scepter would let such a word pass his lips, and the army would look away from the battle as soon as the ships were dragged down (14.83–102; see [[odysseus]]). Agamemnon gives way (14.104–108).

**Plato.** In the fourth book of the *Laws* the Athenian Stranger asks where the new city that the Cretans are founding will stand. About eighty stades from the sea, Clinias answers, with good harbors (704b). The sea, the Athenian says, is a pleasant neighbor from day to day, but in truth a salty and bitter one, filling a land with trade and retail and breeding shifty and faithless characters (705a). It is well, too, that the country has no fir or pine fit for shipbuilding, so that the city cannot easily imitate its enemies in base imitations (705c). Minos, master of the sea, once forced the people of Attica to pay a hard tribute, and they had no warships to resist him. Better for them, says the Athenian, to have lost the seven children many times over than to have turned from steadfast hoplites into fighters from ships, used to leaping ashore and running back to their ships, thinking it no shame not to dare to die where they stand, and finding ready excuses for throwing away their arms and for certain flights that are “not shameful, as they say” (706a–c). Such phrases come from soldiering at sea. Then: “One could learn this from Homer too, that such a practice was not good. For Odysseus reviles Agamemnon, when the Achaeans were then hard pressed in battle by the Trojans, for ordering the ships to be dragged down to the sea” (706d), and he quotes the verses (706e–707a). Homer too knew, he concludes, that triremes standing by at sea are a bad thing for hoplites in battle; with such habits even lions would learn to flee from deer (707a). A naval power, moreover, cannot give its honors rightly, since its safety comes from steersmen, captains and rowers, men of every sort. Clinias objects that the Cretans say the sea fight at Salamis saved Greece; the Athenian answers that the land battles of Marathon and Plataea began and finished that salvation and made the Greeks better, and the sea fights at Salamis and Artemisium did not (707a–d).

**The text.** Plato's verses differ from ours in four places. He has ἕλκειν for ἑλκέμεν, “to drag” (14.97). Where our text says that the Trojans may have their prayers answered “winning as they are already” (ἐπικρατέουσί περ, 14.98), his says “eager as they are” (ἐελδομένοισί περ). For πόλεμον, “will not hold out in the war” (14.100), he has the genitive πολέμου, and where Odysseus ends “leader of the people” (ὄρχαμε λαῶν, 14.102), the *Laws* end “such things do you propose” (οἷʼ ἀγορεύεις). The sense does not change.

**The reading.** Odysseus' argument is about this battle: an army that sees its ships launched will stop fighting. Plato makes it a lesson about habits, and about cities. The ships behind an army teach it to run, and the flights that the Stranger hears called “not shameful” are the doctrine of the maxim with which Agamemnon closed his proposal, that there is no blame in fleeing from evil (14.80). Plato quotes only the rebuke; the maxim it answers is left unspoken.
