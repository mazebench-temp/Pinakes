The first and greatest source of the sublime, says the author of *On the Sublime*, is the power of forming great conceptions. He puts it in a phrase: “sublimity is the echo of a great mind” (*On the Sublime* 9.2). Homer is his chief witness. Among his examples is a single line of Book 4, Strife who “plants her head against the sky and walks upon the earth” (4.443). One might say, he comments, that this is the measure not so much of Strife as of Homer (9.4).

The point is that the size of the image is the size of the mind that conceived it. Strife is small when she first rears her head, and then she spans the whole distance between the earth and the sky. Only a poet who thinks on that scale could give her that stature. Later in the same chapter the author turns to the battle of the gods in Books 20 and 21, and to Ajax's prayer to Zeus in Book 17, to die in the light if he must die.

Homer's lines on Strife's growth had a long life. Virgil gave the same growth to his Rumor (see [[virgil-rumor]]).
