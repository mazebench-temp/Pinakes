The *Trojan Women* was staged in 415 BCE. Troy has fallen, and its women wait to be shared out among the victors. Menelaus arrives to fetch Helen. He came to Troy, he says, to punish the man who deceived his hospitality, and the army has given him Helen to kill, or to take home to be killed in Argos. Hecuba asks him to let Helen speak and to let her answer, and the scene becomes a trial with Menelaus as judge.

Helen blames others (914–965): Hecuba, for bearing Paris; Priam, for not killing the child foretold as a firebrand; the three goddesses, whose contest made her Aphrodite's prize; and Menelaus, who sailed off to Crete and left her alone with his guest. "Punish the goddess," she says, and be stronger than Zeus, who rules the other gods but is Aphrodite's slave. After Paris' death, she adds, she tried to escape to the Greeks, letting herself down from the walls on ropes, and the watchmen caught her.

Hecuba answers (969–1032). Hera and Athena, she says, would never have been so foolish as to compete for a prize of beauty at all. As for Aphrodite: "My son was exceedingly handsome, and your own mind, when it saw him, became Cypris" (987–988). "All folly (ἀφροσύνη) is Aphrodite for mortals," and the goddess's name rightly begins like the word (989–990). Why did Helen not cry out at Sparta, when her brothers Castor and Polydeuces were still alive? And at Troy, Hecuba says, she often urged Helen to leave, and offered to take her secretly to the Greek ships. Menelaus agrees that Helen went willingly, and has her led away to be killed in Greece, though not, he promises Hecuba, on board his own ship. The audience knew from the Odyssey that he would take her back as his wife.

The debate is the one Book 3 begins. There Aphrodite comes in person, in the likeness of an old wool-worker, to call Helen to Paris' bed. Helen knows the goddess, and refuses: "Go and sit beside him yourself" (3.406). Aphrodite threatens her with her hatred, and Helen goes, afraid (3.413–420). Homer shows both the goddess and the woman who resists her. Priam's judgment, that the gods are to blame and not Helen (3.164), is Helen's plea in the play, and Hecuba's reply takes the goddess out of the story. For the same arguments in prose, see [[gorgias-helen]].
