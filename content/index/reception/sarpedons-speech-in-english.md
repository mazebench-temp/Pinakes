**Denham.** Sir John Denham, the poet of *Cooper's Hill*, rendered the speech in twenty-nine lines of couplets, “Sarpedon's Speech to Glaucus, in the Twelfth Book of Homer”. He enlarges the honors of the Lycian kings, “Our flocks, our herds, and our possessions more”, the tributes of land and sea heaped on their tables, music at the feast, and the people on “Xanthus' shore” who behold and adore them as gods. The kings, the Lycians are to say, deserve their greatness, “Since what they act transcends what they command”. He adds an antithesis of his own, “Or if death sought not them who seek not death”, and ends with the enemy, or the Lycians, or both, falling “A common sacrifice to honour”. Pope praised “Denham's strength” in the *Essay on Criticism*: “And praise the easy vigour of a line, / Where Denham's strength, and Waller's sweetness join”.

**Pope.** Alexander Pope's first published translation was “The Episode of Sarpedon, from the twelfth and sixteenth books of Homer's Iliads”, which appeared with his *Pastorals* in May 1709, in the sixth part of Tonson's *Miscellany*, when he was twenty-one. The speech, he wrote in a note, had been rendered in English by Sir John Denham, “after whom the translator had not the vanity to attempt it for any other reason, than that the episode must have been very imperfect without so noble a part of it”. Sir William Trumbull, shown the specimen in manuscript, urged him to translate the whole Iliad, and the episode went into the complete translation to which it led the way (Whitwell Elwin, introduction to Pope's *Works*, 1871; see [[pope]]). In Pope's Iliad the speech begins “Why boast we, Glaucus! our extended reign, / Where Xanthus' streams enrich the Lycian plain” and ends “Brave though we fall, and honour'd if we live, / Or let us glory gain, or glory give!”

**The parody.** When *The Rape of the Lock* was reprinted in 1717, Pope gave Clarissa a speech, added, in his own note, “to open more clearly the moral of the poem, in a parody of the speech of Sarpedon to Glaucus in Homer”. It runs Sarpedon's reasoning through the beauties of the drawing room. “Say, why are beauties praised and honoured most”, Clarissa begins; their glories are vain “Unless good sense preserve what beauty gains”, so that men may say, “Behold the first in virtue as in face!”; and “since, alas! frail beauty must decay”, what remains is to use their power well and keep good humor (*The Rape of the Lock* 5.9–34). Her first line is closer to Homer's than Pope's translation is: Sarpedon asks why the two of them are “honored most” (τετιμήμεσθα μάλιστα, 12.310). A note of Gilbert Wakefield's, reprinted in Elwin's edition, points out that two lines of the parody echo Denham's version: the beauties are decked “with all that land and sea afford”, as Denham's kings feast on “all the tributes land and sea affords”, and are “angel-like adored”, as his kings are adored as gods.

**Milton.** Theodore Buckley's notes to Pope's Iliad set beside the speech the words with which Satan, in the second book of *Paradise Lost*, takes on alone the journey out of Hell. He would ill become his throne, he says, if any danger could deter him: “Wherefore do I assume / These royalties, and not refuse to reign, / Refusing to accept as great a share / Of hazard as of honour, due alike / To him who reigns”, and more of hazard to him who sits highest in honor (*Paradise Lost* 2.445–456; see [[milton]]). In Satan's mouth Sarpedon's argument, that the honors of a king are paid for in danger, becomes his reason for going alone.

**Arnold.** In the first of his Oxford lectures *On Translating Homer* (1861), Matthew Arnold made “that tonic speech of Sarpedon” his test passage. George Chapman's rhymed version shows, he argued, that rhyme pairs lines that Homer keeps apart. Homer's “Neither would I myself go forth to fight with the foremost, / Nor would I urge thee on to enter the glorious battle”, in Arnold's own hexameters, stops at the end of the verse, and the next line begins an opposed movement, “But—for a thousand fates of death stand close to us always—”; Chapman's rhyme of “advance” with “chance” carries the ear back to the line it should have left. Pope's version he quoted with admiration, “Nothing could better exhibit Pope's prodigious talent”, but, as Bentley said, “You must not call it Homer”. Its antithesis, “The life which others pay, let us bestow, / And give to fame what we to nature owe”, is excellent in its way, but “neither the antithesis itself, nor the couplet which conveys it, is suited to the feeling or to the movement of the Homeric ἴομεν”, “let us go” (12.328). In the second lecture he quoted Francis Newman's version and found its diction bad throughout, “eld”, “in sooth”, “liefly”, “sith”. Newman, who held that Homer was quaint and should be translated so, answered that it is quaint “to address a young friend as ‘O Pippin’!”; Arnold agreed, “although I do not think it was quaint in Sarpedon to address Glaucus as ὦ πέπον” (*Last Words*, 1862; see [[arnold]]).

**Lord Granville.** Arnold also told a story from Robert Wood's *Essay on the Original Genius and Writings of Homer* (1775). In 1762, at the end of the Seven Years' War, Wood, then Under-Secretary of State, was sent with the preliminary articles of the Treaty of Paris to the President of the Council, Lord Granville, a few days before Granville died. Wood proposed to come back another time, but Granville would not let him go, “saying, it could not prolong his life to neglect his duty”, and repeated in Greek the last lines of Sarpedon's speech, from “My friend, if, once we had escaped this war” to “let us go” (12.322–328). He dwelt above all on the third line, “then neither would I myself fight among the foremost” (12.324), which recalled his own part in public affairs, and repeated the last word, ἴομεν, several times. Then he had the treaty read, and approved, in his own words, “the most glorious war, and most honourable peace, this nation ever saw”. Arnold told the story, he said, because it showed the English aristocracy at the height of its culture; because it illustrated Goethe's saying that in Homer's view our life is “a conflict and a hell”, and what is tonic and fortifying in that view; and because it showed that the passage was one in which Pope would be at his best.
