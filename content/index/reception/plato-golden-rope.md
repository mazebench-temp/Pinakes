**Homer.** Zeus' rope is a trial of strength that no one takes up. All the gods and goddesses may hang on it and pull, and they will not drag down “Zeus, the highest counselor” (8.22); if he pulled in earnest he would haul them up with earth and sea, bind the rope around a peak of Olympus, and all of it would “hang in midair” (8.23–26). The binding is the last proof of his strength (see [[the-golden-rope]]).

**Plato.** In the *Theaetetus* Socrates expounds, in order to examine it, a doctrine that nothing ever is, but all things are always coming to be, out of motion and mixture with one another. All the wise, he says, except Parmenides, agree on this, Protagoras, Heraclitus and Empedocles, and so do the summits of poetry, Epicharmus in comedy and Homer in tragedy: Homer, by calling Ocean “the origin of the gods, and mother Tethys” (14.201, 14.302), said that all things are the offspring of flow and motion (152d–e). Who could stand against such an army and such a general without being laughed at? Then come the proofs. Fire and heat are born of motion and friction; bodies are preserved by exercise and ruined by idleness; the soul gains and keeps its learning by study, which is motion, and forgets in idleness; calm and stillness rot and destroy (153a–c). “And to crown it all” (τὸν κολοφῶνα), Socrates brings in the golden rope: by it Homer means nothing else than the sun, and shows that as long as the revolution of the heavens and the sun are in motion, all things are and are kept safe among gods and men; but if this should stand still, as though bound (ὥσπερ δεθέν), all things would be destroyed and, as the saying goes, everything would be turned upside down (153c–d). Theaetetus agrees that this is what it shows.

**The reading.** The gloss turns Homer's picture inside out. In the Iliad the binding is Zeus' act of power, and what it leaves hanging in midair is the world. In the *Theaetetus* the binding is the end of the world: the sun, bound, would stop, and with it everything that motion keeps in being. Socrates offers the reading in play, as the last stroke of a case he means to test, and the dialogue turns at once to the perception of color (153d–154a). But the reading makes the rope what later readers would make it, a bond between heaven and the things on earth (see [[homers-golden-chain]]).
