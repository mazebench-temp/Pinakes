**The ending.** The poem that began with the wrath of Achilles ends with the funeral of the man he killed. For nine days the Trojans bring in wood, on the tenth they burn Hector, and at dawn on the eleventh they quench the pyre with wine, gather the white bones into a golden chest, cover it with soft purple robes, lay it in a trench under great stones and heap up the mound, with lookouts posted all around, “lest the well-greaved Achaeans should attack too soon”; then they feast in the house of Priam (24.777–803; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]]). Priam had asked Achilles for nine days of lament, the burial and the feast on the tenth, the tomb on the eleventh, “and on the twelfth we will fight, if we must” (24.664–667), and the poem stops on the eleventh day, with the truce still holding. The last line names the funeral and the man: “So they held the funeral of Hector, tamer of horses” (24.804). Hector's epithet in the genitive, Ἕκτορος ἱπποδάμοιο, is the poem's last word (see [[tamer-of-horses]]).

**The variant.** Leaf reports another form of the line. “The remarkable variant given by Schol. T is evidently designed for the incorporation of the Iliad into the Cycle, where it was immediately followed by the Aithiopis ascribed to Arktinos. This told of the deaths of Penthesileia, Thersites, Antilochos, Memnon, and Achilles. So the continuation of the Iliad by Quintus opens with the coming of Penthesileia and her Amazons” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.804; see the translation's note on 24.804). Leaf does not print the variant itself. On his account the line was rewritten so that the funeral led straight on to the next poem, and the Iliad became one episode in a continuous story of the war (see [[amazons]], [[thersites]] and [[antilochus]]).

**What the poem foretells.** The Iliad knows the deaths that the *Aethiopis* told, and gives them in advance. Hector, dying, foretells that Paris and Phoebus Apollo will destroy Achilles at the Scaean gates (22.359–360); in this book Thetis tells her son that “death and mighty fate stand close beside you” (24.132), and Achilles tells Priam that Peleus fathered “one son, doomed to an untimely death” (24.540; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). Andromache, in her lament, foresees the sack of the city, the women carried off in the ships and her son hurled from the tower (24.728–739; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]] and [[little-iliad]]). The poem tells none of these things as they happen. It ends with a funeral, as the funeral of Patroclus filled the book before it (see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]).

**Virgil.** On the walls of Juno's temple at Carthage, Aeneas sees the war in a series of pictures. After Achilles dragging Hector three times around the walls and selling the lifeless body for gold, and Priam stretching out unarmed hands, he sees himself among the Achaean chiefs, “the Eastern ranks and the arms of black Memnon”, and Penthesilea in her fury leading the Amazons with their crescent shields, a warrior maiden who dares to fight with men (*Eoasque acies et nigri Memnonis arma. / ducit Amazonidum lunatis agmina peltis / Penthesilea furens*, *Aeneid* 1.483–493; see [[virgils-hector]]). The pictures pass from the ransom of Hector to the two allies whose deaths the *Aethiopis* told next, Memnon and Penthesilea.
