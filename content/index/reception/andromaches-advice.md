Andromache's speech moves from lament to a plan. Achilles killed her father and her seven brothers, and her mother, whom he set free for a ransom, died in her father's halls; Hector is now father, mother, brother and husband to her (6.413–430). Then she asks him to stay “here on the tower”, not to make his child an orphan and his wife a widow, and gives him a soldier's advice. He should station the army by the wild fig tree, where the city can be scaled and the wall is open to assault. Three times the best of the Achaeans have tried it there, the two Ajaxes, Idomeneus, the sons of Atreus and Diomedes, whether a seer told them of it or their own heart urged them on.

**The athetesis.** Aristarchus marked the seven lines as spurious. The scholia of the Venetus A manuscript, which preserve much of his work, give his reason: the words do not suit Andromache, who sets herself up as a general against Hector (ἀντιστρατηγεῖ). Two other difficulties are plain from the text. The three attacks are told nowhere in the Iliad. And Hector's answer does not mention the fig tree. “All these things are on my mind too, lady,” he says, and goes on to shame, to fame, and to the day when Troy will fall (6.441–465).

**The case for the lines.** Monro and Allen print them, and the translation keeps them. The advice suits the plea. If Hector commands at the wall, he will not fight on the plain, where she foresees his death (6.407–410). The fig tree is a landmark of the poem's Troy: the fleeing Trojans rush past it toward the city (11.167), and Hector and Achilles run past it in the chase around the walls (22.145; see [[the-fig-tree]]). And “all these things” can be taken as Hector's answer to everything she has said, the advice included.

**A weak place.** Homer says that Poseidon and Apollo built the wall for Laomedon (7.452–453, 21.441–447). He does not say why one stretch of it should be weak. Pindar tells a story that would explain one. The two gods took Aeacus, the grandfather of Achilles and of Ajax, to work with them, and when three serpents leapt at the new wall, two fell dead and one got in; Apollo foretold that Troy would be taken where Aeacus' hands had worked (*Olympian* 8.31–46). Whether Andromache's place by the fig tree was meant to be that stretch cannot be known.

The objection says as much about its critic as about the poem. It judges the speech by what befits the speaker, the rule of fitness (τὸ πρέπον) by which the Alexandrian critics often tested a line. Hector ends the scene with a rule of his own, when he sends Andromache back to her loom and distaff: “war will be the concern of men” (6.490–493; see [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]]).
