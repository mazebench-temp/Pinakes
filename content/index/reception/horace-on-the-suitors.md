**The Epistles.** Writing to Lollius Maximus, Horace reads Homer as a teacher of conduct, clearer and better than the philosophers: the Iliad shows the follies of kings and peoples, and the Odyssey, in Ulysses, what virtue and wisdom can do (*Epistles* 1.2.1–22; see [[horace-on-the-opening-of-the-odyssey]]). Then he turns on his own kind: “We are mere numbers, born to consume the fruits of the earth, the suitors of Penelope, good-for-nothings, and the young men of Alcinous, busier than was right in caring for their skin, who thought it fine to sleep till the middle of the day and to lull their cares to rest to the sound of the lyre” (*Nos numerus sumus et fruges consumere nati, / sponsi Penelopae nebulones Alcinoique / in cute curanda plus aequo operata iuventus*, 1.2.27–31). The suitors stand beside the young Phaeacians as the type of men who eat and do nothing.

**The Satires.** The fifth satire of the second book continues Ulysses' consultation of Tiresias in the underworld. “Answer me this too, Tiresias, besides what you have told me”, Ulysses asks: by what arts and means can he recover his lost fortune? (*Hoc quoque, Tiresia, praeter narrata petenti / responde*, *Satires* 2.5.1–3). Tiresias tells him to hunt cunningly everywhere for old men's wills (*captes astutus ubique / testamenta senum*, 2.5.23–24), and carries the advice to its end. If the old man is a lecher, Ulysses is not to wait to be asked, but to hand Penelope over obligingly to his better (2.5.75–76). Ulysses protests: “Do you think she can be led astray, so honest and so chaste, whom the suitors could not drive from the straight course?” Tiresias answers: “Yes, for the young men who came were sparing of great gifts, and not so much eager for love as for the kitchen. That is why your Penelope is honest; but once she has tasted a little profit from one old man, shared with you, she will be like a dog that can never be frightened away from a greasy hide” (*venit enim magnum donandi parca iuventus / nec tantum veneris quantum studiosa culinae. / sic tibi Penelope frugi est; quae si semel uno / de sene gustarit tecum partita lucellum, / ut canis a corio numquam absterrebitur uncto*, 2.5.76–83; see [[horace]]).

**Homer's suitors.** Homer's Tiresias foretold the suitors as overbearing men who eat up Odysseus' livelihood, “wooing your godlike wife and offering bride-gifts” (a plain rendering of *Odyssey* 11.116–117). Horace's Tiresias takes away the gifts and keeps the eating. In the Odyssey the suitors do give gifts when Penelope asks for them: Antinous a great robe, Eurymachus a necklace of gold and amber, Eurydamas earrings, Peisander a necklace, and each of the others something (*Odyssey* 18.290–301). Odysseus is glad to see her draw them out and charm them with soft words, while her mind intends other things, νόος δέ οἱ ἄλλα μενοίνα (*Odyssey* 18.281–283): the half-verse with which Antinous complains of her to the assembly, “but her mind has other things in view” (*Odyssey* 2.92). Penelope herself tells them that suitors in the past brought their own cattle and sheep for a feast, and gave splendid gifts, and did not eat another man's livelihood without payment (*Odyssey* 18.275–280; see [[the-suitors]], [[feast]] and [[bride-price]]). That is Horace's kitchen.
