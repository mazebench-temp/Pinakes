summary: In the fourth book of the Aeneid Jupiter sends Mercury to Carthage to bid Aeneas, who lingers with Dido, set sail. The messenger binds on golden sandals and takes his wand, as Hermes does when Zeus sends him to Calypso, stops on Mount Atlas and swoops down to the sea like a bird skimming the fish-filled rocks, as Hermes skims the waves like a gull. Homer's god gives the command to the goddess who holds the hero; Virgil's gives it to the hero himself.

**Homer.** After the second council of the gods Zeus turns to his son: “Hermes, since you for your part are our messenger in all else”, tell the nymph of the lovely tresses the gods' unerring counsel, the homecoming of Odysseus (*Odyssey* 5.28–42; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). “So he spoke, and the guide, the slayer of Argus, did not disobey” (*Odyssey* 5.43). Hermes binds on his golden sandals, which carry him over the waters and over the boundless earth as fast as the blasts of the wind, and takes the wand with which he bewitches the eyes of men and wakes others who are sleeping (*Odyssey* 5.44–49; see [[sandals]] and [[wand]]). These seven verses are those with which he sets out to meet Priam (*Odyssey* 5.43–49 = *Iliad* 24.339–345; see [[horace-mercury-and-priam]]). The rest is the Odyssey's own: he steps on Pieria, falls from the upper air upon the sea, and hurries over the wave like a gull that wets its thick plumage in the brine as it hunts fish (*Odyssey* 5.50–54; see [[the-gull]] and [[hermes]]). He finds Calypso singing at her loom, gives her Zeus' command, warns her to fear his wrath, and goes (*Odyssey* 5.55–148). He never sees Odysseus, who is weeping on the shore (*Odyssey* 5.81–84), and it is Calypso who tells Odysseus that she will send him away, without a word of Zeus (*Odyssey* 5.160–170; see [[calypso]]).

**Virgil's first flight.** Mercury flies to Carthage twice in the *Aeneid*. In the first book Jupiter sends down “the son of Maia” so that the land and the new citadel of Carthage may open in welcome to the Trojans, and Dido, not knowing fate, may not keep them from her borders; he flies through the great air with the oarage of his wings and stands on the shores of Libya, and the queen receives the Trojans with a kindly mind (*Aeneid* 1.297–304; plain renderings throughout).

**The second.** In the fourth book Rumor carries the news of Dido and Aeneas to Iarbas, a Libyan king whose offer of marriage Dido refused, and he prays to Jupiter against the stranger she has taken into her kingdom (*Aeneid* 4.196–218). Jupiter turns his eyes to the royal city and to the lovers “forgetful of their better fame”, and sends Mercury to the Dardan leader who lingers in Carthage and does not look to the cities granted him by fate: “let him sail: this is the sum, let this be our message” (*naviget! haec summa est, hic nostri nuntius esto*, *Aeneid* 4.219–237). Mercury obeys at once, as Hermes “did not disobey”: “First he binds on his feet the golden sandals, which carry him aloft on their wings over the sea or over the land as fast as the rushing wind” (*et primum pedibus talaria nectit / aurea, quae sublimem alis sive aequora supra / seu terram rapido pariter cum flamine portant*, *Aeneid* 4.238–241). “Then he takes his wand: with it he calls pale souls out of Orcus and sends others down to gloomy Tartarus, gives sleep and takes it away, and unseals the eyes in death” (*dat somnos adimitque, et lumina morte resignat*, *Aeneid* 4.242–244). Homer's wand only bewitches the eyes of men and wakes the sleeping; Virgil, Merry observes, extends its power to causing or breaking the sleep of death (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 5.43–49). The power over the dead belongs to the wand of the Odyssey's last book, where “the Cyllenian” calls out the souls of the suitors (Ἑρμῆς δὲ ψυχὰς Κυλλήνιος ἐξεκαλεῖτο), holding the wand that bewitches the eyes of men and wakes the sleeping, in the words of this book (*Odyssey* 24.1–5, a plain rendering). Homer gives Hermes that title only there; Virgil calls his Mercury the Cyllenian three times in this passage (*Aeneid* 4.252, 4.258, 4.276).

**Atlas and the bird.** Trusting in his wand, Mercury drives the winds and swims through the stormy clouds, until he sees the summit and the steep sides of Atlas, who props the sky on his head: his pine-crowned head girt with black clouds and beaten by wind and rain, snow on his shoulders, rivers rushing from the old man's chin, his beard stiff with ice (*Aeneid* 4.245–251). There the god of Cyllene first stops, poised on his wings; then he sends himself headlong with his whole body to the waves, “like a bird that flies low near the water around the shores, around the fish-filled rocks” (*avi similis, quae circum litora, circum / piscosos scopulos humilis volat aequora iuxta*, *Aeneid* 4.252–255), and so flies between earth and sky to the sandy shore of Libya, “coming from his mother's father” (*Aeneid* 4.256–258). Atlas is the height from which Virgil's god drops to the sea, as Pieria is Homer's (*Odyssey* 5.50). He is Mercury's grandfather, since Maia, the mother of Hermes, is a daughter of Atlas (Hesiod, *Theogony* 938); and in Homer he is the father of Calypso (*Odyssey* 1.52, 7.245). Homer names his bird, the λάρος, the gull (see [[gull]]); Virgil's is nameless.

**The message.** Homer's Hermes finds the goddess at home, singing as she weaves with a golden shuttle (*Odyssey* 5.61–62), and the man on the shore, weeping for home (*Odyssey* 5.81–84). Virgil's Mercury lands among the huts of Carthage and finds the man at work on the woman's city, “founding citadels and making new buildings”, with a sword starred with yellow jasper and a cloak of Tyrian purple that Dido made for him, working gold into the weave (*Aeneid* 4.259–264). He attacks at once: is Aeneas now laying the foundations of lofty Carthage and, a wife's man, building her a fine city, forgetful of his own kingdom? He gives Jupiter's message, bids him think of Ascanius, to whom Italy and the Roman land are owed, and in the middle of his speech vanishes into thin air (*Aeneid* 4.265–278). Aeneas is struck dumb, his hair stands on end, and he burns to flee (*Aeneid* 4.279–282). Homer's god speaks to the goddess, who yields with a protest against the jealousy of the gods (*Odyssey* 5.116–144; see [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]); Virgil's speaks to the man, and the queen learns of the departure from her own foreboding and from Rumor (*Aeneid* 4.296–299).
