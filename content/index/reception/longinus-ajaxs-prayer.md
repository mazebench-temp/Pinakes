**Homer.** Zeus has turned the battle against the Achaeans, and Ajax sees it: every Trojan missile strikes home, and theirs fall to the ground in vain (17.626–633; see [[even-a-fool-can-see]]). Someone must take the news to Achilles, who does not know that his companion has fallen, but Ajax can see no one to send, “for they are covered in mist, the men themselves and the horses alike” (17.640–644). The mist is Zeus' own. He poured it around the fighters over the body when the Achaeans first closed around Patroclus, while the rest of the battle went on in sunlight (17.268–273, 17.366–377; see [[zeus]]). Then Ajax prays. Walter Leaf explains the ἀλλά that opens the prayer: the thought, he writes, seems to be “All is lost, unless thou, Father Zeus, will save us — none other can” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.645). Zeus pities him “as he shed tears”, scatters the mist, and the sun shines out on the whole battle (17.648–650), and Ajax sends Menelaus to look for Antilochus (17.651–655; see [[ajax]] and [[antilochus]]).

**Longinus.** The ninth chapter of *On the Sublime* finds the first and greatest source of the sublime in greatness of mind, and draws most of its examples from Homer's gods (see [[longinus]] and [[longinus-strife]]). After them, and after the lawgiver of the Jews, the author asks leave to add one more example from the poet, from the human sphere, to show how Homer is accustomed to step up with his heroes into heroic greatness. A mist and a night in which nothing can be done, he says, suddenly cover the battle of the Greeks, and Ajax, at a loss, prays, and he quotes the prayer as far as “and in the light destroy us”. His comment: “This is truly the feeling of Ajax. He does not pray to live, for that request would have been too humble for the hero; but since in the useless darkness he could put his courage to no noble use, chafing that he is idle in the battle, he asks for light as quickly as may be, sure in any case to find a burial worthy of his valor, even if Zeus should be ranged against him” (*On the Sublime* 9.10). The next sentence turns from the hero to the poet, who rages with his battles like Hector at the ships (9.11; see [[longinus-homers-fury]]).

**What the critic changed.** The quotation stops short of the half-verse that ends the prayer, ἐπεί νύ τοι εὔαδεν οὕτως, “since that is now your pleasure” (17.647). Homer's Ajax accepts the will of Zeus: if the Achaeans must die, let it be in the light. The critic's Ajax would die in the light “even if Zeus should be ranged against him” (κἂν αὐτῷ Ζεὺς ἀντιτάττηται): the resignation has become defiance. The critic also gives the prayer a motive of his own, a death worthy of the hero's courage. In Homer it comes out of the search for a messenger. Ajax needs light to find a man who can tell Achilles, and the first thing he does with it is to send Menelaus to find one. The prayer is for “the sons of the Achaeans” (17.645), and the unspoken object of “destroy” is the army; the critic makes it one man's prayer for his own end. He calls the darkness sudden, though in Homer it has lain over the body since the Achaeans closed around it, and he names it with ἀχλύς, which in Homer is the mist that comes over a man's eyes as he faints or dies, or that a god pours over them or takes away (5.696, 16.344; 20.321, 5.127; see [[achlys]]). Homer's words are ἀήρ and ὀμίχλη, “mist” and “fog” (17.644–645, 17.649). Nor does the critic mention the tears. In Homer it is Ajax's weeping that Zeus pities, and the light is the answer to it (17.648; see [[pity]] and [[shedding-tears]]).

**Two examples of Ajax.** The chapter's first example of greatness of mind is also Ajax. A thought, the author says, can be admired on its own, without a voice, for its greatness, “as the silence of Ajax in the Nekyia is great, and more sublime than any speech” (*On the Sublime* 9.2). That is the silence with which the shade of Ajax, still angry at losing the arms of Achilles, turns away from Odysseus without a word and goes off among the other souls into Erebus (*Odyssey* 11.543–564; see [[the-judgment-of-arms]] and [[silence]]). In both passages the critic finds the hero's greatness in what he refuses: an answer to the man who wronged him, and a prayer for his own life.
