**Homer.** After Odysseus has bathed in the river, Athena makes him taller and stouter, sends his thick hair down like the hyacinth flower, and pours grace on his head and shoulders, “as when a man pours gold around silver, / a skilled man whom Hephaestus and Pallas Athena have taught / every kind of craft, and he accomplishes lovely works” (*Odyssey* 6.229–235; see [[gold-poured-around-silver]] and [[athena]]). The verses return when Athena makes him beautiful for Penelope (*Odyssey* 23.156–162).

**Strabo.** In the first book of his *Geography* Strabo defends Homer against Eratosthenes, who said that every poet aims at entertainment, not instruction (*Geography* 1.2.3, plain renderings throughout). The ancients, Strabo answers, called poetry a first philosophy that introduces us to life from childhood; Homer described distant places as well as near ones, and did not make everything a marvel but used myth in the service of knowledge (*Geography* 1.2.3, 1.2.7). Cities and lawgivers accepted myths for their usefulness, since men love to learn and the love of stories prepares for it (*Geography* 1.2.8). So the poet, referring his myths to education, took great care for the truth, and also put falsehood in, accepting the one and using the other to lead and command the crowd: “As when a man pours gold around silver, so he added myth to true adventures, sweetening and adorning his telling, but looking to the same end as the historian and the man who tells what is” (ὡς δʼ ὅτε τις χρυσὸν περιχεύεται ἀργύρῳ ἀνήρ, οὕτως ἐκεῖνος ταῖς ἀληθέσι περιπετείαις προσεπετίθει μῦθον, ἡδύνων καὶ κοσμῶν τὴν φράσιν, *Geography* 1.2.9). He took the Trojan War, which happened, and adorned it with myths, and the wandering of Odysseus in the same way; to hang an empty tale of marvels on nothing true is not Homer's way. A falsehood persuades better with some truth mixed in, as Polybius also says of the wandering of Odysseus, and as the verse says of Odysseus' tale to Penelope, “he spoke many falsehoods, making them like true things” (*Odyssey* 19.203): not all, but many, or they would not have been like the truth (*Geography* 1.2.9; see [[strabo]]). Then Strabo gives the wandering its starting points in history. Aeolus ruled the islands around Lipara; inhospitable men, the Cyclopes and the Laestrygonians, held the country around Aetna and Leontini; and the strait was closed to the men of that time, with Charybdis and the rock of Scylla held by robbers (*Geography* 1.2.9; see [[cyclopes]] and [[scylla]]).

**The simile turned.** In Homer the gold is the grace that the goddess pours over the man, and the likeness is a craftsman's finish laid on fine metal (see [[gold-poured-around-silver]]). Strabo makes the silver the truth and the gold the myth: the poet's ornament is laid over the history, and the history is still there beneath it. The simile that makes Odysseus more beautiful becomes a figure for the poem that tells of him.
