**In Homer.** The insult is spoken twice, both times by an Achaean to Achaeans (see [[achaean-women-no-longer-men]]). Thersites, urging the army to sail home and leave Agamemnon to his prizes, calls them “Weaklings, wretched disgraces, Achaean women, Achaean men no longer” (2.235; see [[thersites]] and [[odysseus-and-thersites]]). In Book 7 Menelaus uses the same words to the opposite end. No one will stand up to Hector, and he calls the silent army “braggarts” (ἀπειλητῆρες, “men of threats”) and “Achaean women”, and puts on his armor himself (7.92–103; see [[menelaus]]). Ἀχαιΐς, the feminine of the people's name, is also the word for the land: Nestor's rebuke begins, “Ah, what great sorrow is coming upon the land of Achaea!” (Ἀχαιΐδα γαῖαν, 7.124). The insult shames by gender, as Hector does when he tells Ajax not to test him “as if I were some feeble boy or a woman, who does not know the works of war” (7.235–236; see [[women-and-war]] and [[elenchos]]).

**In Virgil.** In the ninth book of the *Aeneid* the Trojans are besieged in their camp while Aeneas is away. Numanus, called Remulus, lately married to Turnus' younger sister, strides before the Italian line shouting. Are they not ashamed, “twice-captured Phrygians”, to be shut up behind a rampart again? The Italians are a hard race: they dip their newborn sons in icy rivers, hunt as boys, and farm and fight all their lives, and even their gray heads wear the helmet. The Trojans wear saffron and gleaming purple, love idleness and dancing, and have sleeves on their tunics and ribbons on their caps. “O truly Phrygian women, for you are not Phrygian men (*o vere Phrygiae, neque enim Phryges*), go over the heights of Dindyma, where the double pipe plays the music you know. The drums and the Berecyntian boxwood flute of the Mother of Ida are calling you. Leave arms to men, and give way to the sword” (*Aeneid* 9.598–620).

Virgil keeps Homer's form, the feminine and the masculine of a people's name set side by side, and changes its use. The insult is no longer a rebuke to one's own side but an enemy's taunt, and it draws on the later picture of the Phrygians as soft Easterners, bright-robed and devoted to the rites of the Great Mother. Homer's Trojans are not drawn so; the one Trojan mocked for his looks is Paris (3.39–55). Aeneas' enemies throw the same scorn at him. Iarbas, the African king whom Dido refused, calls him “that Paris with his half-man retinue” (*Aeneid* 4.215–217), and Turnus prays to befoul in the dust the hair of “the half-man Phrygian”, curled with hot iron and dripping with myrrh (12.97–100).

**The answer.** Ascanius, who until then had shot only at wild beasts, prays to Jupiter and vows a white bullock with gilded brow. Jupiter thunders on the left, and the arrow passes through Numanus' temples. “Go, mock courage with proud words! This is the answer the twice-captured Phrygians send the Rutulians” (9.621–637). In Homer the rebuke is answered otherwise. Agamemnon holds Menelaus back, and it is Nestor's reproaches that make nine champions stand up (7.104–169).
