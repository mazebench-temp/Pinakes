**Homer.** Achilles' immortal horses stand apart from the battle and weep for their charioteer from the moment they learn that Hector has killed him. Automedon's whip and his words, soothing or threatening, will not move them, back to the ships or into the battle. They stand as still as a pillar on a tomb, their heads bowed to the ground, the tears running down to the earth and their manes soiled in the dust (17.426–440; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[tomb]]). Zeus sees them and pities them, asks why the gods gave them to a mortal, and breathes might into them, and they carry Automedon back into the fight (17.441–458; see [[nothing-more-miserable-than-man]]). They mourn again at the funeral games, when Achilles will not race them: they stand grieving for the gentle charioteer who used to wash their manes and pour oil on them, their manes resting on the ground (23.280–284).

**Virgil.** In the eleventh book of the *Aeneid* Aeneas sends the body of Pallas, whom Turnus has killed, home to his father Evander, with a thousand men to escort it (*Aeneid* 11.26–28, 11.59–63). The procession brings the prizes of the battle, the horses and weapons taken from the enemy, captives bound to be killed at the pyre, and trunks dressed in the arms of the enemy leaders (11.78–84); then old Acoetes, once Evander's armor-bearer, beating his breast and tearing his face, and chariots drenched in Rutulian blood (11.85–88). “Behind, the war-horse Aethon, his trappings laid aside, goes weeping and wets his face with great drops” (*post bellator equus positis insignibus Aethon / it lacrimans guttisque umectat grandibus ora*, 11.89–90). Others carry the spear and the helmet, “for Turnus the victor has the rest” (*nam cetera Turnus / victor habet*, 11.91–92). The Trojans and Etruscans follow, and the Arcadians with their arms reversed, and Aeneas says his last farewell (11.92–98).

**Servius.** The ancient commentary explains the horse twice over, by custom and by Homer. It was a rule of military discipline, Servius says, that the horse was led to the tomb and the armor-bearer carried the arms reversed, and the poet seems to have kept the custom; that the war-horse is led and a phalanx follows is Roman usage, which kept the dignity of a royal youth at his funeral, since among the Romans what was granted to a man alive was not taken from him dead. *positis insignibus* means without its trappings. “That he says the horse weeps is no wonder, since Homer even brought one on prophesying”, and Pliny, he adds, says that the horse alone besides man weeps and feels grief; Aethon is a horse's name, given also to a horse of Aurora (Servius on *Aeneid* 11.89). The prophesying horse is Xanthus, to whom Hera gives a voice to foretell Achilles' death (19.404–417; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Pliny's own words, in his account of the horse, are that horses foresee battle and mourn their lost masters, and sometimes shed tears of longing for them (*Natural History*, Book 8). On the lines before, Servius notes that Virgil has turned man and beast around. Grief has left Acoetes so nearly without human sense that he cannot walk by himself, and the horse has so felt its master's death that it passes into human feeling, weeping and following the body of its own accord; so the man “is led” (*ducitur*) and the horse “goes” (*it*), though going belongs to a man and being led to a horse (on 11.85). From “Turnus the victor has the rest” he infers that Turnus took more than the sword-belt, since only the spear and the helmet are left (on 11.91).

**Homer and Virgil.** Homer's horses are immortal, the gods' gift to Peleus, and they weep apart from the battle for the man who drove them; Zeus pities them and sends them back into the fight. Virgil's horse is mortal, a war-horse taken out of the war with its trappings removed, and it weeps in the funeral procession. Homer's pair stand fixed like a pillar on a tomb; Virgil's horse is led to one. Behind it come the arms that the victor left, as in Homer the victor has the armor. Zeus asks whether it is not enough that Hector has the armor and boasts of it for nothing (17.450), and Alcimedon tells Automedon that Hector wears it on his own shoulders and glories in it (17.472–473; see [[exchange-of-armor]]). The name of Virgil's horse is Homer's too: Aethon is one of the four horses Hector calls by name in *Iliad* 8 (8.185; see [[the-horses-of-hector]]).
