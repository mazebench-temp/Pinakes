**Plato.** In the third book of the *Republic* Socrates goes through what may not be told of the heroes to the young guardians. Achilles must not be thought so fond of money as to take gifts from Agamemnon, or to give back a corpse only for a ransom (*Republic* 3.390e–391a). Then he hesitates, for Homer's sake, to say that it is not even holy to say such things of Achilles, or to believe others who say them: his words to Apollo, the most destructive of the gods, on whom he would take vengeance if he had the power (22.15, 22.20; see [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]); “that toward the river, though it was a god, he was disobedient and ready to fight” (καὶ ὡς πρὸς τὸν ποταμόν, θεὸν ὄντα, ἀπειθῶς εἶχεν καὶ μάχεσθαι ἕτοιμος ἦν); and that he gave the hair sacred to the other river, the Spercheius, to the dead Patroclus (23.151). That he did these things must not be believed, nor the draggings of Hector around the tomb, nor the slaughter of the captives at the pyre. The young must not think that Achilles, the son of a goddess and of Peleus, most self-controlled of men and third from Zeus, reared by the wisest Chiron, was so full of confusion as to hold within him two opposite diseases, meanness with love of money, and arrogance toward gods and men (*Republic* 3.391a–c; see [[plato]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]]).

**The poem.** Plato's charge has the poem behind it. The river, in the likeness of a man, calls to Achilles from a deep eddy to drive the Trojans out of his streams and do his grim deeds on the plain, for his streams are full of corpses (21.212–221). Achilles answers, “So it shall be, Scamander, nurtured by Zeus, as you bid”, and in the same breath refuses: he will not stop killing the Trojans until he has penned them in the city and made trial of Hector (21.222–226). He rushes on the Trojans, and when the river has complained to Apollo, he leaps down from the bank into the midst of him (21.227–234). Walter Leaf found it not easy to say what Achilles promises (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.223). It had been suggested that the river's request was a ruse to get Achilles into his power, and that Achilles fell into the trap and leapt into the river to drive the Trojans out of it, not to kill them; Leaf answers that this “involves reading a great deal into the text”, since we should have expected to be warned expressly of the god's deceit. The other reading, after Monro, takes the river's whole speech for irony, since he bids Achilles do what is plainly impossible, and Achilles' answer for irony in return: “I will do as you bid”, in Leaf's paraphrase, while he does the very reverse. This, Leaf says, “also makes severe demands upon a hearer's imagination”, and the real explanation must be sought elsewhere (on 21.223). The river is a god in the poem's own words: “the great god did not stop” (21.248), and Hera calls off Hephaestus' fire because “it is not fitting / to batter an immortal god like this for the sake of mortals” (21.379–380; see [[scamander]]). Socrates' “third from Zeus” is the lineage that Achilles had just set against a river's over the body of Asteropaeus: “Peleus, son of Aeacus; and Aeacus was the son of Zeus” (21.189; see [[asteropaeus]] and [[zeus-is-mightier-than-rivers]]).

**Proclus.** In his essays on the *Republic* Proclus answers these charges one by one (*On the Republic* I 146–153 Kroll). Achilles, he says, is of all Homer's heroes the most careful about the divine. He counsels the Greeks to serve Apollo and to appease his priest, he obeys Athena at once when she comes to him, and he pours libation and prays to Zeus with the knowledge that befits the gods, cleansing the cup that he kept for Zeus alone and standing in the middle of the enclosure (I 146–147 Kroll; 16.225–232). The fight with Xanthus, he goes on, is not hard to answer. Achilles was not disobedient to the god himself. He contended either with the visible water that hindered his attack on the enemy, or with one of the local powers, and he had gods for allies, since he stood against the river with Athena and Poseidon at his side (I 148–149 Kroll; 21.284–304). Poetry, Proclus says, weaves its contests through every kind: it tells now of men fighting one another, now of the higher kinds, as in the so-called Theomachy, and now of heroes standing against daemonic natures, to show those who can understand that the first of the last things are in a way equal to the last of the first, above all when the gods themselves move and guard them. Achilles was not alone in this. Heracles too is said to have fought the Achelous, and Achilles, who measured his own life against Heracles', did not shrink from such contests either (I 149 Kroll; compare 18.117–121, and the Achelous of Achilles' boast, 21.194; see [[heracles]] and [[achelous]]).

Proclus answers the other charges as well. The hair had not yet become the river's: Achilles had promised it to the Spercheius on his return home, and when he knew that he would not return, it was right to cut it in honor of his friend (I 149 Kroll). The captives whom Socrates names were taken in this book, twelve young men drawn alive out of the river “as the penalty for Patroclus” (21.26–28), and killed at the pyre in Book 23 (23.175–176). Achilles, Proclus says, did nothing crueler to them than if he had killed them where he found them, like the rest; they fared better, their bodies wholly consumed by fire, than if they had been torn by beasts, or had suffered what Lycaon suffered, to whom Achilles says, “Lie there now among the fishes, who will lick the blood / from your wound and care nothing for you” (21.122–123). By the more secret teaching of his master, he adds, the whole business of the pyre imitated the theurgists' immortalizing of the soul, and the twelve were set beside Patroclus' soul as its attendants (I 151–153 Kroll; see [[human-sacrifice]] and [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). His answer to the dragging of Hector is told under [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]], and his reading of the battle of the gods, Hephaestus and the river among them, under [[proclus-on-the-battle-of-the-gods]].
