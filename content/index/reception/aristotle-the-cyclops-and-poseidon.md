summary: Fragments of Aristotle's Homeric Problems, preserved in the scholia on Odyssey 9, ask how a son of Poseidon could be a Cyclops, why Odysseus dared to tell the Phaeacians, who were Poseidon's descendants, that he had blinded him, why he slighted the god with “not even the earth-shaker will heal your eye”, and why Poseidon was angry over the punishment of so wicked a son. Aristotle answers with other strange offspring of gods, with the old enmity of the Phaeacians and the Cyclopes, with the god's unwillingness to heal the wicked, and with a father's right to help his injured son.

**A Cyclops born of Poseidon.** Polyphemus is the son of Poseidon and the nymph Thoosa, daughter of Phorcys (*Odyssey* 1.68–73), and he calls on the god as his father (*Odyssey* 9.519, 9.529; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). How then, Aristotle asked, did he come to be a Cyclops, when he was a Cyclops neither on his father's side nor on his mother's? He answered with other stories: horses are born of Boreas and mares, and the horse Pegasus of Poseidon and Medusa; what is strange, then, in this savage creature's being born of Poseidon? (*Homeric Problems* fr. 395 Gigon, from the scholia on *Odyssey* 9.106; plain renderings throughout). The Iliad tells the first story, of the mares of Erichthonius, with whom the North Wind lay “in the likeness of a dark-maned stallion” (*Iliad* 20.221–225; see [[boreas]]); Hesiod the second, of Medusa, with whom the dark-haired god lay in a soft meadow, and from whom, when Perseus cut off her head, great Chrysaor and the horse Pegasus sprang out (*Theogony* 278–281; see [[gorgon]]).

**The Phaeacians.** Why did Odysseus not fear to tell the Phaeacians, themselves descended from Poseidon, that he had blinded the god's son? Because, Aristotle answers, he knew that they were hostile to the Cyclopes, who had harassed them in Hypereia until they moved to Scheria (*Homeric Problems* fr. 396 Gigon, from the scholia on *Odyssey* 9.333 and 9.345; *Odyssey* 6.4–8; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[phaeacians]]).

**The earth-shaker will not heal.** The Cyclops ended his invitation with the claim that his father, if he wished, would heal him, “and no one else, / neither of the blessed gods nor of mortal men” (*Odyssey* 9.520–521), and Odysseus answers that not even the earth-shaker will heal his eye (*Odyssey* 9.523–525). Why did Odysseus slight Poseidon so senselessly? Antisthenes said that he knew that Poseidon was not a physician, but Apollo was; Aristotle, that Poseidon could heal him but would not, because of the Cyclops' wickedness (*Homeric Problems* fr. 397 Gigon, from the scholia on *Odyssey* 9.525; see [[antisthenes]] and [[apollo]]).

**Why the god was angry.** Why, then, was Poseidon angry, not over the saying but over the blinding? The poem says that he is angry “because of the Cyclops, whom he blinded of his eye” (*Odyssey* 1.69), though the Cyclops was utterly wicked and was eating the companions. Aristotle answers that a free man does not stand to a slave as a slave stands to a free man, nor do those who are near the gods stand in the same relation to those who are far from them. The Cyclops deserved punishment, but not at Odysseus' hands; he was for Poseidon to punish, if it is everywhere lawful for a father to help his injured son. The end of the text is damaged; it names the companions as the beginning of the injustice (fr. 397 Gigon). The poem itself gives the anger its course: Poseidon does not kill Odysseus but drives him wandering from his native land (*Odyssey* 1.74–75; see [[poseidon]]), and his son's prayer is heard (*Odyssey* 9.526–536; see [[curses]]).
