Glaucus and Diomedes have found that their grandfathers were guest-friends. They agree to avoid each other's spears and to exchange armor, so that the armies may know it (6.215–231; see [[xenia]] and [[guest-gifts]]). Then the narrator speaks: Zeus took away Glaucus' wits, for he gave gold for bronze. The phrase for his folly is the one Agamemnon uses of his own delusion, “Zeus took away my wits” (19.137). Whether the narrator is laughing at Glaucus, judging the bargain soberly, or pointing to his generosity is debated (see [[glaucus]] and [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]). Later Greeks took the words χρύσεα χαλκείων, “gold for bronze”, as a byword for an exchange in which one side gives far more than it gets.

**Plato.** In the *Symposium*, Alcibiades tells how he once offered himself to Socrates in return for his wisdom. Socrates answered that if Alcibiades saw in him a beauty far beyond his own good looks, and wanted to trade beauty for beauty, he meant to get much the better of the bargain: “you are trying to get truth in exchange for seeming, and in fact you intend to trade ‘gold for bronze’”. Then he warned him to look more closely, in case he was nothing at all: “the sight of the mind begins to see sharply when the sight of the eyes begins to lose its edge” (*Symposium* 218e–219a). Alcibiades would be Diomedes, getting gold for bronze, and Socrates the Glaucus who is asked to give it. Socrates declines the trade.

**Aristotle.** In the fifth book of the *Nicomachean Ethics* Aristotle asks whether a man can be treated unjustly with his own consent. A man can be harmed willingly, he answers, but no one is treated unjustly willingly. “The man who gives away what is his own, as Homer says Glaucus gave to Diomedes ‘gold for bronze, the worth of a hundred oxen for the worth of nine’, is not treated unjustly; for the giving is in his own power, but being treated unjustly is not: there must be someone who does the injustice” (*Nicomachean Ethics* 5.9, 1136b9–13). A few lines later he adds that the decent man tends to take less than his share, and gains more of another good, such as good repute or what is simply noble (1136b20–22). For Aristotle the bad bargain is a free gift, and no one wronged Glaucus.

**Cicero.** The phrase passed into the Greek that educated Romans mixed into their letters. Atticus had asked Cicero to answer a letter of his “gold for bronze”, giving back more than he had been sent. Cicero began his reply by refusing (he would answer “not gold for bronze, as you ask”, and would keep to Atticus' order of topics), and ended it: “not, as you asked, gold for bronze, but like for like” (*Letters to Atticus* 6.1.1, 6.1.22).

Erasmus gathered the phrase into his *Adages* as *Diomedis et Glauci permutatio*, “the exchange of Diomedes and Glaucus”.
