**Homer.** Sleep, waiting unseen in the tallest fir on Ida while Hera goes up to Zeus, is like a shrill-voiced bird “which in the mountains / the gods call chalcis, and men cymindis” (14.290–291; see [[sleep]] and [[chalcis-and-cymindis]]). The Iliad gives the language of the gods three other times: the Hundred-Hander whom the gods call Briareus and men Aegaeon (1.403–404), the mound that men call Batieia and the immortals the tomb of Myrine (2.813–814), and the river that the gods call Xanthus and men Scamander (20.74; see [[language-of-gods-and-men]]).

**Plato.** The *Cratylus* asks whether names are correct by nature or only by agreement. Hermogenes, who holds the second view, asks what this correctness is. Socrates sends him first to the sophists, then, since he will not learn from Protagoras, to Homer and the other poets (391b–d). Homer speaks of names in many places, he says, “but the greatest and finest are those in which he distinguishes, for the same things, the names that men call them by and those the gods call them by”; for the gods, plainly, call things by the names that are correct by nature (391d). He gives three cases. The river at Troy that fought Hephaestus in single combat, “whom the gods call Xanthus, and men Scamander” (391e, on 20.73–74): is it not a solemn thing to know why it is more correct to call it Xanthus? “Or, if you like, about the bird of which he says χαλκίδα κικλήσκουσι θεοί, ἄνδρες δὲ κύμινδιν: do you think it a trifling lesson, how much more correct it is for the same bird to be called chalcis than cymindis?” And Batieia and Myrine, and many others, in this poet and in others (392a). But these questions, he decides, are perhaps too great for him and Hermogenes to find out; the two names of Hector's son, Scamandrius and Astyanax, are more within human reach (392b; see [[plato-astyanax]]).

Socrates' three cases are the poem's own, though he shortens one of them: Homer's gods call the mound not Myrine but “the tomb of much-bounding Myrine” (2.814; see [[batieia]] and [[myrine]]). His question about the bird is never answered in the dialogue. The argument that follows, on the names that men and women gave Hector's son, works only with human names.

**Proclus.** In his commentary on the dialogue Proclus gives an answer. Inspired poets, he says, ascribed many names to the gods' own speech and set the human names against them, taking the first from inspiration and the second from perception and opinion, and Homer shows this. The names the gods give are smoother, more euphonious and of fewer syllables than men's: Xanthus than Scamander, chalcis than cymindis, Myrine than Batieia. The river is Xanthus, “yellow”, because, as Aristotle reports, the animals that drink from it have yellower coats; men, thinking superficially, called it Scamander because its water runs through a trench (σκάφη) made by hand. The chalcis was named for its clear and euphonious voice, ringing like bronze (χαλκός), and the Chaldaeans too call it so, having heard the name from the gods; the cymindis was named for the smallness of the bird. Myrine is named from the soul that was allotted that place by the gods, Batieia perhaps from a plant that grows there in abundance. The three pairs show three differences between divine and human knowledge (*On the Cratylus* 71; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).

Aristotle's report is in the *History of Animals*, which says that the Scamander is thought to make sheep yellow, and that this is why Homer is said to call it Xanthus instead of Scamander (3.12; see [[scamander]] and [[aristotle]]). The same work describes the cymindis as a real bird, seldom seen because it lives in the mountains, black, the size of the hawk called the dove-killer, and long and slender in shape; “cymindis” is what the Ionians call it (*History of Animals* 9.12; see [[chalcis-and-cymindis]]). By Aristotle's account the bird is not small, and its human name is the name in use in Ionia.
