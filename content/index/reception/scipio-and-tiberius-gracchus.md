**Plutarch.** The *Life of Tiberius Gracchus* ends with the city after his death. The senate, trying to soothe the people, gave up its opposition to the distribution of the public land; and since the people were bitter and plainly waiting for revenge, it sent to Asia Nasica, whom the people cursed as defiled with the murder of an inviolable and sacred person, and there he wandered in disgrace and soon died at Pergamum (*Tiberius Gracchus* 21.1–3). “It is no wonder that the people so hated Nasica, when even Scipio Africanus, whom the Romans seem to have loved more justly and more than any other, came near to losing the people's goodwill, because first, at Numantia, when he learned of the death of Tiberius, he cried out from the verses of Homer, ὣς ἀπόλοιτο καὶ ἄλλος ὅτις τοιαῦτά γε ῥέζοι; and then, when Gaius and Fulvius asked him in an assembly what he thought of the death of Tiberius, he gave an answer that showed no liking for what Tiberius had done in public life” (*Tiberius Gracchus* 21.4–5). The people interrupted him, as they had never done before, and Scipio was provoked to abuse them; the details, Plutarch says, are in his *Life of Scipio*, which has not survived (*Tiberius Gracchus* 21.5). Scipio was the younger Scipio, under whom Tiberius had served in Africa, “who had married his sister” (*Tiberius Gracchus* 4.4).

**Cicero.** Cicero twice gives Scipio's answer, made, as he tells it, to the tribune Gaius Carbo, who put the question to him in a public meeting to stir up trouble: Tiberius “seemed to have been justly killed” (*iure caesum videri*, *Pro Milone* 8; *On the Orator* 2.106). In the defense of Milo it is an argument that killing is sometimes lawful: Cicero asks the jury whether they think Africanus mad.

**The verse.** In the poem the line is Athena's answer to Zeus. Zeus has told the gods how Aegisthus married the wife of the son of Atreus and killed him on his return, though the gods had sent Hermes to warn him that vengeance would come from Orestes: “and now he has paid back all at once” (*Odyssey* 1.32–43). Athena agrees, “yes, truly that man lies fallen in a fitting destruction; / so may any other perish too who did such things”, and turns at once to Odysseus (*Odyssey* 1.46–48). In the poem the wish falls on the man who took a returning king's wife and killed him; Scipio turned it on a tribune of the people (*Tiberius Gracchus* 8).
