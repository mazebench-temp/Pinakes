**Homer.** The dancing floor on the Shield is compared to one that Daedalus made for Ariadne at Cnossus, the only place in the description where a mortal or a city is named (18.590–592). χορός is both the dance and the floor it is danced on; here it is the floor, which Hephaestus inlays, and a few lines later the dance, around which the crowd stands (18.590, 18.603; see [[the-dancing-floor-of-ariadne]], [[daedalus]] and [[ariadne]]).

**Pausanias.** Pausanias ends his account of Lebadea and its oracle of Trophonius with the works of Daedalus. Two of them are in Boeotia, a Heracles at Thebes and the Trophonius at Lebadea, and as many wooden images in Crete, a Britomartis at Olous and an Athena at Cnossus; “and among the Cnossians there is also the dance of Ariadne, which Homer too mentioned in the Iliad, worked in relief on white stone” (ἐπειργασμένος ἐπὶ λευκοῦ λίθου). The Delians have a small wooden Aphrodite, its right hand worn away by time, ending in a square base instead of feet. Pausanias believes that Ariadne had it from Daedalus and took it with her when she followed Theseus; the Delians say that Theseus, robbed of her, dedicated it to Delian Apollo, so as not to take home a reminder of Ariadne and of his grief. Of the other works of Daedalus he knows of none still in being: those dedicated by the Argives in the Heraeum and those brought to Gela in Sicily from Omphace have been destroyed by time (*Description of Greece* 9.40.3–4; see [[pausanias]]).

**The dance and the floor.** Pausanias' χορός is a carving, a dance shown in relief on stone, and he counts it among the works of Daedalus; he does not say that he saw it. Homer's is a place made for dancing, and the comparison is of the god's work to the mortal's. Plato's Socrates, too, knows Daedalus as a sculptor, and tells Meno that his statues run away unless they are tied down, like true opinions until they are bound by reasoning (*Meno* 97d–98a; see [[aristotle-the-tripods-of-hephaestus]]). Lucian, too, speaks of what Homer made on the shield about Ariadne and “the dance that Daedalus fashioned for her” (*On the Dance* 13; see [[the-singer-on-the-shield]]).
