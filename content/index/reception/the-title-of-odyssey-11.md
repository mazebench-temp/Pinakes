The title of *Odyssey* 11 in Merry's edition is Νέκυια (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, heading of Book 11); this library calls the book “The Land of the Dead”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter of the alphabet, this one is λ, lambda (see [[title-of-book-5]]). Its story is told under [[odysseus-in-the-land-of-the-dead]], what the dead are in Homer under [[the-land-of-the-dead]], and the whole of Odysseus' tale, which runs from *Odyssey* 9 to 12, under [[the-tale-to-alcinous]].

**The word.** Νέκυια is formed from νέκυς, “a dead man”, the word of the book's formula for the souls that gather at the pit, ψυχαὶ … νεκύων κατατεθνηώτων, “the souls of the dead who have died” (*Odyssey* 11.37; see [[the-dead-who-have-died]]); Homer does not use νέκυια itself. The Suda knows it as the name of a festival of the dead, which the Persians called φρουδίγα (Suda ν 146), and as the title of a comedy by Sopater (Suda σ 846, citing Athenaeus). A compound names the place where such questioning was done, the νεκυομαντεῖον, “oracle of the dead”: Periander of Corinth sent messengers to the one on the river Acheron in Thesprotia, to ask the dead Melissa where a deposit of a guest-friend lay (Herodotus 5.92η, a plain rendering; see [[herodotus]] and [[acheron]]).

**Early witnesses.** The *Minos*, transmitted with Plato's dialogues, uses the name of the book when it praises Homer's Minos: “and in the Nekyia of the Odyssey he has made Minos judging, holding a golden scepter, not Rhadamanthys” (Ὀδυσσείας ἐν Νεκυίᾳ, *Minos* 319d, plain renderings; see [[minos]]). Hereas of Megara, as Plutarch reports him, said that Peisistratus had inserted a verse on Theseus “into Homer's Nekyia”, ἐς τὴν Ὁμήρου νέκυιαν (Plutarch, *Theseus* 20, as Merry quotes it in his critical note on *Odyssey* 11.631; see [[theseus-line]]). In Plutarch's *Table Talk* Elpenor is the one “of the Homeric souls that he named in the Nekyia” who, his body still unburied, wanders as if on the border, not yet mingled with those in Hades (*Table Talk* 9.5, as Merry quotes it on *Odyssey* 11.51; see [[elpenor]]). Strabo writes that his predecessors told “the story of the Homeric Nekyia”, τὰ περὶ τὴν νέκυιαν τὴν Ὁμηρικήν, at Lake Avernus near Cumae, where there was said to have been an oracle of the dead to which Odysseus came, and placed the Cimmerians there; Agrippa's clearing of the woods, he says, showed it all to be myth (*Geography* 5.4.5; see [[cimmerians]] and [[strabo]]). Elsewhere, defending the view that Odysseus' wanderings were set around Sicily and Italy, he asks what poet or historian persuaded the people of Cumae to speak of Pyriphlegethon, the Acherusian lake and “the oracle of the dead at Avernus” (*Geography* 1.2.18, plain renderings).

**Proclus' Nekyias.** Proclus uses the name freely. Odysseus “shows it in the Nekyia” when he calls what he saw of Heracles a phantom (*On the Republic* I 172 Kroll; see [[heracles]]); Plato took the figure of Minos with his golden scepter “from Homer's Nekyia” (I 168; see [[minos]]); and the tale to Alcinous, to which Plato alludes at the start of the myth of Er, means “the Nekyia of Odysseus in the house of Alcinous”, the sights of Hades that he tells (II 111 Kroll; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-9]]). He gives the name to Plato's own myths as well: he bids the reader consider “the Nekyias in both” poets, Homer's composed in poetic and divine madness and Plato's worked out in emulation of Homer's (I 168), and says that Plato, beginning his Nekyia in the *Republic* with the jest about the tale to Alcinous, all but says outright that he has set Homer's Nekyia before himself as his model (I 169; see [[plato-the-choice-of-lives]]); the myth of the *Phaedo* is “the Nekyia of the *Phaedo*” (II 179 Kroll; see [[plato]] and [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).

**A rhapsody of its own.** Merry thought the episode of Elpenor probably a later addition, from a time “when the νέκυια was recited as a separate rhapsody, and when there were temptations to enlarge and develope the original story” (Merry on *Odyssey* 11.51–83), and he took the last sights, from Minos to Heracles, for another such addition (Merry on *Odyssey* 11.565; see the Odyssey translation's note on 11.565–567). He reports Lauer's view that the whole book is the work of a Boeotian poet, since Boeotia was devoted to the worship of Hades and Persephone and had many oracles of the dead, of Amphiaraus at Thebes, of Teiresias at the springs of Tilphusa and of Trophonius at Lebadea, with an opening into the underworld at Coronea. Merry accepts the argument for the catalogue of heroines, whose women all come from Minyan and Theban legend, if *Odyssey* 11.321–325 are left out, the more because the Boeotians, as Lauer reminds him, were fond of lists of noble women, like Hesiod's *Catalogue of Women*; and he adds that the Catalogue of Ships was known by the title “the Boeotia” (Merry on *Odyssey* 11.225; see [[amphiaraus]], [[coronea]] and [[catalogue-of-ships]]).

**What the title covers.** The book is all Odysseus' tale, going on from *Odyssey* 10, except for the pause in the hall of Alcinous (*Odyssey* 11.333–384), where Odysseus breaks off after the heroines and goes on at the king's request. What he calls up is a consultation, not a descent. “Odysseus did not, like Aeneas, make a descent into the netherworld,” Merry writes, “but he sits at the edge of the trench, and questions the shades as they come forward and taste the blood” (on *Odyssey* 11.565): the rite is performed at the beginning of the book in the verses of Circe's instructions (*Odyssey* 11.23–50; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]), and at the end the din of the dead drives him back to the ship, and the stream of Oceanus carries it away (*Odyssey* 11.632–640). Merry calls the opening of the last book of the Odyssey, where Hermes leads the souls of the suitors to the meadow of asphodel, “the second νέκυια” (Merry on *Odyssey* 10.515; *Odyssey* 24.1–14, a plain rendering; see [[the-meadow-of-asphodel]]).

**Merry's headings.** Merry's titles for the four books of the tale are Ἀλκίνου ἀπόλογοι, Κυκλώπεια (Book 9); Τὰ περὶ Αἰόλου καὶ Λαιστρυγόνων καὶ Κίρκης (Book 10); Νέκυια (Book 11); and Σειρῆνες, Σκύλλα, Χάρυβδις, βόες Ἡλίου, “the Sirens, Scylla, Charybdis, the cattle of the Sun” (Book 12; Merry, headings of Books 9–12; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-9]], [[the-title-of-odyssey-10]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-12]]). Like the Cyclopeia, the Nekyia names the adventure and not the persons met in it.

**Later Nekyias.** Aeschylus wrote a play called the *Soul-Summoners*: in its fragments a chorus of the people who live by a lake honors Hermes as its ancestor, and someone is foretold a death from the spine of a sea creature (frs. 273, 275 Nauck; see [[aeschylus]]). Virgil's sixth book sends Aeneas down to the dead (see [[virgil-the-unburied]], [[virgil-the-silence-of-dido]] and [[virgil-the-punished-in-tartarus]]); Plato ends the *Republic* with the souls of Homer's heroes choosing their next lives (see [[plato-the-choice-of-lives]]); and Horace's Ulysses goes on questioning Tiresias (see [[horace-on-the-suitors]]).
