summary: Proetus sends Bellerophon to Lycia with “grim signs” scratched on a folded tablet, the only passage in Homer that describes anything like writing. Readers since antiquity have asked whether the signs were letters, and the tale of a man who carries his own death warrant has been compared with David's letter sent by the hand of Uriah.

Proetus will not kill Bellerophon himself: “his heart held back in awe from that”. He sends him to his wife's father, the king of Lycia, with σήματα λυγρά, “grim signs”, which he has scratched (γράψας) on a folded tablet, “many things to destroy a man's life”, and tells him to show them to the king. The king entertains his guest for nine days before he asks “to see the sign” that he brings from his son-in-law. When he has received “the evil sign”, he sends Bellerophon against the Chimaera (6.171–179; see [[bellerophon]] and [[proetus]]).

**The words.** σῆμα is any sign, and γράφω elsewhere in Homer means “scratch, graze” (see [[writing]]). The one other sign that a hero scratches is the mark Ajax puts on his lot before the duel with Hector. The herald carries the lot around the ranks, and no one knows it until it comes to Ajax, who “knew the mark on the lot when he saw it” (ἐπιγράψας 7.187, σῆμα 7.189). That sign meant something only to its owner. Proetus' signs are meant for another king, far away, who asks “to see the sign” (6.176). The translation's “signs” and “scratching” leave open what he saw.

**Ancient readers.** Pliny the Elder took the passage as evidence that writing tablets were in use before the Trojan War (*Natural History* 13.69). Josephus, arguing that the Greeks learned letters late, reports the view that Homer himself did not leave his poetry in writing: it was remembered, and put together later from the songs, which is why it has so many inconsistencies (*Against Apion* 1.12). The question returned when F. A. Wolf argued in his *Prolegomena ad Homerum* (1795) that the poems were composed without writing, and the tablet of Proetus has been cited on both sides ever since.

**What the signs were.** Nothing in the passage requires an alphabet. The signs may be pictures, or marks agreed between the two kings. The poet may be describing the alphabetic writing of his own day, then new in Greece, or the tale may keep a memory of the scripts of the Bronze Age palaces (see [[writing]]). The object itself is old. A wooden writing board with ivory hinges was recovered from the shipwreck at Uluburun, off the southern coast of Turkey, which sank in the late fourteenth century BCE. And the folded tablet kept its use for secret messages. Herodotus tells how the exiled Demaratus warned Sparta of Xerxes' plans: he scraped the wax from a folded tablet (δελτίον δίπτυχον), wrote on the wood, and covered the letters with wax again, so that the tablet would seem blank to the guards on the road (7.239). The question is not settled. Many readers think that the poet knew of writing and kept it out of the heroes' world, except in this one tale.

**The letter that kills its bearer.** In the second book of Samuel, David sends Uriah, whose wife he has taken, back to the army with a letter to Joab: “Set ye Uriah in the forefront of the hottest battle, and retire ye from him, that he may be smitten, and die” (2 Samuel 11.14–15). Folklorists name after this story the motif of a man who carries his own death warrant, “the Uriah letter”, and Bellerophon's tablet is its best-known Greek example. The two tales are often compared. Whether one borrowed from the other, or both drew on a story told widely in the eastern Mediterranean, cannot be shown. A fragmentary Sumerian legend of Sargon of Akkad, from the early second millennium BCE, appears to use the same device. The first half of Bellerophon's story, the queen who accuses the young man she failed to seduce, has its own famous parallel in the wife of Potiphar (Genesis 39.7–20; see [[anteia]]).

**The proverb.** The tale became proverbial. The Suda records “Bellerophon against himself”, “said of those who bring evils on themselves” (κ 41), and gives the king the name that later writers used for him, Iobates, who “was charged by the letters of Proetus to kill Bellerophon” (ι 400). In Plautus' *Bacchides* a slave who has delivered the tablets that get him tied up complains, “your son has made me a Bellerophon” (*Bacchides* 810–811). In *Hamlet*, Claudius sends the prince to England with a sealed commission for his death, and Hamlet opens it and writes a new one (act 5, scene 2).
