**The problem.** The Achaeans have been at Troy for nine years (2.134, 2.295), and until Book 7 their ships have lain on the shore unprotected. On the evening of the duel, when “many long-haired Achaeans are dead” (7.328), Nestor proposes a mound over the dead and against it towers, gates and a ditch; after a day's truce the Achaeans build a wall with towers and a staked ditch (7.327–343, 7.433–441; see [[nestor]] and [[the-building-of-the-wall]]). From then on it is one of the main scenes of the war. The Trojans camp on the plain before it (8.553–565), Hector breaks its gate (12.445–471), and Apollo knocks it down as a boy knocks down sand by the sea (15.361–366). Why was it built so late?

**Ancient answers.** Thucydides placed it at the start of the war: the Greeks must have won a battle when they landed, or they could not have walled their camp (1.11.1; see [[thucydides-and-the-wall]]). Strabo, following Demetrius of Scepsis, took Homer at his word and blamed both armies, the Achaeans for leaving their camp open for nine years so near the city, the Trojans for never attacking it (13.1.36). Aristotle, as Strabo reports, cut the knot: the wall never existed, and the poet who invented it made it vanish (see [[aristotle-and-the-wall]]). The gods' scene that follows the building (7.443–464) was suspected by some ancient critics, and by modern ones. It tells in brief what Book 12 tells at length (12.5–6 ≈ 7.449–450, 12.31 ≈ 7.462), and it has Poseidon and Apollo build Laomedon's wall together (7.452–453), where Poseidon later says that he built it while Apollo herded Laomedon's cattle (21.446–449).

**Modern answers.** For the analytic critics the wall's lateness was a sign of later work. Denys Page argued that the Iliad Thucydides knew did not yet tell of the building, and that the passages of Book 7 were added afterward (*History and the Homeric Iliad*, 1959). Others answer from within the poem, and Achilles himself explains the wall. While he fought, he tells the embassy, Hector would not lead the battle out from the city; now that he has withdrawn, Agamemnon has built a wall and a ditch, and even so cannot hold Hector back (9.348–355; see [[achilles]]). The wall measures what his absence has cost. Many readers also note how the Iliad draws the whole war into its few days: the Catalogue musters the army as if at Aulis, and Helen names the Achaean leaders to Priam as if he saw them for the first time (see [[the-boeotia]] and [[the-view-from-the-wall]]). The fortifying of the camp may be another such scene.

**The wall's end.** The poem that builds the wall also removes it from the world (12.3–35), so that its absence from the Troad needs no excuse. Ruth Scodel compared the destruction by the rivers and Zeus' rain to the floods that end an age in Near Eastern and Greek myth; Book 12 calls those who fell at Troy “the race of demigods” (ἡμιθέων γένος ἀνδρῶν, 12.23), the only use of the word in Homer (“The Achaean Wall and the Myth of Destruction”, *Harvard Studies in Classical Philology* 86, 1982). Poseidon fears that the wall's fame “will reach as far as the dawn spreads” (7.451), and Zeus answers that Poseidon's own fame will reach as far (7.458). The wall's fame lasts only in the poem (see [[kleos]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]).
