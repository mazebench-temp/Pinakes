Aristotle's examples of activity (ἐνέργεια), the quality that sets things before the eyes, are lines of Homer in which lifeless things act like living ones: Sisyphus' stone that rolls back “shameless” (*Odyssey* 11.598); the arrow that “flew off” the corselet (*Iliad* 13.587); the arrow “eager to fly into the throng” (4.126); the spears that “stood fixed in the ground, longing to glut themselves on flesh” (11.574 = 15.317); the spear point that sped through the chest “in its fury” (15.542); and, in a simile, the waves “arched and white-crested, some in front, and others behind” (13.799). The Iliad lines agree with our text, though Aristotle shortens 13.587 to ἔπτατʼ ὀιστός, “the arrow flew”; in the Odyssey line he has ἐπὶ δάπεδόνδε, “down to the ground”, where our text has ἔπειτα πέδονδε, “then to the plain”.

The shamelessness of the stone is a metaphor by analogy: “as the stone is to Sisyphus, so the shameless man is to the one he treats shamelessly” (*Rhetoric* 3.11, 1412a6–7). Homer's art, Aristotle says, is to make everything moving and alive, “and activity is movement” (*Rhetoric* 1412a10–11). See [[before-the-eyes]], [[metaphora]] and [[homer]].
