Shakespeare's play takes its love story from the medieval tradition (see [[from-briseis-to-cressida]]), but its Greek camp owes much to Homer, whom Shakespeare could read in George Chapman's *Seven Books of the Iliads* (1598). That volume included Book 2, and from Book 2 comes Thersites.

Homer's Thersites is a man "of unmeasured words", who quarrels with the kings "idly and against all order" to make the army laugh (2.212–216). He is the ugliest man at Troy and hated most by Achilles and Odysseus, whom he always abuses (2.216–221). Shakespeare keeps the type and enlarges the part. Ulysses calls him "a slave whose gall coins slanders like a mint" (1.3). He attaches himself first to Ajax and then to Achilles as a licensed railer, and he mocks everyone, Greek and Trojan. As Odysseus beats him with the scepter in the Iliad (2.265–269), Ajax beats him on stage (2.1).

But the play also gives him the last word on the war. Watching Cressida give herself to Diomedes in the Greek camp, he says: "Lechery, lechery; still wars and lechery; nothing else holds fashion" (5.2). In Homer the army laughs at Thersites, and calls Odysseus' beating of him the best thing Odysseus has done (2.270–277). In Shakespeare the railer's view of the heroes comes close to the play's own.
