In *Politics* 3.14 Aristotle sorts the kinds of kingship. The Spartan kind, he says, is a generalship held for life. The king has no power to put a man to death, except on campaign, as among the ancients, "by the law of the hand". Homer shows it: Agamemnon put up with abuse in the assemblies, but once the army had marched out he had power even to kill. As proof Aristotle quotes the end of Agamemnon's speech before the first battle: whomever he sees hanging back from the fighting will have no hope of escaping the dogs and the birds, "for death is in my hand" (πὰρ γὰρ ἐμοὶ θάνατος).

Those last words are not in our manuscripts, where the speech ends with the dogs and the birds (2.393). Either the text Aristotle used had an extra phrase here, or he supplied one from memory. Modern editions, including Monro and Allen, do not print them.

The passage is also a reading of Book 2. The abuse Agamemnon endured in assembly is presumably Achilles' in Book 1 and Thersites' in this book (2.225–242). Aristotle saw what the book dramatizes: a king who cannot command the assembly still holds, on the field, the power of death.
