**Aristotle.** The first book of the *Metaphysics* reviews what earlier thinkers said about the causes. Most of the first philosophers, Aristotle says, thought that the only principles of all things were principles in the form of matter: that of which all things consist, from which they first come to be and into which they finally perish, while the substance remains. Thales, the founder of this kind of philosophy, says that the principle is water, and so declared that the earth rests on water. He took the view perhaps from seeing that the nourishment of all things is moist, and that heat itself comes from the moist and lives by it, and that the seeds of all things have a moist nature. Then Aristotle adds: “There are some who think that the very ancient men, long before the present generation, who first gave accounts of the gods, also held this view of nature. For they made Oceanus and Tethys the parents of becoming (τῆς γενέσεως πατέρας), and the oath of the gods water, which they themselves call Styx; for what is oldest is most honored, and an oath is the most honored thing” (983b27–33). Whether this opinion about nature is in fact old and ancient may be unclear, he goes on, but Thales at least is said to have spoken in this way about the first cause.

**The poets behind it.** Aristotle names no poet, but both of his proofs are in Book 14. “The parents of becoming” are Hera's “Oceanus, the origin of the gods, and mother Tethys” (14.201), with the gods widened to all that comes to be, as Sleep widens them when he calls Oceanus “the origin of all” (14.246; see [[oceanus]] and [[tethys]]). The oath is the one that Sleep demands of Hera, “by the inviolable water of Styx”, with one hand on the earth and the other on the sea, and the Titans below for witnesses (14.271–279; see [[styx]] and [[oath]]). In Book 15 Hera swears again by the water of Styx, which, the verse says, is the greatest and most dread oath for the blessed gods (ὅς τε μέγιστος / ὅρκος δεινότατός τε, 15.37–38), and the Catalogue makes the Titaressus a branch of “the water of Styx, the dread oath” (2.755). Aristotle's reasoning makes the water of the oath the oldest thing. Homer does not say that the Styx is old; Hesiod does. In the *Theogony* Styx is the eldest (πρεσβυτάτη) of the daughters of Oceanus, and Zeus made her the great oath of the gods because she came first to his side in the war against the Titans (*Theogony* 383–403, 775–777; see [[hesiod]]).

**The first principle.** In the last book of the *Metaphysics* Aristotle comes back to the old poets. They hold, he says, that not the first powers, “such as Night and Heaven, or Chaos, or Oceanus”, are king and rule, but Zeus (Ν 4, 1091b4–6). Here Oceanus stands among the first things, and the poets' world is ruled by a latecomer.

Plato's Socrates had read the same verse as proof that all things flow (see [[plato-oceanus-and-tethys]]). Aristotle reads it as water, the element of Thales; Strabo, as the Ocean that encircles the earth at its limits (see [[strabo]]).
