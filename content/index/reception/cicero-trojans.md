Hector's answer to Andromache begins with shame: he would feel shame (αἰδέομαι) before the Trojans and the Trojan women if he shrank from the war like a coward (see [[aidos]]). He speaks the same line again in Book 22, alone before the gates as Achilles comes on. He led his people to ruin by his own recklessness, when Polydamas had told him to take the Trojans back into the city; now he feels shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women, for fear some lesser man will say, “Hector trusted his own strength and destroyed the people” (22.99–107). In both places the line is the voice of a man who lives in the eyes of his city.

Cicero made it his own, in the Greek that he mixed into his letters to Atticus. In 59 BCE, when the men then in power offered him a mission abroad, he wrote that he had long wanted to see Alexandria and the rest of Egypt, and to get away from Rome for a while, “but at this time, and with these men sending me, αἰδέομαι Τρῶας καὶ Τρῳάδας ἑλκεσιπέπλους. For what will our optimates say, if any are left? That I was bought off my principles by some reward?” Then he quoted Hector's other line from Book 22, “Polydamas will be the first to put reproach on me” (22.100), and named his Polydamas: “our Cato, who to me is worth a hundred thousand” (*Letters to Atticus* 2.5.1). What history would say of him six hundred years on, he added, he feared still more than the gossip of the living.

The Trojans come back whenever he has to choose a side. In 50 BCE, as the quarrel between Caesar and Pompey came to a head, he feared to change his vote, feeling shame “not only before Pompey but before the Trojans and the Trojan women”, and Polydamas came to mind again (7.1.4). In 49 BCE, deciding whether to follow Pompey out of Italy, he wrote “αἰδέομαι Τρῶας”, and that the duty of a friend as well as of a citizen called him back (7.12.3); a few weeks later, asking who these optimates were who would drive him out while they stayed at home themselves, he still wrote, “whoever they are, αἰδέομαι” (8.16.2). By 45 BCE the half-line was enough (13.13.2).

Hector's Trojans are the people he defends, and he will die rather than face their reproach. Cicero's are the men of his own class, whose judgment he could not do without.
