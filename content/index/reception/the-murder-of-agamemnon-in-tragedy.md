title: The murder of Agamemnon in tragedy

summary: Nestor's account in Book 3 of the Odyssey is the poem's fullest telling of the murder and the vengeance, and the tragedians of Athens changed it at the points they made central. Homer's Aegisthus wins Clytemnestra with words and kills the king, while she, “for her mind was good”, at first refused; his Orestes comes back from Athens to kill Aegisthus, where the tragic Orestes, reared in Phocis, kills his mother and goes to Athens to be judged; and his Menelaus comes home on the day of the funeral feast, where Euripides' Menelaus is at Nauplia on the sixth day after the matricide.

**Nestor's account.** Telemachus asks Nestor how Agamemnon died, where Menelaus was, and how Aegisthus came to kill “a far better man” (*Odyssey* 3.248–252), and Nestor answers with the poem's fullest telling of the story (*Odyssey* 3.254–312; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). While the others toiled at Troy, Aegisthus won Agamemnon's wife with words. She refused at first, and a singer was with her to guard her, until Aegisthus took him to a desert island and left him for the birds (*Odyssey* 3.262–272; see [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]]). Aegisthus killed the king and ruled Mycenae for seven years, and in the eighth “brilliant Orestes came, an evil for him, / back from Athens, and killed his father's slayer”, and gave the Argives a funeral feast; on the same day Menelaus came home (*Odyssey* 3.303–312). The tragedians of Athens kept the persons and changed the story where they made it their own.

**Who planned and who struck.** In Nestor's account Aegisthus acts. He “kept bewitching the wife of Agamemnon with many words” (*Odyssey* 3.264), and he “devised these grim things at home, / killing the son of Atreus” (*Odyssey* 3.303–304). In Aeschylus' *Agamemnon* the plan is the queen's. The watchman in the first scene speaks of “a woman's heart that plans like a man” (γυναικὸς ἀνδρόβουλον … κέαρ, *Agamemnon* 11), and Clytemnestra, standing over the body, tells how she cast a net around her husband and struck him three times (1379–1387; see [[aeschylus]]). The Aegisthus of the tragedy keeps two marks of the Odyssey's. He stayed at home while the others fought, as Homer's Aegisthus sat “undisturbed, in a corner of horse-pasturing Argos” (*Odyssey* 3.263): Cassandra foresees “a cowardly lion rolling in the bed, / keeping house” (λέοντʼ ἄναλκιν ἐν λέχει στρωφώμενον / οἰκουρόν, *Agamemnon* 1224–1225), and the chorus calls him “Woman!”, who kept house while the men came home from battle (1625–1627). And he is a coward. Merry sets the “cowardly lion” beside Nestor's “the coward Aegisthus” (ἀνάλκιδος Αἰγίσθοιο, *Odyssey* 3.310; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.310; see [[aegisthus]]).

**Clytemnestra's good mind.** Homer's queen “at first refused the shameful deed, / brilliant Clytemnestra; for her mind was good”, and gave way only “when the fate of the gods fettered her to be overcome” (*Odyssey* 3.265–266, 3.269; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 3.269). Nestor gives her no part in the killing, though Athena, in Mentor's likeness, says that Agamemnon perished “by the guile of Aegisthus and of his own wife” (*Odyssey* 3.234–235), and the shade of Agamemnon will say that she contrived his death (*Odyssey* 11.409–410, 11.429–430; see [[clytemnestra]]). The tragic Clytemnestra needs no seducer. Aeschylus gives her a cause of her own: Agamemnon, she tells the elders, sacrificed his own child, the dearest fruit of her labor, as a charm against the winds from Thrace, and no one drove him from the land for it (*Agamemnon* 1415–1420; see [[aeschylus-aulis]]).

**Back from Athens.** Homer's Orestes comes “back from Athens” (ἂψ ἀπʼ Ἀθηνάων, *Odyssey* 3.307; see [[athens]]). In the tragic legend, Merry notes, he came from Phocis, and Aeschylus makes him visit Athens only after the deed (on 3.307; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 3.307). So it is in the plays. In the *Agamemnon* Clytemnestra tells her husband that their son is not at home, since “Strophius the Phocian”, a friend bound to the house by guest-friendship, is rearing him (*Agamemnon* 877–881). In the *Libation Bearers* Orestes comes to his father's door as “a stranger, a Daulian from Phocis” (ξένος μέν εἰμι Δαυλιεὺς ἐκ Φωκέων, 674), and kills Aegisthus and then his mother, when Pylades reminds him of Apollo's oracles (899–902; see [[libation-bearers]] and [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]]). In the *Eumenides* Apollo sends him, pursued by his mother's Furies, to “the city of Pallas”, to sit clasping her ancient image, “for I persuaded you to kill your mother” (*Eumenides* 79–84), and there Athena declares him acquitted, the votes being equal (752–753; see [[areopagus]]). Euripides keeps the Phocian childhood: the old servant stole the boy away when Aegisthus was about to kill him, and gave him to Strophius “to rear in the land of the Phocians” (*Electra* 14–18). At the end of the play the Dioscuri send him, a matricide who may no longer set foot in Argos, to Athens to clasp the image of Pallas (*Electra* 1250–1257; see [[euripides]]).

**At the sacrifice.** Homer says only that Orestes killed Aegisthus. Euripides sets the killing at a sacrifice. Aegisthus, met in his garden, greets the two travelers, “Greetings, strangers! Who are you? Where do you come from? From what land?”, hears that they are Thessalians on their way to sacrifice to Olympian Zeus at the Alpheus, and asks them to share his feast, since he is sacrificing an ox to the Nymphs (*Electra* 779–787). As he bends over the entrails, Orestes strikes him in the spine with the cleaver (838–843). In the Odyssey it is Nestor who welcomes strangers at a sacrifice and asks them, after the meal, who they are and where they sail from (*Odyssey* 3.31–74; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]); Homer's Aegisthus sacrifices too, burning thigh-pieces on the gods' altars and hanging up offerings when he has won the queen (*Odyssey* 3.273–275).

**The day Menelaus came.** Nestor ends with Menelaus: “and on the same day Menelaus, good at the war cry, came to him, / bringing many possessions, as much as his ships could carry as their load” (*Odyssey* 3.311–312; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). Euripides' *Orestes* opens on the sixth day after the mother's death. Orestes lies sick with madness, the Argives are to vote that day on whether brother and sister shall be stoned, and their hope is Menelaus, who “has come to this land from Troy” and lies at anchor in the harbor of Nauplia after long wandering (*Orestes* 39–56; see [[orestes-play]]). Homer's Menelaus comes home on the day of the funeral feast, when the vengeance is done; Euripides' comes in the days of his nephew's trial, and does not save him (see [[menelaus]]).

**The example.** In the Odyssey the vengeance is an example for a son. Nestor ends his first account of it by telling Telemachus to be valiant, “so that some one of those born hereafter may speak well of you” (*Odyssey* 3.196–200; see [[so-good-a-thing-is-a-son]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]), and Telemachus answers that the Achaeans will carry Orestes' fame wide (*Odyssey* 3.203–204). In tragedy the same deed is the killing of a mother, and the story follows the son beyond it, to the Furies, the trial and the verdict.
