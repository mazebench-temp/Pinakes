Before Xerxes' invasion of 480 BCE, the Greeks sent envoys to Gelon, the ruler of Syracuse, to ask for his help. He offered a large army and fleet on condition that he have the supreme command. The Spartan envoy refused in Homeric words: "Surely Agamemnon, the descendant of Pelops, would groan aloud" to hear that the Spartans had lost the command to Gelon (7.159), a line modeled on Nestor's "surely old Peleus, driver of horses, would groan aloud" (7.125).

Gelon then asked for command of the fleet. The Athenian envoy answered that Athens, with the largest fleet in Greece, would yield it to no one but the Spartans. The Athenians, he said, were the oldest people in Greece and the only Greeks who had never left their land, "and Homer the epic poet said that the best man who came to Ilion at ordering and marshaling an army was one of ours" (7.161).

The claim rests on the Catalogue's praise of Menestheus: no man on earth was his equal "at marshaling horses and shield-bearing men" (2.553–554). The envoy leaves out the next line, "Nestor alone could rival him, for he was older" (2.555). The praise is large for a leader who does little in the rest of the Iliad, and it was useful to Athens. On another Athenian use of the Catalogue, see [[solon-and-salamis]].
