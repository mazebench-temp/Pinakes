The traditional titles of Book 9 are Πρεσβεία πρὸς Ἀχιλλέα, “the embassy to Achilles”, and Λιταί, “the Prayers”. The first names the story of the night, the envoys sent from Agamemnon's hut with the offer of gifts (9.89–713; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]] and [[embassy]]). The second names the allegory at the center of Phoenix's speech, the lame and wrinkled daughters of Zeus who follow Blind Folly and heal the harm she does (9.502–512; see [[litai]] and [[ate]]). Plato already knew the episode by the second name, before the time to which the division of the poem into twenty-four books is usually dated; in that division this book is Ι, iota (see [[title-of-book-5]]).

**The Prayers.** The oldest witnesses use the second name, and use it for the whole episode. In Plato's *Hippias Minor* the sophist Hippias proves that Homer made Achilles “most simple and most true” from what Achilles says to Odysseus “in the Prayers” (ἐν Λιταῖς), and recites his first words, “hateful to me as the gates of Hades …” (364e–365b; see [[plato-hippias-minor]]). In the *Cratylus* it is Cratylus who quotes “what Achilles says in the Prayers to Ajax”, “Telamonian Ajax, sprung from Zeus, lord of the people, all that you have said seems to have been spoken after my own heart”, and turns it as a compliment on Socrates, whose account of names has pleased him (428c, on 9.644–645). Both passages come from Achilles' answers to the envoys, not from Phoenix's allegory. The name suits the book as a whole: λιταί are entreaties, and the book is full of men entreating. Phoenix's mother begged him by his knees (λισσέσκετο, 9.451), Agamemnon “has sent the best men to entreat you” (λίσσεσθαι, 9.520), the elders begged Meleager (λίσσοντο, 9.574), and Diomedes wishes that Agamemnon had never entreated Achilles at all (λίσσεσθαι, 9.698; see [[supplication]]).

**The embassy.** Strabo, arguing that Homer gives Odysseus the wisdom of the orator, lists the places where he shows it: “in the Testing, in the Prayers, in the Embassy, in which he says, ‘But when he let the great voice go from his chest, and words like the snowflakes of winter, then no other mortal could rival Odysseus’” (*Geography* 1.2.5). The Testing (Διάπειρα) is Agamemnon's test of the army in Book 2, where Odysseus turns the men back from the ships (see [[the-test-of-the-army]]). The Prayers is Book 9. Strabo's Embassy is another: the verses he quotes are Antenor's memory of Odysseus and Menelaus, who came to Troy “on an embassy” on Helen's account (3.205–224; see [[the-embassy-to-troy]]). The fuller title of Book 9 names the man to whom the envoys went; Homer tells of more than one embassy.

**The words.** πρεσβεία, “embassy”, is a word of later Greek, formed from πρέσβυς, “old man, elder”, whose plural πρέσβεις came to mean “envoys”. Homer's word for an envoy's errand is ἀγγελίη, the word for Menelaus' embassy to Troy (3.206, 11.140) and for Tydeus' to Thebes (4.384). Achilles uses it as he sends the envoys away: “declare my message—for that is the prize of the old” (ἀγγελίην ἀπόφασθε· τὸ γὰρ γέρας ἐστὶ γερόντων, 9.422; the first words again at 9.649; see [[prize-of-the-old]]). He calls the carrying of his answer the privilege of the old, and the later title, made from the word for an elder, says much the same.
