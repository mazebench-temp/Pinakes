summary: In the first book of the Aeneid Dido comes to Juno's temple at Carthage as Diana leads her dances on the banks of the Eurotas or the ridges of Cynthus among her mountain nymphs, Virgil's version of the simile that likens Nausicaa among her handmaids to Artemis among her nymphs. Valerius Probus, as Gellius reports, judged it the least happy of all Virgil's borrowings from Homer. The same book draws on the rest of Odyssey 6. The shipwrecked Aeneas meets his mother disguised as a huntress and asks whether she is a goddess, Phoebus' sister, and when he appears before Dido his mother has given him beauty, as silver is set in gold.

**Homer.** When the clothes are washed and spread to dry, Nausicaa and her handmaids eat beside the river and play with a ball, and she begins the song and dance (*Odyssey* 6.96–101). The poet likens her among them to Artemis among her nymphs on the mountains, and the point of the likeness is the last verse but one: “she is easy to know, though all are beautiful” (*Odyssey* 6.108; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]] and [[nausicaa]]). Odysseus, waking, makes the likeness himself: “to Artemis, the daughter of great Zeus, / I liken you most nearly, in looks and stature and build” (*Odyssey* 6.151–152).

**Dido.** In the first book of the *Aeneid* Aeneas, cast ashore in Libya, has come unseen into Carthage and stands in the temple of Juno, gazing at the pictures of the war at Troy, when the queen comes to the temple, “Dido, most beautiful in form, with a great throng of young men around her” (*regina ad templum, forma pulcherrima Dido, / incessit magna iuvenum stipante caterva*, *Aeneid* 1.494–497; plain renderings throughout). Then the simile: “As on the banks of the Eurotas or along the ridges of Cynthus Diana leads her dances, and a thousand mountain nymphs follow her and gather on this side and on that; she carries her quiver on her shoulder, and as she walks she overtops all the goddesses; joy thrills Latona's silent heart: such was Dido, so she bore herself, glad, through their midst, pressing on the work and her kingdom to come” (*Qualis in Eurotae ripis aut per iuga Cynthi / exercet Diana choros, quam mille secutae / hinc atque hinc glomerantur oreades; illa pharetram / fert umero, gradiensque deas supereminet omnis: / Latonae tacitum pertemptant gaudia pectus: / talis erat Dido, talem se laeta ferebat / per medios, instans operi regnisque futuris*, *Aeneid* 1.498–504). Homer's mountains, Taygetus in Laconia and Erymanthus on the borders of Arcadia, become the Eurotas, the river of Sparta, and Cynthus, the hill of Delos against which Leto leaned when she bore Apollo, next to the palm (ἀγχοτάτω φοίνικος, *Hymn to Apollo* 16–18; see [[taygetus]], [[erymanthus]] and [[delos]]). Then the queen takes her seat at the doors of the goddess and gives laws and judgments to her people (*Aeneid* 1.505–508).

**Probus' judgment.** Aulus Gellius, writing of how far a translator of Greek verses should follow the words, recalls hearing from pupils of Valerius Probus, “a learned man, and well skilled in reading and weighing old writings”, that Probus used to say that Virgil had translated nothing from Homer so unhappily as “these most lovely verses that Homer made on Nausicaa” (*Attic Nights* 9.9.12). Gellius sets out the Greek and Virgil's Latin (9.9.12–13), and then Probus' reasons. First, in Homer the maiden Nausicaa, playing among her companions in a lonely place, is rightly and fitly likened to Diana hunting on the mountain ridges among the goddesses of the wild; but Dido, walking through the middle of her city among the Tyrian lords, grave in dress and bearing, “pressing on the work and her kingdom to come”, has nothing in her that answers to the sport and the hunting of Diana (9.9.14). Then, Homer speaks plainly and openly of Diana's pursuits and pleasures in the hunt, while Virgil, having said nothing of the goddess's hunting, only makes her carry a quiver on her shoulder, as though it were a burden and a pack (9.9.15). Probus also wondered at the joy of Leto. Homer's Leto rejoices with a joy that is genuine and inward, alive in the very sanctuary of her heart, for that is the meaning of γέγηθε δέ τε φρένα Λητώ, “and Leto is glad at heart”; Virgil, imitating it, made joys that are sluggish and light and hesitant and float, as it were, on the surface of the breast, for Probus did not know what else *pertemptant* could mean (9.9.15). Besides all this, Virgil seemed to have left out the very flower of the whole passage, following only scantily the verse “she is easy to know, though all are beautiful”, since no greater or fuller praise of beauty could be spoken than that one girl excelled among all who were beautiful and was easily known among them all (9.9.16–17). Merry quotes the judgment, leaving out the objection about Leto's joy (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 6.102).

**The huntress in the wood.** Virgil had used the scene of *Odyssey* 6 before, earlier in the same book. Shipwrecked on the Libyan shore, Aeneas goes out with Achates to learn what land he has come to, and his mother meets him in the middle of the wood, “with a maiden's face and dress, and a maiden's arms”, like a Spartan girl or the Thracian Harpalyce, a huntress, her bow hung from her shoulders and her hair loose to the winds, and asks whether they have seen one of her sisters (*Aeneid* 1.314–324). Aeneas answers with Odysseus' question: “What shall I call you, maiden? For your face is not mortal, nor does your voice sound human; surely a goddess: Phoebus' sister, or one of the race of the nymphs?” (*O quam te memorem, virgo? Namque haud tibi voltus / mortalis, nec vox hominem sonat: O, dea certe— / an Phoebi soror? an nympharum sanguinis una?*, *Aeneid* 1.327–329). Then he begs her, whoever she is, to tell him under what sky and on what shores of the world they are tossed, since they wander without knowing either the men or the places, driven there by the wind and the great waves (*Aeneid* 1.330–334). Merry sets the verses beside Odysseus' first words to Nausicaa, “are you some goddess, or a mortal?” (*Odyssey* 6.149; Merry on *Odyssey* 6.149, who cites them as *Aeneid* 1.331). Odysseus, too, knows no one “of the people who hold this city and land”, and asks to be shown the city (*Odyssey* 6.176–178). Venus answers that she is not worthy of such honor, for Tyrian girls are wont to carry the quiver, and tells him of Dido and the city she is building (*Aeneid* 1.335–370), as Nausicaa tells Odysseus the name of her people and her father (*Odyssey* 6.194–197). As she leaves him she shows herself a goddess, and she wraps him and Achates in a mist so that no one may see them on their way into the city (*Aeneid* 1.402–414), as Athena wraps Odysseus in mist on his way into the city of the Phaeacians (*Odyssey* 7.14–17; Merry on *Odyssey* 7.15; see [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]).

**Made beautiful.** When the mist parts in Dido's presence, Aeneas stands forth shining in the clear light, “like a god in face and shoulders; for his mother herself had breathed on her son beauty of hair and the bright light of youth and a glad grace in his eyes: such beauty as hands add to ivory, or when silver or Parian marble is set in yellow gold” (*os umerosque deo similis; namque ipsa decoram / caesariem nato genetrix lumenque iuventae / purpureum et laetos oculis adflarat honores: / quale manus addunt ebori decus, aut ubi flavo / argentum Pariusve lapis circumdatur auro*, *Aeneid* 1.589–593). Athena does the same for Odysseus after his bath, before Nausicaa looks on him: she makes him taller and stouter, sends the thick hair down from his head like the hyacinth flower, and “as when a man pours gold around silver”, pours grace on his head and shoulders (*Odyssey* 6.229–235; see [[gold-poured-around-silver]]). Merry sets Virgil's silver set in gold beside Homer's (on *Odyssey* 6.232, citing the verse as *Aeneid* 1.596). In Homer “the girl gazed at him” and wished that such a man might be called her husband (*Odyssey* 6.237, 6.244–245; see [[nausicaas-wish]]). In Virgil the queen is struck first by the sight of him (*obstipuit primo aspectu Sidonia Dido*, *Aeneid* 1.613), and Venus sends Cupid in the likeness of Ascanius to set her on fire with love (*Aeneid* 1.657–660).
