The Iliad has two kinds of leaves. In Book 2 they count. The Achaeans stand in the meadow of the Scamander “numberless, as the leaves and flowers” of spring, and Iris warns the Trojans that the enemy is coming “like leaves or like the sands” (2.468, 2.800; see [[leaves-and-flowers]] and [[like-leaves-or-sand]]). In Book 6 they measure a life: one generation grows while another passes away (see [[the-generations-of-leaves]]). Apollo, refusing to fight Poseidon for the sake of mortals, uses the second kind for all mankind: wretched men “who, like leaves, now flourish in their strength, eating the fruit of the field, and now waste away and are lifeless” (21.462–466). Later poets joined the two. Their leaves are numberless, and they are the dead.

**Bacchylides.** In his fifth ode, for Hieron of Syracuse (476 BCE), Heracles goes down to the house of Hades to fetch the dog, and by the streams of the Cocytus he sees the souls of wretched mortals, “like the leaves that the wind shakes on the gleaming headlands of Ida, where the sheep graze” (Bacchylides 5.63–67). Among them he meets the ghost of Meleager.

**Virgil.** Aeneas, at the river of the underworld, sees the crowd of the dead pour down to the bank: mothers and men, the bodies of great-hearted heroes, boys and unmarried girls, young men laid on the pyre before their parents' eyes, “as many as the leaves that fall in the woods at the first frost of autumn, or the birds that flock to land from the deep sea, when the cold season drives them over the ocean to sunny lands” (*Aeneid* 6.305–312). Virgil's leaves fall in autumn, and they count. The pairing of birds with leaves is Homer's too: in Book 2 the simile of the birds on the Asian meadow comes just before the leaves and flowers (2.459–468; see [[birds-on-the-asian-meadow]]).

**Dante.** Dante read no Greek, and his guide through Hell is Virgil. At the same river he watches the souls throw themselves into Charon's boat: “As in autumn the leaves fall away one after another, until the branch sees all its spoils upon the ground, so the evil seed of Adam cast themselves from that shore one by one, at a sign, like a bird to its call” (*Inferno* 3.112–117). He keeps Virgil's leaves and Virgil's bird, but his leaves fall singly, until the branch is bare.

**Milton.** Satan, standing on the shore of the burning lake, calls up his legions, “angel forms, who lay entranced / Thick as autumnal leaves that strow the brooks / In Vallombrosa, where the Etrurian shades / High over-arched embower” (*Paradise Lost* 1.301–304). The leaves count the fallen again, and Milton gives them a place, the wooded valley of Vallombrosa in Tuscany.

Glaucus spoke of the living, who follow one another as the leaves do. His successors speak of the dead, who are as many as the leaves.
