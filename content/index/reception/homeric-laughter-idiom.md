ἄσβεστος = ἀ- (not) + σβέννυμι (quench, extinguish): unquenchable. The phrase ἄσβεστος γέλως is used twice of the gods, with the same verse: here, as Hephaestus bustles about with the wine, and in the Odyssey, when he has caught Ares and Aphrodite in his bonds and called the gods to see, and “unquenchable laughter rose among the blessed gods / as they saw the craft of Hephaestus of much wisdom” (*Odyssey* 8.326–327; see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]]). Its third use is the suitors'. On the day of their death Athena rouses in them ἄσβεστον γέλω, “unquenchable laughter”, and leads their minds astray, and they laugh with jaws that seem no longer their own (*Odyssey* 20.345–347, a plain rendering; see [[the-suitors]]).

From the gods' scenes European languages took the idiom: French *rire homérique*, German *homerisches Gelächter*, English "Homeric laughter". In both the laughter comes from something the lame god does: once his serving, once the trap he sets. Proclus, answering Plato's objection to the first, read the gods' laughter as their unfailing work in the world that Hephaestus makes (*On the Republic* I 126–128 Kroll; see [[plato-homeric-laughter]]).
