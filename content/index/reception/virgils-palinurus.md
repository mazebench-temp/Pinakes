**Homer.** Odysseus sits at the steering oar of his raft, and sleep does not fall on his eyelids as he watches the Pleiades, Boötes and the Bear (*Odyssey* 5.270–277; see [[alone-without-a-share]] and [[steering-oar]]). Poseidon's wave throws him from the raft, and the steering oar flies from his hands (*Odyssey* 5.315–316); the garments that Calypso gave him weigh him down under the water (*Odyssey* 5.321). For two nights and two days he drifts, and on the third, lifted by a great wave, he sees the land near at hand (*Odyssey* 5.388–393). When a wave carries him onto the rugged shore he seizes a rock with both hands (*Odyssey* 5.425–429), and when at last he reaches the river he fears that if he sleeps in the woods he may become “the prey and spoil of wild beasts” (*Odyssey* 5.473). Then Athena pours sleep on his eyes (*Odyssey* 5.491–493).

**The helmsman asleep.** Palinurus leads Aeneas' fleet from Sicily toward Italy. At midnight, while the rowers sleep on their benches, the god Sleep comes down from the stars in the likeness of Phorbas, sits on the stern and bids him rest: the sea carries the fleet, the breezes blow evenly, and he will take the helmsman's work himself (*Aeneid* 5.833–846; plain renderings throughout). Palinurus will not trust the calm sea, “and clinging fast to the tiller he never let it go, and kept his eyes on the stars” (*clavumque adfixus et haerens / nusquam amittebat oculosque sub astra tenebat*, *Aeneid* 5.852–853). The god shakes over his temples a bough wet with the dew of Lethe, and when sleep has loosened his limbs he flings him headlong into the sea with part of the stern and the tiller (*Aeneid* 5.854–861). Aeneas finds the ship drifting without its helmsman, steers it himself through the night, and grieves that Palinurus, who trusted too much in a calm sky and sea, will lie naked on an unknown shore (*Aeneid* 5.867–871).

**The swim.** In the underworld Palinurus tells Aeneas the rest. The tiller to which he clung was torn away and dragged down with him as he fell (*Aeneid* 6.349–351). “Three wintry nights the violent South Wind carried me over the measureless sea on the water; at the fourth dawn I barely saw Italy, lifted high on the top of a wave” (*tris Notus hibernas immensa per aequora noctes / vexit me violentus aqua; vix lumine quarto / prospexi Italiam summa sublimis ab unda*, *Aeneid* 6.355–357). Little by little he swam to land, and he was already safe, “had not a cruel people attacked me with the sword as, weighed down by my wet clothes, I clutched with hooked hands the rough peaks of the cliff, and taken me in their ignorance for prey” (*Aeneid* 6.358–361). Merry sets Palinurus' sight of Italy from the top of a wave beside Odysseus' sight of the land (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 5.393).

**What Virgil kept.** The helmsman awake at night with his eyes on the stars, the steering oar lost in the fall, the nights of drifting and the sight of land from the crest of a wave at the next dawn, the wet clothes that drag the swimmer down, the hands on the rock and the fear of being taken for prey are all in Odysseus' voyage. Virgil adds a night to the drift and turns the fear into the event. Homer's steersman does not sleep, and the sleep that comes to him at the end is Athena's gift on land; Virgil's god of sleep is his helmsman's undoing at sea. Odysseus reaches the shore and lives; Palinurus reaches it and is killed.
