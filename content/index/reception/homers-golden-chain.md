Homer's rope is a dare, not a fixture of the world. No one hangs it, and its point is the strength of Zeus, who could haul up the gods with earth and sea and leave it all hanging from a peak of Olympus (8.18–27; see [[the-golden-rope]]). Philosophers took it as a figure for the order of the world. Plato's Socrates read it as the sun, whose motion keeps all things in being (see [[plato-golden-rope]]), and Aristotle quoted it for the mover that nothing can move (see [[aristotle-golden-rope]]).

**The chain of beings.** σειρά, the later form of Homer's σειρή, came to mean a line or series as well as a cord: the Byzantine lexicon called the Suda glosses it “chain” and adds that it also means a lineage (σ 274). The Neoplatonists used it for the orders of gods and causes that descend from the first principle, and Proclus does so throughout his *Commentary on the Parmenides* (see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). There he describes the way all things come from the One, some directly, some through one intermediary, some through two or more, but all of them simply from the One, as “the truly golden chain of beings” (τὴν χρυσῆν ὄντως σειρὰν τῶν ὄντων, book 6, col. 1100 Cousin). The adverb ὄντως, “truly”, marks the borrowed phrase: the golden chain, rightly understood, is the order of all that is.

**Bacon.** In *The Advancement of Learning* (1605) Francis Bacon argues that a little philosophy may incline a man to atheism, but more brings him back to religion. When a man passes on from the second causes to “the dependence of causes and the works of Providence”, then, “according to the allegory of the poets, he will easily believe that the highest link of Nature's chain must needs be tied to the foot of Jupiter's chair” (book 1).

**Milton.** In *Paradise Lost* the new world hangs from heaven. Chaos tells Satan that heaven and earth, another world, now hang over his realm, “linked in a golden chain / To that side Heaven from whence your legions fell” (2.1004–1006), and Satan, nearing the light, sees the empyrean and, “fast by, hanging in a golden chain, / This pendent World, in bigness as a star / Of smallest magnitude close by the moon” (2.1051–1053; see [[milton]]).

**Pope.** Pope's Iliad (1715–1720) reads the chain back into Homer. His Jupiter tells the gods: “Let down our golden everlasting chain”, a chain whose strong embrace, he adds, holds heaven and earth and sea; and when he fixes it to Olympus, the vast world “hangs trembling in my sight”. In Homer the rope exists only in Zeus' dare (see [[pope]]).

**Aurea Catena Homeri.** The name passed to alchemy. Goethe, recalling the mystical and alchemical books he read as a young man, says that he was particularly pleased with the *Aurea Catena Homeri*, “Homer's golden chain”, “in which nature, though perhaps in fantastical fashion, is represented in a beautiful combination” (*Poetry and Truth*, book 8).
