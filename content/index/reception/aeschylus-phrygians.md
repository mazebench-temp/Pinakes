**The play.** The tragedy is known by two names, the *Phrygians* and the *Ransom of Hector*. Like the *Myrmidons*, the play of Achilles' withdrawal and of the death of Patroclus, it is lost (see [[aeschylus-myrmidons]]). What survives is ten fragments in Nauck's collection, quoted by later writers for a thought, a phrase or a single word (frr. 263–272 Nauck). They do not tell the plot, but the title says what the play was about, and three of them can be set beside the Iliad.

**The dead feel nothing.** Stobaeus quotes five lines from the play: “And if you wish to do good to the dead, or to harm them, it makes no difference either way, since the dead feel neither joy nor pain. Yet Nemesis stands above us, and Justice exacts the dead man's anger” (fr. 266 Nauck; see [[aeschylus]]). The speaker is not known. The two thoughts are the two lines with which Apollo ends his complaint to the gods in the Iliad, in the other order: “Let him beware, brave as he is, that we do not grow indignant with him; / for in his rage he defiles the senseless earth” (24.53–54; see [[the-senseless-earth]] and [[nemesis]]). Apollo's verb, νεμεσσηθέωμεν, “grow indignant”, is the verb of Aeschylus' Nemesis. Cicero, arguing that burial concerns only the body, takes the dragging of Hector for his example and keeps only the first half of the thought (see [[cicero-the-body-of-hector]]).

**Hector's wife.** A scholiast on the opening line of Euripides' *Andromache* quotes two lines of the play: “the offspring of Andraemon of Lyrnessus, from where Hector brought his dear wife” (fr. 267 Nauck; a word is lost in the first line). In the Iliad Andromache is the daughter of Eetion, “in Thebe under Placus, lord over the Cilician men” (6.395–397; see [[andromache]], [[eetion]] and [[thebe]]). Lyrnessus is the city from which Achilles took Briseis, “when he sacked Lyrnessus and the walls of Thebe” (2.690–691; see [[lyrnessus]] and [[briseis]]). Aeschylus, or the tradition he followed, made Lyrnessus Andromache's home.

**Hector's weight.** The lexicographer Hesychius cites a corrupt word from the play, which he explains as the weight of Hector, or what was set against it (fr. 270 Nauck). If the gloss is right, the play weighed the body against the ransom. In the Iliad the ransom is chosen and weighed out in gold, “ten talents of gold in all” (24.232), but nothing is weighed against the body, and the weighing of the body in gold is what Achilles swore he would never accept: “not even if he should bid them weigh out your own body in gold, / Priam, descendant of Dardanus” (22.351–352; see [[the-ransom-of-hector]]). Virgil's Achilles sells the lifeless body for gold (see [[virgils-hector]]).

**The silent Achilles.** In Aristophanes' *Frogs* Euripides charges Aeschylus with seating a single figure veiled, “some Achilles or Niobe”, not showing the face, a mere show of tragedy that utters not a syllable, while the chorus sang four strings of songs on end, so that the spectator sat waiting for the moment when Niobe would say something, and the play went on (*Frogs* 911–920). Niobe was the heroine of another of Aeschylus' plays (see [[niobe-play]]). Aristophanes does not say which play's Achilles he means. The Iliad's last book has both: the Achilles who sits in his hut when Priam comes in, and the Niobe of his story to Priam (see [[the-children-of-niobe]]).
