**Book 17.** When Hector has stripped Patroclus of Achilles' armor, he drags the body away to behead it and throw it to the dogs, and lets it go only when Ajax comes up with his shield like a tower (17.125–131; see [[hector]] and [[ajax]]). The body is dragged again and again in the book. Hippothous ties a strap around the tendons at the ankle and pulls it by the foot until Ajax kills him (17.288–303; see [[hippothous]]); the two sides tug it this way and that, as men stretch an ox-hide drenched with fat (17.389–395; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]); and Zeus covers the fighters with mist because he hates that Patroclus should become a spoil for “the Trojan dogs” (17.270–273). In the next book Iris tells Achilles what Hector means to do: above all he is eager to drag the body off, and his heart bids him cut the head from the tender neck and fix it on the stakes (18.175–177).

**Achilles.** In the later books Achilles does to Hector much of what Hector meant to do to Patroclus. He had vowed not to bury Patroclus until he brought back Hector's armor and head, and to cut the throats of twelve Trojan youths before the pyre (18.334–337). He pierces the tendons of Hector's feet, ties him to his chariot with thongs of ox-hide and drags him in the dust (22.395–404); he tells the dead Patroclus that he will give Hector to the dogs to tear raw (23.21), and kills the twelve on the pyre (23.175–176); and for days he drags the body three times around Patroclus' tomb, until Apollo keeps all harm from it with the golden aegis (24.14–21). Homer says of the first that Achilles devised ἀεικέα … ἔργα, “shameful deeds”, for Hector (22.395 = 23.24), and of the second κακὰ … ἔργα, “evil deeds” (23.176). In Book 24 Apollo tells the gods that the dragging is neither finer nor better for Achilles, and that they may yet be angry with him, good man though he is, for in his fury he is defiling the dumb earth (24.50–54).

**Plato.** Among the things that must not be believed of Achilles, Plato's Socrates names his defiance of Apollo and of the river, the lock vowed to the Spercheius that he gave to the dead Patroclus, and “the draggings of Hector around the tomb of Patroclus, and the slaughters of the captives at the pyre”: all these, he says, we shall deny were truly told, and we shall not let our people believe that the son of a goddess and of Peleus, most self-controlled of men and third from Zeus, reared by the wisest Chiron, was so full of confusion as to hold two opposite diseases, meanness with love of money and arrogance toward gods and men (*Republic* 3.391a–c; see [[plato]] and [[achilles]]).

**Aristotle.** A note in the scholia on 24.15, which Gigon prints among the fragments of Aristotle's *Homeric Problems*, asks why Achilles dragged Hector around the tomb, against the customs about the dead. Its first answer is that lawless acts belong to those who begin them, not to those who defend themselves, and that Hector was the first to try to outrage Patroclus so. It gives the evidence from three passages: Iris' report of what Hector intended (18.175–177), the simile of the hide that both sides drag (17.389–395), and the verse in which Hector drags the body to cut off the head (17.125–126). Since Hector had broken the custom, Achilles turned the same things against him. A man who is prevented from doing what he wishes has done the act in intention, while his victim has suffered nothing; so Hector got back what had been done, and it was the more humane course that he suffered only what his side had been able to do, not all that they intended. Then comes a second solution, which the note gives to Aristotle by name: the thing was customary then, since even now in Thessaly they drag bodies around tombs (*Homeric Problems*, fr. 389 Gigon; see [[aristotle]] and [[problems-and-solutions]]).

**Proclus.** In his essays on the *Republic* Proclus takes up Plato's charge and gives both answers. The ancients had said, he writes, that this was a Thessalian custom, and the poet of Cyrene, that is Callimachus, bears witness: “still, as of old, the Thessalian man drags the slayers around the tomb of the dead”; and the dragging and the captives were taken to complete the rites owed to Patroclus. And if Hector dragged Patroclus, dead as he was, to cut off his head and give him to the Trojan dogs (17.126–127), and Achilles knew it from Iris, who told him of the stakes and of Patroclus becoming a plaything for the Trojan dogs (18.175–179), then by dragging Hector around Patroclus' tomb Achilles gave him his fitting penalty. He honored his friend, exacted the penalty for Hector's intention, and, since Hector had not done what he purposed, gave him back to his own people and let him be buried. So, Proclus says, the poet shows Achilles serving the will of the higher powers and planning gentler things for Hector, and tending the body with his own hands: when the maids had washed it, anointed it with oil and put a cloak and tunic around it, Achilles himself lifted it onto the bier (24.587–589; *On the Republic* I 150–151 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]], [[callimachus]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]).

**The answer and the poem.** Both defenders find Achilles' justification in Book 17, in what Hector meant to do to the body, and both go outside the poem for a custom. Homer gives Achilles no such reason; his vow is made in anger at Patroclus' death (σέθεν κταμένοιο χολωθείς, 18.337). His vows match Hector's purpose, the head (18.335; 17.126) and the dogs (23.21; 17.127), and the poem's words for what he does are its own judgment on it. In the poem the gentler course follows the gods' command, as Proclus allows: Zeus sends Thetis to tell Achilles that the gods are angry with him and that he must accept a ransom, and Achilles agrees (24.104–140; see [[thetis]]).
