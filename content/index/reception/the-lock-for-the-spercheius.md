title: The lock for the Spercheius
greek: τήν ῥα Σπερχειῷ ποταμῷ τρέφε τηλεθόωσαν
kind: anecdote
tags: [sacrifice]
summary: Achilles had grown his hair for the river Spercheius and gave it instead to the dead Patroclus. Later Greeks read the passage as evidence. Pausanias inferred from it that cutting the hair for rivers was an old custom of all the Greeks, and saw it done in his own day; Strabo took it for proof that the Spercheius was Achilles' own river. Aeschylus' Orestes lays on his father's tomb one lock for the river that reared him and another for mourning; Achilles' one lock serves both.
source: Pausanias, Description of Greece 1.37.3, 8.20.3, 8.41.3; Strabo, Geography 9.5.9; Aeschylus, Libation Bearers 6–7

**Homer.** When the wood has been heaped, Achilles stands apart from the pyre and cuts off his fair hair, “which he had grown thick and long for the river Spercheius”, and speaks to the river: his father Peleus had prayed that when his son came home he would cut his hair for it and sacrifice a holy hecatomb, fifty uncut rams, into its waters, where it has a precinct and a fragrant altar; the old man's prayer was in vain, “But now, since I am not going home to my dear native land, / I would grant my hair to the hero Patroclus to take with him.” He puts the hair in his companion's hands (23.141–153; see [[spercheius]], [[peleus]] and [[hair]]). The Myrmidons have already covered the body with the hair they cut as they walked behind it (23.135–136; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]).

**Pausanias.** Near the crossing of the Cephisus, Pausanias saw two statues by the river, one of Mnesimache, the other a dedication to the Cephisus, her son cutting his hair. “That this has been the custom of all the Greeks from of old”, he says, “one may infer from the poetry of Homer, who says that Peleus vowed to the Spercheius to cut Achilles' hair if he came home safe from Troy” (*Description of Greece* 1.37.3; see [[pausanias]]); this Cephisus is the river of Attica, not the Cephisus of the Catalogue. He found the custom alive in Arcadia. Where the Neda comes nearest to the city of Phigalia, the boys of the Phigalians cut their hair for the river (8.41.3), and in the Arcadian story of Daphne, Leucippus “was growing his hair for the Alpheus” when he braided it like a girl's and went to join her in the hunt (8.20.3; see [[alpheus]]). Leucippus' verb for growing the hair, τρέφω, is the one used of Achilles' lock (23.142). Walter Leaf cites all three passages on this line (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.141).

**Strabo.** Strabo uses the passage for geography. Homer names the Spercheius often as a local river, and so shows that the land on both sides of Thermopylae along the Malian Gulf belonged to Achilles: the proof that the river was his own is that Achilles says he grew his hair for it, and that Menesthius, one of his captains, is called the son of the Spercheius and of Achilles' sister (*Geography* 9.5.9; 16.173–175; see [[strabo]] and [[menesthius]]).

**Aeschylus.** At the opening of the *Libation Bearers*, in lines that survive incomplete, Orestes lays at his father's tomb a lock “for Inachus, for my nurture” (πλόκαμον Ἰνάχῳ θρεπτήριον), and “this second one, for mourning” (τὸν δεύτερον δὲ τόνδε πενθητήριον, *Libation Bearers* 6–7; see [[libation-bearers]] and [[orestes]]). Leaf notes that Aeschylus keeps the two apart; the lock grown for a river in return for one's nurture was usually cut at coming of age, and Achilles still wears his because he left home very young (on 23.141). In the Iliad one lock is both: grown for the river, it is given to the dead.

**Plato and Proclus.** Plato's Socrates counts the gift among the things that must not be believed of Achilles, quoting the half-verse in which he gives the hair to Patroclus (*Republic* 3.391b; 23.151). Proclus answered that the hair was owed to the river only on a homecoming that would never come, and so had never become the river's (*On the Republic* I 149 Kroll; see [[plato-achilles-and-the-river]]).
