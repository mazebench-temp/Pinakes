**Homer.** Meleager has refused the elders of the Aetolians and the priests they sent, his father at the door of his chamber, his sisters and his mother, and the companions dearest to him. Only when the Curetes are climbing the towers and setting fire to the city does his wife beg him, weeping. She does not argue. She tells him what happens to men whose city is taken, the men killed, the city leveled by fire, the children and the women led away, and his heart is stirred “as he heard of these evil deeds” (9.574–596; see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]] and [[cleopatra]]).

**Aristotle.** The seventh chapter of the first book of the *Rhetoric* gathers the commonplaces for arguing that one good or evil is greater than another. One of them is division:

{{quote:aff5c56c-d4a2-5d23-ab1d-c795737ed710}}

Putting things together and building one on another, as Epicharmus does, works in the same way (see [[epicharmus]]). The capture of a city, said in a word, is one evil; told in its parts, it is many, and it moves the hearer.

**Aristotle's text.** The verses he quotes are not quite ours. He has κάκʼ, “ills”, where ours has κήδεʼ, “sorrows”; λαοὶ μὲν φθινύθουσι, “the people perish”, for ἄνδρας μὲν κτείνουσι, “they kill the men”; and he stops at the children, without “the deep-girdled women” (9.592–594). Whether he quoted from memory or from a text that read so cannot be known.

**Phoenix's method.** Aristotle's commonplace fits the speech that contains the verses. Phoenix himself persuades by division. He does not say that everyone begged Meleager; he names them one after another, elders, priests, father, sisters and mother, companions, and last the wife (9.574–591). And Agamemnon's offer, which the envoys have just recited item by item, is a greatness made of parts (9.122–157, 9.264–299; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]).
