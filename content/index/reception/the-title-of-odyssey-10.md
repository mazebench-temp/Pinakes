The title of *Odyssey* 10 in Merry's edition is Τὰ περὶ Αἰόλου καὶ Λαιστρυγόνων καὶ Κίρκης, “the events with Aeolus and the Laestrygonians and Circe” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, heading of Book 10); this library calls the book “Aeolus, the Laestrygonians and Circe”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter of the alphabet, this one is κ, kappa (see [[title-of-book-5]]). Its three stories are told under [[odysseus-and-aeolus]], [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]] and [[odysseus-and-circe]], and the death at its end under [[the-death-of-elpenor]]; the whole tale, which runs from *Odyssey* 9 to 12, is [[the-tale-to-alcinous]].

**The words of the title.** The heading names the three adventures for those Odysseus met: a man, Aeolus son of Hippotes; a people, the Laestrygonians; and a goddess, Circe (see [[aeolus]], [[laestrygonians]] and [[circe]]). Two of its genitives are Homer's own. Αἰόλου is in the companions' talk of the “gifts from Aeolus” and in Odysseus' return to “the glorious house of Aeolus” (*Odyssey* 10.36, 10.60), and Κίρκης comes in fourteen lines of the book, first in “the halls of Circe”, from which Odysseus sees the smoke rising (*Odyssey* 10.150). Λαιστρυγόνων is not in the poem. Homer names the people in the plural once, “the mighty Laestrygonians” (Λαιστρυγόνες, *Odyssey* 10.119); otherwise he speaks of their king, “Antiphates the Laestrygonian” (*Odyssey* 10.106, 10.199), and of their city, “Telepylus of the Laestrygonians”, Τηλέπυλον Λαιστρυγονίην (*Odyssey* 10.82; see [[antiphates-the-laestrygonian]] and [[telepylus]]). The construction, τὰ περί with a genitive, “the matters concerning”, is the one in which Strabo speaks of “the stories told about Circe and Medea”, τὰ περὶ Κίρκης καὶ Μηδείας μυθευόμενα, which in his view Homer knew and moved out into the Ocean; in the next chapter he counts “the sizes of the Cyclopes and the Laestrygonians”, μεγέθη Κυκλώπων καὶ Λαιστρυγόνων, among the marvels that the poet plainly invents (*Geography* 1.2.10–11, plain renderings; see [[strabo]]).

**Three arrivals.** The book introduces each of the three as soon as the ship arrives, with a name and a lineage. “And we came to the island of Aeolia; and there lived / Aeolus, son of Hippotes, dear to the immortal gods” (*Odyssey* 10.1–2); “on the seventh we came to the steep citadel of Lamus, / Telepylus of the Laestrygonians” (*Odyssey* 10.81–82); “And we came to the island of Aeaea; and there lived / Circe of the lovely tresses, a dread goddess with human voice, / own sister of baleful Aeetes” (*Odyssey* 10.135–137; see [[aeolia]], [[aeaea]] and [[aeetes]]). The first and the third arrivals are told in the same verse, with Αἰαίην for Αἰολίην (see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). The Laestrygonians' arrival names a city, “the steep citadel of Lamus”, and Merry takes Lamus for a second name of their king (Merry on *Odyssey* 10.81).

**What the title covers.** The three adventures take very unequal shares of the book. Aeolus has seventy-nine lines (*Odyssey* 10.1–79), the Laestrygonians fifty-three (*Odyssey* 10.80–132), and Circe the rest, from the departure that brings the one remaining ship to Aeaea to the end of the book (*Odyssey* 10.133–574): more than three quarters of its 573 lines, for the text printed here omits *Odyssey* 10.456 (see the translation's note on 10.456). Neither the title nor the poem's summary names what the stay with Circe leads to. When Odysseus asks to be sent home, Circe tells him that he must first go “to the house of Hades and of dread Persephone, / to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias” (*Odyssey* 10.491–492; see [[teiresias]] and [[persephone]]); she tells him the way across Oceanus and the rite at the pit (*Odyssey* 10.504–540; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]), and the book ends with the death of Elpenor and the companions' grief at the new journey (*Odyssey* 10.551–574; see [[elpenor]]). The rite is performed in the next book in the same verses (*Odyssey* 11.25–50; see the translation's note on 10.516–537), the book that Merry heads Νέκυια, “the Nekyia” (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-11]]). Nor is the adventure with Circe over: the twelfth book begins with the return to Aeaea to bury Elpenor and with Circe's warnings of what lies ahead (*Odyssey* 12.1–150), though Merry's heading of that book names only the Sirens, Scylla, Charybdis and the cattle of the Sun (Merry, headings of Books 11 and 12; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-9]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-12]]).

**The summary to Penelope.** When the poem tells in a few lines what Odysseus told his wife of his wanderings, the book's three adventures follow one another in the heading's order and under its names: “and how he came to Aeolus, who received him with a ready heart / and sent him on his way; but it was not yet his lot to reach his dear native land, / but the storm wind snatched him up again / and carried him, groaning heavily, over the fish-filled sea; / and how he came to Telepylus of the Laestrygonians, / who destroyed his ships and his well-greaved companions, / all of them, and Odysseus alone escaped with his black ship; / and he told of the deceit and the many devices of Circe” (*Odyssey* 23.314–321, a plain rendering). Circe's line, Κίρκης κατέλεξε δόλον πολυμηχανίην τε, uses the words of this book. δόλος, “trick, deceit”, is what Eurylochus suspected when he stayed outside her house, “for he suspected a trick” (*Odyssey* 10.232; in his own report, 10.258), and what Circe asks Odysseus at her table, “Do you suspect some other trick?” (*Odyssey* 10.380; see [[dolos]]). The noun πολυμηχανίη, “many devices”, is Homer's only here, but its adjective is the epithet that Circe gives Odysseus three times in this book, “Son of Laertes, sprung from Zeus, Odysseus of many devices” (*Odyssey* 10.401, 10.488, 10.504; see [[of-many-devices]]). The next line goes on to the house of Hades, and the line after it is the verse of Circe's errand, “to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias” (*Odyssey* 23.322–323; *Odyssey* 10.492, 10.565).

**Merry's headings.** Merry's titles for the books of the tale name its stages: Ἀλκίνου ἀπόλογοι, Κυκλώπεια, “the tales to Alcinous; the Cyclopeia” (Book 9); this book's three names (Book 10); Νέκυια (Book 11); and Σειρῆνες, Σκύλλα, Χάρυβδις, βόες Ἡλίου, “the Sirens, Scylla, Charybdis, the cattle of the Sun” (Book 12; Merry, headings of Books 9–12; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-9]] and [[the-title-of-odyssey-12]]). The headings of the books on either side name the episodes themselves, the Cyclopeia, the Nekyia, the Sirens and the rest; this one names, in a phrase, the persons of its three adventures.

**Later tellings.** Horace, listing the striking wonders that Homer keeps for later in the Odyssey, puts this book's king first: “Antiphates and Scylla, and with the Cyclops Charybdis” (*Antiphaten Scyllamque et cum Cyclope Charybdim*, *Ars Poetica* 145, a plain rendering; see [[horace-on-the-opening-of-the-odyssey]]). Ovid gives the book's three adventures, in the heading's order, to a companion of Ulysses, Macareus, who tells of Aeolus son of Hippotes and the winds shut in an ox's hide, of the city of the Laestrygonian Lamus and its king Antiphates, and of the year with Circe (*Metamorphoses* 14.223–308; see [[ovid]]).
