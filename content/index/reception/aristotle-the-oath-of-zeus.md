**Homer.** On the day Heracles was to be born, Zeus boasts among the gods that a man of his blood born that day will be lord over all who dwell around him (19.100–105). Hera answers that he will prove false, and asks him to swear a mighty oath (καρτερὸν ὅρκον, 19.108; see [[hera]] and [[oath]]). “So she spoke; and Zeus did not perceive her treachery at all, / but swore a great oath, and in this was greatly blinded” (19.112–113; see [[the-birth-of-heracles]] and [[ate]]). Elsewhere it is his nod that binds him. When Thetis asks him to nod or refuse, he answers: “Come, I will nod my head to you, so that you may be certain; / for this, from me, among the immortals is the greatest / sign; for no word of mine can be taken back, or prove false, / or go unfulfilled, once I nod my head to it” (1.524–527; see [[neuo]] and [[zeus]]).

**Aristotle.** Aristotle's lost *Homeric Problems* collected difficulties in the poems with their solutions (see [[problems-and-solutions]]). A note in the scholia on 19.108, which Gigon prints among its fragments, asks why Hera demanded that Zeus swear. Is it simply because he does not do what he says? Then why did she not ask for a nod, but made him swear as well, as though he would lie unless he swore? For the poet says that Zeus speaks the truth, and the note quotes the end of his promise to Thetis (1.527). The answer has two parts. First, the whole matter is mythical: Homer does not say these things on his own authority, or bring them in as things that actually happened, but recalls them as a story handed down about the birth of Heracles. Second, the story has Hera make Zeus swear for good reason. Those who fear that what they dread may turn out otherwise secure themselves with every safeguard; and Hera, contending over no small matter, and knowing that Zeus would be indignant once he saw Heracles made a slave, bound him with the strongest necessity. The note ends, “Thus Aristotle” (fr. 387 Gigon; see [[aristotle]]).

**The teller and the oath.** The first answer meets a difficulty that Walter Leaf also felt. Here and only here the doings and words of the gods are told by a man in the story, not by the poet, and Agamemnon knows too much for a mortal of the ways of the gods; Leaf took the episode for a piece of an older poem on Heracles, worked in (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.95, 19.114; see the translation's note on 19.95–133). The second answer looks to Hera's motive. Leaf looks rather to Zeus' fault, and finds his blind folly not in any trick of Hera's words but in his rashness in swearing an unconditional oath, limited to a single day (on 19.111). In the poem the oath once sworn cannot be undone, and Zeus answers it with a second, that Blind Folly will never come back to Olympus (19.127–129).
