*Troilus and Cressida* puts three of Book 3's questions to its characters, and gives them harsher answers.

**Is she worth keeping?** In the Trojan council (2.2) Priam reports the Greeks' latest offer: deliver Helen, and all the rest of the damage will be forgotten. Hector says "Let Helen go"; she "is not worth what she doth cost / The keeping". Troilus answers that a thing is worth what it is valued at, and calls her "a pearl / Whose price hath launched above a thousand ships, / And turned crowned kings to merchants", taking up Marlowe's famous line. In the end Hector, who has argued that the laws of nature and of nations demand her return, agrees to keep her for the sake of their honor. In Iliad 3 the old men on the wall grant her beauty and still want her sent home (3.156–160). Antenor makes the proposal openly in Book 7, and Paris refuses it (7.347–364).

**Love or war?** In 3.1 Paris and Helen are at home, listening to Pandarus sing of love. Paris would have armed that day, he says, but "my Nell would not have it so". In the Iliad Aphrodite carries him from the duel to his chamber, and he takes Helen to bed while Menelaus hunts for him on the field (3.380–382, 3.441–450).

**Who deserves her?** In 4.1 Paris asks Diomedes, "Who, in your thoughts, deserves fair Helen best, / Myself or Menelaus?" It is the question the duel of Book 3 was fought to settle. Diomedes answers "Both alike", and curses her: for every false drop in her veins a Greek life has been lost, and for every scruple of her weight a Trojan has been killed. Homer's old men called her terribly like the immortal goddesses (3.158). Shakespeare's Greek sees only the cost.
