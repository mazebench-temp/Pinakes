**Aeneas after Pallas.** In the tenth book of the *Aeneid* the news reaches Aeneas that his men are on the edge of ruin, and he mows down all that is nearest with the sword, driving a broad path through the ranks with his blade and seeking Turnus, proud of his new slaughter. Pallas and Evander are before his eyes, the table to which he first came as a stranger, and the right hands given (*Aeneid* 10.510–517); Pallas, Evander's son, has been killed (10.532–533). What follows is Achilles' revenge for Patroclus in Book 21, gathered into fifty lines.

**The captives.** First Aeneas takes four young men, sons of Sulmo, and as many reared by Ufens, alive, to sacrifice them as offerings to the shades and drench the flames of the pyre with captive blood (*viventis rapit, inferias quos immolet umbris / captivoque rogi perfundat sanguine flammas*, 10.517–520). Achilles, when his hands grew weary with killing in the river, chose twelve young men out of it alive “as the penalty for Patroclus, son of Menoetius, who had died”, and at Patroclus' pyre he kills them with the bronze (21.26–32, 23.175–176; see [[human-sacrifice]]).

**Magus.** Then Aeneas hurls his spear at Magus. Magus cunningly runs in under it, the spear flies quivering over him, and he clasps Aeneas' knees and begs as a suppliant, by the shade of Aeneas' father and the hopes of the growing Iulus, for his life: he has a lofty house, with talents of chased silver buried deep and weights of gold wrought and unwrought; the Trojans' victory does not turn on him, and one life will not make so great a difference. Aeneas answers that he should keep the silver and gold for his sons: “Turnus did away with such trading in war first, already when Pallas was slain” (*belli commercia Turnus / sustulit ista prior iam tum Pallante perempto*). Then he takes the helmet in his left hand, bends back the suppliant's neck and drives the sword in to the hilt (10.521–536). The scene is Lycaon's. Lycaon too runs in under the lifted spear and clasps Achilles' knees, and the spear passes over his back and stands fixed in the ground (21.67–72); Achilles too refuses him for his friend's sake: “for before Patroclus met his fated day, / until then it was somehow dearer to my heart to spare / Trojans, and many I took alive and sold” (21.100–102); and Achilles too kills him with the sword (21.116–119; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[supplication]]). Magus' offer is like the one that Adrastus made to Menelaus for his life, “bronze and gold and iron worked with much toil” stored in the house of his wealthy father (6.46–50; see [[apoina]]).

**Tarquitus.** A little later Tarquitus, whom the nymph Dryope bore to Faunus, comes against Aeneas exulting in shining arms. Aeneas pins his corselet and the great weight of his shield with the spear, strikes his head to the ground as he begs in vain and makes ready to say much, rolls the warm trunk over and speaks above it with a hostile heart: “Lie there now, fearsome one. No excellent mother will bury you in the earth or weigh down your limbs with your father's tomb. You will be left to the wild birds, or sunk in the flood, the wave will carry you, and hungry fishes will lick your wounds” (*Istic nunc, metuende, iace … piscesque impasti volnera lambent*, 10.550–560). This is Achilles' boast over Lycaon, whom he has flung into the river: “Lie there now among the fishes, who will lick the blood / from your wound and care nothing for you; nor will your mother / lay you on a bier and lament for you, but Scamander / will carry you, eddying, into the broad bosom of the sea” (21.122–125). Tarquitus is a nymph's son, as Asteropaeus, whose body Achilles leaves to the eels and fishes in the same book, was a river's grandson (21.141–143, 21.200–204; see [[asteropaeus]]).
