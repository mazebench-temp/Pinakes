**Homer.** Agamemnon, accepting Odysseus' counsel, charges him to choose “young men, the best of all the Achaeans”, to bring the gifts from his ship (19.193–195), and they carry them into the assembly behind him: “and with him the other young men of the Achaeans carried the gifts” (19.248; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]] and [[odysseus]]). κούρητες, in both lines, is a by-form of κοῦροι, “young men”, found only in this book (see [[kouretes]]). With a capital and another accent the same letters are the Curetes, the people who besiege Calydon in Phoenix's story of Meleager (9.529–599; see [[curetes]]).

**Strabo.** The Curetes come into Strabo's account of Aetolia and Acarnania. He begins with Homer's Curetes, who fought the Aetolians around Calydon, and argues that they were Aetolians of Pleuron (*Geography* 10.3.1–6). Then he turns to the other Curetes, the attendants of the gods in the Cretan and Phrygian stories of the infant Zeus and of the Mother of the Gods, whom the historians brought together with the first because of the name (10.3.7). Before he leaves the name he gives its etymologies. Some say that the Curetes of Aetolia were so called because they dressed like girls (κόραι), for the Greeks had such fashions: the Ionians were called “with trailing tunics”, and Leonidas' men combed their hair before they went out to fight, and were despised for it by the Persians and admired in the battle. Care of the hair is a matter of growing it and of cutting it (κουρά), and both belong to girls and to boys (κόροι), so that the name can be derived in many ways. Most likely, he thinks, the armed dance was first brought in by men who took such care of their hair and dress and were called Curetes, and it gave the same name to others, more given to war and living under arms, the Curetes of Euboea, Aetolia and Acarnania. “And Homer too called the young soldiers so”, and he quotes Agamemnon's order and the line in which the young men carry the gifts (10.3.8; see [[strabo]]). The Ionians “with trailing tunics” are Homer's too (13.685; see [[ionians]]).

**His text.** Strabo's lines differ from ours. Agamemnon tells Odysseus to bring the gifts “from the swift ship”, δῶρα θοῆς παρὰ νηός, where ours has δῶρα ἐμῆς, “from my ship”; the verb is ἐνεγκεῖν for ἐνεικέμεν; and all that “we promised Achilles yesterday” has χθιζοί, agreeing with “we”, for χθιζόν (19.194–195). Walter Leaf thinks δῶρα ἐμῆς a false archaism and counts Strabo's δῶρα θοῆς among the ancient remedies (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.194; see the translation's note on 19.194). In the second line Strabo has κούρητες Ἀχαιοί, “the Achaean young men”, for κούρητες Ἀχαιῶν, “young men of the Achaeans” (19.248).

**The young men and the people.** For Strabo the young soldiers and the people of Pleuron share one name, and he looks for a single origin. The index keeps them apart, as the printed text does by capital and accent: κούρητες are “young men” in Book 19, and Κουρῆτες the people in Book 9 (see the translation's note on 19.193 and 19.248). Leaf too takes κούρητες here for κοῦροι, and notes that at 9.529 the word is a proper name (on 19.193). Earlier in the same book Strabo reports two other etymologies of the people's name: from κουρά, because they cut their hair short in front so that enemies could not seize it, or from Mount Curium above Pleuron (10.3.6; see [[curetes]]).
