**Pope.** Alexander Pope's Iliad (1715–1720) turns the simile into a nocturne. It begins “As when the moon, refulgent lamp of night”, and adds what Homer does not have: planets that roll around the moon's throne, stars unnumbered that gild the pole, a yellower verdure shed over the dark trees, and a flood of glory bursting from all the skies. In Homer the moon is named once, and the stars, the lookout places, the headlands and the glens are simply seen, while the shepherd is glad at heart (8.555–559; see [[stars-around-the-moon]] and [[pope]]).

**Wordsworth.** In the “Essay, Supplementary to the Preface” of his *Poems* (1815) William Wordsworth argued that the poetry written between *Paradise Lost* and Thomson's *Seasons* contains hardly a new image of external nature. How low the knowledge of the most obvious phenomena had sunk, he says, is evident from Dryden's description of night in one of his tragedies, “and Pope his translation of the celebrated moon-light scene in the Iliad”. A blind man, attending to what others said around him, could have described these things with more truth. “Dryden's lines are vague, bombastic, and senseless; those of Pope, though he had Homer to guide him, are throughout false and contradictory.” Yet no passage of descriptive poetry, he adds, had so many and such ardent admirers.

**Arnold.** Matthew Arnold, in his lectures *On Translating Homer*, given at Oxford and published in 1861, took the next lines as his example of Homer's plain narrative:

{{quote:571a0630-c99f-55ed-821c-0f311cd3e32c}}

“Everyone knows the passage at the end of the eighth book of the *Iliad*, where the fires of the Trojan encampment are likened to the stars”, he begins. He gives a literal version of the lines that follow the simile, then Pope's, which begins “So many flames before proud Ilion blaze”, and concludes: “It is for passages of this sort, which, after all, form the bulk of a narrative poem, that Pope's style is so bad.” In elevated passages Pope is powerful, “but in plain narrative, where Homer is still powerful and delightful, Pope, by the inherent fault of his style, is ineffective and out of taste” (lecture 1). The translator, for Arnold, must above all feel that Homer is rapid, plain and direct in his words and in his thought, and noble (see [[arnold]]).

**Tennyson.** Alfred Tennyson rendered the whole close of the book, from the Trojans' shout of applause for Hector to the horses waiting for the dawn, in twenty-two lines of blank verse, his “Specimen of a Translation of the Iliad in Blank Verse”, printed in 1863 and collected the next year among the “Experiments” of the *Enoch Arden* volume. The year before, in his *Last Words* on translating Homer, Arnold had found Tennyson's blank verse unsuited to Homer, for its “extreme subtlety and curious elaborateness of thought”. The specimen keeps close to the Greek. Its last line has the horses standing by their chariots, “waiting for the dawn”, and a footnote gives a version “more literally”, in which they wait for “the thronèd morn”, Homer's ἐΰθρονον Ἠῶ (see [[tennyson]]).

Tennyson gave his version the reference “Iliad 8. 542–561”. His numbering counts the book without the four verses that Monro and Allen print in brackets from the Second Alcibiades, and his version has the savor rolled into heaven, but no hecatombs and no gods who refuse them. Pope's version has the hecatombs, and the gods' refusal (see [[the-refused-sacrifice]]).
