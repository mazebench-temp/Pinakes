**The dialogue.** In the *Ion* Socrates argues that each art judges what Homer says in its own field, so that the rhapsode, who has no art of his own, can judge none of it (see [[plato-ion-the-seers-passages]]). Nestor's advice to his son on the turn is for a charioteer to judge (*Ion* 537a–c; see [[nestors-lesson-in-driving]]), and the drink that Hecamede gives the wounded Machaon is for a doctor (538b–c; see [[plato-asclepiads]]). Then Socrates quotes the simile with which Iris goes down into the sea to fetch Thetis, and asks: “Shall we say that these things are for the fisherman's art to judge rather than the rhapsode's, what he says, and whether well or not?” Ion answers, “Clearly, Socrates, the fisherman's” (538c–d). The seer's passages come next (538d–539d).

**Plato's text.**

> ἡ δὲ μολυβδαίνῃ ἰκέλη ἐς βυσσὸν ἵκανεν,
> ἥ τε κατʼ ἀγραύλοιο βοὸς κέρας ἐμμεμαυῖα
> ἔρχεται ὠμηστῇσι μετʼ ἰχθύσι πῆμα φέρουσα.

“And she reached the depths like a lead weight, which, rushing eagerly along the horn of an ox of the fields, goes among the fish that eat raw flesh bringing them harm” (538d). Four words differ from our text. Plato has ἵκανεν, “reached”, where ours has ὄρουσεν, “sped”; ἐμμεμαυῖα, “rushing eagerly”, where ours has ἐμβεβαυῖα, “mounted on”; μετʼ ἰχθύσι, “among the fish”, where ours has ἐπʼ ἰχθύσι, “to the fish”; and πῆμα, “harm”, where ours has κῆρα, “doom”. Walter Leaf reports that the second and the fourth were known to Didymus, who gave as his reason for πῆμα that it is out of place to speak of κήρ, “doom”, for fish, a reason Leaf calls absurd; he finds ἐμμεμαυῖα “quite as possible” as the text, since the poem gives the same eagerness to deadly missiles, the arrow “eager to fly into the throng” and the spears “longing to glut themselves on flesh” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.80; 4.126, 11.574; see the translation's note on 24.80–82). Our text ends the verse with a phrase the poems use of men and birds that bring death, κῆρα φέρουσα, as the cranes fly toward the Pygmies “bringing slaughter and doom” (3.6; see [[cranes-and-pygmies]]); πῆμα φέρουσα is found nowhere in Homer.

**The fisherman and the poem.** Socrates asks only whether Homer describes the tackle rightly, which a fisherman knows and a rhapsode does not. What the tackle was is still disputed: Leaf reports that Aristarchus, and Aristotle according to Plutarch, took the horn for a little tube on the line above the hook, weighted with lead, and that Haskins took it for a lure of horn weighted with lead and drawn through the water (on 24.80; see [[the-lead-weight]]). In the poem the simile measures a goddess. Iris leaps into the sea between Samos and rocky Imbros, “and the water groaned over her”, and goes down to Thetis' cave as straight as the sinker (24.77–83; see [[iris]] and [[thetis]]). Socrates' question leaves out the likeness, which is the poet's, and keeps the fishing, which is the fisherman's.
