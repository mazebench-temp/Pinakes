The traditional title of Book 11 is Ἀγαμέμνονος ἀριστεία, “the Aristeia of Agamemnon”, the king's day of excellence in battle. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter, this one is Λ, lambda (see [[title-of-book-5]]). The title is built like Διομήδους ἀριστεία, the title of Book 5. Its noun is not Homeric: ἀριστεία, “prowess”, is formed from the verb ἀριστεύω, “be the best”, and so from ἄριστος, “best” (see [[aristos]] and [[the-aristeia]]). The book's story of the king's day is told under [[the-aristeia-of-agamemnon]].

**Aelian's list.** The name is old. Aelian, writing that the ancients once sang the poems of Homer in separate parts, gives the names they used, and “the Aristeia of Agamemnon” stands in his list beside “a Doloneia”, the Catalogue of Ships, the battle at the ships, the Patrocleia, the Ransom, the Games for Patroclus and the Obliteration of the Oaths (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[title-of-book-10]]). Where an episode of that name began and ended in performance is not known. Herodotus' quotation shows that the Aristeia of Diomedes ran on past the end of our Book 5 (see [[title-of-book-5]]), and an episode need not have kept to the later bounds of a book.

**What the title covers.** In our text Agamemnon's part runs from 11.15 to 11.283. He calls the Argives to arm and arms himself, in the fullest arming scene of the poem (11.15–46; see [[arming-scene]]); he is the first to break the Trojan line, and kills Bianor and Oileus, Isus and Antiphus, and the two sons of Antimachus, who beg in vain to be taken alive (11.91–147); he drives the Trojans past the tomb of Ilus to the Scaean gates (11.148–180); he kills Iphidamas and Coon, sons of Antenor, after Coon has pierced his arm (11.218–263); and when the wound grows painful he mounts his chariot and is driven back to the ships (11.264–283). Zeus has made that moment the turn of the day: Hector is to give ground while Agamemnon rages among the champions and to attack when he leaves the field (11.186–194; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). When he goes, Hector announces it to the Trojans: “Their best man is gone, and a great triumph has been given me / by Zeus, son of Cronus” (οἴχετʼ ἀνὴρ ὤριστος, 11.288–289).

**What it leaves out.** The rest of the book, two-thirds of it, belongs to other men. Hector charges and kills nine Achaean leaders (11.284–309); Diomedes and Odysseus make a stand until Paris shoots Diomedes through the foot and Socus wounds Odysseus (11.310–488); Paris wounds Machaon (11.489–520); Ajax gives ground before the Trojans step by step (11.521–574); Paris wounds Eurypylus (11.575–595). The verb of the title runs through these scenes, but always of others: in Odysseus' resolve, “whoever would be best in battle” must stand his ground (11.409); Machaon is struck “while he was the best in the fighting” (11.506); Nestor is “the best of all in counsel” (11.627); Mulius, whom the young Nestor killed, “was the best of them in fighting” (11.746); and Peleus charged Achilles “always to be the best” (11.784; see [[always-to-be-the-best]]). By evening “the best men” lie among the ships, “shot or stabbed” (11.658–659, 11.825–826; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). The book named for one man's prowess is also the book that puts the best of the Achaeans out of the fight.

**Nestor and Patroclus.** The title also passes over the two parts of the book that reach furthest beyond it. Nestor's story of his war with the Epeians (11.670–761), the longest of his stories of his youth, is an old man's account of his own first day of prowess. He killed Itymoneus in the raid and Mulius in the battle, took fifty chariots, and at the end “all gave thanks to Zeus among the gods, and to Nestor among men” (11.761; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). And Patroclus' errand to Nestor's hut is, in the narrator's words, “the beginning of evil for him” (11.604). The plan that Nestor gives him (11.794–803) he puts to Achilles in Book 16 (16.36–45), and so the episode that Aelian calls the Patrocleia begins in a book named for Agamemnon (see [[patroclus]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).
