**Homer.** On Lemnos Hera asks Sleep to lull Zeus and offers him a golden throne made by Hephaestus, with a footstool on which to rest his feet at the banquet (14.233–241; see [[throne]] and [[footstool]]). Sleep refuses. The last time he lulled Zeus for her, when Heracles was sailing home from Troy, she roused blasts of grievous winds over the sea and carried Heracles off to Cos, and Zeus woke in a rage and would have thrown Sleep into the sea had Night not saved him (14.249–262; see [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]] and [[night]]). Then Hera offers a wife, one of the younger Graces, and Sleep, who has always longed for Pasithea, accepts on her oath (14.263–280; see [[sleep]], [[pasithea]] and [[graces]]).

**Virgil.** The Trojans have barely lost sight of Sicily when Juno sees them (*Aeneid* 1.34–37). Turning her anger over in her heart, she comes to Aeolia, the land of storms, where king Aeolus keeps the struggling winds penned in a vast cave (1.50–63). A race hateful to her, she tells him, is sailing the Tyrrhenian sea, carrying Ilium and its conquered gods to Italy; let him strike force into the winds and sink the ships, or scatter the Trojans and their bodies over the sea (1.65–70). Her price is a bride. She has twice seven nymphs of surpassing beauty, *quarum quae forma pulcherrima Deiopea*, “of whom the one most beautiful in form is Deiopea”; she will join her to him in lasting marriage and make her his own, to spend all her years with him and make him the father of fine children (1.71–75). Aeolus answers that her task is to know what she wants and his to obey; to her he owes his kingdom, his scepter and the favor of Jupiter, and his place reclining at the feasts of the gods (1.76–80). He strikes the hollow mountain with his spear, and the winds rush out over land and sea (1.81–86).

**What Virgil took.** The two bargains are alike in kind: the queen of the gods buys a lesser god's help with a bride, one of a company of goddesses, named for her beauty or for the lesser god's longing. Homer's Hera promises one of the Graces “to marry and to be called your wife” (δώσω ὀπυιέμεναι καὶ σὴν κεκλῆσθαι ἄκοιτιν, 14.268); Juno promises Deiopea in lasting marriage (1.73). Virgil has joined in one scene what Homer told in two. The storm against a hero at sea belongs in the Iliad to the earlier deception, which Sleep remembers with fear; the bride belongs to the present one. Virgil's Juno buys the storm with the bride, and her victim is again a hero at sea whom the king of the gods means to bring safe to land; Zeus brought Heracles back from Cos to Argos (15.28–30). Even the feast comes back: Hera's throne and footstool are for Sleep to use at the banquet (14.241), and Aeolus thanks Juno for his couch at the banquets of the gods (1.79).

The gods differ. Sleep has once barely escaped the anger of Zeus, and he bargains for an oath by the Styx before he moves (14.270–279); Aeolus asks no oath and obeys at once. And Homer's deception succeeds, while Virgil's storm is undone: Neptune feels the sea in turmoil, sees through “the tricks and the anger of Juno” and calms the waves (*Aeneid* 1.124–143; see [[virgil-neptunes-chariot]]). In *Iliad* 14 Poseidon is the god who profits from Hera's deceit (14.354–362; see [[poseidon]]).
