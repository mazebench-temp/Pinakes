**Homer.** When Iris brings him Zeus' order to leave the battle, Poseidon answers that Zeus has spoken arrogantly, “when I am his equal in honor” (15.186). They are three brothers, sons of Cronus and Rhea; all things were divided in three, and when the lots were shaken each got his share, Poseidon the gray sea, Hades the misty darkness and Zeus the wide sky, while the earth and high Olympus are common to all. So let Zeus, “strong though he is”, stay in his third share (15.187–195; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]] and [[poseidon]]). Zeus' message had claimed rank of another kind: he is far stronger, “and the elder by birth” (15.165–166, 15.181–182; see [[zeus]]).

**Callimachus.** Callimachus' *Hymn to Zeus* tells of the god's birth in Arcadia and his nursing in Crete, and then of his youth. “Fair was your growing, fair your nurture, heavenly Zeus; quickly you came to manhood, and soon the down came on your cheeks. But while you were still a child you devised all things complete; so your brothers, though they were born before you, did not begrudge you heaven for your allotted home” (55–59). Then he turns on the poets: “The ancient singers were not altogether truthful. They said that the lot gave the sons of Cronus their three homes. But who would draw lots for Olympus and for Hades, unless he were a fool indeed? One casts lots for equal shares, and these are as far apart as can be. May my fictions be such as would persuade a listener's ear! It was not lots that made you king of the gods, but the works of your hands, your might and your strength, which you set beside your throne” (60–67).

**What he denies.** Callimachus names no poet, but his words answer the tellings of the division that survive. His πάλος, the lot, is the lot that was shaken in Homer (παλλομένων, 15.191), and his διάτριχα, “in three”, is the word of the Homeric *Hymn to Demeter*, where Hades got his share “when at the first the division was made in three” (διάτριχα δασμὸς ἐτύχθη, 86). Poseidon appealed to the lot to make himself the equal of Zeus. Callimachus answers that a lot is cast only between equal shares, and the sky and the house of the dead are not equal; Zeus' rule was not drawn but won. He follows Hesiod, too, where Homer and Hesiod differ. In the Iliad Zeus is the elder (15.166, 15.182; 13.355); in the *Theogony* he is the youngest of Rhea's children (453–458, 478), and Callimachus' elder brothers yield him heaven of their own accord. The might and strength that sit beside his throne are Hesiod's Kratos and Bia, children of Styx, who are never apart from Zeus (*Theogony* 385–388; see [[hesiod]]).

In the Iliad the lot is the ground of a quarrel that Poseidon gives up only at Iris' warning that the Erinyes follow the elder (15.200–217; see [[erinyes]]). Other later readers took the division as Homer told it: Plato begins the myth of judgment in the *Gorgias* with it, “as Homer says” (523a), and Plutarch sets the gods, content with a third each, against Pompey and Caesar, two men for whom the Roman empire was not enough (*Pompey* 53.6–7; see [[plato]] and [[plutarch]]).
