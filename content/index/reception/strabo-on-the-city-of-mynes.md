**Homer.** Briseis, brought back to Achilles' hut, laments Patroclus. She saw the husband to whom her parents gave her killed before the city, and her three brothers fall (19.291–294); and when “swift Achilles killed my husband / and sacked the city of divine Mynes”, Patroclus would not let her weep (19.295–297; see [[briseis]] and [[patroclus]]). She does not name the city. The Catalogue does: Achilles took Briseis “out of Lyrnessus after much toil, / when he sacked Lyrnessus and the walls of Thebe, / and struck down Mynes and Epistrophus, fighters with the spear” (2.690–692; see [[lyrnessus]], [[mynes]] and [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]]).

**The second dynasty.** In the thirteenth book of the *Geography* Strabo sets out what those who have studied the poet infer: the whole coast of the Troad was under the Trojans at the time of the war, divided into nine dynasties but subject to Priam. Achilles, finding the Ilians walled in, made war outside and took the cities around, “Twelve cities of men I have sacked with my ships, / and eleven, I say, on foot throughout deep-soiled Troy” (9.328–329), and among them were Thebe, Lyrnessus and Pedasus of the Leleges. Briseis was taken at Lyrnessus, “in whose capture he says Mynes fell, as Briseis shows when she laments Patroclus”, and Strabo quotes the lines: by “the city of divine Mynes” the poet means Lyrnessus, as ruled by Mynes, and shows that Mynes fell there fighting. Chryseis was taken at Thebe, Eetion's city, and Strabo counts Eetion's realm as a second Trojan dynasty, “after the one under Mynes” (*Geography* 13.1.7; see [[eetion]] and [[strabo]]).

**Not Thebe.** He returns to the lines when he reaches the plain behind Adramyttium. Thebe was Eetion's city. Since Achilles, when he sacked Lyrnessus and the walls of Thebe, killed Mynes and Epistrophus, Briseis' words cannot mean Thebe, “for that was Eetion's”, but Lyrnessus. Both towns lay in what was later called the plain of Thebe, fought over first by Mysians and Lydians and then by the Greeks who came from Aeolis and Lesbos, and held in his day mostly by the people of Adramyttium; both were deserted, Thebe sixty stades from Adramyttium and Lyrnessus, a strong place, eighty-eight in the other direction (13.1.61; see [[thebe]]).

**Missing from the Catalogue.** Strabo uses the lines a third time to answer a question: why the Cilicians and the Leleges, so close to the Trojans, are not named in the Trojan Catalogue. Their leaders were dead and their cities sacked before it was made. Eetion and his seven sons fell to Achilles, as Andromache tells Hector (6.414–416, 6.421–423), “and in the same way those under Mynes lost both their leaders and their city”: “and struck down Mynes and Epistrophus” (2.692), “and sacked the city of divine Mynes” (19.296; *Geography* 13.3.1; see [[cilicians]] and [[leleges]]).

**Mynes and Briseis.** Strabo takes the city's name from its lord and lets Mynes fall in its defense; he does not say that Mynes was Briseis' husband, whom she leaves unnamed. Walter Leaf reports that tradition made him her husband, though nothing in Homer shows him to be anything but her king (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.296; see [[mynes]]).
