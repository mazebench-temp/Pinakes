In our manuscripts Zeus' message ends "and sorrows hang over the Trojans" (2.15). The Dream repeats the words to Agamemnon (2.32), and Agamemnon repeats them to the elders (2.69).

Aristotle knew another text. In the *Sophistical Refutations*, discussing arguments that turn on accent, he notes how critics solved a difficulty "about the dream of Agamemnon". It was not Zeus himself, they said, who declared δίδομεν δέ οἱ εὖχος ἀρέσθαι, "and we grant him to win glory"; rather, he told the Dream to grant it. The *Poetics* names the critic who proposed this, Hippias of Thasos, of whom nothing else is known.

The difficulty was that the promise is false, and a god who makes it lies. Read δίδομεν, the verb is indicative, and Zeus says "we grant". Read διδόμεν, it is an infinitive with the force of a command, and the clause becomes part of Zeus' instructions: "grant him to win glory". The false promise now belongs to the Dream, not to Zeus. Greek was written without accents in Homer's time and long after, so the question was how to read the letters aloud.

No manuscript has these words at 2.15. Almost the same clause, with τοι ("to you") for οἱ ("to him"), ends Poseidon's promise to Achilles at 21.297. Since the solution turns on Zeus giving an order, the words evidently stood in Zeus' speech to the Dream in the text Aristotle used, most likely at the end of 2.15, where ours has "and sorrows hang over the Trojans".

Our text still tells of a deception. Zeus sends the Dream to bring about Achaean deaths (2.3–4), and the narrator calls Agamemnon a fool for believing it (2.36–38). But it contains no promise in Zeus' own voice. The same objection, that a god must not deceive, led Plato to strike the scene from his ideal city: see [[plato-lying-dream]].
