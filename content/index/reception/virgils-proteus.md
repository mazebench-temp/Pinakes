summary: The fourth book of Virgil's Georgics ends with Homer's capture of Proteus retold. The shepherd Aristaeus, who has lost his bees, is sent by his mother, the nymph Cyrene, to the seer Proteus in the Carpathian sea; at noon the old man counts his seals like a herdsman, is seized and bound, turns into fire, beast and river, and at last reveals the anger of Orpheus for Eurydice. Ovid brings Proteus back twice in the Metamorphoses, once as the “Carpathian seer”.

**The fourth Georgic.** The last book of the *Georgics*, on bees, ends by asking which god found the art of raising a new swarm when a hive is lost (*Georgics* 4.315–316). The shepherd Aristaeus has lost his bees to disease and hunger, and complains to his mother, the nymph Cyrene, at the source of the river Peneus (*Georgics* 4.317–332). She tells him where to go: “There is in Neptune's Carpathian gulf a seer, / sea-blue Proteus, who measures the great sea with fishes / and with a chariot of two-footed horses” (*Georgics* 4.387–389). He is now revisiting the harbors of Emathia and his native Pallene; the nymphs revere him, and old Nereus too, “for the seer knows all things, / what are, what have been, what are soon to come”, and he pastures Neptune's huge herds and ugly seals beneath the waves (*Georgics* 4.390–395). He must be taken in chains, since he will give no counsel without force and cannot be moved by prayer (*Georgics* 4.396–400). When he is held, he will become a bristling boar, a black tiger, a scaly serpent, a tawny lioness, the crackle of flame, or thin water; the more he turns into every shape, the tighter the bonds must be drawn, “until, his body changed, he is such as / you saw him when he closed his eyes at the start of sleep” (*Georgics* 4.405–414; plain renderings throughout).

**Homer's scene in Latin.** Cyrene does for her son what Eidothea did for Menelaus: she tells him the old man's habits and his wiles, takes him to the ambush herself, and anoints him with the fragrance of ambrosia (*Georgics* 4.415–418), as Eidothea set ambrosia under each man's nose against the smell of the seals (*Odyssey* 4.444–446; see [[eidothea]]). At the hour of greatest heat Proteus comes up from the sea to his cave, the seals lie down to sleep along the shore, and he, “like the keeper of a fold on the mountains” when evening brings the calves home, sits on a rock in their midst and counts them over (*Georgics* 4.425–436), as Homer's Old Man comes out at midday, counts his seals and lies down among them “like a herdsman among his flocks of sheep” (*Odyssey* 4.400–413, 4.450–453). Aristaeus rushes on him with a great shout and binds him, and the old man, “on his side, not forgetful of his art” (*suae contra non immemor artis*), turns himself into every marvel, into fire and a fearful beast and a flowing river (*Georgics* 4.437–442): Homer's verse is “nor did the old man forget his crafty art” (*Odyssey* 4.455). When no trick gives him a way out, he comes back to himself and asks with a man's voice who sent the boldest of young men to his home, and what he wants; “You know, Proteus, you know it yourself”, Aristaeus answers (*Georgics* 4.443–449), as Menelaus answers, “You know, old man” (*Odyssey* 4.462–465). The seer's knowledge of what is, what has been and what is to come is the Iliad's verse on Calchas, who “knew the things that are, the things to come, and the things that were before” (*Iliad* 1.70; see [[calchas]]).

**What he tells.** Homer's Proteus tells Menelaus of the sacrifice he owes the gods and of the deaths of Ajax and Agamemnon (*Odyssey* 4.472–537). Virgil's tells Aristaeus that the anger of a god pursues him: Orpheus is punishing him for Eurydice, who did not see the water snake in the grass at her feet as she fled from him, and the story of Orpheus' descent to the dead, his backward look and his death follows (*Georgics* 4.453–527). “So Proteus spoke, and threw himself into the deep sea” (*Georgics* 4.528), as Homer's Old Man “plunged beneath the billowing sea” (*Odyssey* 4.570). Cyrene then gives the remedy: four bulls and four heifers sacrificed to the nymphs, and offerings to Orpheus and Eurydice on the ninth day (*Georgics* 4.530–547). Aristaeus obeys, and bees swarm from the carcasses (*Georgics* 4.548–558). Menelaus too, to get home, must go back to the Nile and offer the hecatombs he owes (*Odyssey* 4.472–480, 4.581–583).

**Where Proteus lives.** Homer's Proteus is “Proteus of Egypt”, and Menelaus finds him on Pharos, a day's sail off the Egyptian coast (*Odyssey* 4.354–357, 4.385; see [[proteus]]). Virgil moves him to the Carpathian sea and gives him a home in Pallene, in the north of the Aegean, and makes him a seer, *vates*, where Homer's is “unerring”.

**Ovid.** Ovid's Achelous, telling Theseus of those who can pass into many forms, begins with Proteus, “dweller in the sea that embraces the earth”: men have seen him as a young man and a lion, a boar, a snake, a bull, a stone, a tree, a river and fire (*Metamorphoses* 8.730–737). Merry sets the list beside Homer's (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.458). In Book 11 Proteus foretells to Thetis that she will bear a son greater than his father; Peleus tries to take the goddess while she sleeps in her cave, and lets her go when she turns into a bird, a tree and a spotted tigress; then “the Carpathian seer” tells him from the middle of the sea to bind her while she sleeps, “and let her not deceive you, feigning a hundred shapes, / but press her, whatever she becomes, until she takes again the form she had” (*Metamorphoses* 11.221–223, 11.238–256). It is Eidothea's counsel to Menelaus, “but you hold him steadfastly” (*Odyssey* 4.419), given by Proteus himself; Merry compares it, and notes that the power to take many forms belongs above all to gods of the sea and of rivers, Nereus, Glaucus, Achelous (on 4.419).
