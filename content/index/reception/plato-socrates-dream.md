**Homer.** Achilles' first answer to Odysseus ends with a threat to go home. Tomorrow, when he has sacrificed and loaded his ships, Odysseus will see them on the Hellespont at dawn, and if Poseidon grants fair sailing, “on the third day I would reach deep-soiled Phthia” (9.356–363; see [[phthia]]). To go home is to choose the long life without fame of his two fates (9.414–416; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]] and [[nostos]]). He does not sail, and he never sees Phthia again. Thetis knows that she will not welcome him home (18.59–60), and Achilles, choosing to kill Hector, says that he will not return to his native land (18.101).

**The dream.** At the opening of the *Crito*, Crito comes to the prison before dawn and finds Socrates peacefully asleep. He brings bad news. The ship from Delos, whose return the city awaited before the execution, has been seen off Sunium; it will arrive today, and tomorrow Socrates must die. Socrates thinks otherwise. He must die on the day after the ship comes, and the ship, he says, will come not today but tomorrow; he judges so from a dream he saw a little earlier that night (43c–44a).

“A woman seemed to come to me, beautiful and fair to see, dressed in white, and she called me and said: ‘Socrates, on the third day you would reach deep-soiled Phthia.’” “A strange dream, Socrates,” says Crito. “A clear one, as it seems to me,” Socrates answers (44a–b).

The woman changes one word of the verse, Achilles' “I would reach” (ἱκοίμην) into “you would reach” (ἵκοιο), and keeps the rest. Counting today as the first day, the third is the day after tomorrow, and Phthia, the home that Achilles threatened to sail to and never reached, stands for the place Socrates will go when he dies.

**Thessaly or Hades.** The rest of the dialogue offers Socrates the other journey. Crito has friends in Thessaly, the country of Phthia, who would honor and protect him if he escaped (45c). Socrates answers with the speech of the Laws of Athens, who mock the picture of him running off to Crito's friends in Thessaly in disguise, to feast there as if he had gone abroad to dinner, and ask whether his friends would care for his children if he went away to Thessaly and not if he went away to Hades (53d–54a). He stays and dies. In the *Apology* he had already set himself beside Achilles, who chose to die avenging Patroclus rather than stay by the ships a laughingstock, a burden on the earth (*Apology* 28c–d, on 18.94–104; see [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]).
