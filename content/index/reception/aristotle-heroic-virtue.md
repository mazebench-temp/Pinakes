**Aristotle.** The seventh book of the *Nicomachean Ethics* makes a new beginning. Of the things to be avoided in character, Aristotle says, there are three kinds, vice, lack of self-control and brutishness (θηριότης). The contraries of the first two are plain, virtue and self-control; “but as against brutishness it would be most fitting to speak of the virtue that is above us, a heroic and divine sort, as Homer has made Priam say about Hector that he was exceedingly good: ‘nor did he seem / the son of a mortal man, but of a god’” (*Nicomachean Ethics* 7.1, 1145a15–22). So if, as they say, men become gods through an excess of virtue, such would be the state opposed to the brutish. As there is neither vice nor virtue in a beast, so there is none in a god: the god's state is something more honorable than virtue, the beast's a different kind of thing from vice. And as a divine man is rare, as the Spartans call one whom they greatly admire (σεῖος ἀνήρ, in their dialect), so the brutish man is rare among men, found mostly among barbarians, and sometimes the result of disease or maiming (1145a22–33; see [[aristotle]]).

**The verse.** Aristotle quotes the second half of a sentence. In the poem it runs: “and Hector, who was a god among men, and did not seem / the son of a mortal man, but of a god” (24.258–259). The first half, ὃς θεὸς ἔσκε μετʼ ἀνδράσιν, says outright what Aristotle infers, that excellence among men can amount to divinity. Priam speaks it to his nine surviving sons as he shouts at them to make the wagon ready: “I wish that all of you / had been killed beside the swift ships in Hector's place”, for he has lost his best sons, godlike Mestor, Troilus who fought from his chariot, and Hector, and “Ares has destroyed them, and these disgraces are all that is left, / liars and dancers, best at beating the dancing floor” (24.253–262; see [[sons-of-priam]], [[mestor]] and [[troilus]]). Aristotle's example of the virtue above the human is a father's grief, spoken in anger against the living. Hecuba's lament in *Iliad* 22 says the same of Hector in other words: the Trojans and the Trojan women “greeted you / as a god” (22.434–435; see [[hector]]).

**The other extreme.** The book that gives Aristotle his divine man also shows the savagery he sets against it, and from the Trojan side as well as the Achaean. Apollo tells the gods that Achilles “knows savage ways, like a lion / that gives way to his great strength and proud spirit / and goes among the flocks of men to take his feast” (24.41–43; see [[lion]]), and Hecuba, calling him an eater of raw flesh, wishes that she could “fix my teeth / in the middle of his liver and eat it” (24.207, 24.212–213; see [[hecuba]]), as Achilles had wished his fury would let him carve Hector's flesh and eat it raw (22.346–347). Among the brutish states Aristotle lists later in the same book are the savages around the Black Sea who delight in raw flesh or in human flesh, and among the morbid ones the man who ate his fellow slave's liver (*Nicomachean Ethics* 7.5, 1148b19–27). He does not cite Homer there.
