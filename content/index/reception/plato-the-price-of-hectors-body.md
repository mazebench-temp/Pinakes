Proclus answered that the heroes took what was offered without asking for it, as a token of the giver's repentance, and that ransom for an enemy's body may have been the custom, or a general's policy. **Plato.** In the third book of the *Republic* Socrates sets the rules for the stories told to the guardians of his city, who must be neither open to bribes nor lovers of money (*Republic* 3.390d). They must not sing that “gifts persuade gods, gifts persuade reverend kings”, nor praise Phoenix for advising Achilles to take the gifts and help the Achaeans, but without gifts not to give up his wrath (see [[plato-phoenix-and-the-gifts]]). “Nor shall we think it right, or agree, that Achilles himself was so fond of money as to take gifts from Agamemnon, and again to release a corpse when he had received a price for it, and otherwise not to be willing” (τιμὴν αὖ λαβόντα νεκροῦ ἀπολύειν, ἄλλως δὲ μὴ ἐθέλειν, *Republic* 3.390e–391a). Socrates shrinks, “because of Homer”, from saying that such things are not even holy to say of Achilles. He goes on to the rest: Achilles' words to Apollo, his fight with the river, the hair he gave to the dead Patroclus, the draggings of Hector around the tomb of Patroclus and the slaughter of the captives at the pyre. All these the city will deny were truly told, and it will not let its people believe that the son of a goddess and of Peleus, reared by the wisest Chiron, had in him two diseases opposed to each other, meanness with love of money, and arrogance toward gods and men (*Republic* 3.391a–c; see [[plato-achilles-and-the-river]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]]). The first disease is the charge of the gifts and the corpse. Plato's words are those of a sale: τιμή, a price, and ἀπολύειν, to release, from the verb that gives the book its title (see [[title-of-book-24]]).

**Proclus.** One of Proclus' essays on the *Republic* has a chapter on “what must be said to Socrates' objections about the love of money that Homer gives the heroes” (*On the Republic* I 143 Kroll). He states the charge again: why did Phoenix advise Achilles to give up his anger if he received gifts, and not otherwise, and why did Achilles take gifts from Agamemnon for the insult, and give back Hector's body only on receiving money? The hearer of such things will grow a terrible and insatiable desire for possessions. His answer, as he says, is brief. Phoenix advised Achilles to take the money and stop his anger, and Achilles took it and let his anger go; both made the gift a proof of the giver's repentance, without satisfying any love of money or taking wealth for the measure of happiness, and neither asked for the money, but accepted it when it was offered. “And if he gave back the body of Hector to his father for money, perhaps we shall say that there was some such custom, to accept ransom for the bodies of enemies” (λύτρα δέχεσθαι τῶν πολεμίων σωμάτων); and one might call this a general's policy too, to cut down the resources of the enemy and increase those of one's own men, who are forced to fight in a foreign land. All this had its reason for those heroes, acting in their circumstances and by other customs, but it is wholly unfit to be heard by those whom the lawgiver of the *Republic* rears, whose natures are philosophic and from whom possessions are taken away altogether (I 144 Kroll).

Then he turns the charge with Homer's own words. Achilles reproaches Agamemnon for his love of money, calling him “most covetous of all men”, and makes light of his own share, going back to his ships “with something small but dear” when he is worn out with fighting (1.122, 1.167–168); he would not take Agamemnon's gifts when they were first offered, since the time for reconciliation had not yet come; and at the games he gave every contestant a fitting gift and honored Nestor, who was too old to compete, above the rest. Phoenix only bade him keep an old Greek custom, for “So too we have heard the famous deeds of the men of old … they could be won with gifts and swayed by words” (9.524–526). These things suited the heroic age and its customs, and Homer imitated them with the highest art (I 144–145 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). Proclus names no line of *Iliad* 24 in his answer.

**The poem.** Achilles never asks for the ransom. In *Iliad* 22 he refused it outright: not ten or twenty times the ransom, “not even if he should bid them weigh out your own body in gold, / Priam, descendant of Dardanus”, would Hector's mother lay him on a bier (22.349–354; see [[the-death-of-hector]]). The ransom is Zeus' plan. He sends Thetis to tell her son that the gods are angry with him because he keeps Hector and has not released him, and Iris to send Priam “to ransom his dear son, going to the ships of the Achaeans, / and bring gifts to Achilles that may warm his heart” (24.112–119; see [[zeus]]). Thetis gives the command — “But come, release him, and accept a ransom for the body” — and Achilles agrees at once: “So be it: let him who brings the ransom carry off the body, / if the Olympian himself so bids with a ready heart” (24.137–140; see [[thetis]]). In the hut it is Priam who presses the gifts on him, “and accept the ransom, / the many things that we bring you” (24.555–556), and Achilles who refuses to be hurried, since he means to release Hector himself and a messenger came to him from Zeus (24.560–562). He leaves two mantles and a tunic of the ransom to wrap the body (24.580–581), and only when it lies on the wagon does he speak of the price, to the dead man: the ransom is not unseemly, and Patroclus shall have his share of it (24.592–595; see [[patroclus]] and [[apoina]]).

Plato was not the first to be troubled by those last lines. The Alexandrians athetized them, as Walter Leaf reports, “because he does not rightly say that he released the body for the sake of gifts; for he was compelled by Zeus, since he would not have exchanged the vengeance for Patroclus for gifts”. Leaf finds the argument insufficient: Zeus' command is itself conditional on the bringing of the ransom, and only the ransom lets Achilles plead that giving up the body is not dishonorable (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.594; see the translation's note on 24.594). On 24.110 he makes the same point of Zeus' “glory”: in the heroic code, as *Iliad* 9 shows, honor lay in receiving something in return.
