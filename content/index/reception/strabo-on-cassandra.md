**The Ilians' claim.** The people of Ilium in Strabo's day said that their city had not been wholly wiped out when the Achaeans took it, and had never been deserted. Their evidence was the Locrian maidens, who began to be sent a little after the war and came every year (*Geography* 13.1.40).

**Strabo's answer.** “These things too are not Homeric,” Strabo replies. Homer knows nothing of Cassandra's violation; he says that she was a maiden at that time, and Strabo quotes the verses on Othryoneus, who had come from Cabesus “at the report of the war” and asked for “the most beautiful in form of the daughters of Priam, Cassandra, without bride-price” (13.363–366; see [[othryoneus]] and [[cassandra]]). Homer does not mention violence either, or say that Ajax perished in the shipwreck through Athena's wrath or for any such cause. He says that Ajax was hated by Athena in common with the rest, for she was angry with them all, since all had sinned against her temple, and that he perished at Poseidon's hands for his boasting. And the Locrian maidens, Strabo adds, were sent only when the Persians already ruled (*Geography* 13.1.40). The argument belongs to a longer one: against the Ilians Strabo goes on to cite the verses in which Homer speaks plainly of the city's destruction, among them “and the city of Priam was sacked in the tenth year” (12.15; *Geography* 13.1.41).

**What Homer says.** In the Odyssey Menelaus repeats what Proteus told him of Ajax's death. Poseidon drove him onto the great Gyraean rocks and saved him from the sea, and he would have escaped his doom, “though hated by Athena”, had he not boasted that he had escaped the gulf of the sea against the will of the gods; Poseidon heard, split the rock with his trident, and Ajax drowned (*Odyssey* 4.499–511; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). The Odyssey speaks of the grievous homecoming that Athena laid on the Achaeans (*Odyssey* 1.326–327) and of their sin against her (*Odyssey* 5.108–109), and Nestor says that Zeus planned a woeful return because not all of them were wise or just, and many came to an evil end through the wrath of the gray-eyed goddess (*Odyssey* 3.130–135); none of these passages says what the sin was. Of Cassandra the Odyssey knows only her death: Agamemnon's shade heard her cry as Clytemnestra killed her over him (*Odyssey* 11.421–423). In the Iliad she is the maiden of Othryoneus' bargain, and the first in Troy to see Priam bring Hector home (24.697–706).

**The ancient commentary.** A note on 13.66, where the son of Oileus is named, tells the story that Strabo denies to Homer. Ajax shamed Cassandra, a suppliant of Athena, in the goddess's precinct, so that the goddess turned the eyes of her image up to the roof. She raised great storms against the Greeks as they came home by Euboea, and many perished. Ajax swam to the rocks called Gyrae and said that he had been saved against the will of the gods; Poseidon in anger split the rock and gave him to the waves, and Thetis, pitying his body, buried it. Athena did not cease from her anger even then, but made the Locrians send maidens chosen by lot to Ilium for a thousand years. “The story is in Callimachus, in the first book of the *Aetia*, and in the poet, in the fourth book of the Odyssey, in outline” (scholium A on 13.66). Another note on the verse of Cassandra's beauty records that some took εἶδος, “form”, for knowledge, the knowledge of her prophecy, and it reconciles the verse with the same praise of her sister Laodice, “most beautiful in form of the daughters of Priam” (3.124; again at 6.252): Cassandra was the most beautiful of the unmarried daughters, Laodice of the married (scholia on 13.365; see [[laodice]] and [[best-in-looks]]).
