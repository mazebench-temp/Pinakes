summary: Phoenix says that Peleus sent him to Troy to make the young Achilles “a speaker of words and a doer of deeds”. Cicero cites the line for the old unity of eloquence and wisdom that Socrates broke apart, Quintilian makes Phoenix the model of the teacher who teaches both speaking and doing, and Strabo asks whether Phoenix went to Troy as a commander or only as Achilles' tutor.

**Homer.** Phoenix cannot be left behind, he says, because Peleus sent him with the boy, who knew nothing yet of war or of assemblies, to teach him both. Assembly and battle are the two places where the poem's men prove themselves, and in his anger Achilles has left both, “never going to the assembly, where men win glory, nor ever to war” (1.490–491; see [[assembly]]). ῥητήρ, “speaker”, occurs only here in Homer, and πρηκτήρ, “doer”, elsewhere only of traders (*Odyssey* 8.162). Phoenix's own speech, the longest in the Iliad, is the lesson in words, offered to bring his pupil back to deeds (9.434–605; see [[phoenix]] and [[speaker-of-words-doer-of-deeds]]).

**Cicero.** In the third book of *On the Orator*, Crassus argues that eloquence must be joined to wisdom and goodness, or it is a weapon put in the hands of madmen. The old Greeks called this joined art of thinking and speaking wisdom. “That ancient learning seems to have been the teacher alike of acting rightly and of speaking well; nor were the teachers separate, but the same men taught how to live and how to speak, like Homer's Phoenix, who says that he was given to the young Achilles by his father Peleus as a companion in war, to make him an orator of words and a doer of deeds” (*oratorem verborum actoremque rerum*, 3.57). Later men with leisure turned to the poets, to geometry and music, or invented dialectic, and Socrates, the first of those who scorned the art of speaking, separated in his discussions the knowledge of thinking wisely from that of speaking with grace. From him came “that split, as it were, between the tongue and the heart”, so that some teach us to be wise and others to speak (3.58–61).

**Quintilian.** Quintilian, arguing in the *Institutio Oratoria* that a boy should be given to the best teacher from the start, invokes Homer: “let him, then, be most excellent both in eloquence and in character, who will teach speaking and doing after the example of Homer's Phoenix” (*qui ad Phoenicis Homerici exemplum dicere ac facere doceat*, 2.3.12).

**Strabo.** The geographer read the verse for a different question. Homer never mentions a Dolopian force at Troy, nor does he send Phoenix, their lord, into danger as he sends Nestor; Pindar did, making Phoenix lead “a bold throng of Dolopians, slingers”. Strabo holds that the Dolopians must be understood as present with Achilles' men, since otherwise Phoenix would seem not to go to war with Achilles at all, but to follow him only as tutor and speaker, or at most as counselor; and he adds that the verses mean to show this too, “for such is ‘to be a speaker of words and a doer of deeds’” (*Geography* 9.5.5; see [[dolopians]]). In the Iliad Phoenix does command, in Book 16, the fourth company of the Myrmidons (16.196).
