**Thucydides.** In the chapters on early Greece that open his history, Thucydides says that the sea first made the Greeks raiders. When the Greeks and the barbarians of the coasts and the islands began to cross more often by ship to one another, he says, they turned to piracy, led by men of some power, for their own gain and to feed the needy; they fell on cities that were unwalled and lived in villages, and plundered them, and got most of their living from it, “since the deed brought as yet no shame, but rather some glory” (Thucydides 1.5.1). His proof is double: some peoples of the mainland still count it an honor to do it well, “and the old poets, who have the question put to those who sail in, everywhere alike, whether they are pirates (εἰ λῃσταί εἰσιν), as though neither those who are asked disowned the deed, nor those who wanted to know reproached them with it” (Thucydides 1.5.2). Men plundered one another by land as well, and the Ozolian Locrians, the Aetolians and the Acarnanians still lived in the old way and kept the habit of going armed (Thucydides 1.5.3; see [[thucydides]]).

**The poets' question.** Thucydides names no poem. The question that he means is in the Odyssey twice, in the same four verses. Nestor puts it to his guests at Pylos when the meal is over, “now that they have taken their fill of food” (*Odyssey* 3.70), and the Cyclops puts it to Odysseus and his men in his cave (*Odyssey* 9.252–255; see [[strangers-who-are-you]] and [[piracy]]). Neither answer takes offense. Telemachus tells Nestor where he has come from and that “this business I speak of is private, not public” (*Odyssey* 3.81–82; see [[prexis]]); Odysseus tells the Cyclops that they are Achaeans driven off their course on the way home from Troy (*Odyssey* 9.259–262). Merry, quoting Thucydides on this line, takes the passage as evidence that piracy was “a recognized employment” in early Greece, and compares Caesar's remark that among the Germans raids made outside the borders of each state carry no disgrace (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.73; *Gallic War* 6.23.6).

**The Hymn to Apollo.** The same four verses stand in the Homeric *Hymn to Apollo*, which Thucydides read as Homer's: he quotes its verses on the festival at Delos as words of Homer “from the prelude to Apollo” (Thucydides 3.104.4–6). There the asker is a god. Apollo, looking for men to serve his temple at Pytho, sees a ship of Cretans from Cnossus sailing “for trade and profit” (ἐπὶ πρῆξιν καὶ χρήματα) to sandy Pylos, leaps on board in the shape of a dolphin, and steers the ship round Malea, past Pylos itself, and on along the Peloponnese to Crisa (388–439). Then, in the likeness of a young man, he asks them who they are, and whether they sail on some business or wander like pirates (452–455). Their leader answers that the stranger looks like no mortal but like the immortal gods, and that they were bound “for Pylos from Crete” (464–470). At Pylos a mortal asks the question of strangers, one of them a goddess in a man's likeness; in the Hymn a god in a man's likeness asks it of mortals who were on their way to Pylos.

**Two readings.** At Pylos the host asks the question only after the strangers have eaten (*Odyssey* 3.69–70; see [[xenia]]), and the pirates of the question are men who stake their lives and bring evil to foreigners (*Odyssey* 3.73–74). For Thucydides the question is evidence of a time when raiding by sea brought no shame. The heroes' own raids fit his reading: Nestor remembers the Achaeans at Troy “roaming after plunder wherever Achilles led” (*Odyssey* 3.106; see [[raiding]]).
