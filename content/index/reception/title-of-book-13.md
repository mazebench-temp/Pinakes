The traditional title of Book 13 is Μάχη ἐπὶ ταῖς ναυσίν, “the battle at the ships”. In the division of the poem into twenty-four books, each named by a letter, this one is Ν, nu (see [[title-of-book-5]]). The book's story is told under [[the-battle-at-the-ships]], and the day it belongs to under [[the-third-day-of-battle]].

**The words.** The Teichomachia of Book 12 is a compound that Homer never uses (see [[title-of-book-12]]). This title is a phrase of the poem's own words. μάχη, “battle”, is everywhere in the Iliad, and so are the ships, which stand for the Achaean camp and for the army's hope of going home (see [[ships]]). In Book 5 Hera, in Stentor's likeness, sets them side by side to shame the Argives: “but now they are fighting far from the city, by the hollow ships” (5.791). In Book 13 Poseidon says the verse again (13.107), marveling at “the Trojans advancing against our ships” (13.101). The son of Oileus knows that a god “bids us fight beside the ships” (13.69), and Paris tells Hector that he has fought without rest “ever since you roused the battle of your companions beside the ships” (ἐξ οὗ γὰρ παρὰ νηυσὶ μάχην ἤγειρας ἑταίρων, 13.778). In the next book Nestor uses the noun and the preposition of the title: the wall has fallen, and the Trojans keep up an unrelenting battle at the swift ships (ἐπὶ νηυσὶ θοῇσι μάχην ἀλίαστον ἔχουσι, 14.57). The ancient commentary finds the whole situation in the book's first line, where Zeus brings the Trojans and Hector “close to the ships” (13.1). The word is full of feeling, it says: not to the wall only, but to the ships, the Greeks' last remaining hope (scholia on 13.1).

**Aelian.** The name is old. Aelian, writing that the ancients once sang the poems of Homer in separate parts, begins his list of the names they used with this one, τὴν ἐπὶ ναυσὶ μάχην, “the battle at the ships”. “A Doloneia”, the Aristeia of Agamemnon, the Catalogue of Ships, the Patrocleia, the Ransom, the Games for Patroclus and the Obliteration of the Oaths follow (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[aelian]] and [[title-of-book-10]]). The list does not keep the order of the poem, and Aelian does not say where the episode began and ended. The Trojans pour over the wall at the end of Book 12, and the Danaans flee among the hollow ships (12.469–471); Patroclus drives the Trojans away only in Book 16 (16.284–305). A rhapsode's “battle at the ships” could have taken in any part of that fighting. The later title adds the article, ἐπὶ ταῖς ναυσίν.

**Strabo.** Strabo uses the phrase of this book. The men called Phthians, he says, are those under Achilles, Protesilaus and Philoctetes, and the poet is his witness: having named Achilles' men in the Catalogue, “in the battle at the ships” (ἐν τῇ ἐπὶ ναυσὶ μάχῃ) he keeps them at rest by the ships with Achilles, and has the men of Philoctetes and of Protesilaus fight under Medon and Podarces. He quotes 13.685–686, 13.693 and 13.699–700 (*Geography* 9.5.7; see [[phthians]] and [[strabo]]). In the same sentence he says “in the Catalogue” (ἐν τῷ καταλόγῳ), and the two phrases name two parts of the poem in the same way. Elsewhere Strabo reports Posidonius' proposal to write Μοισῶν for Μυσῶν “in the thirteenth” (ἐν τῷ τρισκαιδεκάτῳ), that is, at 13.5 (*Geography* 7.3.3; see [[strabo-on-the-mare-milkers]]). By then the book could be cited by its number as well as by its subject.

**What the title covers.** The book begins at the ships and never leaves them. Zeus brings Hector and the Trojans to them and looks away (13.1–9); Poseidon comes over the sea from Samothrace and rallies the Achaeans, and the close ranks around the two Ajaxes stop Hector's charge (13.10–168). Then the fighting spreads along the camp: Idomeneus and Meriones on the left (13.206–672), the two Ajaxes at the breach where Hector first broke in, “where the ships of Ajax and of Protesilaus were, / drawn up on the shore of the gray sea”, and the wall was lowest (13.679–722). The book ends with Polydamas' counsel, Hector's rebuke of Paris, Ajax's challenge and the shout of both armies (13.723–837).

**What it leaves out.** The title names a place, and passes over the two who carry the book. Poseidon's journey and his two disguises fill its first quarter, and the narrator gives both his reason and his limit: he helps the Achaeans secretly, “in the likeness of a man”, since Zeus was born first and knew more (13.351–357; see [[poseidon]] and [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). And the book is Idomeneus' great day, from his arming in his hut to the moment when his feet no longer carry him nimbly and he gives ground step by step (13.240–516; see [[idomeneus]]). Books 5 and 11 are named for the aristeiai of Diomedes and Agamemnon; no title names his (see [[title-of-book-5]] and [[title-of-book-11]]).

Nor does the book bring the battle to the ships themselves. In Book 13 no Trojan lays a hand on a ship. Hector's threat to cut his way through the huts and ships to the sea ends against the close ranks (13.143–148), and the Trojans fight beside the ships, not on them. The next two books have titles of their own, Διὸς ἀπάτη, “the Deception of Zeus”, and Παλίωξις παρὰ τῶν νεῶν, “the pursuit back from the ships”. The second takes its words from Book 15, where Zeus foretells, and the narrator repeats, the turn of the battle that is to come, παλίωξιν παρὰ νηῶν (15.69, 15.601). In that book Hector lays hold of the stern of the ship that brought Protesilaus to Troy and calls for fire (15.704–725), and in Book 16 the first ship burns, until Patroclus puts out the fire and leaves it half-burned (16.112–124, 16.293–294; see [[protesilaus]]). As with the fight at the wall, the title names the action of one book; the battle at the ships runs from Book 13 into Book 16.
