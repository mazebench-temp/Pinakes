The *Phoenician Women* (about 410 BCE) is Euripides' play on the war of the Seven against Thebes, fought between the sons of Oedipus: Eteocles holds the city, and his brother Polynices has come back with an Argive army to take it. After the prologue, the young Antigone, who is kept indoors like any girl of good family, is allowed onto the roof of the palace with the old servant who looks after her, once he has made sure that no one in the streets can see her (88–201).

From there she looks down on the army camped on the plain, and asks, again and again, who this one is. The old man answers, among them Tydeus, Parthenopaeus, Amphiaraus, Capaneus, and her own brother Polynices, whom she longs to embrace. He can name them because he has been in their camp, carrying a truce to her brother.

The scene is modeled on the view from the wall in Iliad 3, and turns it around. In Homer an old king asks a young woman to name the enemy, and she answers; in Euripides a young woman asks, and an old man answers. Helen looks at the army that came because of her, and misses her own brothers in it (3.236–244); Antigone looks at an army led by her brother against her city. The old man's knowledge comes from an embassy under truce, as Antenor's knowledge of Menelaus and Odysseus came from their embassy to Troy (3.205–224).

Statius gave Antigone the same scene again in the seventh book of his *Thebaid*, with the old Phorbas as her guide on the walls.
