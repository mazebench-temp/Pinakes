**The oracle at Delos.** After the fall of Troy Virgil's Aeneas comes to Delos, where King Anius, the priest of Phoebus, receives the exiles, and he prays in the god's temple of ancient stone: “Give us a home of our own, Thymbraean; give the weary walls, and a race, and a city that will last; preserve this second Pergamum of Troy, the remnant left by the Greeks and by pitiless Achilles” (*Aeneid* 3.84–87). The threshold and the god's laurel tremble, and a voice answers: “Hardy sons of Dardanus, the land that first bore you from your fathers' stock will take you back to its rich breast. Seek out your ancient mother. Here the house of Aeneas will rule over all shores, and his children's children, and those who will be born from them” (*hic domus Aeneae cunctis dominabitur oris / et nati natorum et qui nascentur ab illis*, *Aeneid* 3.94–98).

**Poseidon's words.** The last two verses are Poseidon's prophecy in Book 20, where he tells the gods why Aeneas must escape: “and now mighty Aeneas shall be lord over the Trojans, / and his children's children, who will be born hereafter” (20.307–308; see [[the-rule-of-aeneas]]). Walter Leaf calls the prophecy the foundation of the legend of the *Aeneid*, says that Virgil translates it here, and infers that Virgil read γένωνται, “will be born”, and not the variant λίπωνται (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.307). *Et nati natorum* is καὶ παίδων παῖδες. But Virgil's house of Aeneas does not rule the Trojans; it rules “over all shores”. Strabo reports that some wrote the Homeric verse as Αἰνείαο γένος πάντεσσιν ἀνάξει, “the race of Aeneas shall rule over all”, meaning the Romans (*Geography* 13.1.53; see [[strabo-on-the-rule-of-aeneas]]).

**The ancient mother.** Anchises takes the ancient mother for Crete, the home of their ancestor Teucer, who first came to the Rhoetean shore and chose a place for his kingdom: “not yet had Ilium and the towers of Pergamum stood; they dwelt in the depths of the valleys” (*Aeneid* 3.102–110). In the Iliad Dardania was founded “since sacred Ilios was not yet / built in the plain”, and its people lived on the slopes of Ida (20.216–218; see [[plato-the-slopes-of-ida]]). The Trojans settle in Crete until a plague drives them to seek the oracle again (*Aeneid* 3.121–146); then the household gods tell Aeneas in a dream that Apollo meant Hesperia, now called Italy: “this is our own home; from here came Dardanus, and father Iasius, from whom our race began” (*Aeneid* 3.163–168). Virgil's Dardanus, the son of Zeus with whom Aeneas' genealogy begins (20.215; see [[dardanus]]), had come to Troy from Italy.

**Neptune's rescue.** In the fifth book Venus begs Neptune for a safe crossing to Italy, and he reminds her of his care for her son: “Nor was my care for your Aeneas less on land, I call Xanthus and Simois to witness. When Achilles, pursuing the Trojan columns, drove them breathless against their walls and gave many thousands to death, and the rivers groaned, choked, and Xanthus could not find his way and roll out to the sea, then I snatched Aeneas away in a hollow cloud, when he had met the strong son of Peleus with neither the gods nor his strength equal, though I longed to overturn from their foundations the walls of perjured Troy, which my own hands had built” (*Aeneid* 5.799–811; see [[virgil-neptunes-chariot]]). The rescue is Book 20's. Poseidon, though no friend of Troy, saves Aeneas from Achilles, whom he calls “at once stronger than you and dearer to the immortals” (20.334), pouring a mist over Achilles' eyes and sending Aeneas flying over the ranks to the edge of the battle (20.318–329; see [[divine-rescue]]). Neptune's walls are the walls of Troy that Poseidon and Apollo built for Laomedon (7.452–453; see [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]]).
