Lucian's *Dialogues of the Dead* are short comic scenes in the underworld, where the Cynic philosopher Menippus mocks the dead who had been rich, powerful or beautiful. In this one he asks Hermes to show him the famous beauties of old. Hermes points to where they lie, but Menippus sees only bones and fleshless skulls, all alike, and asks to be shown Helen, since he could never tell which she was. "This skull is Helen," says Hermes.

Menippus: "Was it for this, then, that the thousand ships were manned from all Greece, and so many Greeks and barbarians fell, and so many cities were laid waste?" Hermes: "But you did not see the woman alive, Menippus. You too would have said it was no cause for blame 'to suffer sorrows for so long for such a woman'." A flower that has dried and lost its color looks shapeless, he adds, but in bloom it is most beautiful. Menippus replies that this is just what surprises him: that the Achaeans did not see they were toiling for a thing so short-lived and so quick to fade.

Hermes quotes 3.157, and his "no cause for blame" (ἀνεμέσητον) echoes the elders' οὐ νέμεσις. The dialogue sets the two judgments side by side. The old men on the wall saw Helen alive, and found the war worth her. The Cynic sees her dead, and finds nothing worth a war.

Menippus' question became famous in English through Marlowe's Faustus: see [[marlowe-helen]].
