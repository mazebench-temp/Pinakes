summary: The Latin love poets turn Calypso into a lover left on the shore. Ovid's Calypso keeps asking Ulysses to tell again the fall of Troy, and when a wave washes away the Troy he has drawn in the sand, she warns him not to trust the sea; Propertius' Calypso sits for many days with her hair unkempt, weeping to the empty sea after he has gone. In the Odyssey it is Odysseus who weeps on the shore, and the poem never shows Calypso after he sails.

**Homer.** When Hermes comes to the island, Odysseus is not in the cave: “he sat weeping on the shore, as he had before” (*Odyssey* 5.82). Calypso finds him there when the god has gone, his eyes never dry of tears, mourning for his homecoming, “since the nymph no longer pleased him”; by night he sleeps beside her under compulsion, and by day he sits on the rocks and the beaches looking out over the barren sea (*Odyssey* 5.151–158; see [[calypso]] and [[odysseus]]). She tells him that he may go, and on the last evening, asking whether he still wishes to leave, she warns him of the sorrows he must fill up before he reaches home (*Odyssey* 5.160–170, 5.203–210). The poem leaves her when she has bathed and clothed him, stocked the raft and sent a fair wind (*Odyssey* 5.263–268).

**Ovid.** In the second book of the *Art of Love* Ovid teaches that beauty does not last and that a lover must cultivate his mind, and gives Ulysses as his example: “Ulysses was not handsome, but he was eloquent, and yet he tormented goddesses of the sea with love. Ah, how often Calypso grieved that he was hurrying away, and said that the waters were not fit for rowing!” (*non formosus erat, sed erat facundus Ulixes, / et tamen aequoreas torsit amore deas*, *Art of Love* 2.123–126; plain renderings throughout). Again and again she asked him for the fall of Troy, and he would tell the same story in another way. Once, as they stood on the shore, she asked for the bloody fate of the Thracian king Rhesus, and with a light rod he drew it on the firm sand: “This is Troy”, and he made walls; “let this be the Simois; think this is my camp. There was a plain”, and he made the plain, “which we sprinkled with the blood of Dolon, while he watched and longed for the Thessalian horses. There were the tents of Thracian Rhesus; this way I rode back by night on the captured horses.” He was drawing more when a sudden wave carried off Pergamum and the camp of Rhesus with its leader, and the goddess said: “Do you see how great the names are that the waves have destroyed, the waves you trust to be faithful to you as you go?” (*Art of Love* 2.127–142).

Ovid's Ulysses tells the night of *Iliad* 10, in which Dolon, who had asked Hector for the horses of Achilles as his reward for spying, was caught and killed, and Diomedes killed Rhesus in his sleep while Odysseus drove off his horses (10.314–331, 10.454–514; see [[dolon]], [[rhesus]] and [[ovid-dolon-and-rhesus]]). The Odyssey tells of no such tales on Calypso's island. There it is Odysseus who calls the sea “dread and grievous” when she bids him cross it, and she who warns him of the sorrows he must fill up before he reaches home, sorrows that, in the storm, he remembers she had placed on the sea (*Odyssey* 5.174–175, 5.206–207, 5.300–302).

**Propertius.** Propertius rebukes Cynthia, who adorns herself while he is in danger, with the grief of the women of legend: “Not so did Calypso, stirred by the departure of the Ithacan, weep long ago to the deserted waters: for many days she sat sad, with her hair unkempt, saying much to the unjust sea, and though she would never see him again, still she grieved, remembering their long happiness” (*at non sic Ithaci digressu mota Calypso / desertis olim fleverat aequoribus*, *Elegies* 1.15.9–14). Hypsipyle, left by Jason, and Evadne, who died on her husband's pyre, follow (*Elegies* 1.15.17–22). Propertius gives Calypso the place, the posture and the tears that Homer gives Odysseus, sitting by the sea day after day, and gives them to her after he has gone, where the Odyssey says nothing.
