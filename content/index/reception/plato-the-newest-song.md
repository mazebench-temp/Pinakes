**Plato.** In the fourth book of the *Republic* Socrates turns from the property of the guardians to their education. A city that starts well grows like a circle: sound rearing and education produce good natures, and good natures that take hold of such an education grow better than those before them (*Republic* 4.424a–b). So, to put it briefly, those in charge of the city must hold to this above all and guard it against corruption, that there be no innovation in gymnastics and music against the established order. They must be on their guard whenever someone says that men pay more heed to the song “which comes newest about the singers”, lest anyone suppose that the poet means not new songs but a new manner of song, and praise that. Such a thing must neither be praised nor so understood. A new form of music is to be feared as a danger to the whole, “for nowhere are the modes of music moved without the greatest laws of the city, as Damon says and I am persuaded” (*Republic* 4.424b–c). Adeimantus asks to be counted among the persuaded, and Socrates concludes that the guardians must build their guardhouse there, in music, since lawlessness slips into it unnoticed, as a part of play and as doing no harm (4.424c–d).

**Plato's text.** Socrates gives the lines in reported speech, with two words that are not in our text:

> ὅταν τις λέγῃ ὡς τὴν ἀοιδὴν μᾶλλον ἐπιφρονέουσʼ ἄνθρωποι,
> ἥτις ἀειδόντεσσι νεωτάτη ἀμφιπέληται

Our text says that men ἐπικλείουσι the song, “praise” it (*Odyssey* 1.351); Plato's men ἐπιφρονέουσι, “pay heed to” it. Our song comes newest ἀκουόντεσσι, “to those who listen” (*Odyssey* 1.352); Plato's comes newest ἀειδόντεσσι, “to the singers”. Merry reports both readings, and takes Plato's verb to point to ἐπικλύουσʼ, “listen to”, as a variant of ἐπικλείουσʼ (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.351; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 1.351–352).

**Proclus.** Eight centuries later Proclus quotes the lines in Homer's own words, ἐπικλείουσʼ and ἀκουόντεσσι, and turns them the other way. Explaining why Zeno defended Parmenides' doctrine by a different route, he says that the younger may support their elders by another method: as Plato himself says in the *Laws*, poets must always keep in their poems the measures of virtue that set souls to work, but vary their harmonies and rhythms, for “men praise the more that song which comes newest to those who listen, says poetry”; so these men too must present their arguments in another arrangement and order, to avoid the quarrelsome and still not depart from the truth of the doctrines (*Commentary on the Parmenides*, col. 701 Cousin; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). The newness that Socrates feared in the manner of the song is the newness that Proclus allows, so long as the substance stays the same.
