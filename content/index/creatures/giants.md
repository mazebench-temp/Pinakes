## The name

Γίγαντες, “Giants”. Merry reports a derivation from γῆ, “earth”, and the root γα- of γεγαώς, “born”, which would make the name the same as γηγενής, “earth-born”; Curtius took γίγας for a reduplication of the root γα- alone (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 7.59).

## In Homer

Homer names the Giants three times, all in the Odyssey; the Iliad never mentions them. Athena names them in the descent of the Phaeacian kings, whose ancestress Periboea was the youngest daughter of their king:

{{quote:af1d5655-cc0e-59b0-95a2-d04e6647fa80}}

Periboea bore Nausithous to Poseidon, and Nausithous was the father of Alcinous (*Odyssey* 7.56–63; see [[eurymedon-king-of-the-giants]], [[periboea-mother-of-nausithous]], [[nausithous]], [[poseidon]] and [[alcinous]]). Alcinous names them with the Cyclopes as peoples to whom the gods come openly, as they come to the Phaeacians, “since we are near to them, / as are the Cyclopes and the savage tribes of the Giants” (*Odyssey* 7.205–206; see [[cyclopes]], [[phaeacians]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). The Laestrygonians who pelt Odysseus' ships with boulders from the cliffs are countless, “not like men but like the Giants” (*Odyssey* 10.119–123, a plain rendering). They are ὑπέρθυμοι, “high-hearted”, an epithet the poems give to heroes and to the Trojans (see [[high-hearted]]), and ἄγρια φῦλα, “savage tribes”, with the word for the “savage Cyclops” (*Odyssey* 2.19) and for men “arrogant and savage and unjust” (*Odyssey* 6.120); and the people Eurymedon destroyed was a λαὸς ἀτάσθαλος, “his reckless people” (*Odyssey* 7.60). Homer does not say where they lived, what they looked like, or how their people came to be destroyed.

## Outside Homer

**Pausanias.** As Merry quotes him (on *Odyssey* 7.59), Pausanias observed that Homer made no mention of the Giants in the Iliad, but in the Odyssey wrote that the Laestrygonians attacked the ships of Odysseus, likened “to Giants and not to men”, and made the king of the Phaeacians say that the Phaeacians are near to the gods, like the Cyclopes and the race of the Giants. In these verses, Pausanias argued, Homer shows that the Giants were mortal and not a divine race, and more clearly still in the verses on Eurymedon, who destroyed his people and perished himself (Pausanias 8.29.2; see [[pausanias]]). Merry adds that Homer knows nothing of the battle of the Giants and the gods of later legend.

**Hesiod.** In the *Theogony*, when Cronus cut off his father's genitals with the sickle, Earth received all the drops of blood that fell, and as the years went round she bore the mighty Erinyes and “the great Giants, gleaming in armor, holding long spears in their hands” (*Theogony* 178–186, a plain rendering; see [[hesiod]] and [[cronus]]); the Muses on Olympus sing of “the race of men and of the mighty Giants” (*Theogony* 50). Merry sets Homer's Giants against Hesiod's, which he describes as monsters with serpent feet, sprung from Uranus and Gaea, who thought to storm Olympus but were hurled back by the thunderbolts of Zeus, and for these he cites Apollodorus (*Library* 1.6.1; Merry on *Odyssey* 7.59; see [[apollodorus]]); the *Theogony* itself tells only of their birth and their arms. On *Odyssey* 7.205 he notes that Hesiod makes both the Giants and the Cyclopes children of Earth (*Theogony* 139–146, 183–186), and that Acusilaus and Alcaeus, as he reports them, gave the Phaeacians the same descent.

**The war with the gods.** In Euripides' *Ion* the women of the chorus, looking at the sculptures of Apollo's temple at Delphi, see the rout of the Giants on the stone walls: Pallas brandishing her Gorgon shield over Enceladus, the thunderbolt blazing in the hands of Zeus and burning Mimas to ashes, and Dionysus slaying another child of Earth with his ivy wands (*Ion* 205–218). It is the battle of the earth-born “which the Giants set against the gods at Phlegra”, where, Creusa says, Earth bore the Gorgon as an ally for her sons, and Pallas killed her (*Ion* 987–991; see [[euripides]] and [[gorgon]]). Plato would not have the young guardians of his city told tales of battles of the Giants, or have such battles embroidered for them (*Republic* 378c), and in the *Sophist* the quarrel of the philosophers over being is “a kind of battle of the Giants” (*Sophist* 246a; see [[plato]]).
