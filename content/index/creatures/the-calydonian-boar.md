## In Homer

{{quote:369e4e11-90f1-5979-8d4e-cbb57e3c8a08}}

Artemis “roused” the boar (ὦρσεν, 9.539), as she had “roused an evil” against the Aetolians (ὦρσε, 9.533); it is the instrument of her anger at the forgotten offering, and Homer gives it no parents and no name (see [[artemis]] and [[oeneus]]). It is described in a single verse, χλούνην σῦν ἄγριον ἀργιόδοντα, “a wild boar, a lurker in the grass, with white tusks” (9.539). χλούνης occurs only here, and its sense is uncertain; it was explained in antiquity as “lying in the grass” (see [[lurker-in-the-grass]]). ἀργιόδους, “white-tusked”, is said of boars and swine and once of hounds (11.292; see [[white-tusked]]), and σῦς ἄγριος is the wild boar of the similes (see [[boar]]).

It did “much harm, as was its way”, to the orchard of Oeneus, throwing tall trees to the ground “with their very roots and with their fruit blossoms” (9.540–542), and it “set many men on the grievous pyre” (9.546; see [[pyres]]). Meleager killed it, “gathering huntsmen from many cities / and dogs” (9.543–545; see [[meleager]] and [[dogs]]); the many cities are Homer's only hint of the gathering of heroes that later poets made of the hunt. Its death did not end the goddess' anger. Over the trophies of the hunt, “the boar's head and its shaggy hide”, she raised “a great din and shouting” between the Curetes and the Aetolians (9.547–549), and the war that followed is the setting of Meleager's anger (see [[curetes]], [[aetolians]] and [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). In Homer the boar is the cause of a war; it is not yet the test of heroes it became.

## Outside Homer

In Bacchylides the boar, “wide in strength, shameless in battle”, ravages the vines and the flocks and kills any man who meets it, and the best of the Greeks fight it for six days (Bacchylides 5.104–120). [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] has Oeneus call together the best men of Greece and promise the hide to the one who kills it. Atalanta hit it first, Amphiaraus second, and Meleager killed it and gave her the hide (*Library* 1.8.2); Euripides' lost *Meleager* had a catalogue of the hunters, Atalanta among them with her hounds and bow (fr. 530 Nauck), and Ovid gives the beast eyes that flash blood and fire and tusks to match an elephant's (*Metamorphoses* 8.282–289; see [[ovid]]).

At Tegea Pausanias was told that Atalanta had won the head and hide because she hit the boar first. The front pediment of the temple of Athena Alea, whose architect, he heard, was Scopas, showed the hunt, with the boar in the middle. The hide hung in the temple, rotted with age and bare of bristles, but Augustus had taken the tusks to Rome, where one had broken and the other, about half a fathom long, was kept in the imperial gardens (Pausanias 8.45.2, 8.45.5–7, 8.46.1, 8.46.5, 8.47.2). Some explained Artemis' title Laphria, under which the Calydonians worshipped her, from her anger against Oeneus, which grew lighter (ἐλαφρότερον) with time (Pausanias 7.18.9–10; see [[calydon]]). The hunt is painted on the François Vase, an Athenian krater of about 570 BC in Florence, with the hunters' names written beside them.
