## Etymology

Τυφωεύς (Typhōeus); later poets also say Τυφάων, Τυφῶν and Τυφώς. The name is usually connected with τύφω ("smoke, smolder"), which suits a creature of fire, and with τυφώς, a whirlwind.

## In Homer

{{quote:07c77e5c-9453-5206-9ae0-9537e78b2b79}}

The simile compares the tramp of the whole army to the groaning of the earth when Zeus, in anger, "lashes the earth around Typhoeus" (see [[the-earth-groans-as-for-typhoeus]]). The verb is in the present: the punishment goes on. The rumbling of the ground, whether from thunder or from earthquakes, is imagined as Zeus still striking at the monster beneath it. The narrator gives the place as hearsay, "where they say (φασί) Typhoeus has his bed" (see [[arimi]]). This is the only mention of Typhoeus in Homer. See [[zeus-and-typhoeus]].

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod, Typhoeus is the youngest child of Gaia and Tartarus. From his shoulders grew a hundred snake heads with flickering dark tongues and eyes that flashed fire, and his voices were of every kind: now the speech of gods, now a bull's bellow, a lion's roar, the yelping of puppies, a hiss (*Theogony* 820–835). He would have ruled gods and men if Zeus had not seen him in time. Zeus blasted him with thunderbolts and hurled him into Tartarus, and from him come the destructive winds (836–880). In the Homeric *Hymn to Apollo* he is Typhaon, whom Hera bore without a father, in anger at the birth of Athena, and gave to the serpent of Delphi to rear (305–355).

Later poets set his prison under volcanoes. Pindar has him pinned under Etna and the land from Cumae to Sicily (*Pythian* 1.15–28), and Aeschylus makes the eruptions of Etna the rage of Typhon (*Prometheus Bound* 351–372). Apollodorus tells the fullest story, in which Typhon cuts the sinews from Zeus' hands and feet before Zeus defeats him and throws Etna on top of him (1.6.3). The Greeks identified him with the Egyptian god Seth (Herodotus 2.144, 2.156).
