## The name

The origin of Σειρῆνες is unknown. W. W. Merry reports three explanations: from σειρά, “cord”, the “enchainers”, which he compares with the Furies' “binding hymn”, ὕμνος … δέσμιος φρενῶν (Aeschylus, *Eumenides* 331–332), and with Ovid's Sirens, who with their singing voice held back ships however fast they sailed (*Ars Amatoria* 3.311–312, as Merry quotes it); from Σείρ, “the sun”, and Σείριος, the bright star; or from a root meaning “to sound sweetly” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 12.39; see [[aeschylus]] and [[ovid]]). Homer gives no explanation. His σειρή is a rope: the golden rope of Zeus (*Iliad* 8.19, 8.25; see [[the-golden-rope]]) and the plaited rope by which Melanthius is hauled up a pillar (*Odyssey* 22.175, 22.192).

## In Homer

Homer names the Sirens only in Odysseus' tale to the Phaeacians and once more when he retells it to Penelope (*Odyssey* 12.39–200, 23.326). They are known by what they do. They sit in a meadow, “the flowering meadow” (*Odyssey* 12.45, 12.159; see [[flowering]]), on an island of their own (*Odyssey* 12.167), with a heap of bones and dwindling skins about them, and whoever comes near and hears their voice is bewitched and never comes home (*Odyssey* 12.39–46; see [[thelgo]] and [[nostos]]). They are two: the dual is used of them three times, “the two Sirens” (Σειρήνοιιν, *Odyssey* 12.52, 12.167) and “the voice of us two” (νωιτέρην ὄπα, *Odyssey* 12.185), and Merry takes Homer to make them two, living on one island (on *Odyssey* 12.39, 12.185; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 12.39, 12.52, 12.167, 12.185). Homer says nothing of their shape, their parents or their names, nor how the men whose bones lie there died. What they offer is knowledge, of “all the things that in wide Troy / the Argives and Trojans toiled through” and of “all that comes to pass upon the bountiful earth” (*Odyssey* 12.189–191), and Merry counts this superhuman knowledge of the past and the future among their peculiar characteristics (on *Odyssey* 12.39). For the passage of their island, see [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]].

## Descriptions

- θεσπέσιαι, “wondrous” (Σειρήνων … θεσπεσιάων, *Odyssey* 12.158), a word often said of sound (see [[wondrous]]): it refers, Merry says, to the wonderful charm of their song, not to the revelations they profess to make (on *Odyssey* 12.158).
- Their song is λιγυρή, “clear” (λιγυρῇ … ἀοιδῇ, *Odyssey* 12.44; λιγυρὴν … ἀοιδήν, 12.183), the word of the wind's blast and of a whip in the Iliad (see [[shrill]]). Their voice is μελίγηρυς, “honeyed” (*Odyssey* 12.187, the only instance in Homer; see [[honeyed-voice]]), from μέλι, “honey”, and γῆρυς, “voice”, and κάλλιμος, “beautiful” (ὄπα κάλλιμον, *Odyssey* 12.192).
- ἁδιναί (Σειρήνων ἁδινάων, *Odyssey* 23.326), the word of swarming flies and bees and of loud and ceaseless lament (see [[swarming]]). D. B. Monro takes it of their voices, “thick-coming”, “with ever resounding song”, and finds the epithet unjustified of the Sirens themselves: the author of the passage, he thinks, had in mind the flies of *Iliad* 2.469 (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 23.326).

## Outside Homer

**Number, names and parents.** A fragment of the *Catalogue of Women* names their island Anthemoessa, “Flowery”, where the son of Cronus gave them a home; the writer who quotes it gives their names as Thelxiope or Thelxinoe, Molpe and Aglaophonus, and says that Hesiod made them bewitch even the winds (*Catalogue of Women* fr. 47 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]). Merry reports that later legend made them three or even four, living on three deserted rocky islets, and gave them names: Eustathius calls Homer's two Aglaopheme and Thelxiepeia, and when they were moved with other Homeric marvels to the coast of Italy they became Parthenope, the Siren of Naples, Ligeia and Leucosia, with Molpe and Thelxinoe added (Merry on *Odyssey* 12.39). Euripides' Helen calls on them as “winged maidens, virgin daughters of Earth” (*Helen* 167–169; see [[euripides]]), and Ovid, Merry notes, makes them daughters of the river Achelous by one of the Muses, with the feathers and feet of birds and the faces of maidens (*Metamorphoses* 5.552–553, as Merry quotes it).

**What they stood for.** The Sirens became a figure for any voice that holds the hearer: Euripides' Hermione was undone by “these words of Sirens” (*Andromache* 930–938), Plato set a Siren singing on each circle of the spindle of Necessity (*Republic* 617b), and Cicero read their song as a promise of knowledge (*De Finibus* 5.49). Merry himself thinks that they personify the hidden dangers of the calm sea, and that their song may be the sound of water splashing in caves and over broken rocks (on *Odyssey* 12.39). For these and the other later writers, see [[the-sirens-in-later-writers]].
