## The name

Σκύλλη is “properly the ‘render’ or ‘tearer’”, W. W. Merry says (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 12.85), as from σκύλλω, “tear, mangle”, a verb not found in Homer, which Aeschylus uses of the Persian dead, torn by the voiceless children of the sea (*Persians* 576–578; see [[aeschylus]]). Circe's description seems to play on σκύλαξ, “puppy”: “Her voice indeed is only as loud as a newborn puppy's” (σκύλακος νεογιλῆς, *Odyssey* 12.86). Merry thinks the connection with the puppy a later idea, which may account for the introduction of the verses on her voice (*Odyssey* 12.86–88) and for the dogs and barking monsters with which the Latin poets surround her (on *Odyssey* 12.85).

## Family

{{quote:b3fa7a4f-103e-502b-8c17-5b2d4292a4d0}}

Her mother is [[crataeis|Crataeis]], “the Mighty One”, who “bore her to be a bane to mortals” (*Odyssey* 12.125). Homer names no father. The *Great Eoiae* ascribed to Hesiod made her the daughter of Phorbas and Hecate (fr. 13 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]), and Merry reports a later legend that made her the daughter of Hecate by Phorcys or by Triton (on *Odyssey* 12.124).

## Descriptions

- δεινὸν λελακυῖα, “yelping terribly” (*Odyssey* 12.85): λελακυῖα is the perfect participle of λάσκω, “shriek, yelp”, with the force of a present. Her voice is no louder than a newborn puppy's, though she herself is a monster (*Odyssey* 12.86–87), a contrast between her vast size and her thin, shrill cry that Merry calls intentionally grotesque (on *Odyssey* 12.86).
- πέλωρ κακόν, “an evil monster” (*Odyssey* 12.87; see [[huge]]).
- ἀθάνατον κακόν, “an immortal evil” (*Odyssey* 12.118), “dread and grievous and savage, not to be fought” (δεινόν τʼ ἀργαλέον τε καὶ ἄγριον οὐδὲ μαχητόν, *Odyssey* 12.119): κακόν, Merry says, may almost be personified into “fiend” (on *Odyssey* 12.118).
- πῆμα βροτοῖσιν, “a bane to mortals” (*Odyssey* 12.125), what her mother bore her to be; Odysseus says she “brought a bane on my companions” (πῆμʼ ἑτάροισιν, *Odyssey* 12.231).
- πετραίη, “of the rock” (Σκύλλην πετραίην, *Odyssey* 12.231), from πέτρη, “rock”: she lives in the rock, sunk to the waist in her cave, with only her heads outside (*Odyssey* 12.93–94).

## Mythology

Homer tells of Scylla only in Odysseus' tale to the Phaeacians. Circe describes the two crags of the strait: on the higher, in a cave turned toward Erebus too high for an arrow to reach, lives Scylla, with twelve feet and six necks, a head on each with three rows of teeth; she fishes for dolphins and dogfish and sea monsters, and no ship has passed her unharmed, for with each head she takes a man. Under the lower crag, a bowshot away, is Charybdis. It is better to lose six men than all, Circe tells Odysseus, and Scylla cannot be fought: he must row past and call on her mother (*Odyssey* 12.73–126; see [[charybdis]] and [[circe]]). He says nothing of her to his companions and arms himself against Circe's word, but while they all look toward Charybdis Scylla seizes six of the strongest from the ship and devours them at her doors as they call on him by name (*Odyssey* 12.222–259; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). After the wreck he is carried back past her crag, and Zeus does not let her see him (*Odyssey* 12.430, 12.445–446). In the summary of his wanderings that he gives Penelope she is “Scylla, whom men have never yet escaped unharmed” (*Odyssey* 23.328, a plain rendering).

## Outside Homer

**The strait.** The ancients placed Scylla and Charybdis in the strait of Messina, Scylla on the Italian side and Charybdis on the Sicilian, and later identified her rock with the Scyllaeum, opposite the Sicilian headland of Pelorum (Merry on *Odyssey* 12.85), a high rock forming a peninsula, as Strabo describes it (*Geography* 6.1.5; see [[strabo]]). Polybius, as Strabo reports him, read Circe's picture of Scylla fishing for dolphins, dogfish and greater sea beasts as the swordfish hunt of the Scyllaeum, and took it for a sign that Homer set the wandering around Sicily (*Geography* 1.2.15–16; see [[polybius]]).

**Later writers.** Plato names Scylla, with the Chimaera and Cerberus, among the natures of old in which many forms grew together into one (*Republic* 588c; see [[plato]] and [[chimaera]]). Timotheus' dithyramb *Scylla* gave Odysseus a lament for the six companions, which Aristotle found unseemly, and pipers who played it dragged the leader of the chorus about, miming the monster's grip (*Poetics* 1454a30–31, 1461b31–32; see [[scylla-of-timotheus]] and [[timotheus]]). For the tragedians' Scyllas, the Latin poets' maiden girt with dogs, and the proverbial pair of dangers, see [[scylla-and-charybdis-in-later-writers]].
