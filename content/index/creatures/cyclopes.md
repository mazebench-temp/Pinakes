## In Homer

Odysseus describes them to the Phaeacians. They are “overbearing and lawless” (ὑπερφιάλων ἀθεμίστων), and trusting in the gods they neither plant nor plow, but wheat and barley and vines grow for them unsown, and Zeus' rain swells them; they have no assemblies for counsel and no ordinances, but live in hollow caves on the peaks of high mountains, each giving law to his own children and wives, and they care nothing for one another (*Odyssey* 9.106–115; see [[themis]]). They have no ships and no shipwrights (*Odyssey* 9.125–130). Polyphemus tells Odysseus that the Cyclopes care nothing for Zeus who bears the aegis, nor for the blessed gods, “since we are far stronger” (*Odyssey* 9.275–276). They were once neighbors of the Phaeacians, in Hypereia, and harmed them, being stronger in might (*Odyssey* 6.4–6), and Alcinous names them with “the wild tribes of the Giants” as peoples near to the gods (*Odyssey* 7.205–206). The renderings of books other than the first are plain renderings, not the translation.

The poem speaks of Polyphemus' eye only in the singular (*Odyssey* 1.69; 9.333, 9.383, 9.503, 9.516; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). It is Hesiod who says that the Cyclopes had a single round eye in the middle of the forehead, and that they were named for it.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod's Cyclopes are three sons of Earth and Sky, Brontes, Steropes and Arges, “Thunder”, “Lightning” and “Bright”, who gave Zeus his thunder and made his thunderbolt; in all else they were like the gods, but a single eye lay in the middle of the forehead, and they were called Cyclopes because it was round (*Theogony* 139–146). Euripides' satyr play *Cyclops* sets the one-eyed sons of the sea god under Etna in Sicily, man-slayers living in lonely caves (*Cyclops* 20–22). Strabo, defending Homer's geography, holds that the poet took his beginnings from history: the inhospitable Cyclopes and Laestrygonians were said to have lived around Etna and Leontini (*Geography* 1.2.9), and Homer perhaps brought the one-eyed Cyclopes over from Scythian history, since the Arimaspians, of whom Aristeas of Proconnesus told in his *Arimaspeia*, are said to be such men (*Geography* 1.2.10; see [[strabo]]). Timotheus and Philoxenus wrote dithyrambs on the Cyclops, and Aristotle says that the two portrayed the Cyclopes differently (*Poetics* 1448a15; see [[cyclops-dithyrambs]]); he also quotes the blinded Polyphemus twice (see [[small-and-of-no-account]] and [[say-that-odysseus-blinded-you]]). Polyphemus the Cyclops is not the Lapith Polyphemus of the Iliad (see [[polyphemus]]).
