## The name

Hesiod makes the name “round-eyed”: “and they were called Cyclopes by name, because a single round eye lay in their forehead” (*Theogony* 144–145; see [[hesiod]]). W. W. Merry rejects Göttling's view that the name alludes to the round walls of the so-called “Cyclopean” buildings, and reports Döderlein's derivation from κλέπτω, “steal”, and κλώψ, “thief”, which would make them a race of robbers (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 9.106; see the Odyssey translation's note on 9.106).

## In Homer

In the Odyssey they are herdsmen, “overbearing and lawless”, who live apart in caves on the peaks of the mountains, each giving the law to his own household, without assemblies, ordinances or ships; trusting in the gods, they neither plant nor plow, yet they care nothing for the gods. Odysseus describes them to the Phaeacians when he tells how he blinded the mightiest of them (*Odyssey* 9.105–130, 9.275–276, 9.399–412; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]] and [[themis]]). They were once neighbors of the Phaeacians, in Hypereia, and harmed them, being stronger in might (*Odyssey* 6.4–6; see [[hypereia-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]), and Alcinous names them with “the savage tribes of the Giants” as peoples near to the gods (*Odyssey* 7.205–206; see [[giants]]).

The poem speaks of Polyphemus' eye only in the singular (*Odyssey* 1.69; 9.333, 9.383, 9.503, 9.516; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). It is Hesiod who says that the Cyclopes had a single round eye in the middle of the forehead, and that they were named for it.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod's Cyclopes are three sons of Earth and Sky, Brontes, Steropes and Arges, “Thunder”, “Lightning” and “Bright”, who gave Zeus his thunder and made his thunderbolt; in all else they were like the gods, but a single eye lay in the middle of the forehead, and they were called Cyclopes because it was round (*Theogony* 139–146). Merry would keep Homer's Cyclopes carefully apart from Hesiod's, the smiths who forge Zeus' thunderbolts; some commentators, he notes, took Homer's for the wild and turbulent forces of the sea, since Polyphemus is a son of Poseidon by the daughter of Phorcys, and the ancients generally placed them in Sicily, near Etna, but “throughout these books we are in a wonder-land, which we shall look in vain for on the map” (Merry on *Odyssey* 9.106). Euripides' satyr play *Cyclops* sets the one-eyed sons of the sea god under Etna in Sicily, man-slayers living in lonely caves (*Cyclops* 20–22). Strabo, defending Homer's geography, holds that the poet took his beginnings from history: the inhospitable Cyclopes and Laestrygonians were said to have lived around Etna and Leontini (*Geography* 1.2.9), and Homer perhaps brought the one-eyed Cyclopes over from Scythian history, since the Arimaspians, of whom Aristeas of Proconnesus told in his *Arimaspeia*, are said to be such men (*Geography* 1.2.10; see [[strabo]]).

Later Greeks gave the name to the builders of the massive old walls of the Argolid. Proetus, Strabo reports, walled Tiryns with the help of Cyclopes, seven of them, called “Bellyhands” because they lived by their craft, whom he had sent for from Lycia, and perhaps the caves near Nauplia and the works in them are named after them (*Geography* 8.6.11). Merry thinks that this masonry was called Cyclopean probably in allusion to the Cyclops' fold, built “with stones set deep in the earth / and with tall pines and high-leafed oaks” (Merry on *Odyssey* 9.184; *Odyssey* 9.185–186).

Timotheus and Philoxenus wrote dithyrambs on the Cyclops, and Aristotle says that the two portrayed the Cyclopes differently (*Poetics* 1448a15; see [[cyclops-dithyrambs]]); he also quotes the blinded Polyphemus twice (see [[small-and-of-no-account]] and [[say-that-odysseus-blinded-you]]). Polyphemus the Cyclops is not the Lapith Polyphemus of the Iliad (see [[polyphemus]]).
