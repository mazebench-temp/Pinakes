## The name

Πολύφημος = πολύς (“much”) + φήμη (“speech, report”): “much spoken of”, the sense in which the Lapith's name is read (see [[polyphemus]]). As a common adjective πολύφημος is “of many voices” or “of many songs”: Zeus' two eagles fly down to “the middle of the assembly of many voices” (μέσσην ἀγορὴν πολύφημον, *Odyssey* 2.150), and Odysseus calls Phemius πολύφημος ἀοιδός, the singer “of many songs” (*Odyssey* 22.376; see [[phemius]]). The renderings of books other than the first are plain renderings, not the translation.

## Family

{{quote:ad8a8a30-fde5-5e81-be18-d99f13a91350}}

Son of [[Poseidon]] and the nymph [[Thoosa]], daughter of the sea god Phorcys (see [[phorcys-the-sea-god]]). The Cyclops himself claims the god as his father when he tries to lure Odysseus back with the promise of a guest-gift and an escort from the famous earth-shaker: “for I am his son, and he claims to be my father” (*Odyssey* 9.517–519). He prays to him in the same words, “if truly I am yours, and you claim to be my father” (*Odyssey* 9.529), and the other Cyclopes, told that “Nobody” is killing him, bid him pray “to your father, lord Poseidon” (*Odyssey* 9.412).

## Descriptions

- ἀντίθεος, “godlike” (*Odyssey* 1.70), the standing epithet of heroes, here given to the giant (see [[antitheos]]). The half-verse ἀντίθεον Πολύφημον also names the Lapith in Nestor's list of the mightiest men (*Iliad* 1.264).
- κρατερός, “mighty” (κρατερὸς Πολύφημος, *Odyssey* 9.407, 9.446), and the one “whose power is the greatest / among all the Cyclopes” (*Odyssey* 1.70–71; see [[kratos]]).
- ἀνὴρ … πελώριος, a monstrous man who pastured his flocks alone, “not like a man who eats bread, but like a wooded peak of high mountains” (*Odyssey* 9.187–192), and πέλωρ, “the monster” (*Odyssey* 9.428). He lives apart and “knows lawless ways”, ἀθεμίστια ᾔδη (*Odyssey* 9.189; again 9.428; see [[themis]]).

## Mythology

Odysseus tells the story to the Phaeacians (*Odyssey* 9.105–566). The Cyclopes are lawless, with no assemblies for counsel and no ordinances; each rules his own children and wives in hollow caves on the mountain peaks (*Odyssey* 9.106–115; see [[cyclopes]]). Odysseus goes up to Polyphemus' cave with twelve chosen companions and a goatskin of dark wine, the gift of Maron, priest of Apollo at Ismarus (*Odyssey* 9.193–205). The giant shuts the doorway with a stone that twenty-two wagons could not shift (*Odyssey* 9.240–243) and eats the companions two at a time, at evening and at the next two meals (*Odyssey* 9.289–293, 9.311, 9.344). Odysseus makes him drunk with the wine and tells him that his name is Nobody, Οὖτις (*Odyssey* 9.360–367); then, with four companions chosen by lot, he drives a stake of olive wood, its point hardened in the fire, into the sleeping Cyclops' eye (*Odyssey* 9.318–335, 9.375–397). When the other Cyclopes come to his shouting, he answers, “Friends, Nobody is killing me by guile, and not by force”, and they go away, telling him to pray to his father (*Odyssey* 9.399–412). The Greeks escape under the rams (*Odyssey* 9.424–463). From the ship Odysseus taunts him twice, the second time with his true name (*Odyssey* 9.475–479, 9.502–505; see [[say-that-odysseus-blinded-you]]), and the Cyclops remembers the old prophecy of the seer Telemus, that he would lose his sight at the hands of Odysseus, and complains that the man was small and of no account (*Odyssey* 9.507–516; see [[small-and-of-no-account]]). He hurls two rocks after the ship (*Odyssey* 9.480–486, 9.537–542) and prays to Poseidon that Odysseus may never come home or, if he must, come late and in misery, having lost all his companions, on another's ship, and find troubles in his house; “and the dark-haired god heard him” (*Odyssey* 9.526–536).

The anger lasts the length of the poem. Teiresias tells Odysseus that the earth-shaker will make his homecoming hard, “angry because you blinded his dear son”, and Athena gives the same reason for not having fought her father's brother on his behalf (*Odyssey* 11.100–103, 13.341–343; see [[poseidon]] and [[athena]]).

## Outside Homer

Euripides' satyr play *Cyclops* moves him to Sicily. The satyrs, shipwrecked while searching for Dionysus, serve as slaves of the Cyclops Polyphemus under Etna, among the one-eyed sons of the sea god (*Cyclops* 18–26); Silenus swears to him “by Poseidon who begot you” (262). Odysseus blinds him with the glowing stake; he cries out that “Nobody” has destroyed him (663, 672–675), and at the end he recognizes the fulfilment of an old oracle: he was to be blinded by Odysseus on his way from Troy, and Odysseus was to pay for it by drifting long on the sea (696–700; see [[euripides]]).

Timotheus and Philoxenus made the Cyclops the subject of dithyrambs, which Aristotle cites as two different portrayals of the Cyclopes (*Poetics* 1448a15; see [[cyclops-dithyrambs]]). In Theocritus' eleventh *Idyll* the young Polyphemus, “the Cyclops who lived among us”, his beard just growing, sings to Galatea on the shore of a love whose only remedy is song; he knows why she runs from him, his one long eyebrow from ear to ear, his single eye and his broad nose (*Idylls* 11.1–18, 11.30–33, 11.80–81). In Ovid, Galatea herself tells the story to Scylla: the Cyclops laughed at the seer Telemus, who foretold that Ulysses would take the one eye in the middle of his forehead, sang his love from a headland over the sea, and, finding her with her lover Acis, crushed him under a mass of rock torn from the mountain, and Acis became a river (*Metamorphoses* 13.738–897; see [[ovid]] and [[galateia]]). In Virgil, Aeneas' men take on board Achaemenides, a companion whom Ulysses' men left behind in the Cyclops' cave, and see the blinded shepherd himself coming down to the sea among his flocks, “a monster horrible, shapeless, huge, whose eye was taken away”, to wash the blood from his eye socket (*Aeneid* 3.588–691, 3.658; see [[virgil]]).
