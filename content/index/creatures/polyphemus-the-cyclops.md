## The name

Πολύφημος = πολύς (“much”) + φήμη (“speech, report”): “much spoken of”, the sense in which the Lapith's name is read (see [[polyphemus]]). As a common adjective πολύφημος is “of many voices” or “of many songs” (see [[of-many-voices]]): Zeus' two eagles come over “the middle of the assembly of many voices” (μέσσην ἀγορὴν πολύφημον, *Odyssey* 2.150), and Odysseus calls Phemius πολύφημος ἀοιδός, the singer “of many songs” (*Odyssey* 22.376; see [[phemius]]). The renderings of the Odyssey's books after the eleventh are plain renderings, not the translation.

## Family

{{quote:ad8a8a30-fde5-5e81-be18-d99f13a91350}}

Son of [[Poseidon]] and the nymph [[Thoosa]], daughter of the sea god Phorcys (see [[phorcys-the-sea-god]]). The Cyclops himself claims the god as his father when he tries to lure Odysseus back with the promise of a guest-gift and an escort from the famous earth-shaker: “for I am his son, and he claims to be my father” (*Odyssey* 9.517–519). He prays to him in the same words, “if truly I am yours, and you claim to be my father” (*Odyssey* 9.529), and the other Cyclopes, told that “Nobody” is killing him, bid him pray “to your father, lord Poseidon” (*Odyssey* 9.412).

## Descriptions

- ἀντίθεος, “godlike” (*Odyssey* 1.70), the standing epithet of heroes, here given to the giant (see [[antitheos]]). The half-verse ἀντίθεον Πολύφημον also names the Lapith in Nestor's list of the mightiest men (*Iliad* 1.264).
- κρατερός, “mighty” (κρατερὸς Πολύφημος, *Odyssey* 9.407, 9.446), and the one “whose power is the greatest / among all the Cyclopes” (*Odyssey* 1.70–71; see [[kratos]]).
- ἀνὴρ … πελώριος, a monstrous man who pastured his flocks alone, “not like / a man who eats bread, but like a wooded peak / of high mountains” (*Odyssey* 9.190–192; see [[like-a-wooded-peak]]), and πέλωρ, “the monster” (*Odyssey* 9.428). He “lived apart, and knew lawless ways”, ἀθεμίστια ᾔδη (*Odyssey* 9.189; again 9.428; see [[themis]]).
- ἄγριος, “savage” (ἄγριος … Κύκλωψ, *Odyssey* 2.19). Before he meets him, Odysseus' proud spirit forebodes “that a man would come upon us, clothed in great courage, / a savage, who knew well neither judgments nor ordinances” (*Odyssey* 9.213–215), and his companions call him “a savage man” when Odysseus taunts him from the ship (*Odyssey* 9.494).
- μένος ἄσχετος, “uncontrollable in his fury” (*Odyssey* 20.19), when Odysseus remembers the day the Cyclops ate his companions. Antinous throws the same words at Telemachus, μένος ἄσχετε, “uncontrollable in your fury” (*Odyssey* 2.85, 2.303; see [[menos]]).

## Mythology

Odysseus tells the story to the Phaeacians (*Odyssey* 9.105–566). He waits in the Cyclops' cave with twelve companions to see him and to learn whether he will give them guest-gifts; the Cyclops shuts them in with a stone that twenty-two wagons could not shift and eats six of them, two at a time. Odysseus makes him drunk with the wine of Maron, tells him that his name is Nobody, and with four companions chosen by lot drives a stake of olive wood, hardened in the fire, into his eye as he sleeps. The other Cyclopes, told that Nobody is killing him, go away; the Greeks escape under the rams, and from the ship Odysseus taunts him and names himself. The Cyclops remembers the prophecy of the seer Telemus, hurls two rocks after the ship, and prays to his father that Odysseus may come home late, without his companions, on someone else's ship, to find bane in his house (see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]], [[the-cave-of-polyphemus]], [[cyclopes]], [[maron]], [[telemus]], [[say-that-odysseus-blinded-you]] and [[small-and-of-no-account]]).

The anger lasts the length of the poem. In the council of the gods Zeus explains that Poseidon, without killing Odysseus, drives him wandering from his native land because he blinded the Cyclops (*Odyssey* 1.68–75; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]), and in Ithaca the old Aegyptius has a son, Antiphus, whom the Cyclops made the last of his suppers (*Odyssey* 2.17–20; see [[antiphus-son-of-aegyptius]]). After the Laestrygonians the companions weep at the memory of the violence of the great-hearted Cyclops, the man-eater (*Odyssey* 10.198–202), and Eurylochus, refusing to go to Circe's house, recalls how the companions came into the Cyclops' fold with bold Odysseus and perished through his recklessness (*Odyssey* 10.431–437). Teiresias tells Odysseus that the earth-shaker will make his homecoming hard, “angry because you blinded his dear son”, and foretells it in the words of the Cyclops' prayer: he will come home late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions, on someone else's ship, and find bane in his house (*Odyssey* 11.100–117); Athena gives the same reason for not having fought her father's brother on his behalf (*Odyssey* 13.341–343; see [[poseidon]] and [[athena]]). Before Scylla Odysseus reminds his men that they escaped from the Cyclops' cave by his excellence, counsel and mind (*Odyssey* 12.208–212); in his own house, among the suitors, he bids his heart endure, as it endured the day the Cyclops ate his strong companions, until cunning brought it out of the cave (*Odyssey* 20.18–21); and he tells Penelope all that the Cyclops did, and how he took vengeance for the companions the Cyclops ate without pity (*Odyssey* 23.312–313). The renderings of these books are plain renderings, not the translation.

## Outside Homer

Euripides' satyr play *Cyclops* moves him to Sicily. The satyrs, driven ashore under Etna while they sail in search of Dionysus, serve as slaves of the Cyclops Polyphemus, one of the one-eyed sons of the sea god who live in lonely caves and kill men (*Cyclops* 11–26); Silenus swears to him “by Poseidon who begot you” (262). Odysseus' wine is the gift of Maron, “the son of the god” (141–143; see [[maron]]); he gives his name as Οὖτις, “Nobody”, and the Cyclops promises to feast on him last of all his companions (549–550). Blinded, the Cyclops cries out that the light of his eye is burned to coal, and that Nobody has destroyed him (663, 672–675), and at the end he recognizes the fulfilment of an old oracle: he was to be blinded by Odysseus on his way from Troy, and Odysseus was to pay for it by drifting long on the sea (696–700; see [[euripides]] and [[euripides-cyclops]]).

Timotheus and Philoxenus made the Cyclops the subject of dithyrambs, which Aristotle cites as two different portrayals of the Cyclopes (*Poetics* 1448a15; see [[cyclops-dithyrambs]]). In Theocritus' eleventh *Idyll* the young Polyphemus, “the Cyclops who lived among us”, his beard just growing, sings to Galatea on the shore of a love whose only remedy is song; he knows why she runs from him, his one long eyebrow from ear to ear, his single eye and his broad nose (*Idylls* 11.1–18, 11.30–33, 11.80–81). In Ovid, Galatea herself tells the story to Scylla: the Cyclops laughed at the seer Telemus, who foretold that Ulysses would take the one eye in the middle of his forehead, sang his love from a headland over the sea, and, finding her with her lover Acis, crushed him under a mass of rock torn from the mountain, and Acis became a river (*Metamorphoses* 13.738–897; see [[ovid]] and [[galateia]]). In Virgil, Aeneas' men take on board Achaemenides, a companion whom Ulysses' men left behind in the Cyclops' cave, and see the blinded shepherd himself coming down to the sea among his flocks, “a monster horrible, shapeless, huge, whose eye was taken away”, to wash the blood from his eye socket (*Aeneid* 3.588–691, 3.658; see [[virgil]]).

**The folk tale.** The story of an ogre blinded by the man he meant to eat is told in many lands, in forms too different from Homer's to be copies of it. W. W. Merry, after J. F. Lauer and Wilhelm Grimm's *Die Sage von Polyphem* (1857), retells a medieval Latin tale of the monk John of Haute-Seille, put into French verse as *Li Romans de Dolopathos*, in which a robber captain blinds a giant with a pretended cure, hangs a day and a night by his hands from a hen-roost, and slips out past him in a ram's skin; a Turkic tale of a one-eyed giant, born of a fairy, blinded with a red-hot knife; the third voyage of Sindbad, whose ogre is blinded with red-hot spits and, with two other giants, stones the escaping rafts; and Serbian, Romanian, Estonian, Finnish and Karelian forms. The escape in a sheep's skin recurs in several; the trick of the name, among these, only in the Estonian tale, where a farm bailiff who pours molten lead into the Devil's eyes has called himself Issi, “Self”, so that the blinded Devil can only cry that Self did it (Grimm adds like tricks from the Vorarlberg and from Hesse). Under all these legends Merry finds a general picture of brute force overmatched by cunning (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix II, on the legend of the blinded Cyclops; see [[nobody]] and [[metis]]).
