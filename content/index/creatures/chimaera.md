## Etymology

χίμαιρα is the ordinary word for a she-goat, and Homer uses it so in describing her: μέσση δὲ χίμαιρα, “and in the middle a she-goat” (6.181). The word is related to χεῖμα, “winter”: a goat that has seen one winter, a yearling. The monster is named for one of her three parts.

## In Homer

{{quote:1b9cc57b-d835-5c1d-9333-f9aa99b25cec}}

ἀμαιμάκετος, “irresistible”, is said of her both times she is named (6.179, 16.329; see [[irresistible]]); in the Odyssey it describes a ship's mast (Od. 14.311). θεῖον γένος οὐδʼ ἀνθρώπων, “of divine race, not of men”, sets her apart from the human enemies of Bellerophon's later tasks. She is a lion in front, a serpent (δράκων) behind and a goat in the middle (see [[lion]], [[serpent]] and [[goats]]), and she breathes “the terrible might of blazing fire” (πυρὸς μένος, 6.182; see [[menos]]). Bellerophon kills her “obeying the portents of the gods” (6.183; see [[teras]]). Homer gives him no winged horse.

She is named once more. Atymnius and Maris, “noble companions of Sarpedon” whom the sons of Nestor kill, were sons of Amisodarus, “who reared the irresistible Chimaera, an evil to many men” (16.317–329). Homer says nothing more of Amisodarus, but his sons fight at Troy beside Bellerophon's grandson (see [[sarpedon]] and [[bellerophon]]).

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod she is born of the brood of Echidna and Typhaon (the Greek of *Theogony* 319 does not make clear whether her mother is Echidna or the Hydra). She breathes “irresistible fire” (ἀμαιμάκετον πῦρ) and has three heads, a lion's, a goat's and a serpent's, and “Pegasus and noble Bellerophon” killed her (*Theogony* 319–325). Two lines there that repeat 6.181–182 are bracketed by editors as borrowed from the Iliad (323–324). [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] gives her the fore-part of a lion, a serpent's tail and a goat's head in the middle, through which she breathed fire, and says, citing Homer, that Amisodarus reared her (*Library* 2.3.1).

Strabo says that the myth was set in the mountains of Cragus in western Lycia, near which a ravine running up from the shore was called Chimaera (14.3.5). Pliny describes a Mount Chimaera near Phaselis, in eastern Lycia, that burns day and night with a flame that never goes out (*Natural History* 2.236). For Plato she is the type of a creature compounded of many natures (*Republic* 9.588c; *Phaedrus* 229d). The bronze Chimaera of Arezzo, an Etruscan work of about 400 BC found in 1553, is in the archaeological museum in Florence. From her name comes “chimera”, a creature of mixed parts or a fancy of the mind.
