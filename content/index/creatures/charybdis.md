## Descriptions

Charybdis is, in W. W. Merry's words, “the personified power of the whirlpool” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 12.104). Homer speaks of her as of a living being: she sucks the water down and sends it up again, vomits and swallows, and when the sea is gone “all within her appeared” (*Odyssey* 12.104–106, 12.237–241). The poem gives her no body and no voice; the roar is the rock's (*Odyssey* 12.241–242).

- δῖα, “brilliant” (δῖα Χάρυβδις, *Odyssey* 12.104, 12.235), the honorific of goddesses, heroes and the sea (see [[brilliant]]). Merry thinks that the later legend which made her a daughter of Poseidon may have grown from the epithet (on *Odyssey* 12.104).
- ὀλοή, “deadly” (τὴν ὀλοὴν … Χάρυβδιν, *Odyssey* 12.113, 12.428; see [[deadly]]), and δεινή, “dread” (δεινήν τε Χάρυβδιν, *Odyssey* 12.260, 12.430; again 23.327).
- Her own verb is ἀναρροιβδέω, “suck down”, from ἀνα- and ῥοιβδέω, “suck in with a rushing noise”, said in Homer only of her (*Odyssey* 12.104, 12.105, 12.236, 12.431; ῥοιβδήσειεν, 12.106). Her other verbs are ἀνίησιν, “sends it up” (*Odyssey* 12.105), ἐξεμέσειε, “vomited it up” (*Odyssey* 12.237; again 12.437), and ἀναβρόξειε, “swallowed up” (*Odyssey* 12.240), the verb of the water that vanishes whenever Tantalus stoops to drink (ἀναβροχέν, *Odyssey* 11.586; see [[tantalus]]).

## Mythology

Homer tells of Charybdis only in Odysseus' tale to the Phaeacians and names her once more in his summary of it for Penelope. Circe describes the lower of the two crags of the strait, a bowshot from Scylla's, with a great wild fig tree in full leaf, under which Charybdis three times a day sends up the black water and three times sucks it down; Odysseus must keep close to Scylla, since it is better to lose six companions than all (*Odyssey* 12.101–110; see [[scylla]] and [[the-fig-tree-of-charybdis]]). In the strait the companions watch her in fear as she seethes up like a cauldron on a great fire and swallows the sea until the sand shows at the bottom, and meanwhile Scylla takes six of them (*Odyssey* 12.234–259; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). After the wreck the South Wind carries Odysseus back to her on his mast and keel; she swallows them, and he hangs from the fig tree above her “like a bat” until she gives them back (*Odyssey* 12.426–444; see [[like-a-bat]] and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). Penelope hears “how he came to the Wandering Rocks and dread Charybdis” (*Odyssey* 23.327, a plain rendering; see [[the-wanderers]]).

## Outside Homer

**The strait.** The ancients placed Charybdis in the strait of Messina, on the Sicilian side, opposite Scylla (Merry on *Odyssey* 12.85). Strabo says that she is shown a little before the city of Messana, in the passage: an extraordinary depth, into which the counter-currents of the strait draw boats, turning them over in a great whirling eddy, and when they have been swallowed and broken up the wreckage is carried to the shore of Tauromenium, which is called Kopria, “the dung heap”, for that reason (*Geography* 6.2.3; see [[strabo]]).

**The tides.** Strabo held that Homer made his myth of Charybdis from the ebb and flow of the sea, adapting what was told of the Sicilian strait, and that Circe's three times a day, when the tide turns only twice in a day and a night, was said not from ignorance but for the sake of tragedy and fear, with which Circe fills her words to turn Odysseus away (*Geography* 1.2.36; see [[like-a-bat]] and [[the-judge-rising-for-supper]] for the rest of his argument). Polybius, as Strabo reports him, had called the “three” an error, either of the copyist or of the information (*Geography* 1.2.16; see [[polybius]]). Merry finds the number less troublesome: we are still in the land of marvel, three is the complete number for a full day's work, and the ebb and flow in narrow straits do not follow the tide outside them (on *Odyssey* 12.105). For the later writers, see [[scylla-and-charybdis-in-later-writers]].
