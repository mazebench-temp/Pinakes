## The custom

In the world of the heroes most goods change hands as gifts, prizes, ransom or spoil (see [[guest-gifts]], [[apoina]] and [[division-of-spoils]]). Buying and selling for a price are at the edges of the story. The words are few: ὦνος, “price” (21.41, 23.746), πέρνημι and its aorist ἐπέρασσα, “sell” across the sea (21.40, 21.78, 22.45, 24.752), ἀλφάνω, “fetch” a price (21.79), and in the Odyssey πρίαμαι, “buy” (Od. 1.430, 15.483). There is no coined money. Values are counted in oxen: gold armor is worth a hundred oxen and bronze armor nine (6.236), a great tripod twelve and a skilled slave woman four (23.702–705), and a captured son of Priam fetched a hundred when he was sold (21.79; see [[cattle]]).

## In the Iliad

The one market in the poem is the fleet of wine from Lemnos, which arrives on the evening the wall is finished:

{{quote:81427023-1f12-5ac5-beff-c7992af3f2b0}}

Euneus, son of Jason, rules Lemnos, and he deals with the army in two ways. The sons of Atreus receive a thousand measures “apart”, as a gift; the rest of the Achaeans buy (see [[wine]], [[euneus]] and [[lemnos]]). What they pay with is the wealth of a camp at war: bronze and iron, hides and cattle, and ἀνδράποδα, people taken in war and sold (7.473–475; see [[bronze]] and [[iron]]). The word, common in later prose, occurs only here in Homer, and its dative ἀνδραπόδεσσι is formed as if from a noun of another declension. Both have been taken as signs that the line is late.

Lemnos was a market for captives as well as a source of wine. Achilles sold Lycaon, a son of Priam, into Lemnos, and the son of Jason paid the price for him (21.40–41, 21.78–79); at the funeral games of Patroclus Achilles sets out as a prize a silver mixing bowl of Sidonian work, which Phoenicians had carried over the sea, and which Euneus had given Patroclus as the price of Lycaon (23.740–747). Hecuba says that Achilles sold her other sons across the sea to Samos and Imbros and Lemnos (24.751–753), and Priam that he has lost many sons, killed or sold to distant islands (22.44–45). The army's wine came from overseas in any case: Nestor tells Agamemnon that his huts are full of wine that the Achaean ships bring every day from Thrace (9.71–72).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the Odyssey trade is carried on by others. Phoenician traders, “famous for ships”, come to Eumaeus' island with countless trinkets, spend a year trading from their ship, and carry off the boy to sell him (Od. 15.415–484). In one of Odysseus' false tales a Phoenician cheat takes him to Phoenicia and means to sell him in Libya (Od. 14.288–297). Athena, in the likeness of the Taphian Mentes, says that she is sailing to Temesa for bronze with a cargo of iron (Od. 1.180–184). Among the Phaeacians, Euryalus insults Odysseus by saying that he looks like the captain of a crew of traders, mindful of cargo and greedy gains, and not like an athlete (Od. 8.159–164). The insult shows that a life of trade was thought beneath a noble.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells his brother how to sail with a cargo in the right season, and advises him to “praise a small ship, but put your cargo in a large one” (*Works and Days* 618–694, 643); their father had crossed the sea from Aeolian Cyme to Ascra in Boeotia, fleeing poverty (633–640). Moses Finley set gift exchange at the center of the Homeric world and argued that trade in it was left largely to outsiders (*The World of Odysseus*, 1954).
