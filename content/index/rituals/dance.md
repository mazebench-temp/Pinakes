χορός means both the dance and the place where it is danced. Towns and lands are praised as εὐρύχορος, “with wide dancing floors” ([[with-wide-dancing-floors]]).

When Aphrodite comes for Helen after the duel, she describes Paris in his chamber, “shining with beauty and fine clothes; you would not say that he had come from fighting a man, but that he was going to the dance, or had just stopped dancing and sat down” (3.392–394). The dancer is set against the fighter. Hector had already set Paris' lyre, his hair and his looks against the strength of Menelaus (3.54–55; see [[lyre]] and [[divine-rescue]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

On the shield of Achilles Hephaestus makes a dancing floor like the one Daedalus made for Ariadne at Cnossus, where young men and girls dance with their hands on each other's wrists (18.590–606). In his grief for Hector, Priam calls the sons he has left “liars and dancers, the best at beating the dancing floor” (24.261).
