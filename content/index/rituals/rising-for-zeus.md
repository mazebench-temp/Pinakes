After his nod to Thetis, Zeus goes to his own house. All the gods rise from their seats to meet their father; none dares to wait for him to come to them, and all stand facing him. Then he sits on his [[throne|throne]] ({{ref:Iliad 1.533-536}}). To rise for someone is to acknowledge his rank (see [[aidos]]). The whole family of gods rises for its father and king, and his supremacy is on display just before Hera challenges it (see [[quarrel-on-olympus]]).

{{quote:0d25aee2-9902-5d9e-b4e6-dc6147c490b2}}

The gods rise for other great arrivals too. When Hera returns to Olympus in anger, they all spring up and greet her with cups (Iliad 15.84–86). In the Homeric Hymn to Apollo the gods leap from their seats when Apollo comes into Zeus's house drawing his bow, and only Leto stays seated beside Zeus (lines 2–9).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus notes that the Spartans, alone among the Greeks, share an Egyptian custom: the young give way to their elders in the street and rise from their seats when they approach (2.80).
