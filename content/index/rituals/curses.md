## Meaning

ἀρή is a prayer, and above all a prayer for harm: a curse. Its verb ἀράομαι is the ordinary word for praying (ἠρᾶθʼ, 1.35), and it also tells of Althaea's prayer for her son's death (ἠρᾶτο, 9.567). καταράομαι, “pray against”, is to curse (κατηρᾶτο, 9.454), and ἐπαρή is the curse laid on someone (ἐπαράς, 9.456). The priest is an ἀρητήρ, “one who prays” (1.11; see [[priesthood]]). Another ἀρή, “ruin, bane” (ἀρὴν … ἀμύναι, 12.334; ἀρῆς ἀλκτῆρα, 14.485, 18.100), is usually treated as a separate word.

## In Homer

A curse is spoken to the gods like any prayer, and differs only in what it asks. When a parent curses a child, the gods who answer are the gods below the earth, and the Erinyes carry the curse out (see [[prayer]] and [[erinyes]]).

**Amyntor.** Phoenix tells Achilles how he lay with his father's concubine at his mother's urging. “My father, suspecting it at once, cursed me many times, and called on the hateful Erinyes that he would never set upon his knees a dear son born of me; and the gods fulfilled his curses, Zeus of the underworld and dread Persephone” (9.453–457). The curse answers the wrong: the son who came between his father and a woman is to have no son of his own. Phoenix says that the gods kept it, and that he made Achilles his son instead (9.492–495; see [[amyntor]], [[phoenix]], [[the-flight-of-phoenix]] and [[concubines]]). Ζεὺς καταχθόνιος, “Zeus of the underworld”, is Hades (see [[hades]] and [[persephone]]).

**Althaea.** In Phoenix's story Meleager stays out of the war “angered by his mother's curses” (ἐξ ἀρέων μητρὸς κεχολωμένος, 9.566). Grieving over the killing of her brother, Althaea “many times … beat the all-nourishing earth with her hands, calling on Hades and dread Persephone, kneeling down … to give death to her son; and the Erinys who walks in darkness heard her out of Erebus” (9.568–572). The curse is heard, but its fulfilment is not told (see [[althaea]], [[meleager]] and [[the-death-of-meleager]]). A prayer to the gods above is made standing, with hands lifted to the sky; this one is made kneeling and striking the ground.

**A mother's Erinyes.** The Odyssey knows the same powers. Telemachus will not send his mother away against her will, for “my mother will call down the hateful Erinyes as she leaves the house” (Od. 2.134–136), and Epicaste, who married her son, left Oedipus all the sorrows “that a mother's Erinyes bring to pass” (Od. 11.279–280). When Athena strikes Ares down in the battle of the gods, she tells him that he is paying his mother's Erinyes for abandoning the Achaeans (21.410–414).

**Other prayers for harm.** Some curses name no Erinys. Chryses asks Apollo to make the Danaans pay for his tears (1.42), and the Cyclops asks Poseidon that Odysseus may come home late and in misery, without his companions, in another man's ship, to find trouble in his house (Od. 9.528–535); both are heard. The oath of Book 3 ends in a curse on whoever breaks it first, “may their brains pour out on the ground like this wine” (3.300; see [[oath]]).

## Outside Homer

In the Homeric Hymn to Apollo, Hera prays to Earth and Sky and the Titans under the earth for a son of her own, apart from Zeus, and strikes the ground with the flat of her hand, and the earth moves (lines 332–341). In Aeschylus the curse becomes a power in its own right. Eteocles calls on “Curse, the mighty Erinys of my father” (*Seven against Thebes* 70), the curse of Oedipus on his sons, and the Erinyes tell Athena that below the earth they are called Ἀραί, “Curses” (*Eumenides* 416–417).
