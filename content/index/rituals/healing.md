## Healers

Homer's healers treat wounds. Sickness comes from the gods, and the plague of Book 1 is Apollo's arrows, ended by the return of Chryseis, purification and sacrifice (see [[plague-and-purification]]). The healer, ἰητήρ or ἰητρός, is a man of skill. [[Asclepius]] is “the noble healer” (ἀμύμονος ἰητῆρος, 4.194), a mortal in Homer, and his sons [[Machaon]] and [[Podalirius]], who lead the men of Tricca, Ithome and Oechalia, are “good healers” (2.731–732). The gods have a healer of their own, Paieon, who spreads pain-killing drugs on the wounds of Hades and of Ares (5.401–402, 5.899–901). In the Odyssey the healer is one of the craftsmen whom a community will invite from abroad, with the seer, the builder and the singer (Odyssey 17.382–385), and Egypt is the land where every man is a healer, “of the race of Paieon” (Odyssey 4.231–232).

## The treatment

The Iliad describes the work in detail twice, when Machaon heals Menelaus (4.188–219) and when Patroclus tends the wounded Eurypylus (11.828–848). The steps are these:

1. **Examining.** The healer feels the wound: “a healer will examine the wound” (ἐπιμάσσεται, 4.190), from ἐπιμαίομαι, “touch, handle”.
2. **Drawing out the missile.** Machaon draws the arrow out of the belt, and its barbs break back (4.213–214). An arrowhead may have to be cut out: a healer, says Idomeneus, is “a man worth many others, to cut out arrows and spread on soothing drugs” (11.514–515).
3. **Cleaning.** Machaon sucks out the blood (ἐκμυζήσας, 4.218, a word found only here); Patroclus washes the black blood away with warm water (11.828–848).
4. **Drugs.** The healer sprinkles on ἤπια φάρμακα, “soothing drugs” (4.218, 11.515, 11.830; see [[soothing-drugs]]), or a bitter root crushed in the hands, which stops the pain and dries the wound (11.846–848).

The sons of Autolycus bind up the young Odysseus' boar wound and stop the black blood with a charm (ἐπαοιδή, Odyssey 19.457–458). There is no such charm in the Iliad.

In Book 4 the healing answers the wound in its own words. The arrow was “a source of black pains” (μελαινέων ἕρμʼ ὀδυνάων, 4.117), and the drugs will “stop the black pains” (παύσῃσι μελαινάων ὀδυνάων, 4.191). The healer is fetched by a herald, as a leader would be (4.192–209; see [[messenger-scene]]). He works while the best men stand round in a circle (4.210–212), and while “they were busy about Menelaus” the Trojan ranks come on (4.220–221). His drugs came to his father from [[Chiron]], “in friendship” (4.219).

## Outside Homer

Asclepius became a god of healing, with his great sanctuary at Epidaurus. Pindar tells how Apollo gave the child to Chiron to be taught, how he healed every kind of sickness and wound, and how Zeus struck him down with the thunderbolt when he was bribed with gold to raise a dead man (*Pythian* 3.45–58). The Hippocratic physicians called themselves Asclepiads. A fragment of the *Sack of Troy* (*Iliupersis*), quoted in the scholia on Iliad 11.515, divides the brothers' skills: Machaon had the lighter hands, to draw missiles from the flesh and to cut and heal wounds; Podalirius could see what is hidden and cure what does not heal.

Plato's Socrates takes the healing of Menelaus as a model of true medicine: the sons of Asclepius sucked out the blood and spread on soothing drugs, and prescribed no regimen (*Republic* 3.405c–408b; see [[plato-asclepiads]]). Virgil gave the wounded Aeneas a healer when the truce of *Aeneid* 12 is broken: Iapyx, to whom Apollo had once offered his own arts, and who chose instead to know the powers of herbs and the practice of healing. He works at the arrowhead in vain until Venus secretly brings dittany from Cretan Ida, and the arrow falls out of the wound (*Aeneid* 12.391–429; see [[virgil-duel-for-lavinia]]).
