## The tablet

Proetus' wife has lied that Bellerophon tried to seduce her, but Proetus will not kill him himself: “he shrank from killing him, for his heart held back in awe from that” (6.167). He sends him instead to his wife's father, the king of Lycia, carrying his own death:

{{quote:80f04962-63ce-55bd-acc9-308e25f7fe93}}

σήματα λυγρά, “grim signs”: σῆμα is any sign or mark, a portent from a god, a token, a grave mound (see [[teras]] and [[tomb]]), and λυγρός is “baneful, grim”. γράψας, “scratching”, is from γράφω, which everywhere else in Homer means to scratch or graze. An arrow “grazed” Menelaus' skin (ἐπέγραψε, 4.139), and a spear-point grazes a bone (γράψεν, 17.599). The word became the Greek for “write” and “draw”, and γράμματα for “letters”. πίναξ is a board: in the Odyssey a platter for meat (Od. 1.141) or a ship's plank (Od. 12.67). πτυκτός, “folded”, from πτύσσω, “fold”, occurs only here. The tablet was made of leaves hinged together and shut, perhaps so that the man who carried it could not see what it said. θυμοφθόρα πολλά are “many things to destroy a man's life”. θυμοφθόρος, “life-destroying”, occurs only here in the Iliad; the Odyssey uses it of poisons, grief and exhaustion (Od. 2.329, 4.716, 10.363).

The tablet is a message and also a token. The king of Lycia entertains the stranger for nine days, and only on the tenth asks “to see the sign that he was bringing from his son-in-law Proetus” (6.176–177). A host feeds his guest before he asks his business (see [[xenia]]). The tablet vouches for its bearer and condemns him at once. Having read it, the king in his turn will not kill his guest: he sends him against the Chimaera, the Solymi and the Amazons, and then lays an ambush for him (6.179–190; see [[bellerophon]], [[chimaera]] and [[ambush]]). Neither king will kill him with his own hands, and each passes the killing on.

## Writing in Homer

Nothing else in the Iliad or the Odyssey mentions writing, reading or letters. The nearest thing is the lot that Ajax marks before his duel with Hector. Each man marks his lot (ἐσημήναντο, 7.175), Nestor shakes them in a helmet, and the herald carries the one that leaps out round the ranks. No one knows it until it reaches Ajax, who had scratched it (ἐπιγράψας, 7.187) and knew the mark on his lot when he saw it (κλήρου σῆμα ἰδών, 7.189). That σῆμα means something only to its owner. Bellerophon's is made to be read by another man, far away. Whether it was written in letters that any reader could follow, or in marks agreed between the two kings, or in pictures, is disputed. The translation says “signs” and “scratching”, which leave the question open. See [[proetus]] and [[lycia]].

The story is set generations before the Trojan War. In the Bronze Age the Greeks of the Mycenaean palaces kept their records in Linear B, a syllabic script written on clay tablets. The script was lost when the palaces fell, around 1200 BC, and writing came back when the Greeks took over the Phoenician alphabet, probably in the eighth century BC. Folding tablets of wood, whose leaves held a surface of wax, were already in use in the Late Bronze Age, so the tablet could belong to either world: to the age in which the story is set, or to the poet's own, when the alphabet was new. Among the earliest Greek alphabetic inscriptions, of the later eighth century BC, are verses scratched on a cup found at Pithecusae, which calls itself the cup of Nestor. Whether the poems were themselves composed with the help of writing is another question (see [[homer]]). For what ancient and modern readers have made of the tablet, see [[bellerophons-letter]].

## Outside Homer

The letter that orders its reader to kill the man who delivers it is a widespread motif, best known from the letter David sent to Joab by the hand of Uriah (2 Samuel 11.14–15; see [[bellerophons-letter]]). Later tellings of the story make the signs plain letters. In Apollodorus, Proetus gives Bellerophon a letter (ἐπιστολαί) to carry to Iobates, in which it was written that he should kill him (*Library* 2.3.1). In English such a message is still called a “Bellerophontic letter”.
