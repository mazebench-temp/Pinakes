A champion steps out in front of his army and challenges (προκαλίζομαι) the best of the enemy “to fight face to face” (ἀντίβιον μαχέσασθαι, 3.20, 7.40). In the two formal duels of the Iliad the challenge becomes an agreement. The armies stop fighting and sit down, the terms are proclaimed, lots are cast, and the two men fight in the open ground between the armies while both sides watch. The word μονομαχία (“fighting alone”) is later Greek. Homer says that the two will fight “alone” (οἴους, 3.91) and “in the middle” (ἐν μέσσῳ, 3.69, 3.90).

The two duels are built alike and differ in their stakes. Paris and Menelaus fight for Helen and her possessions, and the oaths make the duel decide the war (3.67–120, 3.245–382). Hector's challenge in Book 7 stakes only the loser's armor and body: the winner may strip the dead man, but must give the body back for burial (7.76–86). In both a lot decides, in Book 3 who throws first (3.316–325), in Book 7 which of nine Achaeans will fight (7.170–191). In both the men throw spears in turn and then close. Neither duel ends in a death. Aphrodite snatches Paris away (3.373–382), and the heralds part Hector and Ajax at nightfall, and the two exchange gifts (7.273–305). The war is not settled by champions.

The Iliad has many other fights of one man against one, but they are encounters in battle, not agreed duels. When Hector at last stands to face Achilles, he proposes terms like those of Book 7, an oath that the winner will give back the loser's body, and Achilles refuses: “there are no trusty oaths between lions and men” (22.254–267).

## Outside Homer

Greek and Roman tradition remembered wars decided, or meant to be decided, by champions. Herodotus tells how Echemus of Tegea killed Hyllus, son of Heracles, in single combat at the Isthmus, and how by the terms agreed beforehand the descendants of Heracles gave up their return to the Peloponnese for a hundred years (9.26). At Thyrea three hundred Spartans fought three hundred Argives while the rest of both armies stood aside (Herodotus 1.82). Livy's Horatii and Curiatii, three brothers against three, fought for Rome and Alba after a treaty sworn with sacrifice (1.24–25). In the Hebrew Bible Goliath proposes that one combat decide which people will serve the other (1 Samuel 17:8–10). Virgil built the last book of the *Aeneid* on Iliad 3: a treaty is sworn with sacrifice before the duel of Aeneas and Turnus, and broken before they can meet (see [[virgil-duel-for-lavinia]]).
