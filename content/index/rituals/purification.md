ἀπολυμαίνομαι is “wash off defilement”, and λύματα are the defilements themselves, the filth that is washed away. While the ship carries Chryseis home, Agamemnon orders the army to purify itself. The men cleanse themselves, throw the defilements into the sea, and sacrifice to Apollo on the shore (see [[sacrifice|sacrifice]]).

{{quote:d1ce1437-18a2-53ac-9304-d0fd3b2a7adf}}

Homer does not say why (see [[plague-and-purification]]). The army has been struck by a god's anger and is about to approach him with sacrifices, and washing is the ordinary preparation for approaching a god (see [[hand-washing|hand-washing]]). Homer does not use the later vocabulary of pollution (μίασμα). Elsewhere in the poems, Achilles cleans a cup with sulfur before pouring a libation to Zeus (Iliad 16.228–230), and Odysseus fumigates his hall with sulfur after killing the suitors (Odyssey 22.481–494).

## Outside Homer

Euripides' Iphigenia gives the principle behind throwing the defilements into the sea: “the sea washes away all the evils of mankind” (*Iphigenia among the Taurians* 1193).
