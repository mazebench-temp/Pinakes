## Words

ἕδνα is a plural, “bride-gifts”; ἔεδνα has a prothetic vowel. A suitor “gives” them (δίδωμι, πορεῖν), and the man who gives the most wins the bride. ἀνάεδνος, with the negative prefix, is a bride “without bride-price” (9.146, 9.288, 13.366). ἐεδνωτής, found only in Idomeneus' taunt (13.382), is one who gives or settles the ἔεδνα, and ἐεδνόομαι is to give a daughter in marriage with ἔεδνα (Od. 2.53). See [[marriage]].

## In the Iliad

**The suitor's gifts.** The poem's formula for a marriage made is ἐπεὶ πόρε μυρία ἕδνα, “after he gave countless bride-gifts”. So Echecles took Polymele to his house (16.190), and so Hector led Andromache from the house of Eetion, on the day that Aphrodite gave her the headdress she throws off when he dies (22.468–472; see [[andromache]]). Borus married Polydora openly, “giving boundless bride-gifts” (16.178). Iphidamas gave a hundred cattle for his bride and promised a thousand goats and sheep, and died before he had any joy of her (11.241–245; see [[iphidamas]]); the word ἕδνα is not used, but the price is counted. The girls who dance on the shield of Achilles are ἀλφεσίβοιαι, “who bring in cattle” (18.593), usually explained as girls for whom suitors will give oxen.

**Without bride-price.** To give a daughter without bride-price is a king's favor. Agamemnon offers Achilles whichever of his three daughters he likes, “without bride-price”, and a dowry besides, “such as no man has ever yet given with his daughter” (9.146–148, 9.288–290; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). The father's gifts are μείλια, “gifts to soothe”, found only in these verses. Achilles refuses (9.388–400). Othryoneus, an ally newly come to Troy, asks Priam for Cassandra on the same terms, and offers a promise instead (13.363–369).

## In the Odyssey

The suitors of Penelope woo her “and offer bride-gifts” (Od. 11.117 = 13.378). Antinous proposes that each of them woo her from his own house, “seeking her with bride-gifts”, and that she marry the man who gives the most (Od. 16.390–392); Leodes, when he has failed to string the bow, says in the same words that the suitor who fails should woo some other Achaean woman so (Od. 21.160–162). Athena warns Telemachus that Eurymachus outdoes the others in gifts and has raised his offer (Od. 15.16–18), and Penelope speaks of following the best of the Achaeans who woos her in the halls, “giving boundless bride-gifts” (Od. 19.528–529). Odysseus says of Nausicaa that the man who prevails with his bride-gifts and leads her home will be blessed above all others (Od. 6.158–159). Neleus married Chloris for her beauty, “after he gave countless bride-gifts” (Od. 11.281–282), and Hephaestus, catching Ares with Aphrodite, will keep them bound until Zeus gives back all the bride-gifts he paid for her (Od. 8.317–320).

Other passages point the other way. If Penelope wishes to marry, Athena tells Telemachus, let her go back to her father's house, and her kin “will prepare the wedding and arrange the ἔεδνα, very many, such as it is fitting should go with a dear daughter” (Od. 1.275–278); Eurymachus repeats the advice in the assembly (Od. 2.195–197). Telemachus complains that the suitors will not go to the house of her father Icarius, so that he himself might “give his daughter in marriage with ἔεδνα” to the man he chose (Od. 2.52–54). These are gifts from the bride's side, like Agamemnon's μείλια, and the debate over the word turns on them. M. I. Finley set the marriage gifts of both kinds in the exchange of gifts between noble houses (“Marriage, Sale and Gift in the Homeric World”, *Revue internationale des droits de l'antiquité* 2, 1955). A. M. Snodgrass argued that the poems combine customs that no single society practiced together, the bride-price and the dowry among them (“An Historical Homeric Society?”, *Journal of Hellenic Studies* 94, 1974).

## Outside Homer

In the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* the suitors of Helen send their bride-gifts to the house of her father Tyndareus. Menestheus offers gold, cauldrons and tripods from his father's house, hoping that no hero would surpass him in possessions and gifts, and Ajax of Salamis offers to drive together the cattle and sheep of the towns along the coast, from Troezen and Epidaurus to Corinth and Hermione, “for he excelled with the long spear” (fr. 68 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]).
