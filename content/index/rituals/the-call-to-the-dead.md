## The custom

Homer tells it once. The Cicones of the inland have routed the Achaeans at Ismarus, and six well-greaved companions of each ship have perished (*Odyssey* 9.59–61; see [[cicones]] and [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]]). The others fled, “grieving at heart, / glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions” (*Odyssey* 9.62–63), and yet the ships did not go on at once:

{{quote:5c1d1366-8755-51a9-8d0a-b6c8ea8880cc}}

The dead lie on the plain where they fell, in an enemy's country from which the living have fled, without the burning and the mound that the dead are owed (see [[honoring-the-dead]]). The rite is a shout, ἀῦσαι, the verb of a loud cry (see [[aute]]); τινα, “someone”, its subject, stands distributively for all the men who were left, and each of them calls three times on each of the dead (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.65; see [[hetairos]]).

The ancient commentators took it for a religious act. One scholium calls it “an ancient custom, to call up the souls of those who perished abroad three times”, done so that if anyone had been left behind on the plain, he might come; Eustathius adds that it was also so that no one still alive, hiding from the enemy, should be left there. Merry rejects that explanation, since the verse says that the men had died (οἳ θάνον ἐν πεδίῳ), and prefers another scholium's: the friends, sailing away to the dead men's country, called on the souls of those who had perished in a foreign land with certain prayers, and believed that they brought them home to their kin (Merry on 9.65; see [[psyche]]). In the Iliad too a rite for the dead is done three times: the Myrmidons drive their horses three times round the body of Patroclus, weeping (*Iliad* 23.13–14; see [[lament]]).

## Outside Homer

Nitzsch distinguished the call from the farewell to the dead, the “last words” (*novissima verba*) that Corynaeus speaks at the funeral of Misenus, after carrying pure water three times around the companions (Virgil, *Aeneid* 6.229–231); Merry finds it hard not to regard the call as a similar religious act, though perhaps less formal (on 9.65). Virgil's Aeneas tells the shade of Deiphobus, whose body he never found, that he raised an empty tomb for him on the Rhoetean shore and called his spirit three times with a loud voice (*magna manis ter voce vocavi*, *Aeneid* 6.505–506), and at the renewed funeral of Polydorus the Trojans lay his soul in the tomb “and with a loud voice call on him for the last time” (*Aeneid* 3.62–68).
