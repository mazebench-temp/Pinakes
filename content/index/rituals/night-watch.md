φυλακή, from φύλαξ, “guard”, and φυλάσσω, “watch over, keep guard”, is the watch an army keeps at night, and the men who keep it are φύλακες or φυλακτῆρες. Homer's watches are kept by men under arms, sitting by their fires, and their duty is to stay awake. The order is given in a verse: “and remember to keep watch, and let each man stay awake” (7.371, Priam to the Trojans; 18.299, Hector). A watch is kept against an ambush by night (see [[ambush]]), and the fires show the enemy that the army is there.

πυρά, the plural of πῦρ, “fire”, is the word for these fires. In Homer it is used only of the Trojans' fires on the plain during the long night of Books 8 to 10 (8.509, 8.554, 8.561, 8.562; 9.77, 9.234; 10.12).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Achaeans answer the Trojan fires with a watch of their own. Nestor proposes it in the assembly at the start of Book 9, “But now let us yield to black night and make ready our supper; and let the sentries, each of them, spend the night beside the ditch they have dug, outside the wall” (9.65–67). The first two of those verses are Hector's, from his orders to the Trojans (8.502–503). Seven captains, each with a hundred young men carrying long spears, go out and sit between the ditch and the wall, kindle fires, and make their supper (9.80–88). Book 9 opens on the Trojans keeping their watch (9.1), and Odysseus tells Achilles of the Trojans camped near the ships, who have “kindled many fires throughout the army” (9.232–234). In Book 10 Agamemnon gazes at the many fires before Ilios (10.11–13), and he and Nestor go the rounds of their own guards, who sit awake in their armor, like dogs that keep watch over sheep in a fold when they hear a wild beast in the woods (10.180–189). The Trojan scout Dolon tells his captors that only the Trojans keep watch at their fires; the allies sleep and leave the watch to them, “since their children are not near them, nor their wives” (10.414–422).
