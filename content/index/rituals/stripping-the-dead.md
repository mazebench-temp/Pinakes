## The custom

A man who killed an enemy took his armor if he could. The phrases are τεύχεα συλᾶν, “strip the armor”, and ἀπὸ τεύχεα δῦσαι, “take off the armor”. ἔναρα are the arms taken from a slain man, and from them comes ἐναρίζω or ἐξεναρίζω, “strip of his arms”. The verb is used so often of killing in battle that it can mean simply “slay”, and the translation renders it now one way, now the other.

The armor was worth taking. It was bronze, and could be gold: Glaucus gives Diomedes golden armor worth a hundred oxen for bronze worth nine (6.234–236). It could be kept, worn, or given to a god. Hector promises, if he kills his opponent, to strip the armor and hang it on the temple of Apollo (7.81–83); Odysseus holds up Dolon's spoils to Athena and sets them in the stern of a ship until an offering can be made to her (10.460–464, 10.570–571); Hector puts on the armor of Achilles that he has stripped from Patroclus (17.192–197). What a man wore when he was killed went to the man who killed him (2.872–875). The spoils of a sacked town were another matter: they were brought to the camp and divided by the army (see [[division-of-spoils]]).

Horses and chariots were the richest spoils of the field. When a man was killed in his chariot, the victor or his companions drove the team off to the ships. See [[horses]] and [[chariot]].

To stoop over a body to strip it was dangerous, and the stripping of the dead and the fight over the body go together (see [[fighting-over-the-body]]). Nestor tells the Achaeans not to hang back for the spoils but to kill men first; they can strip the corpses on the plain at leisure afterward (6.66–71).

The custom had its restraints. Before his duel in Book 7, Hector proposes that the victor keep the armor and give back the body for burial (7.76–86), and before their last fight he asks Achilles for the same agreement, which Achilles refuses (22.254–267). Achilles himself, when he sacked Thebe, killed Eetion “but did not strip him, for he felt awe in his heart”, and burned him in his armor and heaped a mound over him (6.416–420; see [[sack-of-thebe]]).
