## The scene

**The domain.** ἐν δʼ ἐτίθει τέμενος βασιλήϊον, “And on it he set a king's domain” (18.550). A τέμενος is land cut off and given to a king or a hero, as the Lycians gave Bellerophon a domain of orchard and plowland (6.194–195), Sarpedon and Glaucus hold one by the Xanthus (12.313–314), and the Calydonians offered Meleager fifty acres, half vineyard and half plowland (9.577–580; see [[bellerophon-in-lycia]] and [[meleager]]). βασιλήϊον is the printed reading; most editors adopt the vulgate βαθυλήϊον, “deep with grain”. Walter Leaf holds both possible, but finds βασιλήϊον meaningful after the plowing of the common field: the royal land, reaped by hired laborers. Such lands, he notes, are apparently the only private property in land known to the Iliad, and he observes that the Shield never mentions slaves (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.550; see the translation's note on 18.550). The ἔριθοι, “hired laborers”, appear twice in the scene (18.550, 18.560); Leaf reports that the word is not found again in Homer except in the compound συνέριθος (*Odyssey* 6.32).

**The reaping.** ἤμων ὀξείας δρεπάνας ἐν χερσὶν ἔχοντες, “were reaping, with sharp sickles in their hands” (18.551). δρέπανον, “sickle”, is named once more in Homer, in Odysseus' challenge to a mowing contest (*Odyssey* 18.368). “Some of the handfuls were falling to the ground in rows along the swath, / and others the sheaf-binders were tying with bands of straw” (18.552–553). The δράγματα are the handfuls that a reaper grasps with one arm and cuts, and ἐπήτριμα is “in rows, one after another” (Leaf on 18.552, 18.211; see [[the-beacon-fires]]). ὄγμος, “the swath”, and δράγματα are the words of the simile of the reapers in *Iliad* 11, who work toward each other through a rich man's field while “the handfuls fall thick” (11.67–71; see [[the-reapers]]). Three binders stand over the work, and behind them boys gather the handfuls in their arms and keep supplying them “relentlessly” (18.554–556).

**The king.** βασιλεὺς δʼ ἐν τοῖσι σιωπῇ / σκῆπτρον ἔχων ἑστήκει ἐπʼ ὄγμου γηθόσυνος κῆρ, “and among them the king in silence, / holding his scepter, stood by the swath, glad at heart” (18.556–557). He does nothing but watch. The scepter is the badge of his office: Leaf, against Ridgeway's claim that a σκῆπτρον is always a symbol of office, notes that it can be a plain staff (*Odyssey* 13.437, 17.199, 18.103), but takes the king here “in its full sense” (on 18.550; see [[scepter]], [[basileus]], [[silence]] and [[kingship-and-authority]]). The scepters of the heralds pass from hand to hand among the judging elders in the city at peace (18.505; see [[the-trial-on-the-shield]]).

**The feast.** κήρυκες δʼ ἀπάνευθεν ὑπὸ δρυῒ δαῖτα πένοντο, / βοῦν δʼ ἱερεύσαντες μέγαν ἄμφεπον: “And apart, under an oak, heralds were making ready a feast, / and had sacrificed a great ox and were busy over it” (18.558–559; see [[heralds]] and [[sacrifice]]). The women “were sprinkling much white barley meal for the laborers' dinner” (18.560; see [[barley-meal]]). Leaf asks whether the ox could be meant for the laborers, since δαῖτα, “a feast”, seems too rich a meal for them, and concludes that it can: a feast is an unusually abundant meal, and δεῖπνον names the meal in relation to the day's work; πάλυνον is the sprinkling of meal on roast meat, as in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 14.77, 14.429), or perhaps, as Hentze took it, the making of a porridge (on 18.560; see [[feast]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Reaping is the Iliad's picture for slaughter. In the simile of Book 11 the armies cut each other down as reapers drive their swaths toward each other (11.67–71; see [[the-reapers]]), and Odysseus, urging Achilles to let the army eat before battle, says that men soon have their fill of war, in which the bronze strews the most straw on the ground and the harvest is smallest, when Zeus tips his scales (19.221–224). On the Shield the reaping is reaping. In the Odyssey Odysseus challenges the suitor Eurymachus to a contest of mowing in spring, each with a curved sickle, fasting till dark (*Odyssey* 18.366–370).

## Outside Homer

The *Shield of Heracles* has a field of the same kind: there was a deep crop, and some reaped the stalks with sharp blades, while others bound them in bands and spread them on the threshing floor (*Shield* 288–291). Leaf cites its βαθὺ λήιον, “a deep crop”, in favor of the reading βαθυλήϊον here (on 18.550). Hesiod tells the farmer to begin the harvest when the Pleiades rise, and when the snail climbs the plants, to sharpen the sickles and rouse the slaves (*Works and Days* 383–384, 571–575; see [[pleiades]]).
