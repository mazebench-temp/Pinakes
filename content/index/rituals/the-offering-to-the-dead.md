## The rite

Homer tells the rite twice: as Circe's instruction (*Odyssey* 10.516–537) and as Odysseus' own deed, in the first person and in the same verses, when he has crossed Oceanus (*Odyssey* 11.23–50): βόθρον ὄρυξʼ, “I dug a pit”, χοὴν χεόμην, “I poured a drink offering”, γουνούμην, “I implored” (*Odyssey* 11.25–29, plain renderings). Its purpose is a consultation. Circe sends him “to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias” (ψυχῇ χρησομένους, *Odyssey* 10.492, 10.565), with the verb used of Agamemnon, who crossed the stone threshold at Pytho “to consult the oracle” of Apollo (χρησόμενος, *Odyssey* 8.81; see [[teiresias]], [[divination]] and [[psyche]]).

{{quote:8d0ef38d-d788-5019-bf6d-c0746689b635}}

The pit stands where an altar would stand for the gods above: a pit in place of an altar, Merry notes, was common in the cult of the gods of the underworld (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.517). Around it the drink offering, χοή, is poured “to all the dead”: honey and milk, then sweet wine, then water, and white barley meal is sprinkled over it (see [[honey]], [[milk]], [[wine]] and [[barley-meal]]). χοὴν χεῖσθαι is the proper term for a drink offering to the dead, and Eustathius, as Merry reports him, calls the χοή proper to the dead, and not σπονδή or λοιβή, the words of libation (Merry on 10.518; see [[libation]]). The whole offering, in Merry's words, gives “the ordinary supports of life” to the dead (on 10.519).

Vows and prayers follow. Odysseus is to implore the dead, promising them a barren heifer, the best he has, to be sacrificed in his halls when he reaches Ithaca, and a pyre heaped with good things, and to Teiresias alone a sheep, all black (*Odyssey* 10.521–525; see [[the-strengthless-heads]] and [[prayer]]). When he has begged “the glorious tribes of the dead with prayers”, he is to sacrifice a ram and a black ewe, “turning them toward Erebus”, and turn himself away toward the streams of the river; then the souls of the dead will come (*Odyssey* 10.526–530; see [[the-tribes-of-the-dead]], [[erebus]] and [[sacrifice]]). While his companions flay and burn the victims and pray to Hades and Persephone, he is to sit with his sword drawn and keep the dead from the blood until he has questioned Teiresias (*Odyssey* 10.531–537; see [[hades]] and [[persephone]]).

**The blood.** In *Odyssey* 11 the blood is what the dead need in order to know the living and to speak. Teiresias bids Odysseus draw back from the pit and hold away his sword, “so that I may drink of the blood and tell you truly” (αἵματος ὄφρα πίω καί τοι νημερτέα εἴπω, *Odyssey* 11.95–96, a plain rendering), and tells him that whichever of the dead he lets come near the blood will tell him truly (*Odyssey* 11.147–149); his mother knows him only when she has drunk the dark blood (*Odyssey* 11.152–153).

**At the edge of the world.** The rite is done at the end of the earth, not beneath it. The ship crosses Oceanus to the shore where the groves of Persephone stand, and Odysseus, Merry observes, never goes down into the depths of the earth: he digs his pit on its surface and waits for the souls to come up and taste the blood (on 10.512; see [[oceanus]]).

**Other gifts to the dead.** The poems know other gifts for the dead, but no other rite that calls them up. Achilles sets two-handled jars of honey and of ointment on the pyre of Patroclus (*Iliad* 23.170), and Merry, on the vow to “heap the pyre with good things”, refers to that pyre and reports Eustathius' list of what custom put on the pyres of the dead: honey, flowers, cloaks and arms (on 10.523; see [[cremation]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). At Ismarus the companions killed on the plain were called three times each before the ships sailed on (*Odyssey* 9.64–66; see [[the-call-to-the-dead]]).

## Outside Homer

**Drink offerings in tragedy.** Merry quotes the tragedians for χοή as the drink offering of the dead (on 10.518). In Aeschylus Orestes, seeing women in black approach his father's tomb, guesses that they are “bringing drink offerings, soothing gifts for those below” (χοὰς φερούσας νερτέροις μειλίγματα, *Libation Bearers* 15), and the play is named from them, Χοηφόροι, “the bearers of drink offerings”. In Euripides Helen sends Hermione to Clytemnestra's tomb with her drink offerings, to pour out milk mixed with honey and foaming wine (μελίκρατʼ … γάλακτος οἰνωπόν τʼ ἄχνην, *Orestes* 112–117), and Iphigenia, among the Taurians, pours for the brother she thinks dead streams of milk from the mountain heifers, Bacchus' wine and the work of the tawny bees, “which are laid down as charms for the dead” (ἃ νεκροῖς θελκτήρια κεῖται, *Iphigenia among the Taurians* 159–166; Merry on 10.519; see [[thelgo]]).

**Oracles of the dead.** Such consultations of the dead, νεκυομαντεῖα or ψυχομαντεῖα, were especially common, Merry notes, where clefts in the ground, dark tarns of unknown depth, hot springs or noxious vapors seemed to offer a passage to or from the underworld, and he quotes Lucian's account of an oracle of the dead at Babylon in the words of this rite, “we dug a pit and slaughtered the sheep and poured the blood around the pit” (*Necyomantia* 9, as Merry quotes it on 10.517).

**Black victims.** White victims were offered to the gods above and black to the gods below (Merry on 10.525). Virgil's Aeneas, before his descent, sacrifices four black bullocks to Hecate, a black-fleeced lamb to the mother of the Eumenides and her great sister, and to Proserpina a barren heifer, *sterilemque tibi, Proserpina, vaccam*, and begins night altars to the Stygian king (*Aeneid* 6.243–252), as Odysseus vows a barren heifer to the dead and a black sheep to Teiresias.
