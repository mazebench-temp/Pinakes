ἄλοχος, “wife”, is literally “bedfellow” (ἀ-, “together”, + λέχος, “[[bed|bed]]”). κουριδίη marks the wedded wife, as against a woman won in war.

Agamemnon refuses to give back Chryseis because he prefers her to [[Clytemnestra]], his wedded wife (κουριδίης ἀλόχου): she is no worse “in form or stature, in mind or in handiwork” ({{ref:Iliad 1.113-115}}). He sets the captive above the queen of his house. Homer's audience knew what Clytemnestra would do when he came home (Odyssey 11.405–434; see [[clytemnestra-and-the-return]]).

{{quote:39dc6e33-680c-5e12-91ee-38396162f017}}

The line between captive and wife is not so sharp for Achilles. He later calls Briseis his “dear wife” and asks whether the sons of Atreus are the only men who love their wives (Iliad 9.336–343), and Briseis recalls that Patroclus promised to make her Achilles' wedded wife (κουριδίην ἄλοχον, Iliad 19.297–299).

Among the gods, Zeus tells Hera that his plans would be hard for her to know, “though you are my wife” ({{ref:Iliad 1.546}}), and the book ends with the two asleep side by side ({{ref:Iliad 1.611}}).

## Outside Homer

In [[Hesiod]], Hera is the last of Zeus's wives, after Metis, Themis, Eurynome, Demeter, Mnemosyne and Leto (*Theogony* 886–929).
