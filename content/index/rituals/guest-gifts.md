## The custom

ξεινήϊα are the things given to a ξεῖνος, a guest: the neuter plural of ξεινήϊος, “of a guest”. The word covers the meal set before a visitor, as when Hephaestus tells Charis to set “fine guest-gifts” before Thetis (18.408), and above all the gifts that host and guest exchange when they part. The meal, the bed and the gifts make the two men ξεῖνοι, guest-friends, and the tie outlasts them. Their sons inherit it with the gifts (see [[xenia]] and [[doron]]).

A gift is valued for its material and its workmanship, and also for its history, who gave it to whom. It is laid up in the house as a κειμήλιον, a treasure “laid by” (see [[possessions]]), and brought out as proof of the bond. Telemachus offers the disguised Athena a gift that “will be a treasure for you from me, such as dear guest-friends give to guest-friends”, and she asks him to keep it until she comes back, when it will be worth a return (Od. 1.311–318). A gift calls for a gift of equal worth.

The Iliad tells the history of several gifts. Oeneus gave Bellerophon a belt bright with purple, and Bellerophon gave Oeneus a two-handled cup of gold, which Oeneus' grandson Diomedes keeps at home in Argos (6.216–221; see [[belt]] and [[cup]]). Amphidamas of Cythera gave Molus the helmet of boar's tusks “as a guest-gift”, and it came to Molus' son Meriones, who lends it to Odysseus (10.266–271). Cinyras of Cyprus sent Agamemnon a corselet “as a guest-gift” when he heard that the Achaeans were sailing for Troy (11.19–23; see [[corselet]]). In the Odyssey Menelaus gives Telemachus a silver mixing bowl that Phaedimus, king of the Sidonians, had given him when he stayed in his house (Od. 4.615–619; see [[mixing-bowl]]), and Odysseus' bow was a gift from Iphitus. The two men met in Messene and exchanged bow for sword and spear, “the beginning of a guest-friendship”, but they never sat at each other's table, for Heracles killed Iphitus (Od. 21.13–38).

Gifts can seal a bond between enemies. After their duel Hector proposes that he and Ajax give each other gifts, so that men may say that they fought and parted in friendship; Hector gives a silver-studded sword, and Ajax a belt bright with purple, in the words of Oeneus' gift (7.299–305; 6.219). And the custom can be turned inside out. Odysseus asks the Cyclops for the guest-gift that is the right of strangers (Od. 9.267–268), and Polyphemus promises to eat Nobody last: “that will be your guest-gift” (Od. 9.369–370).

The exchange of armor between Glaucus and Diomedes is the Iliad's most famous gift. The armor is given on the field between men who were about to fight, to renew their grandfathers' bond, and it is unequal: gold for bronze, “the worth of a hundred oxen for the worth of nine” (6.230–236).

## Outside Homer

Moses Finley made gift exchange the key to the society of the poems: no gift without a return, and the return binds (*The World of Odysseus*, 1954). Guest-friendship between men of different cities, inherited in their families and kept up with gifts, remained a living institution in historical Greece (Gabriel Herman, *Ritualised Friendship and the Greek City*, 1987).

“Gold for bronze” became proverbial for a bad bargain (see [[gold-for-bronze]]). Socrates tells Alcibiades that he is trying to exchange gold for bronze (Plato, *Symposium* 218e–219a), and Aristotle takes Glaucus as his example of a man who gives away more than he gets and is not wronged, since the giving was in his own power (*Nicomachean Ethics* 5.9, 1136b9–13). Sophocles' Ajax, about to die by the sword Hector gave him, says that the proverb is true: the gifts of enemies are no gifts, and bring no good (*Ajax* 661–665).
