## The scene

ἐν δὲ νομὸν ποίησε περικλυτὸς ἀμφιγυήεις / ἐν καλῇ βήσσῃ μέγαν οἰῶν ἀργεννάων, / σταθμούς τε κλισίας τε κατηρεφέας ἰδὲ σηκούς: “And on it the renowned god, lame in both legs, made a pasture / in a lovely glen, a great one, for white sheep, / and farmsteads and roofed huts and pens” (18.587–589). νομός is pasture, the grazing ground to which the cattle of the scene before were hurrying (18.575; see [[the-herd-and-the-lions]]). A βῆσσα is a mountain glen. The sheep are ἀργεννός, “white”, the color of Homer's flocks (see [[sheep]] and [[white]]). Walter Leaf takes σταθμοί as a general name, like the modern “sheep-station”, including both the κλισίαι, huts for the shepherds, and the σηκοί, folds for the sheep, and notes the unusual position of μέγαν, “a great one”, and of ἰδέ, “and” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.587; see [[hut]]). The god's title ends the verse, as it ends the first verse of the next scene, the dancing floor (18.590; see [[lame-in-both-legs]]).

The three verses have nothing but the place: no shepherd, no beast of prey, no action. Leaf quotes Heyne's judgment that they are very like an emblem brought in from elsewhere, too bare and meager after the richly adorned verses before them, “a remark with which it is hard not to sympathize” (on 18.587). Placed after the herd attacked by lions, they show the flocks safe in their folds.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The steading of the similes is a place under threat. A shepherd who has only grazed a lion as it leapt into the fold slips away among the farm buildings (κατὰ σταθμοὺς δύεται), and the sheep, left undefended, flee in panic (5.136–142; see [[wounded-lion]]); two lions reared in the mountains raid the farmsteads of men until they are killed (5.554–558; see [[two-lions]]); flies buzz around a shepherd's steading in spring, when the milk wets the pails (2.469–471, 16.641–643; see [[flies-around-the-milk-pails]]). In the next book the gleam of Achilles' shield is likened to a fire that sailors see from the sea, burning high in the mountains in a lonely farmstead (19.375–378; see [[the-beacon-fires]]). The Odyssey's fullest picture of a sheepfold is the cave of the Cyclops, with its pens (σηκοί) full of lambs and kids, each kept apart by age (*Odyssey* 9.218–223).
