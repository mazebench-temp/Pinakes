## The scene

**The vineyard.** ἐν δʼ ἐτίθει σταφυλῇσι μέγα βρίθουσαν ἀλωὴν / καλὴν χρυσείην: “And on it he set a vineyard heavily laden with clusters, / fine and golden” (18.561–562). ἀλωή is an orchard or a threshing floor elsewhere in the Iliad, and a vineyard here (5.499, 9.534; see [[like-a-sapling]]); σταφυλή is the bunch of grapes, and βρίθω, “be weighed down”, is said of a laden thing. The vineyard is gold, the grapes on it are black, μέλανες δʼ ἀνὰ βότρυες ἦσαν (18.562), and it stands on silver poles, κάμαξι διαμπερὲς ἀργυρέῃσιν, “propped all through on silver poles” (18.563), set up, as Walter Leaf explains, for the vines to climb (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.563). As in the plowing, where the golden field grows dark behind the plow (18.548–549; see [[the-plowing]]), the colors of the picture are the colors of its metals.

**The trench and the path.** “And around it he drew a trench of dark blue, and around that a fence / of tin; and there was a single path to it, / by which the carriers went when they gathered the vintage” (18.564–566). κυανέην names the material, κύανος, as the tin that follows shows, though κύανος is not among the metals that Hephaestus cast into the fire (Leaf on 18.564; 18.474–475; see the translation's note on 18.564, [[kyanos]] and [[tin]]). The κάπετος is a trench, here a ditch around the vineyard. Trench and fence shut the vineyard in, and a single path leads to it, the carriers' way.

**The carriers.** παρθενικαὶ δὲ καὶ ἠΐθεοι ἀταλὰ φρονέοντες / πλεκτοῖς ἐν ταλάροισι φέρον μελιηδέα καρπόν: “And girls and young men, with light hearts, / were carrying the honey-sweet fruit in woven baskets” (18.567–568; see [[honey-sweet]]). ἀταλὰ φρονέοντες is the glee of children; Leaf notes that ἀταλός is regularly used of children, and that at the vintage festival “gravity was banished” (on 18.567). Girls and young men are the dancers of the Shield's last scene too (18.593; see [[dance]]).

**The song.** In the middle of them a boy plays lovely music on a clear-toned lyre and sings the Linus song in a delicate voice, and the others follow him, “stamping all together, / with song and with shouting”, skipping with their feet (18.569–572; see [[the-linus-song]] and [[lyre]]). Leaf takes ῥήσσοντες as beating time with the feet, or perhaps breaking into song, and ἰυγμός, “shouting”, as either a shout in a neutral sense or a share in the Linus dirge, since the scene as a whole is joyous (on 18.571, 18.572; see the translation's note on 18.571–572). If the Linus song was a lament for the passing summer, as Leaf thinks, it suited the vintage (on 18.570).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Vineyards are wealth in Homer. The Calydonians offered Meleager a domain half of vineyard and half of plowland (9.577–580), and towns of the Catalogue are “rich in vines” (see [[vine-rich]] and [[rich-in-grapes]]). In the Odyssey the vineyard of Alcinous shows every stage of the vintage at once: some grapes drying on level ground in the sun, others being gathered, others trodden, and in front unripe grapes shedding their blossom (*Odyssey* 7.122–126). Odysseus proves himself to his father by naming the trees that Laertes gave him as a boy, among them fifty rows of vines, each ripening in turn, with clusters of every kind on them (*Odyssey* 24.340–344). The fruit of the vintage is wine, “honey-sweet” like the grapes, as in the cup that is given to the plowmen on the Shield (18.545; see [[wine]]).

## Outside Homer

The *Shield of Heracles* has a vintage of its own, with some men gathering the grapes with sickles in their hands and others carrying white and black clusters in baskets from the great rows of vines, beside a golden row of vines, the work of Hephaestus, shaking with its leaves and its silver poles and heavy with grapes that had turned black (*Shield* 292–300). Hesiod tells the farmer to pick all the grapes when Orion and Sirius have come into mid-heaven and rosy-fingered Dawn sees Arcturus, to lay them in the sun ten days and nights and in the shade for five, and on the sixth to draw off into jars the gifts of Dionysus (*Works and Days* 609–614; see [[orion]]).
