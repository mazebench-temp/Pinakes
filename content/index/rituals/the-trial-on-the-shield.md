## The scene

**The quarrel.** The scene follows the weddings of the city at peace (18.490–496; see [[the-two-cities]]). The people have gathered in the place of assembly, and two men are quarreling “over the penalty / for a man who had perished” (18.498–499). The penalty, ποινή, is the price a killer pays to the kin of the dead. Ajax describes the custom to Achilles in the embassy: a man accepts the penalty from the killer of his brother or his son, “and the killer stays there among his people, having paid much” (9.632–636; see [[poine]]). At 18.499 Zenodotus and most copies read ἀποκταμένου, “slain”, which Walter Leaf thinks brings out the homicide better than the printed ἀποφθιμένου, “who had perished”, though both are possible (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.499).

**What they dispute.** “The one vowed to pay it all, / declaring it to the people, and the other refused to take anything” (18.499–500). The translation follows Leaf: μηδέν, which occurs only here in Homer, implies will, a refusal, where the mere denial of a fact would need οὐδέν; πιφαύσκων is “declaring” by speech, or possibly “displaying” the full price (on 18.500; see the translation's note on 18.497–508). So read, the killer offers to pay in full, and the kinsman will take no payment. “And both were eager to win a decision at the hands of an arbiter” (18.501). The arbiter, ἴστωρ, is a man who knows and decides (see [[istor]]); ἐπί, “at the hands of”, has no exact parallel in Homer, and πεῖραρ is “an issue, an end of the matter” (Leaf on 18.501).

**The people and the heralds.** “And the people were cheering both, supporting either side; / and heralds were holding the people back” (18.502–503). Leaf likens the partisans, ἀρωγοί, to the friends and neighbors whom a litigant brought to court in old English law to overawe the other side (on 18.502); the Massaliot text read ἐπίπνυον, “incited”, for ἐπήπυον, “shouted assent”. He compares the opening of the trial before the Areopagus in Aeschylus' *Eumenides*, where Athena bids the herald make proclamation and hold back the crowd (*Eumenides* 566; Leaf on 18.503; see [[heralds]]).

**The elders.** “And the elders / sat on polished stones in the sacred circle, / and held in their hands the scepters of the far-voiced heralds; / with these they then sprang up, and gave their judgments in turn” (18.503–506). The circle is a seat of stone set apart for justice, and Leaf compares the Phaeacians' place of assembly, fitted with quarried stones, and their seats of polished stone (*Odyssey* 6.266–267, 8.6), and the place in the Achaean camp “where their place of assembly and of justice / was, where too the altars of the gods had been built for them” (11.807–808; on 18.504). The plural σκῆπτρα is loose: the herald hands the staff to each speaker in turn, as he puts it into Menelaus' hand before he speaks at the games (23.567–568; Leaf on 18.505; see [[scepter]]). ἤϊσσον, “sprang up”, is a word of rapid motion, and Leaf takes the scene as more like an excited political debate than a judicial decision in the modern sense; Döderlein referred the verb to the litigants (on 18.506).

**The two talents.** “And in the middle lay two talents of gold, / to give to the one among them who spoke the straightest judgment” (18.507–508). The gold is for whoever “among them” speaks the straightest judgment, and “them” are most naturally the elders who give their judgments in turn; who put up the talents, and who decides which judgment is the straightest, the poem does not say. The judgment is δίκη, and it is spoken “straightest” (ἰθύντατα; see [[themis]]). At the games for Patroclus Menelaus offers to judge his own dispute with Antilochus, since no one will find fault with it: his judgment will be straight (ἰθεῖα, 23.579–580).

## In the poem

The trial is a quarrel brought to judgment by speech before the people, and the Iliad began with a quarrel in an assembly that no one could settle (see [[assembly]] and [[neikos]]). The custom it shows is the one Ajax holds up to Achilles in Book 9: a penalty that ends a killing, accepted by the kin of the dead. Achilles refused gifts then, and in Book 18 he seeks no penalty for Patroclus but Hector's life (18.91–93; see [[poine]]). Elsewhere the poem knows judgment as the work of men who carry the scepter: the judges “who guard / the ordinances that come from Zeus” (1.238–239), and the men on whom Zeus sends a storm because “in the assembly” they “judge crooked ordinances / and drive out justice” (16.386–388). The Shield shows the other side, the straight judgment that wins a prize.
