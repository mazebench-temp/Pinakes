## Meaning

ὀλολυγή and the verb ὀλολύζω name the cry, as the sound of the word imitates it. Merry insists that it is not a shriek of horror but a religious shout of joy and satisfaction at the moment the sacrifice is consummated (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.450).

## In Homer

In the Iliad the old women of Troy come with Hecuba to Athena's temple, “And with a loud cry they all lifted up their hands to Athena”, as Theano lays the robe on the goddess's knees and prays; Athena shakes her head (6.297–311; see [[theano]] and [[the-mind-of-the-gods-is-not-quickly-turned]]). At Pylos the women of Nestor's house raise it when Thrasymedes' axe cuts through the sinews of the heifer vowed to Athena (*Odyssey* 3.449–452; see [[thrasymedes]]). Penelope, having put barley grains in a basket and prayed to Athena to save her son, cries out, “and the goddess heard her prayer” (*Odyssey* 4.759–767; see [[penelope]]). When Eurycleia sees the suitors lying dead, she is about to raise the cry, and Odysseus holds her back: “Rejoice in your heart, old woman, and hold back, and do not cry out; it is not holy to boast over slain men” (*Odyssey* 22.407–412; see [[eurycleia]]). Merry cites this passage above all for the joy of the cry: Odysseus bids her rejoice in her heart instead (on 3.450).

## Outside Homer

In the Homeric *Hymn to Apollo* goddesses and women raise the cry for the god. When Apollo leaps into the light at his birth, “all the goddesses raised the cry” (*Hymn to Apollo* 119); when he leaps from the ship at Crisa like a star and kindles a flame in his shrine, the wives and fair-girdled daughters of the Crisaeans raise it at the rush of Phoebus, “for he cast great fear into each of them” (*Hymn to Apollo* 440–447; see [[apollo]] and [[crisa]]). There the cry is raised in fear.

Aeschylus' Clytemnestra tells how, when the beacon came, the women raised the cry “by a woman's custom” through the city at the seats of the gods (*Agamemnon* 594–597), as the watchman had bidden her raise it (*Agamemnon* 26–29); his Eteocles bids the chorus of Theban women raise “the sacred, propitious” cry as a paean, “the Greek custom of the cry at sacrifice” (*Seven against Thebes* 267–270). Herodotus thought that the cry at sacred rites came first from Libya, where the women use it much and well (*Histories* 4.189.3).
