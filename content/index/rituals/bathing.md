## The custom

λούω is “wash, bathe”, and λοετρά are “baths”. The bath was a tub (ἀσάμινθος) into which water heated in a tripod over the fire was poured; the bather was then anointed richly with olive oil (λίπʼ ἐλαίῳ) and dressed in clean clothes (18.344–353; Od. 8.433–437, 10.358–365). ἀσάμινθος is usually taken as a word that Greek borrowed from an older language of the Aegean, like other words ending in -νθος. Bathtubs of clay have been found in Bronze Age palaces, among them the one at Pylos that its excavators called the palace of Nestor.

## In the Iliad

The Iliad tells the bath of living men only once, when Odysseus and Diomedes come back from the night raid of Book 10 (10.572–577). Its other baths are given to gods, planned, wished for, prepared in vain, refused, or given to the dead.

- **Gods.** Hebe bathes Ares when Paeëon has healed the wound Diomedes gave him (5.905), and Hera cleanses her body with ambrosia and anoints it with oil before she goes to beguile Zeus (14.170–172).
- **Planned and wished for.** Nestor tells the wounded Machaon to sit and drink until Hecamede heats a bath and washes off the clotted gore (14.5–7). Agenor, thinking of flight, imagines bathing in the river at evening, cooling off his sweat, and going back to Ilios (21.556–561).
- **Prepared in vain.** Andromache orders her handmaids to set a great tripod on the fire, so that Hector may have a warm bath when he comes home from the battle, not knowing that Athena has already struck him down “far from baths” (22.442–446).
- **Refused.** Achilles swears that no water shall come near his head until he has burned Patroclus, heaped his mound and cut his hair (23.39–47).
- **The dead.** Apollo bathes Sarpedon's body in the river and anoints it with ambrosia (16.667–680); Achilles' companions heat water in a tripod, wash Patroclus and anoint him with oil (18.343–353); and Achilles has the handmaids wash and anoint Hector's body away from Priam's sight (24.582–588).

A bath for a horse is part of a stallion's ease: the horse of the simile, broken loose, runs to the river where he is “used to bathing” (6.508 = 15.265; see [[the-stallion]]), and Patroclus used to wash the manes of Achilles' horses with clear water and pour oil on them (23.280–282).

## In the Odyssey

In the Odyssey the bath belongs to the welcome of a guest, before the meal: at Pylos Nestor's youngest daughter Polycaste bathes Telemachus (Od. 3.464–468); at Sparta Telemachus and Peisistratus step into polished tubs and the handmaids bathe and anoint them (Od. 4.48–51); in the house of Alcinous the handmaids bathe Odysseus before the feast, in water that Arete has had heated in a tripod (Od. 8.433–437, 8.449–457), and in Circe's house a handmaid bathes him to take the weariness from his limbs (Od. 10.358–365). The Phaeacians count warm baths among their pleasures, with the feast, the lyre, dancing, changes of clothes and beds (Od. 8.248–249). Silver bathtubs were a king's gift: Polybus of Egyptian Thebes gave two to Menelaus (Od. 4.128). At the end of the poem the housekeeper bathes Odysseus and a Sicilian servant bathes Laertes, and Athena makes each of them taller and stronger to look at as he steps from the tub (Od. 23.153–163, 24.365–370).
