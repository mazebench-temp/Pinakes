## The scene

ἐν δʼ ἐτίθει νειὸν μαλακὴν πίειραν ἄρουραν / εὐρεῖαν τρίπολον: “And on it he set a soft fallow field, rich plowland, / broad, thrice plowed” (18.541–542). ἐν δʼ ἐτίθει, “and on it he set”, opens three of the countryside scenes, the plowing, the king's harvest and the vintage, and the river at the rim (18.541, 18.550, 18.561, 18.607). The νειός is fallow land, plowed but not sown, as in the simile of the Ajaxes as a yoke of oxen (13.703; see [[oxen-at-the-plow]]); ἄρουρα is plowland, the earth that bears crops (see [[earth]]). Walter Leaf follows Ridgeway in taking the scene as the plowing of the common field, which the whole community began on one day, hence “many plowmen”; the field is fallow because, he supposes, only half the land was tilled in alternate years, and τρίπολον probably means that it was plowed three times before it was sown (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.541; see the translation's note on 18.541–549). “But our information is very scanty,” he adds.

πολλοὶ δʼ ἀροτῆρες ἐν αὐτῇ / ζεύγεα δινεύοντες ἐλάστρεον ἔνθα καὶ ἔνθα, “and many plowmen on it / were wheeling their teams and driving them this way and that” (18.542–543). The teams are not said to be oxen or mules; in Book 10 mules are better than oxen at dragging the jointed plow through deep fallow (10.351–353). The plowman, ἀροτήρ, is named once more in the Iliad, in Achilles' funeral games (23.835; see below).

οἳ δʼ ὁπότε στρέψαντες ἱκοίατο τέλσον ἀρούρης, / τοῖσι δʼ ἔπειτʼ ἐν χερσὶ δέπας μελιηδέος οἴνου / δόσκεν ἀνὴρ ἐπιών: “And whenever they turned and reached the end of the field, / then a cup of honey-sweet wine would be put into their hands / by a man who came up to them” (18.544–546). The τέλσον is the headland, the strip at the end of the furrows where the plow was turned (Leaf on 18.544; see the translation's note on 13.703–707). The verbs are iteratives and optatives of repeated action: whenever they reached it, a man gave them wine (see [[wine]], [[cup]] and [[honey-sweet]]). Leaf reports that Fick would cut out 18.544–546 altogether as “curious, not to say ridiculous” lines (on 18.545). τοὶ δὲ στρέψασκον ἀνʼ ὄγμους, / ἱέμενοι νειοῖο βαθείης τέλσον ἱκέσθαι, “and they would turn back along the furrows, / eager to reach the end of the deep fallow” (18.546–547). ὄγμος is the furrow here and the reaper's swath in the next scene (18.552, 18.557; see [[the-kings-harvest]]).

## The wonder

{{quote:2404a644-f3fc-52c3-9ba4-11012ea588ca}}

ἣ δὲ μελαίνετʼ ὄπισθεν, ἀρηρομένῃ δὲ ἐῴκει, / χρυσείη περ ἐοῦσα: the field is gold, and yet behind the plowmen it turns dark and looks like earth that has been plowed. Leaf thinks the line points to some means of darkening the bright gold, by alloy or by a kind of glaze, and that the poet was thinking of some actual work that he had seen (on 18.548). It is the only thing on the Shield that the narrator calls a wonder, θαῦμα, though the armor of Peleus and the self-moving tripods of Hephaestus were each “a wonder to see” (18.83, 18.377; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]]). The likeness of the picture to life is the point, as when the fighters of the city at war fight “like living mortals” (18.539).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The plowman's work is a picture of labor in the similes. The two Ajaxes stand together like two wine-dark oxen straining at the plow through a fallow field, with only the yoke between them (13.703–707; see [[oxen-at-the-plow]]); in the Odyssey Odysseus longs for the sunset as a plowman longs for his supper whose oxen have dragged the plow all day through the fallow (*Odyssey* 13.31–35), and he challenges the suitor Eurymachus to plow a straight furrow with a matched pair of oxen (*Odyssey* 18.371–375). Demeter lay with Iasion “in a thrice-plowed fallow”, νειῷ ἔνι τριπόλῳ (*Odyssey* 5.125–127). Achilles' prize of iron in the funeral games will spare its winner's shepherd or plowman a journey to the city for iron for five years (23.832–835).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod gives the farmer the plowing's seasons: when the Pleiades set (*Works and Days* 383–384, 614–617; see [[pleiades]]), and when the crane's cry is heard from the clouds (448–451); he bids him plow in spring, plow the fallow again in summer, and sow it while the soil is still light (462–464). The *Shield of Heracles*, in its own picture of a city and its fields, has plowmen breaking the earth with their tunics girt up (*Shield* 286–287).
