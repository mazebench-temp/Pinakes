Making cloth, spinning and weaving, was the work of women in the Homeric household, from queens to slaves. Weaving at the upright [[loom|loom]] meant walking back and forth along it (ἐποίχεσθαι) to carry the weft across the warp.

Agamemnon refuses to free [[Chryseis]]: she will grow old in his house in Argos “as she walks to and fro at the loom and comes to share my bed” (ἱστὸν ἐποιχομένην, {{ref:Iliad 1.31}}). Loom and [[bed|bed]] are the two services of a captive woman (see [[captive-women]]). When he says he prefers her to his wife, one of the qualities he names is her handiwork (ἔργα, {{ref:Iliad 1.115}}), the usual word for women's work at the loom (see [[marriage|marriage]]).

{{quote:964d6c5c-df68-5604-91e7-f60176f0eb60}}

Weaving marks the women of the Iliad. Helen weaves the battles of the war into a purple cloth (Iliad 3.125–128), and Andromache is at her loom when she hears the cries from the wall (Iliad 22.440–448). Hector foresees her as a captive in Argos, weaving at another woman's loom (Iliad 6.456): the fate Agamemnon promises Chryseis.

## Outside Homer

Clay loom weights, from looms like the one Homer describes, are among the commonest finds in Greek houses. At Athens a robe woven by chosen women was presented to Athena at the Panathenaea.
