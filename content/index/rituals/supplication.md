λίσσομαι is “beg, entreat”. It can be used of any urgent request, but its full sense is the formal plea of the suppliant, who puts himself in another's power and claims protection by ritual gestures: sitting or kneeling at the other's feet, clasping his knees ([[gounata]]), touching his chin. Zeus himself watches over suppliants (Odyssey 9.270, 13.213), so a supplication properly made puts heavy pressure on the one supplicated. Book 1 contains a supplication refused and a supplication granted, and the plot of the Iliad hangs between them.

## In Book 1

**Chryses.** The priest comes to the ships with a boundless ransom and the [[fillets|fillets]] of Apollo on a golden [[scepter|scepter]], and begs (λίσσετο) all the Achaeans ({{ref:Iliad 1.15}}). He touches no one. His claim rests on the god's emblems, on the ransom ([[apoina]]), and on reverence for Apollo (ἁζόμενοι, {{ref:Iliad 1.21}}). The army shouts its assent “to respect the priest” (see [[aidos]]), but Agamemnon drives him away, and the refusal brings the plague (see [[wrath-of-apollo]]).

{{quote:da958e60-0546-506c-9375-4f70add0023e}}

**The word in the assembly.** Agamemnon uses the verb in scorn: “I will not beg you to stay” ({{ref:Iliad 1.174}}). Nestor uses it in earnest: “I myself implore you to let go your anger against Achilles” ({{ref:Iliad 1.283}}).

**Achilles' instructions.** Achilles tells Thetis to go to Olympus and entreat (λίσαι) Zeus, reminding him of the time she saved him from the other gods, and to “sit beside him and clasp his knees” (λαβὲ γούνων; {{ref:Iliad 1.393-407}}). A past service strengthens the suppliant's claim (see [[binding-of-zeus]]). Thetis answers, “I will clasp his knees (γουνάσομαι), and I think I will persuade him” ({{ref:Iliad 1.427}}).

**Thetis and Zeus.** Twelve days later Thetis finds Zeus sitting apart on the highest peak of Olympus and performs the rite in full. She sits before him, clasps his knees with her left hand, takes him beneath the chin with her right, and speaks “in supplication” (λισσομένη; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]] and [[supplication-type-scene]]).

{{quote:f8b26c60-33a7-5aae-b690-892febc76407}}

Zeus sits long in silence. Thetis holds on, “clinging close” (ἐμπεφυυῖα, literally “grown into him”), and asks again: nod, or refuse.

{{quote:cc7e863d-4f7c-554d-ba92-17f3e241aa22}}

Zeus nods ({{ref:Iliad 1.528-530}}). Hera has guessed how it was done: “early this morning she sat beside you and clasped your knees” ({{ref:Iliad 1.557}}).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Athena recalls the same scene from the other side: Thetis “kissed his knees and took him by the chin” (Iliad 8.370–372). The noun λιταί, “entreaties”, becomes the Prayers of Phoenix's allegory, lame daughters of Zeus who follow after Ate and bring harm to the man who rejects them (Iliad 9.502–512). The poem's last great supplication answers its first. In Book 24 Priam, a father who comes with a ransom like Chryses, clasps Achilles' knees and kisses his hands, and is heard (Iliad 24.477–479; see [[book-1-and-book-24]]).

## Outside Homer

Supplication remained a living institution. Suppliants sat at altars or held branches wound with wool, as in Aeschylus' *Suppliant Women*, and to kill a suppliant was sacrilege. Thucydides tells how the killing of Cylon's followers, who had taken refuge at the altars in Athens, left a curse on those who killed them and on their descendants (1.126).
