Homer's words for sacrificing are ἱερὰ ῥέζειν, “to do sacred things”, and ἕρδειν, “to do”. Both come from the root of ἔργον, “work”: sacrifice is the deed done for the gods. The later common word θύω does not have this sense in Book 1; at {{ref:Iliad 1.342}} θύει means “rages”. What reaches the god is the κνίση, the savor of fat burning on the altar, which rises to the sky with the smoke.

## In Book 1

**A claim on the god.** Chryses' prayer rests on the [[thigh-pieces|thigh-pieces]] he has burned for Apollo ({{ref:Iliad 1.40-41}}). Achilles wonders whether the god is angry over a hecatomb owed him, and whether, if he receives “the savor of lambs and unblemished goats”, he will end the plague ({{ref:Iliad 1.65-67}}).

**On the shore.** When the ship has left for Chryse, Agamemnon orders the army to purify itself. The men throw the defilements into the sea and offer Apollo perfect hecatombs of bulls and goats on the shore, and the savor rises to the sky (see [[purification|purification]] and [[hecatomb|hecatomb]]).

{{quote:d3c2bfdb-d464-53e3-b1d5-1ecd60c03af9}}

**At Chryse.** Odysseus has come to offer a holy hecatomb “so that we may appease the lord” ({{ref:Iliad 1.443-444}}; see [[propitiation|propitiation]] and [[return-of-chryseis]]). The rite is told step by step (see [[sacrifice-type-scene]]):

1. The victims are set in order around the [[altar|altar]] (ἑξείης ἔστησαν, {{ref:Iliad 1.448}}).
2. The men wash their hands and take up the [[barley-grains|barley grains]] ({{ref:Iliad 1.449}}; see [[hand-washing|hand-washing]]).
3. Chryses prays with raised hands ({{ref:Iliad 1.450-456}}; see [[prayer|prayer]]).
4. The barley is thrown forward (οὐλοχύτας προβάλοντο, {{ref:Iliad 1.458}}).
5. The victims' heads are drawn back (αὐέρυσαν), their throats cut (ἔσφαξαν), and they are flayed (ἔδειραν) ({{ref:Iliad 1.459}}).
6. The thigh-bones are cut out (μηρούς ἐξέταμον), wrapped in a double fold of fat (κνίσῃ ἐκάλυψαν δίπτυχα ποιήσαντες), and raw flesh is laid on them (ὠμοθέτησαν) ({{ref:Iliad 1.460-461}}).
7. The old priest burns them on split wood and pours [[wine|wine]] over them, while young men stand by with [[five-pronged-forks|five-pronged forks]] ({{ref:Iliad 1.462-463}}; see [[libation|libation]]).
8. When the thigh-pieces are burned, the men taste the entrails (σπλάγχνα πάσαντο, {{ref:Iliad 1.464}}).
9. They cut up the rest (μίστυλλον), thread it on [[spits|spits]], roast it with care, and draw it off ({{ref:Iliad 1.465-466}}).
10. They feast, and each has an equal share ({{ref:Iliad 1.467-468}}; see [[feast|feast]]).
11. The young men fill the [[mixing-bowl|mixing bowls]] and serve the drink, pouring the first drops for the gods ({{ref:Iliad 1.470-471}}).
12. All day they sing the [[paean|paean]] to Apollo, and the god is glad ({{ref:Iliad 1.472-474}}).

{{quote:ea32f38e-b852-5be7-b138-45b19fe84406}}

The division is the heart of the rite: the god's portion goes up in smoke, and the men eat the meat together. Much the same lines describe Agamemnon's sacrifice before battle in Book 2 (Iliad 2.421–432) and Nestor's at Pylos (Odyssey 3.430–463).

## Outside Homer

[[Hesiod]] explains why the gods get the bones. At Mecone, Prometheus hid the meat in the ox's stomach and wrapped the white bones in glistening fat, and Zeus chose the fat; “since then the tribes of men on earth burn white bones for the immortals on fragrant altars” (*Theogony* 535–557). Burnt thigh bones and tail vertebrae found at the altars of Greek sanctuaries show that the god's portion described in Homer was really offered.
