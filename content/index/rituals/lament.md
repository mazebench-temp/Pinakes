## The custom

γόος is the wailing lament for the dead, and γοάω “to wail, lament”. The θρῆνος is the dirge, sung by singers. The fullest account in Homer is Hector's funeral. His body is laid on a bed, singers are set beside it, “leaders of the dirge”, who sing the mournful song, “and the women groaned in answer”. Then Andromache, Hecuba and Helen in turn lead the wailing, each holding or addressing the dead, and after each the women, and at the last the whole people, answer with their groans (24.719–776). The half-line of the response, ἐπὶ δὲ στενάχοντο γυναῖκες, “and the women groaned in answer”, closes laments for Patroclus and for Hector (19.301, 22.515, 24.722, 24.746).

The gestures are the women's. Hecuba tears her hair and throws her veil away when she sees her son dragged (22.405–407); Briseis falls on the body of Patroclus, shrieking, and tears her breast, her neck and her face (19.282–285); Thetis and the Nereids beat their breasts (18.50–51); Andromache holds Hector's head in her hands (24.724). Men wail too. Achilles leads the lament over Patroclus, with his hands on his friend's chest (18.314–317), and the Myrmidons drive their chariots three times around the body, weeping (23.12–14).

A lament speaks to the dead, and in the speaking the mourner speaks of herself. Andromache's laments for Hector tell of her widowhood and foresee her son's slavery or death (22.477–514, 24.725–745), and when Briseis mourns Patroclus the women answer “for Patroclus as a pretext, but each for her own sorrows” (19.301–302). The dead are owed lament as they are owed burning and a mound (see [[cremation]] and [[tomb]]). When Diomedes kills the two sons of Phaenops, what their father is left with is “lamentation and grievous sorrows” (5.156), and Dione pictures Diomedes' wife waking the household with her wailing (5.413). The living can be lamented too. Thetis and her sisters mourn Achilles, who is still alive, because he will not come home (18.50–64), and Hector's household mourns him “while he was still alive” (6.500).

## Elsewhere in Homer

At Achilles' funeral, in the Odyssey, the daughters of the old man of the sea stand around the body wailing, and the nine Muses sing the dirge, answering one another, so that no Argive could be seen without tears (Od. 24.58–62).

## Outside Homer

Laments were sung at Greek funerals throughout antiquity, and the lament passed into tragedy, where a dead man is mourned by the chorus and the actors in turn. At Athens Solon was said to have restricted the mourning of women at funerals, among other things forbidding them to tear their flesh or to sing set laments (Plutarch, *Solon* 21). Margaret Alexiou traced the ritual lament from Homer to the folk laments of modern Greece (*The Ritual Lament in Greek Tradition*, 1974).
