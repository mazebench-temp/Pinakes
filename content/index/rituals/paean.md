The παιήων is a song addressed to a god, above all Apollo, for deliverance from danger or in thanks for it. In Homer Παιήων is also the name of the gods' own physician (Iliad 5.401, 5.899–900); later the name became a title of Apollo.

After the sacrifice and the feast at Chryse, the young men of the Achaeans spend the whole day seeking the god's favor “with song and dance” (μολπῇ), singing a beautiful paean and hymning (μέλποντες) the Far-Worker, and Apollo is glad as he listens. μολπή is song together with dance. The god who came down with his bow to send the plague now takes pleasure in song, and the plague is over (see [[propitiation|propitiation]] and [[return-of-chryseis]]).

The paean is also a song of victory: when Hector is dead, Achilles tells the young Achaeans to sing one as they go back to the ships (Iliad 22.391–392).

## Outside Homer

A god Paiawon is named on a Linear B tablet from Knossos (KN V 52), centuries before Homer. In the Homeric Hymn to Apollo the Cretans follow the god up to Pytho singing “Iēpaiēon” (lines 514–519).
