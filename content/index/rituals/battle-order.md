## Words

An army stands in στίχες, “ranks” or files, from στείχω (“walk in line”), and it moves in φάλαγγες, “battalions” or lines of battle; the later Greek phalanx takes its name from the word. A close mass of men is a πύργος, “tower”, rendered “column” (4.334, 4.347). Ahead of the mass fight the πρόμαχοι, “the champions”, men who fight in front of the rest; to step out in front is προμαχίζω (3.16). A leader “marshals” (κοσμέω) his men: the Athenian Menestheus had no equal “at marshaling horses and shield-bearing men” (2.554), and the leaders are κοσμήτορες λαῶν, “marshals of the people” (1.16).

## Nestor's order

Before the first battle Nestor advised Agamemnon to “separate the men by tribes and by clans … so that clan may help clan, and tribe tribe”, and so to learn which leaders and men were brave and which cowards (2.362–368). Then he gives the horsemen their orders (4.301–309). They are to keep their horses in hand and not be thrown into confusion; no one, “trusting in his horsemanship and his manhood”, is to fight the Trojans alone ahead of the others, nor to fall back; and “whoever from his own chariot reaches an enemy's chariot, let him thrust with his spear, since that is far better”. He ends with an example: “This is how the men of old sacked cities and walls” (4.308; see [[mythological-paradigm]]).

The review shows other leaders at their work. The Cretans arm around Idomeneus, who is “among the champions”, while Meriones urges on the rearmost battalions (4.252–254). The battalions of the two Ajaxes come on thick and dark like a storm cloud (4.274–282; see [[storm-cloud]]). Menestheus and Odysseus stand waiting with their men “until another column of Achaeans should come on” (4.331–335). When the armies advance, each leader gives orders to his own men and the rest go in silence (4.427–431).

## Chariots and the question of tactics

Nestor's charioteers keep a line and fight from their chariots. Elsewhere in the Iliad a hero usually drives to the fighting, gets down and fights on foot, while his driver keeps the chariot close behind for a quick escape: in the same book Agamemnon leaves his chariot with Eurymedon and goes on foot (4.226–231), and Diomedes leaps down from his (4.419; see [[chariot]]). Some have seen in Nestor's orders a memory of massed chariot warfare, such as the armies of the Near East fought in the Late Bronze Age; others take “the men of old” as the old man's way of lending authority to his own advice. How far Homeric battle reflects real warfare, and of what period, is much disputed. P. A. L. Greenhalgh studied the chariots (*Early Greek Warfare*, 1973), and J. Latacz argued that the Iliad's battles are fought by masses of men in close order, like the early hoplite phalanx, with the champions in front (*Kampfparänese, Kampfdarstellung und Kampfwirklichkeit in der Ilias, bei Kallinos und Tyrtaios*, 1977).

## Outside Homer

Xenophon's Socrates recommends a similar arrangement for any army: the best men in front and in the rear and the worst in the middle, so that they may be led by the ones and pushed by the others (*Memorabilia* 3.1.8). He does not name Homer.
