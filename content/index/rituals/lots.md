A κλῆρος is a lot, a token that its owner can recognize. The lots are put together in a helmet and shaken (πάλλω) until one jumps out, and the man whose lot it is “gets it by lot” (λαγχάνω). The later word for a lot, πάλος, is named from the shaking. κλῆρος can also mean what is allotted, a man's share of land (15.498).

Before the duel of Book 3, Hector and Odysseus, one from each side, measure out the ground and then shake the lots “in a bronze-fitted helmet, to see which of the two should first let fly his bronze spear” (3.316–317). While the lots are shaken the people of both armies pray, with lifted hands, that whichever of the two brought these troubles may die (3.318–323). Hector shakes them “looking away” (ἂψ ὁρόων), so that the choice is not his:

## Elsewhere in Homer

A lot leaves a choice to chance, or to the gods. When nine Achaeans offer to meet Hector in Book 7, each marks his lot and puts it in Agamemnon's helmet, the people pray for the man they want, and the lot that leaps out is the one they wished for, Ajax's (7.170–191). The world itself was divided by lot: Poseidon says that when the three sons of Cronus shook lots, he won the sea, Hades the darkness below and Zeus the sky, and the earth and Olympus were left common to all three (15.187–193). At the funeral games of Patroclus lots give the chariots their places at the start (23.352–357). In the Odyssey Odysseus and Eurylochus shake lots “in a bronze-fitted helmet” to decide who will go to Circe's house (Odyssey 10.206–207), in the words of Iliad 3.316.

## Outside Homer

Athens filled most of its offices by lot. In Herodotus' debate on constitutions, Otanes counts it among the marks of the rule of the people that it “holds office by lot” (πάλῳ, 3.80.6).
