## The custom

Homer has no single word for a truce. It is made with oaths, ὅρκια, and the phrase for making one, ὅρκια πιστὰ ταμεῖν, is literally “to cut trusty oath-offerings”, from the victims killed over it. The translation renders it “swear a trusty truce” (3.73, 3.94, 3.252; see [[oath]] and [[oath-formulas]]). The state it creates between enemies is φιλότης, “friendship” (3.73, 3.94; see [[philotes]]). The later Greek word for a truce, σπονδαί, “libations”, comes from the wine that sealed it; in Homer the word names only the libations of an agreement (2.341, 4.159; see [[libation]]).

A truce in the Iliad is proposed and carried by heralds, sworn before Zeus, and made for a stated purpose and a stated time: until a duel is fought, until the dead are burned, until a funeral is done. Each one looks past its end to more fighting (see [[honoring-the-dead]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

**For the duel.** In Book 3 Paris proposes that the others make friendship and a trusty truce while he and Menelaus fight for Helen (3.67–75). The armies lay their armor on the ground (3.113–115), and Agamemnon swears the oaths over lambs and wine in Priam's presence (3.245–301). The truce was meant to end the war, and in Book 4 Pandarus breaks it with an arrow, at the gods' contriving (4.64–72, 4.104–126; see [[the-oaths-sworn]] and [[pandarus-breaks-the-truce]]).

**For Hector's funeral.** In the last book Achilles asks Priam how many days he needs to bury Hector, so that he may hold back the army for that long (24.656–658). Priam asks for eleven: nine days to mourn and gather wood, the tenth to burn Hector and feast, the eleventh to heap the mound; on the twelfth they will fight, if they must (24.660–667). Achilles grants it and takes the old man's right wrist, so that he will not be afraid (24.669–672). Even so, the Trojans set watchmen around the mound while they build it, for fear the Achaeans will attack (24.799–800).

**In the Odyssey.** The Odyssey ends with a truce. When Odysseus and his men are about to slaughter the kinsmen of the suitors, Athena stops the fighting, and in the likeness of Mentor she makes oaths between the two sides (Od. 24.542–548).

## Outside Homer

In classical Greek warfare the truce for the dead was a regular institution, and asking for it had a meaning. The side that sent a herald to ask for its dead, to take them up “under a truce” (ὑποσπόνδους), admitted that it had lost the battle, and the victors set up a trophy. After the battle of Delium the Boeotians refused to give back the Athenian dead while the Athenians held the sanctuary there, and Thucydides tells the dispute at length (4.97–101). Plutarch tells how Nicias, after a victory near Corinth, gave up the credit for it rather than leave two Athenian bodies unburied, and asked for them under a truce (*Nicias* 6). The Athenians remembered that they had once won back the dead of the Seven against Thebes from the Cadmeians and buried them at Eleusis (Herodotus 9.27), and Euripides put the story on the stage in his *Suppliants*. In the eleventh book of the *Aeneid* the Latins send envoys to ask for their dead, and Aeneas grants them a truce of twelve days (see [[virgil-truce-for-the-dead]]).
