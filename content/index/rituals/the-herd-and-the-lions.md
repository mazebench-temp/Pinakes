## The scene

**The herd.** ἐν δʼ ἀγέλην ποίησε βοῶν ὀρθοκραιράων· / αἳ δὲ βόες χρυσοῖο τετεύχατο κασσιτέρου τε: “And on it he made a herd of straight-horned cattle; / the cows were made of gold and of tin” (18.573–574; see [[cattle]], [[straight-horned]], [[tin]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). The narrator names the metals of the picture, the gold and tin that Hephaestus cast into the fire (18.474–475). μυκηθμῷ δʼ ἀπὸ κόπρου ἐπεσσεύοντο νομὸν δέ, “and lowing they hurried out of the dung-yard to the pasture” (18.575): μυκηθμός, “lowing”, is found only here in the Iliad, and κόπρος, the dung, is here the byre itself, as when the calves come back to the yard in the Odyssey (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.575; *Odyssey* 10.411; see the translation's note on 18.575). The pasture lies “beside a sounding river, beside a bed of swaying reeds” (18.576; see [[rivers]]). ῥοδανόν, the manuscripts' word, is not found again in Greek; Leaf takes it to mean the same as ῥαδινός, “pliant, waving”, and reports a scholiast who read παρὰ Ῥοδανόν, a river (on 18.576; see the translation's note on 18.576).

“And golden herdsmen walked in line with the cattle, / four of them, and nine dogs, fleet of foot, followed them” (18.577–578). κύνες πόδας ἀργοί, “dogs, fleet of foot”, is found only here (see [[dogs]]).

**The lions.** σμερδαλέω δὲ λέοντε δύʼ ἐν πρώτῃσι βόεσσι / ταῦρον ἐρύγμηλον ἐχέτην: “But two terrible lions among the foremost cows / had seized a bellowing bull” (18.579–580; see [[lion]] and [[bulls]]). ἐρύγμηλος, “bellowing”, occurs only here; Leaf derives it from ἐρυγμός, “roaring” (on 18.580). The bull, “lowing loudly, / was being dragged off; and the dogs and the young men went after him” (18.580–581). “The two had torn open the hide of the great bull / and were gulping down the entrails and the black blood” (18.582–583). λαφύσσετον is a dual of λαφύσσω, the verb of the lion that gulps down the blood and entrails of a cow in the similes of *Iliad* 11 and 17 (11.176 = 17.64; see [[lion-among-cattle]] and [[dark-blood]]).

**The herdsmen and the dogs.** οἳ δὲ νομῆες / αὔτως ἐνδίεσαν ταχέας κύνας ὀτρύνοντες, “and the herdsmen / were setting on their swift dogs and urging them, for nothing” (18.583–584; see [[swift-dogs]]). Leaf explains ἐνδίεσαν from δίημι, which is found elsewhere only in the middle, δίεσθαι, “chase” or “drive” (on 18.584); it is the verb of the simile earlier in the book, where shepherds cannot drive a hungry lion from a carcass (δίεσθαι, 18.162; see [[lion-on-a-carcass]]). “For the dogs shrank back from biting the lions, / but standing very close they kept barking, and kept out of the way” (18.585–586). δακέειν μέν is “as for biting” (Leaf on 18.585), and ὑλάκτεον, “kept barking”, is the only barking in the Iliad.

## On the Shield

The herd and the lions is the fourth scene of the Shield's countryside, after the plowing, the king's harvest and the vintage, and before the pasture of sheep and the dance (see [[the-plowing]], [[the-kings-harvest]], [[the-vintage]], [[the-sheep-pasture]] and [[the-shield-of-achilles]]). It is the only scene of violence on the Shield outside the city at war. There too herds were attacked by the river where they came to drink, and the herdsmen killed; here the attackers are beasts, and the herdsmen live but can do nothing (18.520–529; see [[the-two-cities]]).

The scene is the world of the Iliad's similes, shown for its own sake. A lion takes the best of a herd while the herdsmen and their dogs keep their distance in the similes for Menelaus over the dead Euphorbus (17.61–67) and for Hector falling on the Achaeans like a lion on cattle grazing in a marsh, whose herdsman keeps always with the first and the last of the cows (πρώτῃσι καὶ ὑστατίῃσι βόεσσιν, 15.630–636; see [[lion-among-cattle]]); the lions of the Shield take a bull “among the foremost cows”. Dogs cower around a lion in Sarpedon's taunt of Hector (5.476; see [[dogs-around-a-lion]]). On the Shield nobody is likened to the lions: they stand for no warrior, and the herd is not an army.

## Outside Homer

The *Shield of Heracles* has lions too, but at war with boars: herds of boars and lions glare at one another, and a great lion and two boars already lie dead, the boars' dark blood dripping to the ground (*Shield* 168–177).
