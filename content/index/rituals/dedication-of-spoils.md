The arms taken from a slain enemy were his killer's to keep (see [[stripping-the-dead]]). They could also be given to a god. Homer has two such gifts, and the poem tells neither to its end.

**Hector's vow.** When he challenges the best of the Achaeans to single combat, Hector sets out what is to happen to the loser. If he himself is killed, the victor may take his armor to the ships and must give back his body; “but if I take his life, and Apollo gives me triumph, I will strip his armor and carry it to sacred Ilios and hang it on the temple of Apollo the Far-Shooter”, and give back the body for burial (7.77–86; see [[single-combat]] and [[temple]]). The armor would honor the god who gave the victory. The heralds part him and Ajax at nightfall with no victor, and the vow is never put to the test (7.273–282; see [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]).

**Dolon's spoils.** When they have killed the Trojan spy, Odysseus and Diomedes strip him, and Odysseus holds the spoils up to Athena, “goddess of the spoil”, high in his hand, with a prayer: “Rejoice in these, goddess; for you, first on Olympus of all the immortals, we will call upon; but once again guide us to the horses and the beds of the Thracian men” (10.458–464; see [[of-the-spoil]] and [[prayer]]). He sets them on a tamarisk bush and marks the place with reeds and branches so that they will find it again in the dark (10.465–468). On the way back Diomedes leaps down and puts the bloody spoils into Odysseus' hands (10.526–529), and at the ships Odysseus lays them “in the stern of the ship … until they could make ready an offering to Athena” (10.570–571). The offering itself is not told, and the poem does not say whose ship it is.

There is no temple in the Achaean camp. Athena's share is held up to her on the spot, hung on a tree, and kept aboard a ship until a sacrifice can be prepared. In the same night Diomedes vows her a heifer with gilded horns, and that sacrifice is not told either (10.292–294; see [[sacrifice]]).

## Outside Homer

In historical Greece the dedication of spoils in the sanctuaries was a matter of course. In Aeschylus' *Agamemnon* the herald imagines the boast of the army that took Troy, that it nailed up these spoils for the gods in their temples throughout Greece (577–579), and in the *Seven against Thebes* Eteocles vows that if the city is saved he will hang the spear-struck clothing of the enemy on the holy houses of the gods (271–278). Herodotus saw at Tegea, hanging around the temple of Athena Alea, the fetters that the Spartans had brought to bind the Tegeans and wore themselves when they were beaten (1.66.4). At Athens the fetters of Boeotian and Chalcidian prisoners hung on the Acropolis, and a bronze four-horse chariot made from a tenth of their ransom carried the verse “they set up these horses for Pallas as a tenth” (Herodotus 5.77.3–4). After Salamis the Greeks chose first-fruits for the gods, among them three Phoenician warships, set up at the Isthmus, at Sunium, and for Ajax at Salamis (8.121.1). In the *Rhesus*, when Hector offers Dolon a choice of the spoils, Dolon tells him to nail them up for the gods at their temples, and asks for Achilles' horses instead (179–182).
