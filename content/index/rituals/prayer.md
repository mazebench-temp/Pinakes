Homer's verbs for praying are εὔχομαι and ἀράομαι, and εὐχωλή is a prayer or a vow. εὔχομαι means at root “declare solemnly”, and in Book 1 it also means “claim” or “boast”: Agamemnon “claims (εὔχεται) to be far the best of the Achaeans” ({{ref:Iliad 1.91}}), and Thetis used to boast in Peleus' halls ({{ref:Iliad 1.397}}). The priest is ἀρητήρ, “the one who prays” (see [[priesthood|priesthood]]).

## The form of a prayer

Chryses' prayers show the three parts of a Greek prayer.

1. **Invocation.** “Hear me” (κλῦθί μευ), with the god's titles and the places where he is honored: god of the silver bow, guardian of Chryse and Cilla, lord of Tenedos, Smintheus.
2. **Claim.** The service that gives the worshiper a right to be heard. The first prayer claims a temple roofed and thigh-pieces burned; the second claims a prayer already granted.
3. **Request.** “Fulfill for me this wish” (κρήηνον ἐέλδωρ), and then the wish.

The two prayers are built alike and ask for opposite things. The first asks Apollo to make the Danaans pay with his arrows; the second, to ward off the ruin from them. Each is answered in the same words: “So he spoke in prayer, and Phoebus Apollo heard him” ({{ref:Iliad 1.43}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.457}}). The whole plague lies between them (see [[wrath-of-apollo]]).

The same “if ever” shapes other appeals in the book (see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Achilles tells Thetis to remind Zeus of her help ({{ref:Iliad 1.394-395}}), and Thetis begins, “Father Zeus, if ever among the immortals I helped you by word or deed, fulfill for me this wish” ({{ref:Iliad 1.503-504}}; see [[supplication|supplication]]).

## Gestures and occasions

The one who prays lifts up his hands: Chryses prays “lifting up his hands” (χεῖρας ἀνασχών, {{ref:Iliad 1.450}}). Achilles, praying to his mother in the sea, stretches his hands out toward it (χεῖρας ὀρεγνύς, {{ref:Iliad 1.351}}). Calchas prays to Apollo when he reveals the god's will ({{ref:Iliad 1.87}}). Achilles wonders whether Apollo is angry over a vow (εὐχωλή) or a [[hecatomb|hecatomb]] left unpaid ({{ref:Iliad 1.65}}), and Calchas answers that he is not ({{ref:Iliad 1.93}}).

## Outside Homer

The same appeal to past favor opens Sappho's prayer to Aphrodite: “come here, if ever before you heard my voice from far away and listened” (fragment 1.5–7).
