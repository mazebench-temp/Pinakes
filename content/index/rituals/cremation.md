The Homeric dead are burned on a [[pyres|pyre]], their bones gathered, and a mound raised over them. Funeral rites are “the prize of the dead” (γέρας θανόντων, Iliad 16.457), and the unburned dead cannot pass the gates of Hades (Iliad 23.71–74).

{{notes}}

## Elsewhere in Homer

The great funerals of the poem show the rite in full. For Patroclus the Achaeans build a pyre a hundred feet each way. Achilles lays on it sheep and cattle, jars of honey and oil, four horses, two of his dogs and twelve Trojan captives, and the pyre does not catch until the winds come at his prayer. At dawn they quench it with wine, gather the bones into a golden bowl and heap a mound (23.108–257). Hector's pyre is built after nine days of gathering wood. The Trojans quench it with wine, lay his bones in a golden chest wrapped in purple robes, and heap a mound over the grave (24.782–804). In the Odyssey the funeral of Achilles follows the same order, and his bones lie with Patroclus' in a golden amphora under a mound by the Hellespont (Odyssey 24.63–84); Elpenor is burned with his armor, and his oar is set up on his mound (Odyssey 11.72–78, 12.8–15).

## Outside Homer

Mycenaean Greeks mostly buried their dead unburned, in chamber tombs and tholos tombs; cremation became common in the Early Iron Age. At Lefkandi on Euboea, about 950 BCE, a man's ashes were laid in a bronze amphora beside a woman buried with gold ornaments, with horses in a pit nearby: a burial that recalls the funerals of the Iliad.
