## In Homer

The vintage is the third scene of the Shield's countryside, after the plowing and the king's harvest: a golden vineyard with black grapes on silver poles, a single path to it, and girls and young men carrying the fruit in woven baskets (18.561–568; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]]). In the middle of them a boy plays the lyre, “and to it he sang the beautiful Linus song / in a delicate voice” (18.570–571), and the others follow him, stamping all together, “with song and with shouting”, skipping with their feet (18.571–572; see [[dance]] and [[lyre]]).

The word is disputed. Zenodotus read λίνος, “the string” of the lyre, which sang to the boy's voice, and some took the vulgate λίνον the same way; Aristarchus took it as a kind of song, as one would say “he sang a paean”, and Walter Leaf reports that this explanation is now generally accepted (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.570; see the translation's note on 18.570 and [[paean]]). Leaf thinks the Linus songs were dirges, perhaps laments for the departing summer, and so suited to the vintage, and takes the name from the refrain αἴλινον, “woe for Linus” (on 18.570). The followers' ἰυγμός, “shouting”, occurs only here in Homer; Leaf takes it either as a shout in a neutral sense or as a share in the dirge, and that a conventional one, since the scene as a whole is joyous (on 18.572). ῥήσσοντες, “stamping”, is beating time with the feet, and Leaf compares the Cretans who follow Apollo and his lyre to Pytho in the *Hymn to Apollo* (516–517; on 18.571).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod made Linus the son of Urania, “whom all mortals who are singers and lyre-players / lament at feasts and dances”, calling on Linus at the beginning and at the end (fr. 1 Evelyn-White, among the fragments of uncertain position; Leaf quotes the lines from a scholium on 18.570). Herodotus found the song in Egypt: the Egyptians, he says, have a song, Linus, which is sung in Phoenicia, in Cyprus and elsewhere under other names, and is the same that the Greeks sing and call Linus; in Egyptian he is Maneros, the only son of the first king of Egypt, who died young and was honored with these dirges (Herodotus 2.79). In Aeschylus' *Agamemnon* the chorus repeats the refrain three times: αἴλινον αἴλινον εἰπέ, τὸ δʼ εὖ νικάτω, “cry woe, woe, but may the good prevail” (121, 139, 159).
