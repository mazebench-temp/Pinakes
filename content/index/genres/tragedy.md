## The genre

τραγῳδία is “goat-song” (τράγος, “goat”, ᾠδή, “song”), a name whose origin was already disputed in antiquity: a goat as the prize, a goat as the sacrifice, or singers dressed as satyrs. Tragedy was performed at Athens at the City Dionysia, where from about 534 BCE three poets each presented three tragedies and a satyr play. Of the hundreds of tragedies written in the fifth century thirty-two survive, seven by [[Aeschylus]], seven by [[Sophocles]], and eighteen under the name of [[Euripides]], one of them, the *Rhesus*, probably not his (see [[euripides-rhesus]]). Most took their stories from the heroic myths, and many from the Trojan War and its aftermath; the few on contemporary history, like Aeschylus' *Persians*, are exceptions.

## After Aristotle

New tragedies continued to be written and old ones revived throughout antiquity; Seneca's Latin tragedies, modeled on the Greek, were the form through which the Renaissance knew the genre. The *Poetics*, rediscovered in the sixteenth century, became the rule book of neoclassical tragedy in Italy and France (see [[unity-of-action]]).
