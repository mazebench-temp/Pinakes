summary: The sixth adventure of Odysseus' homecoming, which fills most of the tenth book of the Odyssey. On Aeaea, the island of Circe, daughter of the Sun, half the crew under Eurylochus found her house in the glens; she gave them a drugged drink, struck them with her wand and penned them in her sties as swine. Going alone to free them, Odysseus met Hermes, who gave him a plant, moly, against her drugs; her drink did not bewitch him, and when he drew his sword on her she knew him, swore to plan him no harm, took him to her bed and made his companions men again. They stayed a year; at their departure she sent him first to the house of Hades, to consult the soul of Teiresias, and the youngest companion, Elpenor, fell from her roof and died (*Odyssey* 10.133–574). When they came back to bury Elpenor she told Odysseus the dangers of the rest of the voyage (*Odyssey* 12.1–150).

## The story

**Aeaea.** The one ship left from the harbor of the Laestrygonians came to the island of Aeaea, where Circe lived, “a dread goddess with human voice, / own sister of baleful Aeetes”, both of them children of the Sun and of Perse, a daughter of Oceanus (*Odyssey* 10.133–139; see [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]], [[circe]], [[aeaea]], [[aeetes]] and [[perse]]). “Some god guided us” into the harbor, and the men lay two days and two nights on the shore, eating their hearts out (*Odyssey* 10.140–143). On the third day Odysseus climbed to a lookout and saw smoke rising from Circe's house through the thick brush and the woods. On his way back to the ship a god sent a great stag across his path; he killed it, bound its feet and carried it on his neck to the ship, and roused his companions: “Friends, we shall not go down yet, for all our grief, / into the house of Hades, before our fated day comes upon us” (*Odyssey* 10.144–177; see [[deer]]). They feasted until sunset (*Odyssey* 10.178–186).

**The lots.** At dawn Odysseus told them what he had seen, an island ringed by the boundless sea and smoke in the middle of it: “friends, we do not know where the darkness is, nor where the dawn” (*Odyssey* 10.187–197). Their hearts broke as they remembered the Laestrygonian Antiphates and the Cyclops, and they wept, “but there was no profit for them in their weeping” (*Odyssey* 10.198–202; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). He divided them into two bands, one under himself and one under Eurylochus, and they shook lots in a helmet; the lot of Eurylochus leapt out, and he set out with twenty-two companions, weeping (*Odyssey* 10.203–209; see [[lots]] and [[eurylochus]]).

**The swine.** In the glens they found Circe's house of polished stone, with mountain wolves and lions around it that she had bewitched with her drugs; the beasts did not attack, but stood up and fawned on the men as dogs fawn on their master (*Odyssey* 10.210–219; see [[thelgo]], [[drugs]] and [[the-dogs-fawning-on-their-master]]). Inside, Circe was singing at her loom. Polites, the dearest of Odysseus' companions, bade them call to her (see [[polites-the-companion]]); she opened the shining doors and invited them in, and all went with her “in their ignorance” but Eurylochus, who stayed behind, “for he suspected a trick” (*Odyssey* 10.220–232; see [[loom]] and [[dolos]]).

**Hermes and the moly.** Eurylochus ran back to the ship, and when he could speak he told how the companions had gone in and had never come out (*Odyssey* 10.244–260). Odysseus took his sword and bow and bade him lead the way; Eurylochus clasped his knees and begged to flee with those who were left, and Odysseus went alone, “for harsh necessity has come upon me” (*Odyssey* 10.261–274). On the way Hermes met him in the likeness of a young man, told him that his companions were penned in Circe's sties and that he would stay there with them, and gave him a remedy: when Circe struck him with her wand he was to draw his sword as though to kill her; she would bid him to her bed, and he was not to refuse it, but first make her swear the great oath of the gods (*Odyssey* 10.275–301; see [[hermes]]). Then he gave him the drug (see [[moly]]):

**Circe overcome.** Circe gave Odysseus the mixed drink in a golden cup, struck him with her wand and bade him go to the sty with the rest of his companions; he drew his sword and sprang at her, and she ran in under it and clasped his knees. No man, she said, had ever withstood her drugs: “Surely you are Odysseus of many turns; he always told me / you would come, the slayer of Argus of the golden wand” (*Odyssey* 10.307–332; see [[of-many-turns]] and [[supplication]]). She invited him to her bed. He would not go up to it until she had sworn not to plan him any other harm; she swore, and he went up into her bed (*Odyssey* 10.333–347; see [[oath]]).

**The companions restored.** Her four handmaids prepared the chairs, the tables, the wine and a bath; he was bathed, anointed and clothed, and the housekeeper set food before him; but he would not eat until his companions were free (*Odyssey* 10.348–387; see [[handmaids]] and [[bathing]]). She opened the sty and drove them out, “looking like fat hogs nine years old”, and anointed each with another drug (*Odyssey* 10.388–392):

**The crew.** At Circe's bidding Odysseus went back to the ship. The men left there crowded around him weeping, like calves around their mothers coming home to the yard, as glad as if they had come home to Ithaca (*Odyssey* 10.400–421; see [[the-calves-and-the-cows]]). They beached the ship and stowed its goods in caves, and all followed him to Circe's house but Eurylochus, who called the venture as reckless as the visit to the Cyclops: “for it was through his recklessness that they too perished” (*Odyssey* 10.422–437; see [[atasthalie]]). Odysseus would have struck off his head, kinsman by marriage though he was, had the others not held him back; Eurylochus followed, afraid of his rebuke (*Odyssey* 10.438–448). Circe bade them stop lamenting and eat and drink until their spirit came back to them (*Odyssey* 10.449–466; see [[our-proud-spirit-was-persuaded]]).

**A year.** “There every day, for a full year's round, / we sat feasting on untold meat and sweet wine” (*Odyssey* 10.467–468). When the year had come round, the companions called Odysseus out: “Strange one, think now at last of your native land” (*Odyssey* 10.469–474). That night he begged Circe by her knees to send him home (*Odyssey* 10.475–486). She would keep none of them against their will, but first he must go “to the house of Hades and of dread Persephone, / to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias”, the one among the dead to whom Persephone has given a mind (*Odyssey* 10.487–495; see [[teiresias]], [[hades]] and [[persephone]]). He wept and asked who would guide them: “No one has ever yet come to the house of Hades in a black ship” (*Odyssey* 10.496–502). She told him to let the North Wind carry the ship across Oceanus to the groves of Persephone, where Pyriphlegethon and Cocytus flow into Acheron, and there to dig a pit, pour offerings to the dead, vow sacrifices and slaughter a ram and a black ewe, and keep the shades from the blood with his sword until Teiresias came to tell him the way home (*Odyssey* 10.503–540; see [[boreas]], [[oceanus]], [[the-groves-of-persephone]], [[acheron]], [[pyriphlegethon]], [[cocytus]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]).

**The departure.** At dawn Circe dressed him and herself, and he went through the house rousing his companions (*Odyssey* 10.541–550). The youngest, Elpenor, who had slept on the roof to be cool, sodden with wine, woke at the noise, forgot the ladder, and fell and broke his neck (*Odyssey* 10.551–560; see [[the-death-of-elpenor]]). When Odysseus told the others where they must go, they sat down and lamented and tore their hair. At the ship they found a ram and a black ewe tied up beside it: Circe had brought them, slipping past unseen, “who could see a god against his will / with his eyes, going either this way or that?” (*Odyssey* 10.561–574; see [[seeing-the-gods]]).

**The return.** From the house of Hades they came back to Aeaea to bury Elpenor. Circe came with her handmaids, bringing bread and meat and wine, and called them men who had gone down alive into the house of Hades, “twice-dying, when other men die once” (*Odyssey* 12.1–27, a plain rendering). That night she took Odysseus apart, heard all he had seen, and told him the dangers to come: the Sirens, the Wandering Rocks, Scylla and Charybdis, and the cattle of the Sun on Thrinacia. At dawn she went away across the island, and sent a following wind behind the ship (*Odyssey* 12.28–150, Greek only; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]).

## In the poem

**Guile and many devices.** In the summary that he gives Penelope, Odysseus tells “the guile and the many devices of Circe”, Κίρκης … δόλον πολυμηχανίην τε (*Odyssey* 23.321, a plain rendering). At the beginning of the tale he names her with Calypso as the two goddesses who kept him in their halls, “longing for me to be her husband; / but they never persuaded the heart in my breast” (*Odyssey* 9.29–33; see [[calypso]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). The tenth book tells the year otherwise: Circe bids them eat and drink, their proud spirit is persuaded, and they stay to feast (*Odyssey* 10.460–468); at the year's end it is the companions who must remind Odysseus of home, and when he asks to go, Circe does not hold him: “stay no longer in my house against your will” (*Odyssey* 10.472–489). Her drugs were given “so that they might utterly forget their native land”, ἵνα πάγχυ λαθοίατο πατρίδος αἴης, but the swine kept their minds (*Odyssey* 10.236–240); a year later the companions bid Odysseus, μιμνήσκεο πατρίδος αἴης, “think now at last of your native land” (*Odyssey* 10.472; see [[the-lotus-eaters]] and [[nostos]]).

**The goddess and the oath.** The oath that Hermes bids Odysseus demand is the oath he demanded of Calypso, in the same two verses: “unless you would bring yourself, goddess, to swear me a great oath / that you will not plan some other evil bane against me myself” (*Odyssey* 10.343–344 = 5.178–179; see [[oath]] and [[the-building-of-the-raft]]). With Calypso the oath answered his suspicion of the raft and the voyage she offered him; with Circe it answers his suspicion of her bed, which comes after it, “so that, having lain together / in bed and love, we may trust one another” (*Odyssey* 10.334–335; see [[philotes]]).

**A prophecy fulfilled.** Circe knows Odysseus as the Cyclops did, by a prophecy. Hermes always told her that Odysseus of many turns would come on his way from Troy (*Odyssey* 10.330–332), as Telemus the seer told the Cyclops that he would lose his sight at the hands of Odysseus (*Odyssey* 9.507–512; see [[telemus]]). The Cyclops' recognition comes too late; Circe's turns the danger into a welcome.

**The seer and the old man of the sea.** Circe sends Odysseus to Teiresias as Eidothea sent Menelaus to Proteus: a goddess tells a hero, held from home, how to make a seer speak, and promises in the same verses that he will tell “the way and the measures of your path” and the homecoming (*Odyssey* 10.538–540; 4.389–390; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]] and [[proteus]]). The instructions of the tenth book are carried out in the eleventh in the same verses, turned to the first person (*Odyssey* 10.516–537; 11.25–50, Greek only; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 10.516–537), and the return to Aeaea in the twelfth repeats the feast and the dawn of the tenth (*Odyssey* 12.23, 12.29–31, 12.142, Greek only; 10.460, 10.476–478, 10.541). On Thrinacia Odysseus warns his men with “the prophecies of Teiresias and of Circe of Aeaea” (*Odyssey* 12.271–275, a plain rendering; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]).

## Outside Homer

For later accounts of Circe, Strabo's view that Homer modeled her on Medea, the sanctuary of Circe at Circaeum in Latium and her tomb near Salamis, and the sons she bore to Odysseus, see [[circe]], [[medea]] and [[telegonus-kills-odysseus]]. Merry sets beside her beasts the tale of Beder and Giauhare in the Arabian Nights, in which the horses, camels, mules, asses and cows that meet King Beder on the shore of Queen Labe try by every gesture to warn him away: they had been princes and nobles whom the queen changed into beasts when she tired of them (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.212).
