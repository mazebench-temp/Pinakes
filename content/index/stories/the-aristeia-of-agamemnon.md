## The story

**The arming.** Strife has made war sweeter to the Achaeans than going home (11.3–14). Agamemnon calls them to arms and arms himself in the fullest arming scene of the poem, with the corselet that Cinyras of Cyprus sent him, its serpents like rainbows, the shield with the grim-faced Gorgon, Terror and Rout, and two spears whose bronze shines up to the sky; Athena and Hera thunder in his honor (11.15–46; see [[arming-scene]], [[corselet]], [[cinyras]], [[shield]] and [[like-rainbows]]).

**The morning.** The men go out on foot ahead of the chariots, and Zeus sends down dew wet with blood (11.47–55). Hector moves among the Trojans like a baleful star, and the two lines cut each other down like reapers and rage like wolves (11.56–77; see [[the-baleful-star]], [[the-reapers]] and [[like-wolves]]). At the hour of the woodcutter's meal, a time of day and not a simile, the Danaans break through, and Agamemnon is first (11.84–92).

**The killing.** He kills Bianor and his companion Oileus and leaves them with their chests shining bare (11.92–100). He kills Isus and Antiphus, two sons of Priam whom Achilles had once taken on Ida and let go for ransom, as a lion crushes the young of a hind in their lair (11.101–121; see [[the-lion-and-the-fawns]] and [[achilles-raids]]). The two sons of Antimachus beg to be taken alive; he refuses them for their father's sake, who once urged the Trojans to kill Menelaus when he came as an envoy, and sends Hippolochus' trunk rolling like a log through the throng (11.122–147; see [[sons-of-antimachus]], [[the-embassy-to-troy]] and [[like-a-log]]). The Trojans fall before him like thickets before a forest fire, and their horses rattle empty chariots over the field (11.148–162; see [[forest-fire]]).

**To the gates.** Zeus draws Hector out of the fighting. The Trojans flee past the mound of Ilus and the fig tree to the Scaean gates and the oak, and Agamemnon follows, killing the hindmost like a lion in a herd, his hands spattered with gore (11.163–180; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]] and [[lion-among-cattle]]).

**Zeus' message.** As he nears the wall, Zeus sends Iris to tell Hector to hold back while Agamemnon rages, until the king is wounded (11.181–209). Hector rallies the Trojans, and the lines form again (11.210–217).

**Iphidamas and Coon.** The poet asks the Muses who first came against Agamemnon (11.218–220). It was Iphidamas, son of Antenor, raised in Thrace. His spear point bends like lead on Agamemnon's belt, and Agamemnon kills him: he sleeps “the sleep of bronze”, far from the wife who gave him no joy (11.221–247; see [[iphidamas]] and [[like-lead]]). His elder brother Coon stabs Agamemnon through the forearm and is beheaded over his brother's body (11.248–263; see [[coon]]).

**The wound.** While the blood flows warm, Agamemnon fights on with spear, sword and stones; when it dries, sharp pains come over him like the pangs of a woman in labor. He mounts his chariot and calls on the Argive leaders to defend the ships (11.264–283; see [[the-woman-in-labor]]):

## In the poem

**A hero's day.** The episode has the parts of an aristeia, a hero's great day: the arming and the shining, the breaking of the enemy's ranks, kill after kill, the opponents of rank, the wound (see [[the-aristeia]]). It has no prayer and no god who renews the hero. Diomedes, wounded, prays to Athena and goes back three times as fierce (5.95–143); Agamemnon's wound ends his day. The day is framed by Zeus from the start. The bloody dew falls before the king kills a man (11.53–55), and Zeus holds Hector back while Agamemnon rages and names the wound in advance, “struck by a spear or hit by an arrow” (11.191). Agamemnon's glory lasts as long as Zeus allows, and its end is the beginning of Hector's day (see [[the-third-day-of-battle]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**The king fights.** Achilles called Agamemnon a coward who had never dared to arm for war with the army (1.225–228), and in the night before this day Diomedes told him to fight among the foremost at dawn (9.707–709). He does. He is first into the Trojan ranks twice (11.91–92, 11.216–217). His killing is among the harshest in the poem. He refuses the suppliants, as he would not let Adrastus be spared in Book 6 (6.55–65), strips the dead, beheads and mutilates, and his hands are spattered with gore (λύθρῳ δὲ παλάσσετο χεῖρας ἀάπτους, 11.169), a half-verse used again only of Achilles on his own day of killing (20.503). Lions and fire mark his way: two lion similes, two half-lines that make him a lion (11.129, 11.239; see [[like-a-lion]]), and the forest fire.

**After.** Agamemnon does not fight again in the Iliad. Later on the same day he proposes, for the third time in the poem, that the army launch the ships and flee (14.65–81), and on the next he comes wounded to the assembly in which he and Achilles are reconciled (19.51–53). Nestor names him among the best who lie wounded (11.658–661; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]] and [[agamemnon]]).

## Outside Homer

Aelian names “the Aristeia of Agamemnon” among the parts of the Iliad that were once sung separately, each under its own name (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14, as reported under [[title-of-book-10]]). The title covers only the first part of Book 11. The wounding of the other chiefs, Nestor's story and Patroclus' errand are left out of it (see [[title-of-book-11]] and [[aelian]]).
