## The story

Athena has gone, and Telemachus has gone back among the suitors (*Odyssey* 1.319–324; see [[athena-visits-telemachus]]). After the meal the herald had given the lyre to Phemius (*Odyssey* 1.150–155; see [[phemius]] and [[lyre]]), and now he sings:

{{quote:6e96a87d-00ee-5934-a614-08a073bcaaa1}}

Penelope comes down the high stairway of her house with two handmaids, stands by the doorpost of the hall holding her shining veil before her cheeks, and bursts into tears (*Odyssey* 1.330–336; see [[upper-room]], [[handmaids]], [[door]] and [[veil]]). She asks the singer to sing the suitors another of the many songs he knows, and to stop this one:

{{quote:fc1b1fd2-9489-523a-89ac-5ef5bac57466}}

Telemachus answers her. Why does she begrudge the trusty singer giving delight as his mind moves him? “It is not the singers / who are to blame, but Zeus, I think, is to blame, who gives / to toiling men, to each, as he wills” (*Odyssey* 1.347–349). There is no blame on the singer for singing the evil fate of the Danaans, “for men praise that song the more / which comes newest to those who listen”; let her heart bear to listen, “for Odysseus was not the only one who lost the day of his homecoming / in Troy” (*Odyssey* 1.350–355; see [[aitios]], [[no-blame]] and [[nostos]]). Then he sends her to her loom and her handmaids, “and speech will be the concern of men” (*Odyssey* 1.356–359; see [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]] and [[mothers-and-sons]]). She goes back amazed, laying her son's wise word to heart, and in her upper room she weeps for Odysseus until Athena casts sleep upon her eyelids (*Odyssey* 1.360–364; see [[penelope]] and [[telemachus]]). The suitors raise a din throughout the hall (*Odyssey* 1.365; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]).

## In the poem

**The poem's own subject.** The first song sung in the Odyssey is the matter of the Odyssey itself, the homecoming (νόστος) of the Achaeans. The proem has told how all the others who escaped destruction were at home, and only Odysseus was held back (*Odyssey* 1.11–15; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]). The song is λυγρός, “grim”, and its cause is Athena's anger, “the grim homecoming that Pallas Athena laid on them from Troy” (*Odyssey* 1.327), sung in the hall that the goddess has just left after setting Odysseus' own homecoming in motion (*Odyssey* 1.76–95; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]] and [[athena]]). W. W. Merry notes that everything Phemius sings for the suitors, or Demodocus for the Phaeacians, is taken from the Trojan war except the song of Ares and Aphrodite; he takes the homecoming of the Achaeans for a single lay from the cycle of the homeward voyages, and the war, ten years after it ended, to have become “matter of national history”; he explains the anger of Pallas by the outrage of Ajax, son of Oileus, to Cassandra in her temple, while at *Odyssey* 4.499 Ajax's death is laid to his insolence against Poseidon (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.326; see the translation's note on 1.327). Penelope's αἰεί, “always”, shows him that the song was already popular (Merry on 1.341).

**The listener.** Penelope hears the song in her upper room; ὑπερωιόθεν, “from her upper room”, adds that she hears it from that distance (Merry on 1.328). She comes down as a lady comes among men, attended and veiled, in the verses that bring her down again in Book 18 (*Odyssey* 1.331–335 = 18.207–211). Her words name the singer's art: songs are θελκτήρια, “enchantments for mortals”, the “deeds of men and gods, which singers make famous” (*Odyssey* 1.337–338; see [[song-and-fame]] and [[kleos]]). What she cannot bear is the subject, not the song: grief not to be forgotten for the man “whose fame is wide through Hellas and the heart of Argos” (*Odyssey* 1.342–344; see [[penthos]]).

**Telemachus' answer.** The son defends the singer in the words of blame that opened the council of the gods. There Zeus complained that mortals blame the gods (αἰτιόωνται) for the sorrows their own recklessness brings them; here Telemachus says that Zeus, not the singer, is to blame (αἴτιος) for what men are given to suffer (*Odyssey* 1.32–34, 1.347–349; see [[who-is-to-blame]]). His saying on the newest song makes the homecomings a recent event, and he counts his father among the dead (*Odyssey* 1.354–355). Aristarchus rejected his last four verses, which are Hector's to Andromache with “speech” for “war” (*Odyssey* 1.356–359; *Iliad* 6.490–493); Merry doubts the rejection, since something startling in his words must account for his mother's amazement (Merry on 1.360; see the translation's note on 1.356–359).

**The singers who make their hearers weep.** The half-verse that brings in Phemius' song, ἀοιδὸς ἄειδε περικλυτός (*Odyssey* 1.325), also sums up the song of Demodocus at which Odysseus hides his face and weeps among the Phaeacians, ταῦτʼ ἄρʼ ἀοιδὸς ἄειδε περικλυτός, “these things the renowned singer sang” (*Odyssey* 8.83), and Odysseus praises Demodocus for singing “the fate of the Achaeans” as if he had been there (Ἀχαιῶν οἶτον, *Odyssey* 8.489), as Telemachus here speaks of “the evil fate of the Danaans” (Δαναῶν κακὸν οἶτον, *Odyssey* 1.350). The two who weep at songs of Troy are husband and wife, each far from the other (see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]] and [[demodocus]]).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates quotes Telemachus' verses on the newest song, reading ἐπιφρονέουσι, “heed”, for ἐπικλείουσι and ἀειδόντεσσι, “to those who sing”, for ἀκουόντεσσι (Merry on 1.351), and warns the guardians of his city against taking the poet to praise a new manner of song, since the modes of music are never changed without the greatest laws of the city (*Republic* 424b–c; see [[plato-the-newest-song]] and [[plato]]).
