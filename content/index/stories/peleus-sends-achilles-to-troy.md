## The story

The day is told three times, and no telling is complete.

**The visit.** In Book 11 Nestor tells it to Patroclus. He and Odysseus came to Peleus' well-built house, “gathering the army throughout bountiful Achaea”, and found Menoetius there with Patroclus, and Achilles. Old Peleus was burning the fat thighs of an ox to Zeus in the courtyard and pouring wine from a golden cup on the burning offerings, and the two young men were busy over the meat. The visitors stood in the doorway. Achilles sprang up in amazement, led them in by the hand, seated them and set before them the hospitality due to guests. When they had had their fill of food and drink, Nestor spoke and urged the young men to come with them, and they were eager to come (11.769–782; see [[nestor]] and [[odysseus]]).

**The charges.** Then the fathers gave their sons their charges, in the visitors' hearing (11.767–768). Peleus told Achilles “always to be the best and to excel above the others” (11.783–784; see [[always-to-be-the-best]]). Menoetius told Patroclus that Achilles was the better born and far the stronger, but Patroclus the elder, and that he must give him shrewd counsel and guide him, and Achilles would listen for his own good (11.785–789; see [[menoetius]] and [[patroclus]]).

**Phoenix's version.** Phoenix tells what else was sent with the boy. Peleus sent Phoenix with him “on that day when he sent you from Phthia to Agamemnon, a child, who knew nothing yet of war, common to all, nor of assemblies, where men become distinguished. For this reason he sent me, to teach you all these things: to be a speaker of words and a doer of deeds” (9.438–443; see [[phoenix]] and [[speaker-of-words-doer-of-deeds]]).

## In the poem

**Each speaker's Peleus.** The three accounts share a frame of verses. Odysseus and Nestor begin alike: “My friend, surely your father … charged you on that day when he sent you from Phthia to Agamemnon” (9.252–253; 11.765–766, where the father is Menoetius). Both end with the same reproach: “So the old man charged you, but you forget” (9.259 = 11.790). Phoenix uses the second verse of the frame as well (9.439 = 9.253). Within the frame each speaker gives Peleus the words his purpose needs. Odysseus, asking Achilles to let go of his anger, remembers a father who counseled restraint, “for kindliness is better” (9.256). Nestor, asking Patroclus to move Achilles or to go out in his place, remembers the charge to excel and the companion's duty to advise. Phoenix, pleading for himself, remembers that he was sent as a teacher. The poem does not say what Peleus said. He may have said it all; each speaker may also shape the scene to his end, as Homer's speakers shape their stories (see [[peleus]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**The embassy as a second visit.** The day in Phthia is the model of the embassy. Odysseus comes to Achilles a second time to bring him to the war. Achilles springs up in amazement (ταφὼν δʼ ἀνόρουσεν, 9.193 = 11.777), welcomes the visitors and feeds them, and after the meal the visitor speaks (9.193–224; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]). In Phthia the young man was eager to go. In Book 9 he refuses.

**The young Achilles.** Nestor's account is the poem's one picture of Achilles at home before the war, a young man busy at his father's sacrifice, not yet a speaker or a warrior. Phoenix adds an earlier childhood, a small boy who would eat only on his knees and spilled his wine on Phoenix's tunic (9.485–491).

## Outside Homer

Later writers took Phoenix's charge, “a speaker of words and a doer of deeds”, as the model of an education in both speech and action (see [[phoenix-the-model-teacher]]). In later tradition, too, Achilles did not go so readily. Thetis, knowing that he would die if he went to war, hid him in girl's clothes among the daughters of Lycomedes on Scyros, where Odysseus found him out (Apollodorus, *Library* 3.13.8). The Iliad knows of Scyros only that Achilles took it and that his son Neoptolemus was being reared there (9.666–668, 19.326–327; see [[scyros]]).
