## The story

{{quote:5de3e051-3cfc-5965-b98b-2b5d23d08729}}

Poseidon says it among the gods when Aeneas, facing Achilles, is about to be killed (20.288–299; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]] and [[poseidon]]). The reasons come in a chain. Zeus would be angry if Achilles killed him; it is fated that he escape (μόριμον, “fated”, only here in Homer); the lineage of Dardanus must not perish “without seed and without a trace”, since Zeus loved Dardanus above all the children born to him from mortal women; and Zeus has come to hate the lineage of Priam (20.300–306; see [[dardanus]], [[priam]] and [[genee]]). So the rule passes to the other branch of the house: “now mighty Aeneas shall be lord over the Trojans, / and his children's children” (20.307–308; see [[might-of-aeneas]]). The Iliad tells no more. It does not say where or when Aeneas will rule, only that he will outlive Achilles: “But when Achilles has met his death and his fate, / then take heart, and fight among the foremost; / for no other of the Achaeans will slay you” (20.337–339; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).

## In the poem

**Two branches.** Aeneas has just told Achilles his lineage. Tros had three sons, and from Ilus came Laomedon, Priam and Hector, from Assaracus came Capys, Anchises and Aeneas (20.230–240; see [[assaracus]], [[capys]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]). Walter Leaf notes that Hector and Aeneas are both fourth in descent from Tros, “third cousins” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.240). Poseidon's prophecy turns the genealogy into a succession: the one line Zeus hates, the other will rule. ἄσπερμος, “without seed”, is found only here in Homer, and ἄφαντος, “without a trace”, was Agamemnon's wish for the whole people of Ilios (6.60).

**The honor of Priam.** Achilles had taunted Aeneas with the same hope:

{{quote:f7e2cbe5-7e82-5be2-a10f-d4172b8347c5}}

The honor and the domain are what the Lycians gave Bellerophon, “half of all his royal honor” and “a domain finer than all others, / a fair one, of orchard and plowland” (6.193–195; see [[bellerophon-in-lycia]]), and what Sarpedon says the Lycian kings hold in return for fighting among the foremost (12.310–321). Aristarchus athetized 180–186 as mean in style and thought and unworthy of Achilles; Leaf answers that Aeneas' pretensions to the crown are explained by the genealogy (on 20.180; see the translation's note on 20.180–186). In Book 13 Aeneas stood at the back of the throng, “always nursing wrath against brilliant Priam, / because, brave as he was among men, Priam did not honor him at all” (13.460–461; see [[aeneas]]). Leaf sees in these passages the traces of a legend of rivalry for the kingship of Troy between the two branches of the royal house, which he thinks may well rest on historical fact: a family claiming descent from Anchises that had at some time ousted, or ruled over, a branch claiming descent from Priam (on 13.460).

**The hatred of Zeus.** “For already the son of Cronus has come to hate the lineage of Priam” (20.306; see [[zeus]]). Leaf takes the aorist as a petulant word, “has come to hate”, referring to Zeus' recent decision to let the war take its course (on 20.306), and he counts the line as inconsistent with Zeus' attitude in the rest of the poem (on 20.293). In Book 22 Zeus pities Hector, who burned the thighs of many oxen for him on Ida and on the citadel (22.168–172), and in Book 24 he calls Hector the dearest to the gods of all the mortals in Ilios (24.66–70). The text prints ἔχθηρε without the augment, where Strabo quotes the line with ἤχθηρε (see the translation's note on 20.306–308).

**The fall of Troy.** The prophecy is answered by Hera's oath against the city. Hera, leaving Aeneas to Poseidon, says that she and Athena have sworn “never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans, / not even when all Troy blazes with ravening fire” (20.315–316; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). The city will burn, and Aeneas will rule the Trojans.

## Outside Homer

**The Hymn to Aphrodite.** Aphrodite, revealing herself to Anchises on Ida, promises him a son: σοὶ δʼ ἔσται φίλος υἱός, ὃς ἐν Τρώεσσιν ἀνάξει / καὶ παῖδες παίδεσσι διαμπερὲς ἐκγεγάοντες, “you will have a dear son, who will rule among the Trojans, and children born to his children in unbroken succession” (*Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* 196–197). She names him Aeneas from the “dread grief” (αἰνὸν ἄχος) that seized her for lying with a mortal (198–199; see [[aphrodite-and-anchises]]). Leaf thinks the Hymn's verses may be merely an echo of the Iliad's (on 13.460).

**Strabo.** Strabo sets Homer against the later stories of Aeneas: that he sailed away with Anchises and Ascanius and settled by Macedonian Olympus, or in Arcadia, or at Aegesta in Sicily, and came at last to Latium; or, as Demetrius of Scepsis held, that he reigned at Scepsis. Homer, he says, agrees with none of them, for he shows Aeneas staying in Troy, taking over the rule and handing it on to his children's children once the race of Priam's sons was gone. He quotes 306–308, and adds that some wrote Αἰνείαο γένος πάντεσσιν ἀνάξει, “the race of Aeneas shall rule over all”, meaning the Romans (*Geography* 13.1.53; see [[strabo-on-the-rule-of-aeneas]]). No manuscript of the Iliad has that reading (Leaf on 20.307; see the translation's note on 20.306–308).

**Virgil.** Virgil gives the prophecy to Apollo. At Delos his oracle sends the Trojans to seek their ancient mother: “hic domus Aeneae cunctis dominabitur oris / et nati natorum et qui nascentur ab illis”, “here the house of Aeneas shall rule over all shores, and his children's children, and those who will be born of them” (*Aeneid* 3.94–98). Leaf calls the Latin lines a translation of the Iliad's and infers that Virgil read γένωνται, “will be born”, as our text does (on 20.307). The rule over all shores agrees with the reading that Strabo reports (see [[virgil-the-house-of-aeneas]] and [[virgil]]).
