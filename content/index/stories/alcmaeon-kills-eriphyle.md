## The story

Amphiaraus, a seer, foresaw that the expedition of the Seven would end in disaster and refused to join it. But he had sworn to let his wife Eriphyle settle any dispute between him and Adrastus, and Polynices bribed her with the necklace of Harmonia; she sent her husband to Thebes, where the earth swallowed him with his chariot (Apollodorus 3.6.2–8; see [[the-seven-against-thebes]] and [[amphiaraus]]). The Odyssey remembers “hateful Eriphyle, / who took precious gold as the price of her own husband” (*Odyssey* 11.326–327), and the seer who “perished at Thebes, for the sake of gifts to a woman” (*Odyssey* 15.247). Amphiaraus had charged his sons to avenge him. Alcmaeon led the Epigoni against Thebes (see [[the-epigonoi]]), and afterward, on an oracle of Apollo, killed his mother. Pursued by her Furies, he wandered until he settled where the oracle sent him, on land that the sun had not yet seen when he killed his mother, the new ground laid down by the silt of the Achelous (Apollodorus 3.7.5; Thucydides 2.102.5–6; see [[alcmaeon]] and [[eriphyle]]).

Euripides wrote two plays on Alcmaeon. In the *Nicomachean Ethics* Aristotle calls absurd the reasons that “compelled” Euripides' Alcmaeon to kill his mother (3.1, 1110a27–29).

## Outside Homer

**The necklace.** Eriphyle's bribe became a byword. Plato's Socrates asks whether the man who enslaves the most divine part of himself to the most godless takes gold for a far more terrible ruin than Eriphyle, ἐπὶ τῇ τοῦ ἀνδρὸς ψυχῇ τὸν ὅρμον δεξαμένη, “who received the necklace for her husband's life” (*Republic* 9.589e–590a; see [[plato]]). Cicero gives her as the example of an argument that proves of a part what it promised to prove of the whole: *Mulierum genus avarum est; nam Eriphyla auro viri vitam vendidit*, “The race of women is greedy; for Eriphyle sold her husband's life for gold” (*On Invention* 1.94; see [[cicero]]). Horace counts the fall of the Argive seer's house among the works of gold, which loves to pass through the midst of guards and to break through rock more strongly than the thunderbolt: *concidit auguris / Argivi domus ob lucrum / demersa exitio*, “the house of the Argive augur fell, sunk in ruin for gain” (*Odes* 3.16.9–13; see [[horace]]).

**The son's deed.** In Ovid, Themis foretells the war of Thebes and its sequel: the seer will see his own shade while he is still alive, as the earth sinks beneath him, and his son, avenging parent on parent, will be *facto pius et sceleratus eodem*, “pious and wicked in the same deed”, driven out of his mind and his home by the faces of the Furies and the ghost of his mother, until his wife asks him for the fatal gold and a sword of the house of Phegeus, his kin by marriage, drains his side (*Metamorphoses* 9.403–412; see [[ovid]] and [[amphiaraus]]).
