## The story

**The appeal.** Telemachus has refused to send his mother away against her will and has told the suitors to leave his halls and feast on their own goods. If they would rather destroy one man's substance without payment, let them, “but I will call upon the gods who live forever, / if ever Zeus will grant that deeds of requital be done; / then, unavenged, you would perish inside my house” (*Odyssey* 2.130–145; see [[telemachus]], [[the-suitors]] and [[the-assembly-of-the-ithacans]]). They are the verses with which he warned the suitors in his own hall the evening before (*Odyssey* 1.378–380 = 2.143–145). Now, before the people, Zeus answers them:

**The reading.** The people are amazed at the birds and ponder in their hearts “what was to be fulfilled” (*Odyssey* 2.155–156; see [[thambos]]). Then the old hero Halitherses, son of Mastor, who alone surpassed those of his age “in knowing birds and in speaking words of fate”, speaks to the men of Ithaca, and above all to the suitors. Odysseus is already near, planting slaughter and doom for all of them; let the Ithacans consider how to stop them. He does not prophesy untried: when the Argives were embarking for Ilios he told Odysseus that he would come home in the twentieth year, after losing all his companions, unknown to all, “and now it is all being fulfilled” (*Odyssey* 2.157–176; see [[halitherses]] and [[the-prophecy-of-halitherses]]).

**The scorn.** Eurymachus, son of Polybus, answers for the suitors. Let the old man go home and prophesy to his own children; “Many are the birds that go to and fro under the rays of the sun, / and not all of them are birds of fate” (*Odyssey* 2.178–182; see [[eurymachus]] and [[birds]]). Odysseus has perished, the seer is after a gift, and the suitors will lay a fine on him if he stirs Telemachus up; they fear no one, “nor do we heed the oracle that you, old sir, / pronounce, never to be fulfilled” (*Odyssey* 2.182–202). Nobody answers him about the birds. Telemachus says that he will speak of these things no more, “for the gods know them already, and all the Achaeans”, and asks for a ship to go to Sparta and Pylos (*Odyssey* 2.208–223; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).

## In the poem

**Zeus' answer.** The eagles come “for him” (τῷ, *Odyssey* 2.146), the moment Telemachus has called on Zeus, as the eagles of the Iliad answer the prayers of Agamemnon and Priam (*Iliad* 8.245–252, 24.314–321; see [[surest-of-birds]] and [[eagle]]). Telemachus asked for requital, not for a sign, and Zeus gives the sign of the requital. It is the second sign of the assembly: the first was a word of omen, the old man's wish that Zeus might bring some good to the man who had called them, at which Telemachus rejoiced (*Odyssey* 2.33–35; see [[aegyptius]] and [[divination]]). It is also the only bird omen in Homer that comes as a pair.

**What the birds do.** The narrator gives the flight in its stages, and every stage is part of the sign. The eagles come from on high, from the peak of a mountain, as the eagle of Helen's reading comes “from the mountain” to snatch the tame goose and the eagle of Penelope's dream comes from the mountain to kill her geese (*Odyssey* 15.175, 19.538, plain renderings); they fly with the wind, side by side, until they are over the middle of the assembly; there they wheel and shake their wings, look down at the heads of all, and “foresaw destruction” (ὄσσοντο δʼ ὄλεθρον, *Odyssey* 2.152), the verb of the Trojan horses that “foresaw sorrows in their hearts” when Achilles shouted from the trench (*Iliad* 18.224) and of the suitor Amphinomus, whose heart forebodes evil (*Odyssey* 18.154). Then they tear each other's cheeks and necks with their talons. The middle participle, δρυψαμένω, may mean that each tore the other or itself; to tear the cheeks is elsewhere the gesture of mourning, as the wife of Protesilaus was left in Phylace, “her cheeks torn in mourning” (*Iliad* 2.700; also 11.393; see [[lament]]). Last, they dart away “on the right across the houses and the city of the people” (*Odyssey* 2.154). The right is the side of good omen, toward the dawn and the sun, as Hector names it when he scorns the birds (*Iliad* 12.238–240); Walter Leaf compares this flight with the eagle that answers Priam, “darting on the right across the city” (*Iliad* 24.320; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.320; see [[wings-as-wide-as-a-door]]). The sign goes on the right for Telemachus, for whom it came; the destruction it foresees hangs over the heads it looked down on, and Halitherses foretells evil not for the suitors only but “for many others too” of the Ithacans (*Odyssey* 2.165–167). The narrator does not explain the sign further, and neither does Halitherses, who declares what it means without saying what the two birds stand for.

**The reader and the scorner.** The scene has the shape of the Iliad's portent at the ditch: a sign from Zeus, a reading, and a scornful answer. There an eagle on the left drops a serpent among the Trojans, Polydamas reads it as a warning, and Hector answers that he does not care whether birds go to the right or the left, and that “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (*Iliad* 12.200–243; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]] and [[one-omen-is-best]]). Here the reader is a seer for whom the narrator vouches (*Odyssey* 2.158–159), and the scorner a suitor who will die by Odysseus' arrow (*Odyssey* 22.79–88). The Odyssey later gives the same verse to Halitherses that the Iliad gives Polydamas, “for he alone saw before and after” (*Odyssey* 24.452; *Iliad* 18.250; see [[before-and-after]]).

**The first of the signs.** The omen begins a series of bird signs that announce the return, each read at once by someone who sees it. As Telemachus leaves Sparta an eagle flies by on the right carrying a tame goose, and Helen reads it in Halitherses' terms, that Odysseus will come home and take vengeance, or is already at home, “and plants evil for all the suitors” (*Odyssey* 15.160–178, a plain rendering; compare φυτεύει, *Odyssey* 2.165); as he lands on Ithaca a hawk tears a dove on the right, and the seer Theoclymenus reads it as the kingship of his line (*Odyssey* 15.525–534); in Penelope's dream an eagle kills her twenty geese and then tells her that it is her husband (*Odyssey* 19.535–553); and an eagle on the left with a dove in its talons turns the suitors from their plot to kill Telemachus (*Odyssey* 20.240–247). The first is the only one shown to an assembly, and the only one that is scorned (see [[divination]] and [[prophecy]]).
