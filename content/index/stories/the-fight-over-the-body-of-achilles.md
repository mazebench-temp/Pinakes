## The story

**The fight.** Homer tells it in the last book of the Odyssey, where the shade of Agamemnon greets the shade of Achilles in the underworld: “Blessed son of Peleus, godlike Achilles, who died in Troy far from Argos”; around him the best sons of the Trojans and the Achaeans were killed, fighting over him, while he lay “in the whirl of dust, great over a great space, forgetful of his horsemanship”, and they fought the whole day, and would never have stopped had Zeus not stopped the battle with a storm (*Odyssey* 24.36–42, a plain rendering; see [[achilles]] and [[agamemnon]]). The verse of the body in the dust is the verse of Cebriones, Hector's charioteer, over whom the Trojans and the Achaeans fought in the Iliad (16.775–776; see [[cebriones]]), and Achilles had lain so, stretched out in the dust, in his grief for Patroclus (18.26–27).

**Odysseus' part.** Homer gives it once, in Odysseus' wish in the storm: he would rather have died “on that day when, in their greatest numbers, bronze-fitted spears / the Trojans hurled at me around the dead son of Peleus” (*Odyssey* 5.309–310; see [[odysseus]] and [[trojans]]). W. W. Merry adds what Homer does not say: in the struggle for the corpse Ajax bore off the body on his shoulders, while Odysseus kept the Trojans at bay (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.309; see [[ajax]]).

**The funeral.** The Achaeans carried the body to the ships, washed it and laid it on a bier; Thetis came out of the sea with the immortal sea goddesses, and Nestor kept the frightened army from flight; the daughters of the Old Man of the Sea dressed him in immortal clothing and the nine Muses sang the lament. They mourned him seventeen days and nights, burned him on the eighteenth, laid his bones in a golden urn with those of Patroclus, heaped a mound over them on a headland above the Hellespont, and Thetis set out prizes for games (*Odyssey* 24.43–92, plain renderings; see [[thetis]], [[muses]], [[patroclus]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). “So not even in death did you lose your name”, the shade of Agamemnon ends (*Odyssey* 24.93, a plain rendering; see [[kleos]]).

**The arms.** The quarrel over his armor followed. Thetis set it as a prize, the sons of the Trojans and Pallas Athena judged, and Odysseus won it from Ajax, a victory he wishes he had never won (*Odyssey* 11.543–551; see [[the-judgment-of-arms]]).

**The death.** The Iliad foresees it: the dying Hector tells Achilles that Paris and Phoebus Apollo will kill him at the Scaean gates (22.359–360; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]] and [[scaean-gates]]). Homer never tells it. In the Odyssey Nestor remembers that Achilles lies at Troy with Ajax, Patroclus and Antilochus (*Odyssey* 3.109–112), and the shade of Agamemnon that he died in Troy, far from Argos (*Odyssey* 24.37).

## Outside Homer

Ovid gives the carrying of the body to Ulysses. Pleading against Ajax for the arms in the *Metamorphoses*, Ulysses says that neither tears nor grief nor fear kept him from lifting the body of Achilles from the ground, *his umeris, his inquam, umeris ego corpus Achillis / et simul arma tuli*, “on these shoulders, on these, I say, I carried the body of Achilles, and his arms with it” (*Metamorphoses* 13.280–285; see [[ovid]]).
