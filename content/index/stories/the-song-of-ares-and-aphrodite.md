## The story

The young Phaeacians have danced around the singer, and Odysseus has marveled at the flashing of their feet (*Odyssey* 8.262–265; see [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[dance]]). Then Demodocus begins:

{{quote:d0424b5d-a66b-50db-bb46-9337879e2822}}

**The snare.** When Hephaestus hears “the heart-grieving tale”, he goes to his smithy, “brooding evil deep in his mind”, sets the great anvil on its block and forges bonds “unbreakable, not to be loosed”. He pours them all around the posts of his bed and down from the roof beam, “fine as spider's webs, which no one could even see, / not even one of the blessed gods” (*Odyssey* 8.272–281; see [[hephaestus]], [[helios]], [[the-bonds-of-hephaestus]] and [[fine-as-spiders-webs]]). Then he makes as if to go to Lemnos, “the well-built citadel, / which is by far the dearest to him of all lands” (*Odyssey* 8.283–284; see [[lemnos]]).

**The capture.** Ares of the golden reins keeps no blind watch: as soon as he sees Hephaestus going away, he comes to the house, “eager for the love of well-crowned Cytherea”. She has just come from her father, the son of Cronus; he takes her hand and bids her come to bed, for Hephaestus has gone to Lemnos, “to the Sintians of savage speech” (*Odyssey* 8.285–294; see [[ares]], [[aphrodite]] and [[sintians]]). The bonds pour around them as they sleep, “and they could not move or lift any of their limbs. / And then indeed they knew that there was no more escape” (*Odyssey* 8.295–299). The Sun has kept watch for Hephaestus and told him, and the god turns back before he reaches Lemnos, stands in the doorway in a savage anger, and calls out to the gods:

{{quote:9f5bf51a-c958-5959-a8c9-ff73e4e0ebf4}}

**The laughter.** The gods gather to the bronze-floored house, Poseidon, Hermes and Apollo, “But the goddesses, being female, stayed each at home for shame”; and unquenchable laughter rises among the blessed gods as they see the craft of Hephaestus (*Odyssey* 8.321–327; see [[olympians]], [[laughter-of-the-gods]] and [[unquenchable-laughter]]). One says to his neighbor, “Evil deeds do not prosper; the slow overtakes the swift”: Hephaestus, slow as he is, has caught Ares, the swiftest of the gods, “and so he owes the adulterer's fine” (*Odyssey* 8.328–332). Then Apollo puts his question to Hermes:

{{quote:bbd52fa5-1e8d-54ac-9976-4dddcc5cdb3d}}

**Poseidon's surety.** Only Poseidon does not laugh. He keeps begging Hephaestus to set Ares free, and promises that Ares will pay all that is due in the presence of the gods. Hephaestus refuses: “pledges for the worthless are worthless things even to accept. / How could I bind you among the immortal gods, / if Ares should go off, escaping both the debt and the bonds?” Poseidon promises to pay it himself if Ares runs away, and Hephaestus answers that it is not possible, nor would it be fitting, to refuse his word (*Odyssey* 8.344–358; see [[poseidon]] and [[pledges-for-the-worthless]]).

**The release.** The might of Hephaestus looses the bonds, and the two spring up at once: Ares goes to Thrace, and laughter-loving Aphrodite to Cyprus, to Paphos, where she has a precinct and a fragrant altar; there the Graces wash her and anoint her with immortal oil and clothe her in lovely garments, “a wonder to see” (*Odyssey* 8.359–366; see [[thrace]], [[cyprus]], [[paphos]], [[graces]] and [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]]).

## In the poem

**The song in full.** Of Demodocus' three songs the first and the last are told in a few verses of reported speech; this one is given at length, and W. W. Merry, following Gladstone, finds it fitted to its place: the graver contests have been relieved by the dancers, the singer who sang the grave lay of the quarrel tunes his lyre to a merrier theme, and in the vein most characteristic of the Phaeacians the poem gives an actual specimen of his art (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.267; see [[demodocus]] and [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]]). It is the one song of the three at which Odysseus does not weep: “but Odysseus / was delighted in his heart as he listened, and so were the others, / the Phaeacians of the long oars” (*Odyssey* 8.367–369; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]).

**Hephaestus' wife.** In the Iliad the wife of Hephaestus is Charis, “the lovely one, whom the renowned god, lame in both legs, had married” (*Iliad* 18.382–383; see [[charis-wife-of-hephaestus]]), and Hesiod gives him Aglaia, “youngest of the Graces” (*Theogony* 945–946; see [[hesiod]]). The scholia say that Homer does not know Hephaestus as the husband of Aphrodite but of Charis, and that Demodocus made the story up himself; Merry accepts that there were separate myths of the god's partner, and reports Nitzsch's remark that the same idea lies beneath both, the union of a goddess of grace with the god of the highest art (Merry on 8.267). In the song the Graces bathe Aphrodite at Paphos (*Odyssey* 8.364).

**Ares and Aphrodite in the Iliad.** The Iliad nowhere calls them lovers, but it keeps them together. Wounded by Diomedes, Aphrodite begs her “dear brother” Ares for his horses, and he gives them to her (*Iliad* 5.355–363; see [[diomedes-wounds-aphrodite]]); in the battle of the gods she takes the felled Ares by the hand and leads him away (*Iliad* 21.416–417; see [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]). Gladstone, as Merry reports, saw in the song an “undesigned coincidence” that gives point to that last trait (on 8.267). In Hesiod, Cytherea bears to Ares Fear and Terror and Harmonia (*Theogony* 933–937).

**The lame god and the swift.** Hephaestus blames his lameness on his two parents, “who I wish had never begotten me” (*Odyssey* 8.311–312), as in the Iliad he tells how his “dog-faced mother” wished to hide him away because he was lame (*Iliad* 18.395–397; see [[fall-of-hephaestus]]); he calls Aphrodite “the dog-faced girl” (*Odyssey* 8.319; see [[dog-face]]). Lemnos, the island he pretends to visit, is where he fell when Zeus hurled him from heaven, and the Sintians took care of him there (*Iliad* 1.590–594; see [[lemnos]] and [[sintians]]). The gods' unquenchable laughter is the verse of *Iliad* 1, where they laugh at Hephaestus himself as he bustles through the halls pouring their nectar (*Iliad* 1.597–600; *Odyssey* 8.326); here they laugh at his captives and at his craft. Their verdict, that the slow overtakes the swift, turns the lameness into his victory (see [[evil-deeds-do-not-prosper]]).

**A god's marriage.** Hephaestus' claim is a husband's. He will hold the lovers until her father gives back the whole bride-price he paid for her (*Odyssey* 8.317–320; see [[bride-price]] and [[zeus]]), and the gods' word for what Ares owes is μοιχάγρια, “the adulterer's fine”, found only here in Homer (*Odyssey* 8.332; see the translation's key words and [[evil-deeds-do-not-prosper]]). The settlement is made by a pledge, and the pledge is Poseidon's (*Odyssey* 8.344–358).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates would not let the young hear of the binding of Ares and Aphrodite by Hephaestus, any more than of Zeus' desire for Hera, among the stories that teach no self-control (*Republic* 390c; see [[plato-zeus-deceived]]). Proclus answered him with an allegory in which Ares divides the opposites of the cosmos, Hephaestus crafts its visible order, both need Aphrodite's harmony, and the bonds, which can be loosed below the moon, are the craftsman's reasons that bind opposites together; Poseidon has them loosed so that birth and death may go on forever (*On the Republic* I 140–143 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). Virgil and Ovid retold the story. Its later readers are gathered under [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite-in-later-writers]].
