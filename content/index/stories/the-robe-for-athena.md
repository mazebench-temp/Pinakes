## The story

**The plan.** The Trojans are giving way before Diomedes, “that savage spearman, mighty master of rout”, whom the seer Helenus calls the strongest of the Achaeans (6.97–98; see [[helenus]] and [[master-of-rout]]). Helenus tells Hector to go to the city and tell their mother to gather the old women (γεραιαί) at the temple of gray-eyed Athena on the height of the city, open its doors with the key, and lay on the goddess's knees the robe that seems to her the loveliest and largest in her hall and the dearest to her heart; and to promise twelve cows, “yearlings, never touched by the goad”, if Athena will pity the city and the wives of the Trojans and their little children, and keep the son of Tydeus from sacred Ilios (6.86–97).

**Hector's message.** Hector goes, and gives his mother the message in Helenus' words (6.269–278). He will not pour a libation himself: he has come from the fighting, and “with unwashed hands I shrink from pouring sparkling wine to Zeus” (6.266–268; see [[hand-washing]] and [[libation]]). He sends Hecuba to the temple and goes to find Paris (6.279–280).

**The robe.** Hecuba sends her handmaids to gather the old women through the city, and goes down herself into the fragrant chamber (6.286–288):

{{quote:ee8d70a6-1700-5b5d-9f29-89bc6c3e804f}}

She takes the one “most beautiful in its embroidery and the largest”, which “shone like a star” and lay beneath the others, and carries it to Athena, with the old women hurrying after her (6.293–296; see [[like-a-star]] and [[robe]]).

**The temple.** Theano, the daughter of Cisseus and wife of Antenor, opens the doors, for “the Trojans had made her priestess of Athena” (6.297–300; see [[theano]] and [[priesthood]]). The women lift their hands to the goddess with the ritual cry, the ὀλολυγή, and Theano lays the robe on Athena's knees and prays:

{{quote:6d83ccd3-0ec9-5127-93d6-ddbc7cd049d1}}

“So they prayed to the daughter of great Zeus” (6.312), and the poem leaves them praying and follows Hector to the house of Paris.

## In the poem

**Three times the same words.** The rite is described three times: as Helenus plans it (6.86–97), as Hector repeats it to his mother (6.269–278), and as it is done (6.286–311), in many of the same lines (see [[message-repeated]]). The changes count. Hector adds “burnt offerings” (θύεα, 6.270). Helenus asked that Athena “hold the son of Tydeus back” from the city (ἀπόσχῃ, 6.96); Theano asks more, that she break his spear and let him fall headlong before the Scaean gates (6.306–307). The vow of twelve yearling cows is repeated each time (6.93–94, 6.274–275, 6.308–309; see [[cattle]] and [[sacrifice]]).

**The refusal.** ἀνένευε δὲ Παλλὰς Ἀθήνη, “but Pallas Athena shook her head” (6.311). ἀνανεύω is to throw the head back, the Greek gesture of refusal, the opposite of the nod of assent (see [[neuo]]). Zeus uses it when he grants Achilles one part of his prayer for Patroclus and refuses the other (16.250–252). The narrator gives the refusal in the same line that closes the prayer, and the women do not know it. The line is built like the one that ends a prayer granted, “So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him” (ὣς ἔφατʼ εὐχόμενος, τοῦ δʼ ἔκλυε Παλλὰς Ἀθήνη, *Odyssey* 3.385; in the plural at 10.295), with a refusal in place of hearing. Both of those prayers vow her a yearling heifer, as the Trojan women do (see [[cattle]]). So too Zeus received Agamemnon's offerings on this same day but did not grant his prayer (2.419–420). Athena's answer was never in doubt. She is the Trojans' enemy, and has been since the Judgment of Paris (24.25–30; see [[athena]]), and in Book 5 she stood beside Diomedes in his chariot against Ares (5.835–863). The Trojans ask a goddess to strike down the man whom she has been helping all day.

**The protector of the city.** Theano addresses her as ἐρυσίπτολις, “protector of the city” (6.305; see [[protector-of-the-city]]). The Trojans call Athena by the title of a city's guardian, and she has a temple on their citadel with a priestess appointed by the people (6.297–300; see [[temple]]). She will not guard this city.

**The robe from Sidon.** The finest robe the queen owns came from the voyage on which Paris brought Helen to Troy (6.290–292; see [[the-abduction-of-helen]], [[sidon]] and [[sidonians]]). The offering meant to save the city is a spoil of the voyage that brought the war upon it.

**Women's worship.** The rite belongs to the women. Hecuba, the old women and the priestess carry it out while the men fight on the plain, and Hector, spattered with blood, will not even pour a libation (6.266–268). The prayers of the Trojan women frame Hector's visit: at the Scaean gates he tells the wives and daughters of the Trojans to pray to the gods, “and sorrows were in store for many” (6.240–241; see [[trojan-women]] and [[hectors-visit-to-troy]]). When he looks for Andromache, he asks whether she has gone to the temple of Athena, “where the other Trojan women with lovely tresses are appeasing the dread goddess” (6.379–380).

**Diomedes and the Scaean gates.** Theano's prayer is not granted. Diomedes does not fall before the Scaean gates: he is wounded in the foot by Paris' arrow in Book 11 and leaves the fighting (11.369–400), and in the Odyssey he comes safely home (*Odyssey* 3.180–182). The man who will fall at the Scaean gates is Achilles, killed by Paris and Apollo, as Hector foretells as he dies (22.359–360).

## Outside Homer

Strabo, describing the image of Athena at Ilion in his own day, notes that it stands, and that Homer's “on the knees” shows the old image to have been seated; he names seated images of Athena at Phocaea, Massalia, Rome and Chios (13.1.41; see [[strabo]]). Modern readers have often compared the Athenian custom of bringing a newly woven robe to the ancient image of Athena at the Panathenaea. The Trojan robe is an old one from the queen's store, and whether the Iliad's scene reflects any real festival cannot be shown (see [[the-robe-and-the-panathenaea]]).

In the temple of Juno at Carthage, Aeneas sees the war at Troy painted on the walls, and among the scenes the Trojan women going with loosened hair to the temple of unfriendly Pallas, carrying the robe and beating their breasts, while the goddess keeps her eyes fixed on the ground, turned away ([[virgil|Virgil]], *Aeneid* 1.479–482). Later in the poem the Latin mothers go up with their queen to the temple of Pallas on the citadel and pray in Theano's words against Aeneas: let her break the spear of the Phrygian pirate and throw him down headlong before their gates (*Aeneid* 11.477–485; see [[virgils-stallion]]).
