## The story

**The warnings.** In the land of the dead the seer Teiresias tells Odysseus that he and his companions may still reach Ithaca, though they suffer, if he will restrain his own heart and theirs when they find the cattle and fat sheep of the Sun grazing on the island of [[thrinacia|Thrinacia]]: left unharmed, the herds let them come home; harmed, they bring destruction on the ship and the companions, and a late and lonely homecoming for Odysseus, in another man's ship, to a house full of trouble (*Odyssey* 11.104–117; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]] and [[who-sees-all-things]]). Circe, herself a daughter of the Sun (*Odyssey* 10.138), repeats the warning in the seer's verses and describes the herds: seven herds of cattle and as many flocks of sheep, fifty in each, which bear no young and never die, tended by the nymphs Phaethusa and Lampetie, whom Neaera bore to the Sun (*Odyssey* 12.127–141; see [[circe]], [[hyperion]], [[phaethusa]] and [[neaera]]).

**Thrinacia.** After Scylla and Charybdis the ship comes to the island, and Odysseus, hearing the cattle low, bids his men row past. Eurylochus answers for the weary crew that they must have their supper on shore, the others agree, and Odysseus makes them swear not to touch the herds but to eat the food that Circe gave (*Odyssey* 12.260–304; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]] and [[eurylochus]]). That night Zeus raises a storm, and for a month the winds hold them on the island. While the bread and wine last the men keep from the cattle; when the stores are gone they hunt fish and birds, and hunger wears at them (*Odyssey* 12.305–332).

**The crime.** Odysseus goes up the island to pray for a way home, and the gods pour sleep on his eyelids. Eurylochus persuades the companions that death by hunger is the most pitiable of all: they will drive off the best of the Sun's cattle and sacrifice them to the gods, vow the Sun a temple if they reach Ithaca, and if he destroys the ship they would rather drown at once than waste away on a desert island. They sacrifice the cattle with oak leaves in place of barley and water in place of wine (*Odyssey* 12.333–365; see [[sacrifice-type-scene]] and [[better-to-perish-once-and-for-all]]). Odysseus wakes to the savor of the fat, and cries out to Zeus and the gods that they lulled him to his blind folly (*Odyssey* 12.366–373; see [[ate]]).

**The Sun's anger.** Lampetie brings the Sun the news, and he demands that the gods punish the companions of Odysseus, or he will go down to Hades and shine among the dead; Zeus promises to shatter their ship with his thunderbolt. Odysseus heard this, he says, from Calypso, who heard it from Hermes (*Odyssey* 12.374–390; see [[helios]], [[zeus]] and [[lampetie]]). The gods show portents: the hides crawl, and the meat lows on the spits. For six days the companions feast; on the seventh they put to sea, and Zeus strikes the ship with his thunderbolt in the open sea, “and the god took away their homecoming” (*Odyssey* 12.391–419). Odysseus alone, astride the keel and mast, is carried back to Charybdis and at last to Calypso (*Odyssey* 12.420–450; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and [[calypso]]).

## In the poem

**The proem.** Of all the adventures of the wanderings the proem names this one only. Odysseus could not save his companions, “for they perished through their own recklessness, / fools, who ate up the cattle of Hyperion the Sun; / and he took away from them the day of their homecoming” (*Odyssey* 1.7–9; see [[atasthalie]], [[nepios]] and [[nostos]]). The telling in *Odyssey* 12 bears the proem out in its words. Odysseus makes his men swear not to kill a cow or a sheep “in evil recklessness” (ἀτασθαλίῃσι κακῇσιν, *Odyssey* 12.300), and after the thunderbolt “the god took away their homecoming” (θεὸς δʼ ἀποαίνυτο νόστον, *Odyssey* 12.419). In the proem the one who takes it is the Sun himself; in the story it is Zeus who strikes, at the Sun's demand. The crime also explains why Odysseus is alone when the poem begins, held by Calypso while all the others who escaped are at home (*Odyssey* 1.11–15).

**The companions.** The first adventure Odysseus tells has the same shape. At Ismarus he urged his men to flee, but “they, the great fools, would not obey”, and drank wine and slaughtered sheep and cattle by the shore until the Cicones came on them with their neighbors (*Odyssey* 9.43–53; see [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]]). Eurylochus, who leads the crime on Thrinacia, is the man who said that the companions killed in the Cyclops' cave perished by their leader's recklessness (*Odyssey* 10.435–437; see [[eurylochus]], [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]] and [[odysseus-and-circe]]). And the crime is done while Odysseus sleeps, as the skin of the winds was opened while he slept (*Odyssey* 10.31–47; see [[odysseus-and-aeolus]]). On Thrinacia the companions are starving, and they break their oath with a sacrifice and the vow of a temple; the proem still calls it their own recklessness.

**The prophecy fulfilled.** Teiresias' words come true in order. The companions perish, and Odysseus comes home late, alone, in another man's ship, the Phaeacians' (see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]), and finds overbearing men eating up his livelihood and wooing his wife (*Odyssey* 11.112–117; see [[the-suitors]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). In his false tale to Penelope the stranger says that Odysseus lost his companions and his ship coming from Thrinacia, because Zeus and the Sun were angry with him, “for his companions had killed his cattle” (*Odyssey* 19.273–277, a plain rendering). The verb of their anger, ὀδύσαντο, plays on the name of Odysseus, as Athena's ὠδύσαο does in Book 1, “Why are you so angry with him, Zeus?” (*Odyssey* 1.62; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.62). When at last he tells Penelope his own wanderings, the killing of the Sun's cattle and the thunderbolt are among them (*Odyssey* 23.329–333).

## Outside Homer

**Aristotle.** The Homeric problems asked about the herds. Aristotle took the numbers as a statement of nature: seven herds of fifty make three hundred and fifty, the days of the twelve lunar months, and the herds neither breed nor die because the number of such days stays always the same; the scholia that report his reading add that among the ancients it was an accursed thing to kill a plowing ox, and that whoever did so suffered at the hands of men or gods, as the companions of Odysseus did (*Homeric Problems* fr. 398 Gigon, from the scholia and Eustathius on *Odyssey* 12.129–130). The second problem was the messenger: why does the Sun, who sees all things and hears all things, need Lampetie to tell him of his own cattle? Aristotle answered that the Sun sees everything, but not all at once, and that being then in another part of his course he did not see what was done on Thrinacia; or that Lampetie reports to him as sight reports to a man (*Homeric Problems* fr. 373 Gigon, from the scholia on *Iliad* 3.277, Agamemnon's oath to the Sun “who sees all things and hears all things”; see [[aristotle]] and [[who-sees-all-things]]).

**Euripides.** In the *Trojan Women* Cassandra foretells the sufferings of Odysseus: dread Charybdis in her narrow strait, the Cyclops, Circe who turns men into swine, shipwrecks, the lotus, “and the holy cattle of the Sun, / which will one day utter a voice from their flesh, / a bitter cry for Odysseus” (*Trojan Women* 435–441; see [[euripides]]).

**Herodotus.** Herodotus tells of sheep sacred to the Sun at Apollonia on the Ionian Gulf, which grazed by day along a river and were guarded at night in a cave by citizens chosen for their wealth and birth, each for a year. When the guard Euenius fell asleep, wolves came into the cave and killed some sixty of the sheep; the Apollonians blinded him, and their flocks bore no young and their land no crops, until the oracles of Dodona and Delphi told them that the gods themselves had sent the wolves, and that Euenius must have the recompense he chose (*Histories* 9.93). Merry sets beside the lowing meat another portent of his: at Sestos, salt fish roasting on a fire leapt and gasped like fish newly caught, and the Persian Artayctes, who had plundered the shrine of Protesilaus, read it as a sign that the dead hero had power from the gods to punish the wrongdoer (*Histories* 9.120; Merry on 12.395; see [[herodotus]]).

**Strabo.** Strabo, who held that Homer set the wanderings of Odysseus in real places around Sicily and Italy and adorned them with poetry, counts “the cattle of the Sun” among the poet's plain marvels, with Oceanus and Hades, the hospitality of goddesses, the size of the Cyclopes and the shape of Scylla, which no one should take for history (*Geography* 1.2.11). He also explains that Sicily, three-cornered in shape, was first called Trinacria and later, for the sake of the sound, Thrinacia (*Geography* 6.2.1).
