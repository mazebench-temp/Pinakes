## The story

**The warnings.** In the underworld the seer Teiresias tells Odysseus that he and his companions may still reach Ithaca, though they suffer, if he will restrain his own spirit and theirs when they come to the island of Thrinacia and find grazing there the cattle and fat sheep of Helios, “who sees all things and hears all things”. If they leave them unharmed and mind their homecoming, they will reach home; if they harm them, he foretells destruction for the ship and the companions, and Odysseus, if he escapes, will come home late and in misery, having lost all his companions, in another man's ship, and will find trouble in his house, overbearing men eating up his livelihood and wooing his wife (*Odyssey* 11.104–117; see [[who-sees-all-things]]). Circe repeats the warning in the same words and describes the herds: seven herds of cattle and as many flocks of sheep, fifty in each, which bear no young and never die, tended by two goddesses, the nymphs Phaethusa and Lampetie, whom Neaera bore to Helios Hyperion (*Odyssey* 12.127–141; see [[circe]] and [[hyperion]]).

**Thrinacia.** After Scylla and Charybdis the ship comes to the island, and Odysseus hears the cattle lowing in their pens and the sheep bleating. He tells his men the warnings and orders them to row past, but Eurylochus answers that they are worn out and asks for supper on shore, and the others agree. Odysseus sees that a god is planning evil, and makes them swear not to kill a cow or a sheep in wicked recklessness, but to eat the food that Circe gave (*Odyssey* 12.260–304; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). In the night Zeus raises a storm, and for a whole month the south wind blows, and the east, and no other. While the bread and wine last the men keep from the cattle; when the stores are gone they hunt fish and birds with bent hooks, and hunger wears at them (*Odyssey* 12.305–332).

**The crime.** Odysseus goes inland to pray to the gods for a way home, and they pour sweet sleep on his eyelids (*Odyssey* 12.333–338). Eurylochus persuades the companions: every death is hateful, but to die of hunger is the most pitiable; let them drive off the best of the Sun's cattle and sacrifice to the immortals, and if they reach Ithaca they will build Helios a rich temple; and if he is angry and wants to destroy the ship, he would rather lose his life once in the waves than waste away on a desert island (*Odyssey* 12.339–351). They drive off the best of the cattle, which are grazing near the ship, and sacrifice them with oak leaves in place of the white barley they no longer have, and pour water for the wine (*Odyssey* 12.352–365; see [[sacrifice-type-scene]]). Odysseus wakes, and as he comes near the ship the savor of the fat reaches him. He cries out to Zeus and the other gods that they lulled him to his ruin with pitiless sleep, while his companions contrived a monstrous deed (*Odyssey* 12.366–373).

**The Sun's anger.** Lampetie brings Helios the news, and he demands that Zeus and the gods punish the companions of Odysseus, who killed his cattle in their insolence, the cattle he delighted in as he went up into the starry sky and turned back to earth; if they do not pay him a fitting recompense, he will go down to Hades and shine among the dead. Zeus tells him to go on shining for gods and men, and promises to strike their ship with his bright thunderbolt and shatter it in the middle of the wine-dark sea. Odysseus heard this, he says, from Calypso, who heard it from Hermes (*Odyssey* 12.374–390; see [[helios]], [[zeus]] and [[thunderbolt]]).

**The end of the companions.** The gods show portents: the hides crawl, and the meat lows on the spits, both roasted and raw, with a sound like the voice of cattle. For six days the companions feast on the best of the Sun's cattle, and on the seventh the wind falls and they put to sea. When no land is in sight Zeus sets a dark cloud over the ship; the west wind breaks the forestays, the mast falls and crushes the helmsman's skull, and Zeus strikes the ship with his thunderbolt. The men are thrown into the sea and carried on the waves like sea crows, and the god takes away their homecoming (*Odyssey* 12.391–419). Odysseus alone, astride the keel and mast lashed together, is carried back to Charybdis, and after nine days the gods bring him to Ogygia and Calypso (*Odyssey* 12.420–450; see [[calypso]] and [[ogygia]]).

## In the poem

**The proem.** Of all the adventures of the wanderings the proem names this one only. Odysseus could not save his companions, “for they perished through their own recklessness, / fools, who ate up the cattle of Hyperion the Sun; / and he took away from them the day of their homecoming” (*Odyssey* 1.7–9; see [[atasthalie]], [[nepios]] and [[nostos]]). The telling in *Odyssey* 12 bears the proem out in its words. Odysseus makes his men swear not to kill a cow or a sheep “in wicked recklessness” (ἀτασθαλίῃσι κακῇσιν, *Odyssey* 12.300), and after the thunderbolt “the god took away their homecoming” (θεὸς δʼ ἀποαίνυτο νόστον, *Odyssey* 12.419). In the proem the one who takes it is the Sun himself; in the story it is Zeus who strikes, at the Sun's demand. The crime also explains why Odysseus is alone when the poem begins, held by Calypso while all the others who escaped are at home (*Odyssey* 1.11–15).

**The companions.** The first adventure Odysseus tells has the same shape. At Ismarus he urged his men to flee, but “they, great fools, did not obey”, and drank wine and slaughtered sheep and cattle by the shore until the Cicones came on them with their neighbors (*Odyssey* 9.43–53). Eurylochus, who leads the crime on Thrinacia, is the man who said that the companions killed in the Cyclops' cave perished by their leader's recklessness (*Odyssey* 10.435–437). On Thrinacia the companions are starving, and they break their oath with a sacrifice and the vow of a temple; the proem still calls it their own recklessness.

**The prophecy fulfilled.** Teiresias' words come true in order. The companions perish, and Odysseus comes home late, alone, in another man's ship, the Phaeacians' (see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]), and finds overbearing men eating up his livelihood and wooing his wife (*Odyssey* 11.112–117; see [[the-suitors]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). In his false tale to Penelope the stranger says that Odysseus lost his companions and his ship coming from Thrinacia, because Zeus and Helios were angry with him, “for his companions had killed his cattle” (*Odyssey* 19.273–277). The verb of their anger, ὀδύσαντο, plays on the name of Odysseus, as Athena's ὠδύσαο does in Book 1, “Why are you so angry with him, Zeus?” (*Odyssey* 1.62; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.62). When at last he tells Penelope his own wanderings, the killing of the Sun's cattle and the thunderbolt are among them (*Odyssey* 23.329–333).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus tells of sheep sacred to the Sun at Apollonia on the Ionian Gulf, which grazed by day along a river and were guarded at night in a cave by citizens chosen for their wealth and birth, each for a year. When the guard Euenius fell asleep, wolves came into the cave and killed some sixty of the sheep; the Apollonians blinded him, and their flocks bore no young and their land no crops, until the oracles of Dodona and Delphi told them that the gods themselves had sent the wolves, and that Euenius must have the recompense he chose (*Histories* 9.93).

Strabo, who held that Homer set the wanderings of Odysseus in real places around Sicily and Italy and adorned them with poetry, counts “the cattle of the Sun” among the poet's plain marvels, with Oceanus and Hades, the hospitality of goddesses, the size of the Cyclopes and the shape of Scylla, which no one should take for history (*Geography* 1.2.11). He also explains that Sicily, three-cornered in shape, was first called Trinacria and later, for the sake of the sound, Thrinacia (*Geography* 6.2.1).
