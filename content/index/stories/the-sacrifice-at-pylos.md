## The story

**On the shore.** The sun rises as the ship from Ithaca comes to Pylos, “the well-built citadel of Neleus” (*Odyssey* 3.1–4; see [[pylos]] and [[pylians]]), and the people are at a sacrifice:

{{quote:02647c34-928b-5cfc-bdcc-e27ab9e69774}}

Nine companies of five hundred are 4,500 men, and nine bulls to a company are eighty-one bulls (see [[bulls]] and [[hecatomb]]). The ship puts in at the moment when the god's portion is on the fire: “When they had tasted the entrails and were burning the thigh-pieces on the altar for the god, / the others put straight in to land” (*Odyssey* 3.9–10; see [[thigh-pieces]] and [[sacrifice]]). Telemachus steps out of the ship behind Athena, and she tells him that he has no more need of shame and must go straight to Nestor (*Odyssey* 3.12–20; see [[telemachus]] and [[athena]]).

**The strangers' share.** They come to “the gathering and the seats of the Pylian men”, where Nestor sits with his sons while his companions make ready the feast, roasting meat and threading more on spits (*Odyssey* 3.31–33; see [[nestor]] and [[spits]]). All the Pylians come together to welcome the strangers. Nestor's son Peisistratus takes them both by the hand and seats them on soft fleeces on the sand beside his brother Thrasymedes and his father; he gives them portions of the entrails and pours wine into a golden cup (*Odyssey* 3.34–41; see [[peisistratus]], [[thrasymedes]], [[xenia]] and [[cup]]). He bids the older stranger pray to Poseidon, “for it is his feast you have come upon in coming here”, and gives the cup to her first, since the other is younger, of his own age (*Odyssey* 3.43–50). Athena prays for glory for Nestor and his sons, for a gracious return to all the Pylians for their hecatomb, and for a safe return for Telemachus and herself when they have done the business that brought them, “and she herself was bringing it all to pass” (*Odyssey* 3.55–62; see [[prayer]] and [[poseidon]]). Telemachus prays in the same way. They roast the outer flesh, divide the portions and feast, and when they have eaten their fill Nestor asks the strangers who they are (*Odyssey* 3.63–74; see [[feast]]).

**The end of the day.** Nestor's tales go on until the sun goes down (*Odyssey* 3.102–329). Then Athena tells the company to cut out the tongues and mix the wine, so that they may pour libations to Poseidon and the other immortals and think of bed, since “it is not fitting / to sit long at the feast of the gods, but to go home” (*Odyssey* 3.330–336). The heralds pour water over their hands, the young men fill the mixing bowls and serve the wine, and the tongues are thrown into the fire while the company stands and pours libations over them (*Odyssey* 3.338–342; see [[hand-washing]], [[mixing-bowl]] and [[libation]]). When Athena has left them in the likeness of a sea eagle, Nestor leads his sons and sons-in-law to his house (*Odyssey* 3.371–387; see [[phene]]).

## In the poem

**At the feast of the god.** The first welcome at Pylos is given inside a sacrifice. The strangers have come upon the feast of Poseidon (*Odyssey* 3.44), and a goddess in a mortal's likeness takes her share of it as a guest and prays to its god. Merry puts the double part in a sentence: as a seeming mortal she prays to a higher power, and as a goddess she answers the prayer herself (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.62). The god she prays to is the one who rages against Odysseus (*Odyssey* 1.19–21), and among her requests is a safe return for Odysseus' son. In the first book Poseidon was away at the ends of the earth, sharing in a hecatomb of bulls and rams among the Ethiopians, while the other gods decided Odysseus' return without him (*Odyssey* 1.22–27; see [[ethiopians]] and [[the-council-of-the-gods]]); the next hecatomb that the poem shows is his too, on the shore at Pylos. The next morning Nestor offers a heifer to Athena, “who came to me in plain sight at the rich feast of the god” (*Odyssey* 3.420), and the second sacrifice of the book answers the first (see [[the-sacrifice-to-athena]]).

**Poseidon's people.** The poem does not say why the Pylians sacrifice to Poseidon. Merry points to the god's kinship with Nestor's house: Neleus was Poseidon's son by Tyro (*Odyssey* 11.235–254; Merry on 3.6; see [[tyro]] and [[neleus]]). In Nestor's story of his youth the Pylians, camped by the Alpheus before a battle, offered sacrifices to Zeus, a bull to the river and a bull to Poseidon, and to Athena a cow from the herd (*Iliad* 11.727–729). The same two gods receive the sacrifices of this book: bulls for Poseidon on the shore, and a heifer for Athena at Nestor's house (see [[cattle]]). The bulls are all black, in a verse that gives the god his own dark epithet, κυανοχαίτης, “dark-haired” (*Odyssey* 3.6; see [[dark-haired]], and [[bulls]] for the color of the victims).

**A public sacrifice.** All Pylos takes part, seated in nine companies, and Nestor sits among them with his sons; the narrator names no one who performs the rite. Merry reports Lobeck's observation that in the only two public sacrifices in Homer, this one and the feast of Apollo on Ithaca on the day of the contest of the bow, the sacrificer is not named, and he calls the next morning's sacrifice, which Nestor begins himself, a household rite (Merry on 3.444; *Odyssey* 20.276–278, 21.258–259). The nine companies answer to the nine towns of Nestor's kingdom, as the scholia observed, and the 4,500 men to the crews of his ninety ships at fifty men a ship, the number of Philoctetes' crews (Merry on 3.7; *Iliad* 2.591–602, 2.719; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 3.7).

**The tongues.** The tongues that Athena bids them cut are those of the morning's bulls (*Odyssey* 3.332). The translation has “cut out”; Merry takes τάμνετε as “cut up” for the offering, as in the Attic phrase ἡ γλῶσσα χωρὶς τέμνεται, “the tongue is cut separately”, so that the tongues had been cut out and laid aside before. They were burned at the last moment for Poseidon, in whose honor the whole day had been kept, as Nitzsch held, and not for Hermes, to whom the Phaeacians pour the last libation before bed (*Odyssey* 7.136–138); the scholiast's reason is that the tongue is the finest part of the victim (Merry on 3.332).

## Outside Homer

Strabo, who held that Nestor's Pylos was the one in Triphylia, placed the sacrifice at the sanctuary of Samian Poseidon at Samicum, the god's most honored sanctuary there, a grove full of wild olives to which all the Triphylians contributed (*Geography* 8.3.13). “This is the sanctuary”, he writes, “in which the poet says the Pylians were found by Telemachus completing the sacrifice”, and he quotes *Odyssey* 3.4–6. The poet may invent what does not exist, he goes on, but when he can, he fits his verses to what does (*Geography* 8.3.16; see [[strabo-on-nestors-pylos]] and [[strabo]]).
