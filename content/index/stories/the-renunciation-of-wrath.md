## The story

**The summons.** At dawn of the day on which he will return to the battle (see [[the-fourth-day-of-battle]]), Thetis brings Achilles the armor of Hephaestus and tells him to call the Achaean heroes to an assembly, “renouncing your wrath against Agamemnon, shepherd of the people”, and to arm at once (19.34–36; see [[thetis]] and [[the-making-of-the-arms]]). He goes along the shore of the sea, shouting terribly, and all come, even the helmsmen and the stewards who dealt out the food and had always stayed by the ships, “because Achilles / had appeared, who had long ceased from grievous battle” (19.40–46). Walter Leaf would take the helmsmen and stewards for a gloss, if the book were not so concerned with feeding (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.43). Diomedes and Odysseus come limping on their spears, and Agamemnon comes last, still wounded by Coon's spear (19.47–53; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]).

**Achilles renounces his wrath.** Achilles stands up and asks the son of Atreus whether their strife for a girl was better for either of them; he wishes Artemis had killed her on the day he took her, for the strife has profited only Hector and the Trojans (19.55–64). Then he lets it go:

{{quote:c61a32e4-743c-5a93-b538-4832146f7aed}}

“So he spoke, and the well-greaved Achaeans rejoiced / that the great-hearted son of Peleus had renounced his wrath” (19.74–75). μῆνιν ἀπειπόντος, “had renounced his wrath”, answers Thetis' μῆνιν ἀποειπών, “renouncing your wrath” (19.35), and the book's traditional title, Μήνιδος ἀπόρρησις, names the same act (see [[title-of-book-19]], [[menis]] and [[achilles]]).

**Agamemnon's apology.** Agamemnon answers from his seat. He is not to blame, but Zeus and Fate and the Erinys, who cast blind folly into his mind on the day he took the prize of Achilles. Blind Folly, the eldest daughter of Zeus, blinded even Zeus on the day Heracles was to be born, when Hera made him swear that the child of his blood born that day would rule all who dwell around; Zeus flung her from Olympus to the works of men. So Agamemnon too was blinded, and he will make amends and give all the gifts that Odysseus promised (19.76–144; see [[agamemnon]], [[ate]] and [[the-birth-of-heracles]]).

**Odysseus' counsel.** Achilles tells Agamemnon to give the gifts or keep them, as he pleases, and wants to lead the army out at once, “for we must not waste time in talk here / nor delay; for a great work is still undone” (19.146–153). Odysseus answers that the army must eat first, since no man can fight all day without food; let the gifts be brought into the middle of the assembly for all to see, let Agamemnon swear that he never lay with Briseis, and let him give a feast in his huts; and in future he will be more just, for it is no cause for blame that a king should make amends to a man when he was the first to grow angry (19.154–183; see [[odysseus]], [[food-and-wine-are-might-and-courage]], [[amends-and-reconciliation]] and [[no-blame-for-a-king-to-make-amends]]). Agamemnon agrees, sends Odysseus with young men to fetch the gifts, and orders Talthybius to make ready a boar for Zeus and the Sun (19.184–197).

**Achilles refuses food.** Achilles answers that the Achaeans should fight fasting now and feast at sunset, when they have avenged the insult; while his companion lies dead in his hut, nothing will go down his throat (19.198–214; see [[patroclus]] and [[fasting]]). Odysseus, the elder, replies that men soon have their fill of battle, that the Achaeans cannot mourn the dead with the belly, for too many fall every day, and that the dead must be buried after a day's weeping and the living must eat (19.215–237; see [[satiety-of-battle]] and [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]]).

**The gifts and the oath.** Odysseus takes the sons of Nestor, Meges, Thoas, Meriones, Lycomedes and Melanippus to Agamemnon's hut. They bring out seven tripods, twenty cauldrons, twelve horses, seven women and Briseis the eighth, and Odysseus weighs out ten talents of gold (19.238–248; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). The gifts are set in the middle of the assembly. Agamemnon stands, cuts the first hairs from the boar, lifts his hands to Zeus, and swears by Zeus, Earth, the Sun and the Erinyes that he never laid a hand on Briseis; he cuts the boar's throat, and Talthybius flings it into the sea (19.249–268; see [[oath]], [[talthybius]] and [[briseis]]).

**The end of the assembly.** Achilles stands and speaks: “Father Zeus, truly great are the blind follies you give to men”; but for Zeus, the son of Atreus would never have stirred his heart or taken the girl, and Zeus must have wanted death for many of the Achaeans. “But now go to your meal, so that we may join in the clash of Ares” (19.270–275). He dissolves the assembly, the men scatter to their ships, and the Myrmidons carry the gifts to Achilles' huts, seat the women and drive the horses to the herd (19.276–281; see [[myrmidons]]).

## In the poem

**Iliad 1 undone.** The scene answers the first assembly point by point. Achilles called that assembly too (1.54), rose to speak in the same verse and began with the same word, “Son of Atreus” (19.55–56; 1.58–59). After that assembly Agamemnon's heralds, Talthybius and Eurybates, took the girl from his hut (1.318–348; see [[taking-of-briseis]]); this one ends with her brought back to it, and Talthybius holds the boar over which the king swears that he never touched her (19.246, 19.250–268, 19.280). In Book 1 Achilles swore by the scepter that the Achaeans would long for him (1.233–244); in Book 19 Agamemnon swears over the boar. In Book 1 Agamemnon took the prize to show “how much greater I am than you” (1.185–186); in Book 19 he makes amends, and is told that a king may do so without shame (19.182–183). See [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]].

**What is renounced.** Achilles gives up the μῆνις, his wrath against Agamemnon, but not his anger. When he sees the armor, “anger entered him the more” (19.16), and he arms “raging against the Trojans” (19.367); the insult he means to avenge is now Hector's (19.208; see [[cholos]] and [[lobe]]). He does not ask for the gifts and does not refuse them (19.147–148). He refuses the meal and the feast that should seal the reconciliation, and eats nothing; Athena drips nectar and ambrosia into his breast instead (19.199–214, 19.303–308, 19.340–354).

**Forms and haste.** The book sets the forms of reconciliation against Achilles' impatience. Achilles will not “waste time in talk” (κλοτοπεύειν, a word found nowhere else in Greek; Leaf on 19.149). Odysseus insists that it be done in order: food for the army, the gifts in public, the oath with a victim, a feast. Agamemnon agrees in every point, and Achilles lets it be done (see [[assembly]] and [[feast]]).

**Blame.** Both men find the cause in Zeus. Agamemnon names Zeus, Fate and the Erinys and tells the story of Ate (19.86–136); Achilles answers that Zeus gives men great blind follies and wanted the Achaeans' death (19.270–274). Neither denies what was done, and Agamemnon pays for it (see [[who-is-to-blame]] and [[divine-intervention]]).

**The text.** Walter Leaf took the story of Heracles' birth for an episode worked in from an independent poem about Heracles, though he granted that “many speakers with a bad case take refuge in telling stories” (on 19.95), and suspected that 137–141, where Agamemnon offers the gifts of the embassy “yesterday”, were added to join the book to *Iliad* 9 (on 19.136, 19.141; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]] and the translation's notes on 19.95–133 and 19.136–141).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates will not have the young believe that Achilles was so greedy as to take gifts from Agamemnon, or praise Phoenix for advising him to take gifts and help the Achaeans but not to give up his wrath without them (*Republic* 3.390e; see [[plato-phoenix-and-the-gifts]]).
