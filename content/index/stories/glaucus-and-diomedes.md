## The story

**The challenge.** While Hector goes to the city, “Glaucus, son of Hippolochus, and the son of Tydeus came together between the two armies, eager to fight” (6.119–120). Diomedes speaks first. He has never seen this man in battle, and now he has come out far in front of all and stood up to Diomedes' spear: “It is the sons of unhappy men who meet my might” (6.123–127). If the stranger is one of the immortals, Diomedes will not fight him. Lycurgus, who fought Dionysus, did not live long (6.128–141; see [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]]). “But if you are one of the mortals who eat the fruit of the field, come nearer, so that you may the sooner reach the bounds of destruction” (6.142–143).

**The answer.** Glaucus asks why he wants to know his lineage, since the generations of men are like the leaves (6.145–149; see [[the-generations-of-leaves]]). Then he tells it: Sisyphus of Ephyra, his son Glaucus, and Bellerophon, whom Proetus sent to Lycia to die, who killed the Chimaera and married the king's daughter, and who was later hated by the gods; Bellerophon's son Hippolochus sent Glaucus to Troy “always to be the best and to excel above the others” (6.150–211; see [[bellerophon-in-lycia]] and [[always-to-be-the-best]]).

**Guest-friends.** Diomedes is glad. He fixes his spear in the earth and speaks “with soothing words” (6.212–214):

He does not remember his father Tydeus, who left him as a small child when the army perished at Thebes (6.222–223; see [[the-seven-against-thebes]]). “So now I am your dear guest-friend in the heart of Argos, and you are mine in Lycia, whenever I come to that land” (6.224–225). There are Trojans enough for the one to kill and Achaeans enough for the other; let them avoid each other's spears, and let them exchange armor, “so that these men too may know that we claim to be guest-friends from our fathers' time” (6.226–231).

## In the poem

**The last of Diomedes' day.** Diomedes has fought through Book 5 and into Book 6 as the terror of the Trojans (see [[the-aristeia-of-diomedes]]). Athena took the mist from his eyes “so that you may clearly tell god from man” (5.127–128), and he wounded Aphrodite and Ares. Now he asks a stranger whether he is a god and says he will not fight one. Readers have asked why he needs to ask. The gift of sight may have been meant for his fight with the gods only; the question may also be a courtesy, the honor of allowing that a brave opponent could be divine, as Odysseus asks Nausicaa whether she is a goddess or a mortal (*Odyssey* 6.149–152). His story of Lycurgus answers Dione's warning of Book 5, that the man who fights the immortals does not live long (5.406–409; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]). His great day ends not in a killing but in a handshake.

**A meeting between the armies.** The scene belongs to a pattern the Iliad uses three times: two men meet “between the two armies”, the first asks the other who he is and threatens him, and the other answers with his lineage (see [[genealogy-in-battle]]). Achilles speaks Diomedes' threats again: “It is the sons of unhappy men who meet my might” to Asteropaeus, whom he kills (21.151 = 6.127), and “come nearer, so that you may the sooner reach the bounds of destruction” to Hector, whom Apollo carries away (20.429 = 6.143; 20.443–444). Aeneas answers Achilles with Glaucus' couplet, “if you wish to learn this too” (20.213–214 = 6.150–151), and Asteropaeus answers with Glaucus' question (21.153 ~ 6.145). Poseidon snatches Aeneas out of the fight (20.318–329), and Asteropaeus dies. Only Glaucus' lineage ends in friendship.

**A guest-friendship.** The bond was made by the grandfathers, when Oeneus entertained Bellerophon for twenty days, and it passes to the grandsons, who have never met (see [[xenia]], [[oeneus]] and [[bellerophon]]). The gifts are its proof. The cup is still in Diomedes' house (6.220–221; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[cup]]). The exchange of armor makes the bond visible to both armies, and it is a guest-friendship honored in a war fought over a guest-friendship betrayed (see [[the-abduction-of-helen]]). In Book 7 Hector and Ajax part after their duel with an exchange of gifts: Hector gives a silver-studded sword, and Ajax “a belt bright with purple” (7.303–305), the gift of Oeneus to Bellerophon (6.219).

**The narrator's verdict.** “Zeus, son of Cronus, took away Glaucus' wits” (6.234). The phrase is the one Agamemnon uses of his own delusion (19.137). The prices are counted in oxen, the Homeric measure of value: gold armor worth a hundred, bronze worth nine (see [[cattle]]). Whether the narrator is joking at Glaucus' expense, judging a bad bargain soberly, or pointing to the generosity of a man who gives far more than he gets is debated (see the translation's note on 6.234–236). Among guest-friends a gift answers a gift, and the one who gives more may gain in honor what he loses in goods; the exchange has been studied in the light of the Homeric economy of gifts (Walter Donlan, “The Unequal Exchange between Glaucus and Diomedes in Light of the Homeric Gift-Economy”, *Phoenix* 43, 1989). The poem does not return to the armor. Glaucus fights on among the Lycians, and Diomedes among the Achaeans.

**Two stories.** The two men each tell a story, and the stories end alike. Lycurgus “was hated by all the immortal gods” (6.140), and Bellerophon, “when he too was hated by all the gods”, wandered alone on the Aleian plain (6.200). Diomedes tells of a man who fought a god; Glaucus answers with a man the gods first helped and then abandoned (see [[gods-and-mortals]]).

**While Hector walks.** The meeting fills the time of Hector's way from the battle to the Scaean gates (6.116–118, 6.237; see [[hectors-visit-to-troy]]). The battle that the gods have left to itself (6.1) pauses between the armies, and the book moves from war to the city.

## Outside Homer

“Gold for bronze” became a proverb for an unequal exchange. In Plato's *Symposium* Socrates tells Alcibiades, who offers his beauty for Socrates' wisdom, that he is trying to get gold in exchange for bronze (218e–219a; see [[plato]]). [[Aristotle]] uses the exchange to ask whether a man can be wronged with his own consent, and answers that Glaucus was not wronged, since it was in his power to give (*Nicomachean Ethics* 5.9, 1136b9–13).

Lycian descent from Glaucus was still claimed in historical times. Herodotus says that some of the Ionian cities chose as kings Lycians descended from Glaucus, son of Hippolochus (1.147).
