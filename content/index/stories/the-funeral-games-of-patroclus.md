## The story

The Achaeans have heaped up the mound of Patroclus and are going back to the ships when Achilles keeps the army there and makes them sit in a wide gathering, and brings prizes out of the ships, “cauldrons and tripods / and horses and mules and the strong heads of oxen, / and fair-belted women, and gray iron” (23.257–261; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]] and [[contests]]). Eight contests follow.

**The chariot race.** The first prizes are a woman skilled in handiwork and a tripod with handles, a mare in foal, a cauldron, two talents of gold, and a two-handled bowl (23.262–270; see [[tripod]], [[cauldron]] and [[phiale]]). Achilles does not compete, since his immortal horses, the gift of Poseidon to Peleus, stand mourning their charioteer (23.271–286; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Five drivers rise: Eumelus, Diomedes with the horses of Tros that he took from Aeneas, Menelaus with his own Podargus and Agamemnon's mare Aethe, Antilochus, who drives his father's horses and is given long advice about the turning post by Nestor, and Meriones (23.287–351; see [[eumelus]], [[diomedes]], [[menelaus]], [[aethe-and-podargus]], [[antilochus]], [[nestor]] and [[meriones]]). They draw lots for their places, and Achilles sets Phoenix as a lookout by the post (23.352–361; see [[turning-post]] and [[phoenix]]). On the way back Eumelus leads, with Diomedes close behind, until Apollo strikes the whip from Diomedes' hand and Athena gives it back and breaks Eumelus' yoke (23.362–400; see [[apollo]] and [[athena]]). Antilochus forces his way past Menelaus where the road narrows (23.401–447). In the gathering Idomeneus and Ajax, son of Oileus, quarrel over who is leading, until Achilles stops them (23.448–498; see [[idomeneus]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). Diomedes comes in first, and Sthenelus takes his prizes; Antilochus is second, “by crafty ways, not by speed”, with Menelaus close behind him, then Meriones, and Eumelus last, dragging his chariot (23.499–533; see [[sthenelus]]). Achilles would give Eumelus the second prize, but Antilochus will not give up the mare, and Achilles gives Eumelus the corselet of Asteropaeus instead (23.534–565; see [[asteropaeus]]). Menelaus accuses Antilochus of a trick and challenges him to swear by Poseidon that he did not hamper his chariot; Antilochus yields the mare, and Menelaus gives her back to him (23.566–613; see [[oath]] and [[amends-and-reconciliation]]). Meriones takes the two talents, and the fifth prize, the bowl, Achilles gives to Nestor, who remembers his victories at the funeral games of Amarynceus (23.614–652; see [[the-funeral-games-of-amarynceus]]).

**The boxing.** For a hard-working mule and a two-handled cup, Epeius, son of Panopeus, boasts that no one will beat him, and only Euryalus, son of Mecisteus, stands up against him, with Diomedes to put the loincloth about him and give him the well-cut straps; Epeius knocks him down with a blow to the cheek and sets him on his feet again (23.653–699; see [[epeius]], [[euryalus]], [[kilt]], [[well-cut]] and [[cup]]).

**The wrestling.** Telamonian Ajax and Odysseus grapple for a great tripod and a woman. Neither can throw the other, and Achilles stops them: “victory is for both” (23.700–739; see [[ajax]] and [[odysseus]]).

**The footrace.** The first prize is a silver mixing bowl made by the Sidonians, which Euneus gave Patroclus as the price of Lycaon (23.740–749; see [[mixing-bowl]], [[euneus]] and [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). Ajax, son of Oileus, leads, with Odysseus close behind; Odysseus prays to Athena, and Ajax slips in the dung of the oxen slain for Patroclus. Odysseus takes the bowl, Ajax the ox, and Antilochus, last, the half talent of gold, and Achilles adds another half talent for his praise (23.750–797).

**The armed duel.** For the armor of Sarpedon, which Patroclus had taken, and the Thracian sword of Asteropaeus, Telamonian Ajax and Diomedes fight in armor until the Achaeans, afraid for Ajax, make them stop and take equal prizes; Achilles gives the sword to Diomedes (23.798–825; see [[sarpedon]] and [[sword]]).

**The weight throw.** For the lump of iron that Eetion used to throw, Epeius, Leonteus, Telamonian Ajax and Polypoetes throw, and Polypoetes throws beyond them all (23.826–849; see [[eetion]], [[leonteus]], [[polypoetes]] and [[iron]]).

**The archery.** Achilles ties a dove to the mast of a ship. Teucer, who makes no vow to Apollo, cuts the cord, and Meriones, who vows a hecatomb, shoots the dove as she circles; Meriones takes the ten double axes and Teucer the single axes (23.850–883; see [[teucer]], [[doves]], [[mast]] and [[axe]]).

**The javelin.** Agamemnon and Meriones stand up to throw, but Achilles gives Agamemnon the cauldron without a contest, as the best of all, and Meriones the spear; Agamemnon gives his prize to the herald Talthybius (23.884–897; see [[agamemnon]] and [[talthybius]]).

## In the poem

**For the dead.** The games belong to the funeral. Achilles sets the prizes “in honor of his companion” (23.748), and Nestor, taking his prize, bids him “likewise honor your companion with funeral games” (23.646). Patroclus is remembered through them. Achilles' horses mourn him and do not race (23.279–284), the bowl is a memorial of his funeral for Nestor (23.618–620), the armor of Sarpedon is his spoil (23.800), the bowl of silver was paid to him as the price of Lycaon (23.746–747), and the oxen killed for him trip Ajax in the footrace (23.775–776). The other prizes come from Achilles' own war: the corselet and the sword of Asteropaeus (23.560, 23.807–808) and the iron of Eetion (23.827–829).

**Games before.** The poem has prepared for them. Hector and Achilles ran around Troy like horses that race around the turning posts for “a tripod or a woman, in honor of a man who has died” (22.162–166; see [[racehorses-at-the-turning-post]]), and the first prizes of the chariot race are a woman and a tripod (23.263–264). Nestor's father sent four prize-winning horses with their chariot to race for a tripod in Elis, and Augeas kept them (11.698–702; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]); Nestor won every contest at the funeral of Amarynceus but the chariot race (23.629–642), and Euryalus' father Mecisteus defeated all the Cadmeians at the funeral of Oedipus (23.677–680; see [[mecisteus]]). In the Odyssey the shade of Agamemnon tells the shade of Achilles that he has seen the funerals of many heroes, “when at the death of a king / the young men gird themselves and make ready the contests”, but none like the games that Thetis held for her son with prizes she asked of the gods (Od. 24.85–92).

**Achilles presides.** The man who quarreled over a prize in Book 1 now gives the prizes. Antilochus, when Achilles proposes to give his mare to Eumelus, says, “for you mean to take away my prize” (23.544), as Achilles said to Agamemnon that he threatened to take away his prize himself (1.161); this time Achilles smiles and gives Eumelus something else (23.555–562). He pities Eumelus, stops the quarrel of Idomeneus and the son of Oileus and the wrestling, adds a gift for Antilochus' praise, gives Nestor a prize for his years, and honors Agamemnon at the end as the best of all without a contest (23.534–538, 23.491–498, 23.734–737, 23.795–796, 23.618–623, 23.890–894; see [[achilles]]). Disputes among the others are settled as the quarrel of Book 1 was not, by men giving way (23.587–611; see [[amends-and-reconciliation]]).

**The gods.** The gods decide what speed and skill cannot. Apollo and Athena change the order of the chariot race (23.382–400), Athena trips Ajax in the footrace (23.774, 23.782–783), and Apollo begrudges Teucer the bird, while Meriones, who vows a hecatomb, hits it (23.865, 23.872–876; see [[divine-intervention]] and [[prayer]]). Antilochus tells Achilles that Eumelus should have prayed (23.546–547).

**Young and old.** Antilochus pleads his youth to Menelaus, and Menelaus forgives it (23.587–590, 23.604); Nestor, too old to compete, is given a prize and remembers his youth (23.618–650); and Antilochus, beaten in the footrace by Odysseus, says that the gods honor older men (23.787–792; see [[old-age-and-memory]]).

## Outside Homer

The book's traditional title is Ἆθλα ἐπὶ Πατρόκλῳ, “the Games for Patroclus” (see [[title-of-book-23]]). Plato's Socrates has the rhapsode Ion recite Nestor's advice about the turn in “the horse race in honor of Patroclus” (*Ion* 537a–c; see [[nestors-lesson-in-driving]]). Aristotle cites a verse of the same advice among the problems solved by an accent (*Poetics* 1461a21–23; see [[the-stump-that-rots]]). [[Pausanias]] quotes Nestor's verses on Arion (Pausanias 8.25.8; see [[arion-the-horse]]). Virgil builds the funeral games for Anchises in the fifth book of the *Aeneid* on these, and Walter Leaf notes that he copies the fall of Ajax in the footrace and the archery at the dove (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.773 and 23.850; see [[virgil-the-games-for-anchises]]). For a chariot race of tragedy, lost at the turning post, see [[the-false-report-of-orestes-death]].
