## The story

**Menelaus over the body.** Menelaus is the first to see that Patroclus has fallen, and he stands over the body “as a mother stands over her calf” (17.1–8; see [[menelaus]], [[fighting-over-the-body]] and [[the-cow-and-her-calf]]). Euphorbus, son of Panthous, who wounded Patroclus first, claims the spoils, since no Trojan or ally hit Patroclus before him (17.9–17; see [[euphorbus]]). Menelaus reminds him of his brother Hyperenor, who scorned Menelaus and did not go home on his own feet, and Euphorbus answers that Menelaus will pay for that brother (17.18–42; see [[hyperenor]] and [[panthous]]). His spear bends back on Menelaus' shield, and Menelaus, praying to Zeus, drives his spear through the young man's neck (17.43–60). No Trojan dares come against him (17.61–69; see [[lion-among-cattle]]).

**Hector comes.** Apollo, in the likeness of Mentes, the leader of the Cicones, turns Hector from chasing the horses of Achilles, “chasing what cannot be caught”, and tells him that Euphorbus is dead (17.70–81; see [[apollo]], [[mentes]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Hector comes through the champions shouting, “like the flame of Hephaestus” (17.82–89). Menelaus weighs shame against danger, and gives way: “When a man is willing, against a god's will, to fight with a man / whom a god honors, soon a great bane rolls down on him” (17.90–105). He leaves the body, turning back again and again, like a lion driven from a farmyard (17.106–113; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]).

**The armor and the body.** Menelaus finds Ajax on the left of the battle and asks him to help bring the body to Achilles, “naked; for Hector of the gleaming helmet has his armor” (17.114–124; see [[ajax]]). Hector has stripped the armor and is dragging the body, “to cut the head from his shoulders with the sharp bronze, / and drag off the corpse and give it to the Trojan dogs” (17.125–127; see [[dogs]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]). When Ajax comes, “carrying his shield like a tower”, Hector springs into his chariot and gives the armor to the Trojans to carry to the city, “to be great fame for him”, and Ajax stands over the body like a lion over its young (17.128–139; see [[the-shield-of-ajax]] and [[the-lion-and-its-cubs]]).

**Glaucus and Hector.** Glaucus, leader of the Lycians, rebukes Hector for leaving Sarpedon to the Argives and shrinking from Ajax, and proposes a trade: if the Trojans drag Patroclus into Ilios, the Argives will soon release Sarpedon's armor, and the Trojans can bring his body into the city (17.140–168; see [[glaucus]] and [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]). Hector answers that “the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus is always stronger” (17.170–182; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). He runs after the men carrying the armor, puts it on, and Zeus, seeing him, foretells his death and fits the armor to his body (17.183–214; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]] and [[exchange-of-armor]]). He promises his allies half the spoils, and fame equal to his own, for the man who drags Patroclus among the Trojans (17.215–236; see [[trojan-allies]]).

**The leaders called.** Ajax fears less for the corpse, which “will soon glut the dogs and the birds of the Trojans”, than for their own heads, and Menelaus calls the leaders of the Achaeans to come and be indignant “that Patroclus should become a plaything for the Trojan dogs” (17.237–255; see [[nemesis]]). The son of Oileus comes first, then Idomeneus and Meriones, and others whose names, the narrator says, no one could tell (17.256–261; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]], [[idomeneus]] and [[meriones]]).

**The fight in the mist.** The Trojans come on with a shout like a wave roaring at the mouth of a river, and Zeus pours a thick mist around the Achaeans who stand around the body, “since he had not hated the son of Menoetius before” (17.262–273; see [[the-wave-at-the-rivers-mouth]] and [[zeus]]). The Trojans drag at the corpse, and Ajax drives them back (17.274–287). He kills Hippothous, who had tied a strap around Patroclus' ankle and was dragging him by the foot, and Phorcys, who stood over Hippothous; Hector's spear, meant for Ajax, kills Schedius (17.288–318; see [[hippothous]], [[phorcys]] and [[schedius]]). The Trojans would have been driven back into Ilios, had not Apollo, in the likeness of the herald Periphas, rebuked Aeneas, and they stand again (17.319–343; see [[aeneas]], [[periphas-son-of-epytus]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). Ajax keeps the Achaeans close around the body, “and the earth was wet / with purple blood” (17.344–365). Around the body the best men fight in mist, while the rest of the armies fight under a clear sun, “with pauses between” (17.366–383). There the two sides pull the corpse back and forth, as men stretch a bull's hide soaked in fat (17.384–393; see [[the-stretched-hide]]):

The narrator sums it up: “such was the toil of men and horses that Zeus over Patroclus / stretched tight that day, an evil toil” (17.400–401; see [[the-rope-of-war]]). Achilles, far off at the ships, does not know that Patroclus is dead (17.401–411; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). On both sides men say that they will not yield the body: an Achaean would rather “let the black earth here / gape for us all”, and a Trojan, “even if it is our fate to be brought down beside this man, / all of us alike, let no one yet hang back from the war” (17.412–425).

**The horses.** The horses of Achilles stand apart from the battle and weep for their charioteer, and Zeus pities them and breathes might into them (17.426–458; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Automedon drives them into the fight alone, then gives the reins to Alcimedon and gets down to fight; Hector and Aeneas come to take the horses, Automedon kills Aretus and strips him, and the two Ajaxes part the fighters (17.459–542; see [[automedon]], [[alcimedon]] and [[aretus]]).

**Athena and Apollo.** Athena comes down from heaven, wrapped in a purple cloud; in the likeness of Phoenix she puts strength in Menelaus and the daring of a fly in his breast, and he kills Podes, Hector's companion at the feast (17.543–581; see [[athena]], [[phoenix]], [[the-daring-of-a-fly]] and [[podes]]). Apollo, in the likeness of Phaenops, son of Asius, taunts Hector, and Zeus takes up the aegis, covers Ida in clouds, thunders, and gives the Trojans the victory (17.582–596; see [[phaenops-son-of-asius]], [[aegis]] and [[ida]]). Peneleos, Leitus and Idomeneus are driven from the fight (17.597–625; see [[peneleos]] and [[leitus]]).

**The prayer for light.** Ajax sees that Zeus is helping the Trojans. He wishes for a messenger to Achilles, but the mist hides the men and the horses alike, and he prays: “and in the light destroy us, since that is now your pleasure” (17.626–647). Zeus pities his tears and scatters the mist, and the whole battle is seen (17.648–650).

**The news.** At Ajax's bidding Menelaus finds Antilochus and sends him running to Achilles with “grievous news, which I wish had never been” (17.651–701; see [[antilochus]]). Menelaus comes back to the body, and tells the Ajaxes that Achilles will not come, “for naked as he is, there is no way he could fight the Trojans” (17.702–714).

**The carrying.** Menelaus and Meriones lift the body and carry it toward the ships, while the two Ajaxes, “with equal spirit, of one name”, hold off the Trojans (17.715–724; see [[the-two-ajaxes]]). The retreat is told in a run of similes: hounds that rush at a wounded boar and scatter when it turns, a fire that sweeps through a city, mules dragging a beam down a mountain path, a wooded ridge that holds back the rivers, and starlings or jackdaws screaming before a falcon (17.725–759; see [[the-hounds-and-the-wounded-boar]], [[the-burning-city]], [[mules-dragging-a-beam]], [[the-wooded-ridge]] and [[a-cloud-of-starlings]]). Much fine armor falls around the ditch as the Danaans flee, “and there was no respite from the war” (17.760–761).

**How it ends.** Antilochus reaches Achilles with the news (18.1–21). The Achaeans flee to the ships and the Hellespont, and the fight over the body goes on: three times Hector seizes it by the feet, three times the two Ajaxes thrust him off, and he would have dragged it away and won great glory, had not Iris come to Achilles, sent by Hera without the knowledge of Zeus (18.148–186). Hector, she tells him, means to cut off the head and fix it on the stakes (18.175–177). Achilles goes unarmed to the ditch, Athena sets the aegis on his shoulders and a flame about his head, and he shouts three times; three times the Trojans and their allies are thrown into confusion, and twelve of their best men die among their own chariots and spears (18.202–231). The Achaeans draw the body out of range and lay it on a bier, and Achilles follows it weeping: he had sent his friend into the war with horses and chariot, and did not receive him home again (18.231–238). Then Hera sends the sun down (18.239–242; see [[the-third-day-of-battle]]).

## In the poem

**The stake.** Both sides know what the body is worth. The Trojans want to drag it into the city and win glory (17.286–287, 17.396, 17.418–419); Glaucus wants it to trade for the armor and the body of Sarpedon, not knowing that Sleep and Death have already carried that body to Lycia (17.160–163; 16.666–683; see the translation's note on 17.163); Hector wants to cut off its head and give the corpse to the dogs (17.126–127; 18.176–177). The Achaeans want to bring it to Achilles (17.104–105, 17.120–121, 17.691–692). The fear in the poem's first lines, that the wrath would make the heroes “the spoil of dogs / and of every bird” (1.4–5), comes closest to fulfillment over this body, and the dogs are named again and again (17.127, 17.241, 17.255, 17.272–273, 17.558; see [[dogs]] and [[birds]]). Hippothous drags the body by a strap around the ankle (17.289–290); Walter Leaf compares the way Achilles will drag the body of Hector (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.290; 22.396–397).

**One place on the field.** Until the body is carried away, the fight stays in one place, “in a little space” (ὀλίγῃ ἐνὶ χώρῃ, 17.394), under the wall of Troy (17.403–404). The narrator sets the rest of the battle elsewhere, fought at ease under the upper air with pauses between (17.370–377), and the fight around the body in a mist so thick that “you would not have said / that the sun was still safe, or the moon” (17.366–369; see [[apostrophe]]). Zeus pours the mist to protect the body (17.268–273), and Ajax prays to be rid of it (17.645–647). Walter Leaf thought the six verses of Zeus' mist hardly genuine, since the Achaeans for whose benefit the darkness is meant are the first to beg for its removal, and took the darkness of Ajax's prayer for the dust raised by the fighting (on 17.268 and 17.644).

**Menelaus.** Menelaus is first to the body and last to leave it. He stands over it, kills Euphorbus and Podes, calls the leaders, finds the messenger, and carries the body with Meriones (17.1–8, 17.43–60, 17.246–255, 17.575–581, 17.651–701, 17.717–735). Twice he leaves it unwillingly, “turning back again and again” the first time, and the second like a lion going from a farmyard at dawn (17.108–113, 17.657–667). In the last part of the book the narrator speaks to him in the second person (17.679, 17.702; see [[apostrophe]]), as in *Iliad* 16 he spoke to Patroclus.

**Both sides.** The narrator gives each army its voice (17.412–423) and its losses. The Trojans and their allies fall heaped together with the Danaans, but far fewer Danaans perish, “for they always remembered / to ward off sheer slaughter from one another in the throng” (17.360–365; see [[battle-order]]). Walter Leaf reports that Zenodotus athetized the couplet that gives the reason, and thought that it deserved the athetesis, and the couplet before it as well (on 17.364).

**The gods.** Zeus stretches the toil over the body (17.400–401), gives Hector great power (17.206), and gives the Trojans the victory with the aegis (17.593–596, 17.626–627); yet he pities Hector, the horses and Ajax (17.198–208, 17.441–456, 17.648; see [[pity]]). Apollo rouses the Trojans three times in the likeness of men, Mentes, Periphas and Phaenops (17.73, 17.323, 17.583), and Athena rouses the Achaeans once, as Phoenix (17.555; see [[apollo]] and [[athena]]).

**Achilles.** The man for whom the body is fought is absent. His armor is on Hector, his horses weep, his messenger runs to him, and he is named in the speeches of both sides (17.105, 17.121, 17.186, 17.473, 17.504, 17.557, 17.641–642, 17.691). He does not know that his friend is dead (17.401–411), and Menelaus doubts that he will come, “naked as he is” (17.708–711; see [[achilles]]). In *Iliad* 18 he comes, unarmed, and ends the fight with his voice (18.217–231).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the *Odyssey* the shade of Agamemnon tells the shade of Achilles how the best of the Trojans and the Achaeans were killed fighting over his body, while he lay, as the Iliad says of the dead Cebriones, “in a whirl of dust”, “great over a great space, forgetful of his horsemanship” (16.775–776 ≈ *Odyssey* 24.39–40; see [[cebriones]]). They fought the whole day, and would not have stopped if Zeus had not stopped them with a storm; then they carried him to the ships, washed him with warm water and oil, laid him on a bier, and wept over him (*Odyssey* 24.36–46).

## Outside Homer

For the book's traditional title, the Aristeia of Menelaus, and its later readers, see [[title-of-book-17]].

Walter Leaf reports that the scholia note that the cyclic poets imitated the end of *Iliad* 17 in telling the death of Achilles: Ajax carries the body, and Odysseus covers the retreat (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.719; see [[ajax]] and [[the-judgment-of-arms]]).
