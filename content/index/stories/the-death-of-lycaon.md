## The story

**The first meeting.** Achilles has filled the river with the dead and taken twelve young men alive, to kill at Patroclus' pyre (21.1–33; see [[human-sacrifice]] and [[the-fourth-day-of-battle]]). As he springs at the Trojans again, he meets a man he knows:

{{quote:931b172d-a8a3-5c59-bcbd-9120fb059754}}

That time Achilles sold him into Lemnos, “and the son of Jason paid the price”; a guest-friend, Eetion of Imbros, ransomed him and sent him to Arisbe, and from there he escaped home (21.40–44; see [[euneus]], [[eetion-of-imbros]] and [[achilles-raids]]). “For eleven days he delighted his heart with his friends, / after he came from Lemnos; but on the twelfth, again, / a god threw him into the hands of Achilles” (21.45–47).

**The second meeting.** Lycaon comes up out of the river “naked, without helmet or shield, and he had no spear either” (21.50): he has thrown them all down, worn out with sweat and weariness. Achilles knows him, and speaks to his own heart:

{{quote:ea359847-5ab8-5add-815a-8f32608325d7}}

**At his knees.** Lycaon comes near and clasps his knees under the spear:

{{quote:0fd61a06-bf2f-553f-be1c-db0716f6ee80}}

He begs for his life (21.74–96). He is as good as a suppliant, for at Achilles' side he first tasted the grain of Demeter, on the day he was taken; when Achilles sold him he fetched the worth of a hundred oxen, and he has been ransomed for three times as much; he is twelve days home, and deadly fate and Zeus have given him to Achilles again. His mother, Laothoe, daughter of old Altes, king of the Leleges, bore Priam two sons, “and you will cut the throats of both” (21.89): Achilles has already killed Polydorus. Let Achilles not kill him, since he is not of the same womb as Hector, who killed his companion (see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]], [[laothoe]], [[altes]] and [[polydorus-son-of-priam]]).

**The answer.** “So the shining son of Priam spoke to him, / begging with words; but the voice he heard would not be soothed” (21.97–98):

**The death.**

{{quote:5ac36b5a-9203-5648-b69f-08d9b4eabefb}}

Achilles takes him by the foot, flings him into the river to be swept away, and boasts over him. The fishes will lick the blood from his wound, no mother will lay him on a bier and lament for him, and Scamander will carry him into the broad bosom of the sea, where a fish will dart up to eat his white fat (21.120–127; see [[fish]]). Then he turns on all the Trojans: let them perish until he reaches the city, for not even the river, to whom they sacrifice bulls and throw horses alive, will protect them, until all of them pay for the slaughter of Patroclus (21.128–135; see [[rivers]] and [[scamander]]). The river grows all the more angry, and ponders how he might stop Achilles from his toil (21.136–138); he puts might into Asteropaeus, and later rises against Achilles himself (see [[asteropaeus]] and [[achilles-and-the-river]]).

## In the poem

**Two meetings.** The narrator tells the first meeting before the second (21.34–48), and Lycaon tells it again in his plea (21.76–81). The first ended in a sale and the second ends in death, and between them Patroclus has died. Achilles names the change himself: before, it was “somehow dearer to my heart to spare / Trojans, and many I took alive and sold” (21.101–102; see [[slavery-and-freedom]] and [[trade]]). The narrator marks both meetings with the same words: Lycaon was led away “against his will” from his father's orchard, and he is to be sent “to the house of Hades against his will” (οὐκ ἐθέλοντα, 21.36, 21.48; see the translation's note on 21.48). The ransom that brought him home bought him eleven days (21.45–46).

**The last suppliant in battle.** Every man who begs for his life in the fighting of the Iliad is killed: Adrastus, whom Menelaus pushes away and Agamemnon stabs (6.37–65); Dolon, struck down as he reaches for Diomedes' chin (10.454–457); the sons of Antimachus (11.130–147); and Tros, son of Alastor, who never reaches Achilles' knees (20.463–472; see [[supplication]] and [[supplication-type-scene]]). Lycaon's is the fullest of these scenes. He reaches the knees and holds them through the whole of his plea, the longest of these pleas, and Achilles' refusal is the only one in which the killer speaks of his own death (21.108–113; see [[patroclus-died-too]]). The verse with which Lycaon begins returns in the Odyssey with Odysseus' name in place of Achilles', in the mouths of the seer Leodes and the singer Phemius, who clasp Odysseus' knees among the suitors' dead; Odysseus kills the one and spares the other (*Odyssey* 22.310–329, 22.342–377).

**The twelve captives.** Lycaon comes to Achilles “dazed” (τεθηπώς, 21.64), the word for the twelve young men whom Achilles has just led out of the river, “dazed, like fawns” (τεθηπότας, 21.29), and he tells Achilles that he will “cut the throats” of both of Laothoe's sons (δειροτομήσεις, 21.89). It is the verb of Achilles' vow over Patroclus' body, “and before your pyre I will cut the throats of twelve / splendid children of the Trojans” (18.336–337), and of the killing at the pyre (23.174–176; see [[human-sacrifice]]). Walter Leaf glosses it as the slaughter of a helpless victim at the altar (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.89).

**Hector.** In the next book Hector, waiting for Achilles before the gate, thinks of laying down his shield, his helmet and his spear and going to him unarmed with offers of Helen and of half the city's wealth, and puts the thought away: Achilles would not pity him or respect him, but would kill him unarmed, like a woman (22.111–125). The words are Lycaon's. Lycaon begged “respect me and pity me” (αἴδεο καί μʼ ἐλέησον, 21.74), and came to Achilles γυμνόν, “naked”, without his arms (21.50; γυμνὸν ἐόντα, 22.124; see [[aidos]] and [[pity]]).

**Priam.** In the last book the father of Lycaon clasps Achilles' knees and kisses his hands, and is heard (24.477–506). When Hermes wakes him in Achilles' hut, he tells him that he has ransomed his son, “and given much”, and that the sons left behind him would give three times as much ransom for him alive (24.685–686): the words of Lycaon's ransom, when Eetion “ransomed him, and paid much” (21.42) and Lycaon came home “paying three times as much” (21.80; see [[apoina]] and [[priam]]).

**The critics.** Walter Leaf notes several difficulties in the episode. At 21.17 Achilles leaves his spear on the bank, and at 21.67 he lifts it; Leaf thinks 21.1–33 are probably of other authorship (on 21.67). Didymus reports that 21.73 was not in Aristarchus' editions, though the manuscripts have it (on 21.73). Bayfield condemned 21.94–96: two of the three verses are borrowed (1.297, 17.204), and Lycaon's plea that he is not Hector's full brother comes oddly just after he has named Polydorus, his own (on 21.94; see the translation's note on 21.94–96). Aristophanes athetized 21.130–135 as added by readers who did not see why the river is angry, and Aristarchus may have agreed (on 21.130; see the translation's note on 21.130–135). Bergk rejected 21.41 as taken from the legend of the Argonauts (on 21.41; see [[jason]]).

## Outside Homer

In Apollodorus' summary of the war, Achilles takes Lycaon in the first raids after the landing: when the Trojans dare not come out, he lies in wait for Troilus and kills him in the sanctuary of Thymbraean Apollo, and going to the city by night he takes Lycaon (νυκτὸς ἐλθὼν ἐπὶ τὴν πόλιν Λυκάονα λαμβάνει, *Epitome* 3.32; see [[apollodorus]]). J. G. Frazer, in the notes to his translation, says that the story seems to have been told in the *Cypria* as well, and refers to Proclus' summary of that poem (Apollodorus, *The Library*, 1921, note on *Epitome* 3.32; see [[cypria]]).

For Virgil's reworking of the episode in the tenth book of the *Aeneid*, where Aeneas avenges Pallas, see [[virgils-lycaon]]. For later uses of Achilles' answer to Lycaon, by Callisthenes in Plutarch's *Alexander* and by Lucretius, see [[patroclus-died-too]], [[callisthenes-quotes-achilles]] and [[lucretius-on-the-great-dead]].
