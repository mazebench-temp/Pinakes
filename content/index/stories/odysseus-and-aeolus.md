## The story

**The floating island.** From the land of the Cyclopes the ships came to Aeolia, where Aeolus lived, “son of Hippotes, dear to the immortal gods, / on a floating island”, walled all around with unbreakable bronze and rising in a sheer rock (*Odyssey* 10.1–4; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]], [[aeolia]], [[aeolus]] and [[hippotes]]). He has twelve children, six daughters and six sons, and has given the daughters to the sons as wives; all day they feast beside their father and mother, and the house, “full of the savor of meat, resounds round about in the court”, and at night they sleep beside their wives (*Odyssey* 10.5–12). For a whole month Aeolus made Odysseus welcome and asked him about Ilios, the ships of the Argives and the homecoming of the Achaeans, and Odysseus told him everything (*Odyssey* 10.13–16).

**The skin of the winds.** When Odysseus asked to be on his way, Aeolus did not refuse, but made ready his escort (*Odyssey* 10.17–18; see [[pompe]]):

{{quote:dc309778-a54a-5075-b557-38ec8e6baa78}}

The son of Cronus who made him dispenser of the winds is Zeus, and the wind he left free is the wind that would carry the ships east to Ithaca (see [[zeus]], [[dispenser-of-the-winds]], [[skin-of-the-winds]], [[the-winds]] and [[zephyrus]]).

**Ithaca in sight.** For nine days they sailed, night and day alike, and on the tenth “our native fields were already coming into sight, / and already we could see men tending fires, we were so near” (*Odyssey* 10.28–30; see [[ithaca]]). Then sleep came upon Odysseus, worn out, for he had handled the sheet of the ship himself all the way and would give it to no other of his companions, “so that we might reach our native land the sooner” (*Odyssey* 10.31–33; see [[hypnos]]).

**The skin opened.** While he slept, the companions said to one another that he was bringing home gold and silver, gifts from Aeolus: he was dear and honored wherever he came, and was bringing many fine treasures from Troy, while they, who had gone the same road, were going home with empty hands. “But come, quickly, let us see what these are, / how much gold and silver is in the skin” (*Odyssey* 10.34–45; see [[hetairos]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). “So they spoke, and the evil counsel of my companions prevailed; / they loosed the skin, and all the winds rushed out” (*Odyssey* 10.46–47). The storm wind seized the ships and carried the men out to sea, weeping, away from their native land. Odysseus woke and pondered “whether I should fall from the ship and perish in the sea, / or endure in silence and remain still among the living”; he endured, and lay in the ship with his head covered, while the storm carried the ships back to the Aeolian island (*Odyssey* 10.48–55).

**Off with you.** On the island they drew water and took their meal, and Odysseus went with a herald and one companion to the house of Aeolus. He found him feasting with his wife and his children and sat down on the threshold. They were amazed, and asked what evil god had attacked him, since they had sent him off with care (*Odyssey* 10.56–66; see [[daimon]]). He answered that his evil companions had led him into blind folly, and with them sleep, and begged them to make it good (*Odyssey* 10.67–69; see [[ate]]). They fell silent, and the father answered:

{{quote:9e85fb10-74df-579e-a0de-c3c0aa8b4193}}

“So saying, he sent me from the house, groaning heavily.” They sailed on, grieving at heart, and the men's spirit was worn down by the painful rowing, “since no escort showed itself any longer” (*Odyssey* 10.76–79; see [[from-there-we-sailed-on]]). Six days later they came to the land of the Laestrygonians (see [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]]).

## In the poem

**Home in sight.** The adventure brings Odysseus nearer home than any other before the Phaeacians carry him there. At Malea he “would have reached my native land unharmed” before the North Wind drove him off course (*Odyssey* 9.79–81; see [[the-lotus-eaters]]); here the native fields are in sight, and the men tending fires on them (*Odyssey* 10.29–30; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). The teller gives the end before he tells it: Aeolus sent the West Wind “to carry the ships and ourselves; but he was not to / bring it to pass; for we were lost through our own folly” (*Odyssey* 10.26–27). In the summary that Odysseus gives Penelope the adventure has four verses and no word of the companions: Aeolus received him with a ready heart and sent him on his way, but it was not yet his lot to reach his dear native land, and the storm wind snatched him up again and carried him over the fish-filled sea, groaning heavily (*Odyssey* 23.314–317, a plain rendering).

**Folly and sleep.** The proem says that the companions “perished through their own recklessness” (*Odyssey* 1.7). Here the teller counts himself among them: αὐτῶν γὰρ ἀπωλόμεθʼ ἀφραδίῃσιν, “for we were lost through our own folly” (*Odyssey* 10.27), and again, ἡμετέρῃ ματίῃ, “through our own folly” (*Odyssey* 10.79; see [[atasthalie]]). To Aeolus he blames his companions and his sleep together: “My evil companions led me into blind folly, and with them sleep, / cruel sleep” (*Odyssey* 10.68–69). The adventure has a counterpart near the end of the tale. On Thrinacia Odysseus goes apart to pray, the gods pour sweet sleep on his eyelids, and Eurylochus begins an evil counsel among the companions, κακῆς ἐξήρχετο βουλῆς, as here “the evil counsel of my companions prevailed”, βουλὴ δὲ κακὴ νίκησεν ἑταίρων (*Odyssey* 10.46; 12.338–339); waking, he cries to Zeus and the other gods that they lulled him to his blind folly in pitiless sleep, while his companions devised a great deed (*Odyssey* 12.370–373; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[ate]]). Merry draws the comparison: it was in his sleep that the companions had brought trouble on him before, by opening the skin of the winds (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.338).

**The gifts and the sleep.** The companions imagine the skin full of the gifts of a host (*Odyssey* 10.35–45; see [[guest-gifts]]). The end of the wanderings answers the scene. Arete bids Odysseus tie the chest that holds the Phaeacians' gifts himself, “so that no one may do you harm on the way, when hereafter / you sleep sweet sleep as you go in the black ship”, and he ties it with a knot that Circe had taught him (*Odyssey* 8.443–448); on the Phaeacian ship he sleeps again, and on Ithaca he counts the gifts and misses nothing (*Odyssey* 13.79–80, 13.215–219, Greek only; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]).

**Hated by the gods.** Aeolus' answer is a rule of hospitality: “for it is not lawful for me to care for or to send on his way / a man who is hated by the blessed gods” (*Odyssey* 10.73–74; see [[themis]] and [[xenia]]). The return itself is his proof: “off with you, since it is as one hated by the gods that you come here” (*Odyssey* 10.75). The Phaeacians, who also give escorts, keep a warning of their own, Nausithous' prophecy that Poseidon would one day strike one of their ships as it came back from an escort (*Odyssey* 8.564–571); they send Odysseus home all the same, and the ship is turned to stone in sight of their city (*Odyssey* 13.159–164, Greek only; see [[nausithous]] and [[phaeacians]]).

## Outside Homer

The skin of the winds was a test case in the ancient quarrel over the truth of the wanderings. Eratosthenes said that one would find where Odysseus had wandered when one found the cobbler who had sewn up the skin of the winds, τὸν σκυτέα τὸν συρράψαντα τὸν τῶν ἀνέμων ἀσκόν; Polybius answered that Aeolus was a man who foretold the times for putting out in the strait of Sicily, where the currents run both ways, and so was called keeper of the winds and held to be a king, and Strabo, who reports them both, takes Polybius' side (*Geography* 1.2.15; see [[strabo]] and [[skin-of-the-winds]]). For the islands of Aeolus north of Sicily and for the Aeolus of Virgil and Ovid, see [[aeolus]] and [[aeolia]].
