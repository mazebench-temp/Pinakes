## The story

{{quote:3b562bd2-be39-54fc-bf14-02eb9c1f5691}}

Zeus hung Hera ὑψόθεν, “from on high”, and she hung “in the upper air and the clouds” (ἐν αἰθέρι καὶ νεφέλῃσιν, 15.18–21; see [[zeus]] and [[hera]]). From her feet he let down two anvils (ἄκμονας … δύω), and around her hands he cast a bond “of gold, unbreakable” (δεσμὸν … χρύσεον ἄρρηκτον, 15.19–20). The gods “chafed throughout high Olympus” (ἠλάστεον, of a grief that will not be forgotten), but none of them could stand beside her and set her free. Any god Zeus caught he would seize and hurl from the threshold, until he reached the earth “with little strength left” (15.21–24; see [[threshold]] and [[olympians]]).

The cause comes in the same sentence. “Yet not even so”, Zeus says, did “the ceaseless pain for divine Heracles” let his heart go: Hera had won over the storm winds with the help of the North Wind, sent Heracles over the barren sea and carried him off to Cos, and Zeus rescued him and brought him back to Argos (15.24–30; see [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]], [[heracles]] and [[boreas]]). The storm came when Heracles was sailing home from his sack of Troy, a generation before the war (14.250–251; see [[heracles-sacks-troy]]). How long Hera hung, and which gods tried to free her, Zeus does not say. The story is told nowhere else in Homer.

## In the poem

**A threat.** Zeus wakes on Ida beside Hera and sees what her trick has done: the Trojans in flight, Poseidon among the Argives, and Hector lying on the plain, vomiting blood (15.4–11; see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]). He does not know, he says, whether she will not again “be the first to reap the fruit” of her mischief, when he lashes her with blows (15.16–17). The anvils are the proof of what he has done before, and he tells the story “so that you may leave off your deceits” (15.31). It is a story from the past told to move a listener in the present, as Hephaestus told his own fall to make Hera yield to Zeus in Book 1 (1.586–594; see [[mythological-paradigm]]). It works. Hera shudders (ῥίγησεν), swears by Earth and Heaven and the water of Styx that Poseidon acts on his own, and goes where Zeus sends her (15.34–46, 15.78–79; see [[styx]]). On Olympus she tells the other gods that Zeus says “in power and in strength he is far the best”, and bids them accept whatever evil he sends (15.104–109).

**The threshold.** The verse in which Zeus hurls the gods echoes the verse of Hephaestus' fall. Hephaestus told Hera how once, “when I was eager to defend you, / he seized me by the foot and hurled me from the divine threshold”, and how he fell all day and came down on Lemnos with “little life left in me” (1.590–593; see [[fall-of-hephaestus]] and [[hephaestus]]). The gods of Book 15 are hurled ἀπὸ βηλοῦ, “from the threshold”, with the same participle, τεταγών, “seizing”, and they reach the earth ὀλιγηπελέων, “with little strength left” (15.23–24). Hephaestus was defending his mother, as these gods were trying to free her; but the poem does not say that his fall was part of this story. Sleep's account of that day has Zeus, waking, “flinging the gods about the house” (ῥιπτάζων, 14.257).

**Bonds and the golden rope.** Zeus' bond is golden and ἄρρηκτος, “unbreakable”, the word for the bonds that Hephaestus forges on his anvil to catch Ares and Aphrodite, δεσμοὺς ἀρρήκτους ἀλύτους, “unbreakable, not to be loosed” (*Odyssey* 8.274–275). Hera herself, with Poseidon and Pallas Athena, once wished to put Zeus in bonds, and Thetis freed him (1.396–406; see [[binding-of-zeus]]); here Zeus binds, and no one can free her. In Book 8 he dares the gods to hang a golden rope from the sky and pull on it all together: they could not drag him down, but he could pull them up with earth and sea and leave it all hanging in midair (8.18–27; see [[the-golden-rope]]). Hera's punishment is the one hanging from the sky that the poem tells as done.

## Outside Homer

**Apollodorus.** The mythographer joins the two stories that Homer keeps apart. Zeus threw Hephaestus out of heaven because he came to help Hera in her bonds, for Zeus had hung her from Olympus when she sent a storm against Heracles as he sailed from Troy after taking it; Hephaestus fell on Lemnos and was lamed in his legs, and Thetis saved him (*Library* 1.3.5; see [[apollodorus]] and [[fall-of-hephaestus]]). He tells the hanging again in his life of Heracles, before Heracles' landing on Cos (*Library* 2.7.1).

**The ancient editors.** Zenodotus left the whole passage out of his text (15.18–31; see [[zenodotus]]). Walter Leaf, who reports it, suspected that Zenodotus may have thought the tale unseemly for the gods, but found the internal evidence strongly in his favor: 15.31 comes in awkwardly, while 15.32 follows naturally on 15.17, and the passage has forms foreign to the old epic dialect, such as μέμνῃ, the contracted ἐκρέμω, ἠλάστεον, and γῆν for γαῖαν. The crude character of the legend, he added, is no proof that the verses are old, and the legend is plainly related to Sleep's story in Book 14 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 15.18). Some copies went on with two more verses, in which Zeus freed Hera from her fetters and threw the masses of metal down at Troy, “so that men to come might learn of it”, and the guides at Troy were said to show such lumps, which the poem had called anvils (the T scholia and Eustathius, quoted by Leaf on 15.21). Leaf thought the verses were meant to explain some meteoric stones in the Troad.

**Anvils.** Leaf explained the anvils as the largest mass of metal that early men commonly handled, and so a ready instrument of torture, and compared the goatherd Melanthius, hoisted up a pillar with a rope (*Odyssey* 22.173–177); he reports Curtius' suggestion that ἄκμονες here are thunderbolts, thought of as heavy stones (Leaf on 15.19). Hesiod measures the height of heaven with a falling anvil: a bronze anvil dropped from the sky would fall for nine nights and days and reach the earth on the tenth (*Theogony* 722–723; see [[hesiod]]).

**Allegory.** Plato's Socrates suggests that the lawgiver named the air, ἀήρ, Hera, disguising the name by putting its beginning at the end (*Cratylus* 404c). The Neoplatonist Proclus, commenting on the passage, makes the air an image of soul, and finds the same thought in Homer: “that is why Homer, hinting at this, makes Hera hang, and under her feet two anvils; for the air has been allotted the two heaviest elements below itself” (*On the Cratylus* 170; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).
