## The story

{{quote:bacffc5f-a805-5f8a-818e-1919b31d69f4}}

Poseidon tells it, and the story is told nowhere else in Homer (see [[poseidon]]). The three are brothers, sons of Cronus and Rhea: Zeus, Poseidon, and Hades, “lord of those below” (ἐνέροισιν ἀνάσσων, 15.187–188; see [[cronus]], [[rhea]] and [[hades]]). “All things have been divided in three, and each has his share of honor” (τριχθὰ δὲ πάντα δέδασται, ἕκαστος δʼ ἔμμορε τιμῆς, 15.189; see [[time]]). δέδασται is a perfect: the division was made once and still holds. The lots were shaken (παλλομένων, 15.191), as men shake lots in a helmet to choose a champion (3.316, 7.181; see [[lots]]), and each brother got his realm by lot (ἔλαχον, ἔλαχε): Poseidon the gray sea, “to dwell in forever”, Hades the misty darkness (ζόφον ἠερόεντα), where the dead go, and Zeus the wide sky, “in the upper air and the clouds” (15.190–192; see [[sea]], [[misty]] and [[wide-sky]]). “But the earth is still common to all, and high Olympus” (15.193; see [[earth]] and [[olympus]]).

The poem does not say when the lots were cast, or who shook them. It knows the events around them: Zeus put Cronus “down beneath the earth and the barren sea” (14.203–204), where he sits with Iapetus in the depths of Tartarus (8.478–481; see [[fall-of-cronus]] and [[titans]]).

## In the poem

**Equal in honor.** Zeus has sent Iris to order Poseidon out of the battle, since he is “far stronger than he in strength, / and the elder by birth” (15.165–166, 15.181–182; see [[iris]] and [[zeus]]). Poseidon answers that Zeus “has spoken arrogantly … when I am his equal in honor” (ὁμότιμον, 15.185–186), and the lots are his proof. Zeus claims the rank of birth; Poseidon claims the lot, which gave each brother a third. From it he draws his conclusion: “So I will not live by the mind of Zeus; but at his ease, / strong though he is, let him stay in his third share” (15.194–195; see [[moira]]). Zeus would do better to rebuke his own daughters and sons, “who will listen to him when he urges them, even under compulsion” (15.197–199). The earth, where the war is fought, is common to all three, and that is the ground of Poseidon's claim to stay in the battle. In *Iliad* 13 the narrator gave the other side: the two are “of one race and of one father's line, / but Zeus was born first and knew more”, and for that reason Poseidon helped the Achaeans only in secret (13.354–357; see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]).

**Iris and the elder.** Iris does not dispute the lots. She asks whether she is to carry back so harsh an answer, and reminds him: “The hearts of good men can be turned. / You know how the Erinyes always follow the elder” (15.203–204; see [[erinyes]]). Poseidon yields, praising a messenger who knows what is due, but his grievance stays in the language of the lot: it is a dread grief when Zeus wishes to rebuke “one who has an equal lot, ordained to the same portion as himself” (ἰσόμορον καὶ ὁμῇ πεπρωμένον αἴσῃ, 15.208–210; see [[aisa]]). He gives way, but threatens “incurable anger” if Zeus spares steep Ilios (15.211–217). Zeus grants that the quarrel was dangerous: even “the gods below, who are around Cronus” would have heard of their fighting, and it would not have been settled “without sweat” (15.224–228).

**The three realms.** The lots give the poem its map of the gods' world. Zeus gathers the clouds and thunders from the sky, on Olympus and on Ida; Poseidon has his golden house in the depths of the sea at Aegae (13.21–22; see [[aegae]]); Hades rules the dead below and is called “Zeus of the underworld” (9.457; see [[zeus-of-the-underworld]]). The battle of the gods puts the three realms into one scene: Zeus thunders from above, Poseidon shakes the earth from below, and Aidoneus, ἄναξ ἐνέρων, “lord of those below”, leaps from his throne in fear that Poseidon will break open the earth and lay bare the houses of the dead (20.56–65).

## Outside Homer

**Hesiod and the Hymn to Demeter.** The *Theogony* has no lots. When the gods had won the war with the Titans, they urged Zeus, by Earth's counsel, to be king, and he divided their honors among them (ἑὰς διεδάσσατο τιμάς, *Theogony* 881–885). There Zeus is the youngest of Rhea's children, born after Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hades and Poseidon (453–458, 478; see [[hesiod]]). The *Homeric Hymn to Demeter* keeps the threefold division. Helios tells Demeter that Aidoneus, her own brother, is no unworthy son-in-law: he got his share of honor “when at the first the division was made in three” (ὡς τὰ πρῶτα διάτριχα δασμὸς ἐτύχθη), and he lives among those whose lord he was allotted to be (83–87). He took Persephone “beneath the misty darkness”, ὑπὸ ζόφον ἠερόεντα, the words of Poseidon's verse (80).

**Pindar.** In Pindar's ode for Diagoras of Rhodes it is the earth that is divided. The old tales say that when Zeus and the immortals were dividing the earth, Rhodes still lay hidden in the sea, and no one drew a lot for Helios, who was away, so that he was left without a share; Zeus would have cast the lots again, but Helios said that he saw land rising from the sea, and the island became his (*Olympian* 7.54–71; see [[pindar]]). Leaf noted that this is another tradition, for in Homer the earth remains common to all (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 15.191).

**Plato.** Socrates begins the myth of the judgment of the dead in the *Gorgias* with the division: “as Homer says, Zeus and Poseidon and Pluto divided the rule, when they took it over from their father” (*Gorgias* 523a; see [[plato]]). Plato says nothing of lots. Pluto, the ruler of the dead, complains to Zeus that souls come to him that do not deserve to, and Zeus makes his own sons, Minos, Rhadamanthys and Aeacus, judges of the dead (523b–524a). In the *Critias* the gods once divided the whole earth among themselves by lot, region by region, not in strife but “by the lots of justice” (δίκης κλήροις, *Critias* 109b–c).

**Callimachus.** The *Hymn to Zeus* rejects the story. Zeus' brothers, though born before him, did not grudge him the sky, and “the ancient poets were not altogether truthful: they said that the lot gave the sons of Cronus their three homes; but who would draw lots for Olympus and for Hades, unless he were a fool? One casts lots for equal shares, and these are as far apart as can be.” It was not lots that made Zeus king of the gods, but the works of his hands, his might and his strength (Callimachus, *Hymn* 1.57–67). The objection turns Poseidon's argument around: he claimed equality because the brothers drew lots, and Callimachus denies the lots because the shares were not equal (see [[callimachus-against-the-lots]]).

**Virgil.** In the *Aeneid* Neptune calms the storm that Aeolus has raised for Juno, and sends the winds back to their king with the message that the rule of the sea and the trident were given by lot to him and not to Aeolus: *non illi imperium pelagi saevumque tridentem, / sed mihi sorte datum* (*Aeneid* 1.137–139; see [[virgil-neptunes-chariot]] and [[virgil]]).

**Apollodorus.** In the *Library* the lots follow the war with the Titans. The Cyclopes armed the three brothers, Zeus with thunder and lightning, Pluto with a helmet and Poseidon with a trident; when the Titans had been shut in Tartarus, the brothers “cast lots for the rule” (διακληροῦνται περὶ τῆς ἀρχῆς), and Zeus got the dominion of the sky, Poseidon that of the sea and Pluto that in Hades (*Library* 1.2.1; see [[apollodorus]]).

**Plutarch and the treatise On the Cosmos.** Plutarch turns the division against Pompey and Caesar: once Crassus was dead, the Roman empire could not contain two men, though they heard and read that among the gods “all things have been divided in three”, “and each has his share of honor” (*Pompey* 53.6–7; see [[plutarch]]). The treatise *On the Cosmos*, handed down among Aristotle's works, quotes Zeus' share as proof that god dwells above: “Zeus got the wide sky, in the upper air and the clouds” (6, 400a).

**Later readers.** The *Suda*, glossing τριχθά, “in three”, takes Olympus in 15.193 as the mountain, joined to the earth, “for if Olympus were a heavenly part of the sky, it would not be common, but private” (τ 1039). The Neoplatonist Proclus read the lots as a standing order of the gods. Commenting on the *Timaeus*, where the goddess of Sais, whom the Greeks call Athena, is said to have received both Athens and Sais as her lot (*Timaeus* 21e, 23d), he asks what it is for gods to get things by lot, and answers that after the division of the whole into heaven and becoming came the division into three of which Poseidon speaks in Homer; the gods' lots are eternal, as his “to dwell in forever” shows (*On the Timaeus* I 136, 141 Diehl). In his notes on the *Cratylus* he sets a Zeus above the three apart from the Zeus who is one of the three sons of Cronus, the brother of Poseidon's verse. This Zeus governs a third of the whole, “according to *all things have been divided in three*”; the second brother is called the “Zeus of the sea” and Poseidon, and the third the “Zeus of the underworld”, Pluto and Hades, as Homer calls Hades Ζεὺς καταχθόνιος (*On the Cratylus* 148; 9.457; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).
