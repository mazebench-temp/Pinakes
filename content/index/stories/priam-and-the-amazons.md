## The story

Helen has named Agamemnon, and Priam, marveling at him, calls him blessed: many young men of the Achaeans have been made subject to him. Then he remembers.

{{quote:6e7db609-0616-5bc0-8eb4-67136c9689a8}}

“But even they were not as many as the bright-eyed Achaeans” (3.190). That is all the Iliad tells. Priam went to “vine-rich” [[phrygia|Phrygia]] and saw the Phrygians in great numbers, “with their quick horses” (αἰολοπώλους), the people of [[otreus|Otreus]] and [[mygdon|Mygdon]], encamped along the banks of the [[sangarius|Sangarius]]. He was counted among them as an ally (ἐπίκουρος) “on the day when the Amazons came” (ἤματι τῷ ὅτε τʼ ἦλθον). ἐστρατόωντο, “they were encamped, on campaign”, suggests an army gathered to meet an invasion. Where the [[amazons|Amazons]] came from, why they came and how the day ended, Priam does not say.

## In the poem

**An old man's measure.** Priam uses the memory as Nestor uses his: the past sets the standard for the present (see [[old-age-and-memory]]). Nestor's heroes were greater than any men alive now (1.260–272). Priam's army of the past was smaller than the one before him, and his praise of Agamemnon ends with the size of the host that has come to take his city.

**Then and now.** The young Priam went to help others; the old Priam is defended by allies, and the Phrygians themselves have come to Troy (2.862–863; see [[trojan-allies]] and [[phrygians]]). The Iliad gives him family ties in that country: Hecuba's brother Asius, son of Dymas, lived in Phrygia by the streams of the Sangarius (16.717–719). Later in the book Helen asks Aphrodite whether she means to lead her still farther on, to one of the cities “of Phrygia or of lovely Maeonia” (3.400–401).

**The generation before.** The only other Amazons in the Iliad are the ones Bellerophon killed as the third of his labors in Lycia, in the same words, “the Amazons, the equals of men” (Ἀμαζόνας ἀντιανείρας, 6.186; see [[equals-of-men]]). In Homer they belong to the time of the fathers, a danger that heroes met before the war at Troy.

## Outside Homer

The Amazons came to Troy after all, but as Priam's allies. The *Aethiopis*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, began with the arrival of the Amazon Penthesilea, a daughter of Ares and Thracian by birth, to fight for the Trojans after Hector's death; Achilles killed her at the height of her valor, and killed Thersites for mocking his supposed love for her. According to the scholia, some ancient texts made the Iliad lead straight into that poem, ending: “So they held the funeral of Hector; and there came an Amazon, daughter of great-hearted Ares, the slayer of men.” Quintus of Smyrna begins his *Posthomerica* with Penthesilea's arrival and her welcome by Priam (book 1), and Virgil set her among the scenes of the Trojan war on Dido's temple at Carthage (*Aeneid* 1.490–493).

In the *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* the goddess, disguised as a mortal girl, tells Anchises that her father is Otreus, “who rules over all of well-walled Phrygia” (111–112; see [[aphrodite-and-anchises]]).
