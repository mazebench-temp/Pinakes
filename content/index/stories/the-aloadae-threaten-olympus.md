## The story

Among the women whom Odysseus sees at the pit is Iphimedeia, the wife of Aloeus, “who declared that she had lain with Poseidon, / and she bore two sons, but they were short-lived, / godlike Otus and far-famed Ephialtes”, the tallest men the earth ever reared and the handsomest after Orion, nine cubits broad and nine fathoms tall at nine years old (*Odyssey* 11.305–312; see [[iphimedeia]], [[aloeus]], [[otus]], [[ephialtes]] and [[orion]]). ## In the poem

**Two deeds.** In the Iliad the same brothers, called only “the sons of Aloeus”, bound Ares and kept him thirteen months in a bronze jar, until Hermes stole him away; Dione tells it as one of the sufferings that gods have endured at the hands of men (*Iliad* 5.385–391; see [[the-aloadae-bind-ares]]). The Odyssey does not mention the binding, and the Iliad does not mention the mountains. In both the brothers come near to overthrowing the gods: Ares “would have perished there” but for Hermes (*Iliad* 5.388–390), and the brothers “would have accomplished it, if they had reached the measure of youth” (*Odyssey* 11.317; see [[would-have-had-not]]).

**The mountains.** Olympus, Ossa and Pelion are the mountains of Thessaly, where much of the legend of the catalogue of women belongs (see [[olympus]], [[ossa-mountain]], [[pelion]] and [[catalogue-of-heroines]]). The ancient critics rejected the two verses of the mountains as impossible, and W. W. Merry thought them an interpolation from a poem of the war of gods and giants, a story that Homer does not tell and the Hesiodic epic does (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.315–316; see the translation's note on 11.315–316 and [[giants]]).

## Outside Homer

The Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* called the Aloadae the sons of Aloeus and Iphimedeia in name, but in truth of Poseidon and Iphimedeia, and said that their father founded Halus in Aetolia (fr. 6 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]). In Pindar, as Merry reports, they were killed by Artemis on Naxos (Merry on 11.318, citing *Pythian* 4.88); for Apollodorus' account, which joins this story to the binding of Ares, see [[the-aloadae-bind-ares]].

Virgil reverses the order of the mountains. Among the days of the moon in the *Georgics* he bids the farmer shun the fifth, on which pale Orcus and the Furies were born, and Earth, in a monstrous birth, bore Coeus and Iapetus and fierce Typhoeus and the brothers sworn to tear down the sky: three times they tried to set Ossa on Pelion, and to roll leafy Olympus on top of Ossa, and three times the Father scattered the heaped mountains with his thunderbolt (*Georgics* 1.276–283). The brothers are not named there, but in the *Aeneid* the Sibyl, describing Tartarus to Aeneas, tells how she saw the twin sons of Aloeus, huge bodies, who tried with their hands to tear down the great sky, *rescindere caelum*, and to thrust Jupiter from his kingdom above (*Aeneid* 6.562, 6.582–584; see [[virgil]]).
