## The story

Aphrodite has dropped her wounded son, and Apollo has taken him up in a dark cloud (5.343–346). Diomedes springs at Aeneas three times, knowing that Apollo holds his hands over him, and at the fourth the god warns him off (5.432–444; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]). Then Apollo takes Aeneas out of the battle.

{{quote:bcc830da-2dac-58c2-a751-498342abb24e}}

Pergamus is the citadel of Troy, and Apollo's temple stands there (see [[pergamus]] and [[temple]]). Leto and Artemis heal him in the great inner shrine (ἄδυτον) and give him glory (see [[leto]], [[artemis]] and [[healing]]). On the field the god leaves something in his place.

{{quote:3c375408-a55f-5099-90af-3cb498be380f}}

Apollo then calls on Ares to drag Diomedes out of the battle, and sits down on the height of Pergamus to watch (5.454–460). Ares, in the likeness of the Thracian leader Acamas, rouses the sons of Priam with the phantom's own story: “A man lies fallen whom we honored as highly as brilliant Hector, Aeneas, son of great-hearted Anchises; but come, let us save our brave companion from the din” (5.467–469). The Trojans rally and Ares covers the battle in night (5.470–511). Then Apollo sends Aeneas back from the rich inner shrine and puts might into his breast (5.512–513).

“And Aeneas took his place among his companions; and they rejoiced to see him coming to join them alive and unhurt and with his might unimpaired; but they asked him nothing” (5.514–516). There was no time for questions: the other toil did not allow it (5.517–518). The phantom is not mentioned again.

## In the poem

**What is an εἴδωλον.** εἴδωλον, from εἶδος, “form, look”, is a likeness with no one inside it (see [[eidolon]]). Apollo makes (τεῦξε) this one “like Aeneas himself, and in armor such as his” (5.450). Elsewhere in Homer the word is used of the dead: the ψυχαί in Hades are εἴδωλα καμόντων, “phantoms of those who have done with toil” (23.72; Odyssey 11.476), and Achilles, waking from his dream of Patroclus, says that in the house of Hades there is “a soul and a phantom, but no mind in it at all” (23.103–104). Only twice does a god make a phantom of the living. Apollo makes this one of Aeneas, and in the Odyssey Athena makes a phantom in the likeness of Penelope's sister Iphthime, to comfort her in a dream (Odyssey 4.795–841).

**Why a phantom.** The armies are fighting over Aeneas' body. Aeneas himself fought over Pandarus' body a little earlier (5.297–302), and the Achaeans wanted the armor as much as the man: Diomedes was “ever eager to kill Aeneas and strip off his glorious armor” (5.434–435; see [[stripping-the-dead]]). The phantom keeps the fight where it was: Trojans and Achaeans go on hacking at each other's shields around it, while the man himself is healed far from the field. Neither side knows the truth, and Ares uses the fallen “Aeneas” to rouse the Trojans (see [[divine-rescue]] and [[divine-intervention]]).

**Aeneas saved.** Aeneas is saved three times in the Iliad, twice in this book, by his mother and then by Apollo, and in Book 20 by Poseidon, who lifts him out of the fight with Achilles because his line is fated to survive and to rule the Trojans (20.302–308, 20.318–329). The temple on Pergamus, the healing goddesses and the phantom are the most elaborate of the rescues (see [[aeneas]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Apollo deceives Achilles in a similar way in Book 21. He takes the shape of Agenor, whom he has just hidden in a mist, and leads Achilles away from the city in a chase across the plain, until the Trojans are safely inside the walls. Then he reveals himself: “You will not kill me, since I am not subject to fate” (21.596–22.13; see [[truth-and-deception]]). In the Odyssey the εἴδωλον of Heracles is in Hades, while “he himself” feasts among the immortal gods (Odyssey 11.601–604).

## Outside Homer

A phantom is at the center of another story of Troy. Stesichorus, in his palinode, said that Helen never went to Troy (Plato, *Phaedrus* 243a–b), and in Plato's *Republic* Socrates says that, according to Stesichorus, the men at Troy fought over her εἴδωλον, not knowing the truth (*Republic* 9.586c; see [[stesichorus-palinode]]). Euripides' *Helen* (412 BCE) puts the real Helen in Egypt while a phantom made by Hera goes to Troy with Paris (see [[helen-in-egypt]]).
