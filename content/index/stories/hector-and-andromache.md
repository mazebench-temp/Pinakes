## The story

**The search.** Hector comes home “to see my household and my dear wife and my infant son” (6.365–366), and she is not there. The housekeeper tells him that Andromache heard that the Trojans were hard pressed and has gone to the great tower of Ilios “like a woman out of her mind”, with the nurse carrying the child (6.369–389; see [[handmaids]] and [[like-a-woman-out-of-her-mind]]). He hurries back through the streets, and at the Scaean gates, where he means to go out to the plain, she comes running to meet him (6.390–394; see [[scaean-gates]]).

**The meeting.** Andromache, daughter of Eetion, king of Thebe under Placus, comes with a handmaid who holds the child, “like a lovely star”, whom Hector calls Scamandrius and the others Astyanax, “for Hector alone was guarding Ilios” (6.394–403; see [[andromache]], [[astyanax]] and [[like-a-star]]). Hector smiles at his son in silence. Andromache stands close, weeping, clings to his hand and speaks (6.404–406; see [[called-him-by-name]]).

**Her plea.** “Strange one, your own might will destroy you, and you have no pity for your infant son or for me, ill-fated, who soon will be your widow” (6.407–409). She would rather die than lose him. Achilles killed her father and her seven brothers when he sacked Thebe, and her mother, ransomed, died in her father's halls (6.410–428; see [[sack-of-thebe]] and [[eetion]]). So Hector is all she has:

{{quote:a07873ef-41d8-55e7-8356-abd8df4d97d6}}

Let him station the army by the wild fig tree, where the wall is easiest to scale and the best of the Achaeans have three times tried it (6.433–439; see [[the-fig-tree]]).

**His answer.**

{{quote:4042d2ed-5830-5c3d-9b18-dd32069bc8e0}}

He knows that a day will come when sacred Ilios will perish, and Priam, and his people (6.447–449; see [[a-day-will-come]]). What troubles him most is not the sorrow of the Trojans, nor of his parents and brothers, but hers:

{{quote:c04ff324-ea1c-581a-9a2a-1fd832e9ffad}}

**The child.** He reaches for his son, and the child shrinks back crying into the nurse's bosom, frightened by the bronze and the horsehair crest nodding from the top of the helmet. His father and mother laugh. Hector takes off the helmet, lays it gleaming on the ground, kisses the boy and dandles him in his arms, and prays (6.466–475; see [[helmet]]):

{{quote:203de985-14df-5cd6-b168-e655a58743e4}}

**The parting.** He gives the child to his wife, and she takes him to her fragrant bosom, “laughing through her tears” (δακρυόεν γελάσασα, 6.482–484). Hector pities her, strokes her with his hand, and comforts her: no man will send him down to Hades beyond his portion, and no man has ever escaped his fate, coward or brave (6.484–489; see [[no-man-escapes-his-fate]] and [[moira]]). Let her go home to her loom and distaff, “and war will be the concern of men” (6.490–493; see [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]]). He takes up his helmet. She goes home, “turning back again and again” (ἐντροπαλιζομένη), shedding big tears, and in the house her handmaids mourn Hector while he still lives, “for they thought he would never come back again from the war” (6.494–502; see [[lament]]).

## In the poem

**Husband and wife.** This is the only meeting of Hector and Andromache in the Iliad. She next sees him from the wall, dead and dragged behind Achilles' horses (22.462–465), and she speaks to him last over his body (24.723–745). The poem's name for the scene is its own: Hector talks with his wife “intimately” (ὀάριζε, 6.516), the talk of husband and wife (see [[title-of-book-6]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]). It takes place at the gate, between the house to which he sends her and the plain to which he goes.

**A lament before the death.** Andromache speaks as the women of the Iliad speak over the dead. She foresees her widowhood and her son's orphanhood, counts the family she has lost, and says what the dead man was to her (6.407–432; see [[lament]]). Briseis, mourning Patroclus, has the same story to tell: her husband killed before her city by Achilles, and her three brothers dead (19.291–296; see [[captive-women]]). Andromache's own laments come later, when she sees Hector dragged (22.477–514) and over his body (24.725–745). In Book 6 she mourns him alive, and her handmaids do the same when she comes home (6.500–502).

**Shame and fame.** Hector's answer turns on two words: αἰδέομαι, “I feel shame” before the Trojans and the Trojan women (6.442; see [[aidos]]), and κλέος, the fame he wins for his father and himself (6.446; see [[kleos]]). He has learned (μάθον) to be brave, always, among the foremost. The line of shame returns word for word when he stands alone before the Scaean gates, waiting for Achilles, and will not go in (22.105 = 6.442).

**What he foresees.** Hector knows how it will end. The lines on Troy's fall are the ones Agamemnon spoke over his wounded brother (6.447–449 = 4.163–165). He pictures Andromache in Argos, at another woman's loom, carrying water from a spring, and a man who sees her weeping and says, “This is the wife of Hector” (6.454–461; see [[messeis]] and [[hypereia]]). Her “day of freedom” will be taken away, and he will not be there to ward off “the day of slavery” (ἐλεύθερον ἦμαρ, δούλιον ἦμαρ, 6.455, 6.463; see [[eleutheros]], [[doulios]] and [[captive-women]]). He answers her fear of widowhood with his own fear of her slavery.

**The helmet and the prayer.** The one thing the child sees of his father is the bronze and the crest, and the father takes off the helmet to hold his son. The prayer that follows asks for the reverse of what he has just foreseen: that his son may be preeminent among the Trojans and rule Ilios with might, and come home from war with bloody spoils while someone says, “He is far better than his father” (see [[better-than-our-fathers]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). The optative makes the words a wish (see the translation's note on 6.479–480). The poem does not say whether the gods heard. In Book 24 Andromache foresees that some Achaean will take the child by the arm and throw him from the tower, in anger for a brother, a father or a son whom Hector killed (24.734–738; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).

**Laughter and tears.** The parents laugh at the child's fright (6.471), and Andromache laughs through her tears (6.484). The laughter comes in the middle of the book's darkest foresight (see [[tears]]).

**Her advice.** Andromache's advice to post the army by the fig tree (6.433–439) was rejected in antiquity by Aristarchus as unfit for a woman to give, and Hector's answer passes it over (see [[andromaches-advice]] and [[aristarchus]]). His last words send her back to the women's work: “the loom and the distaff” (6.491). Telemachus uses the same lines to his mother in the Odyssey, with “speech” and then “the bow” in place of war (*Odyssey* 1.356–359, 21.350–353).

## Outside Homer

Sappho told of the wedding. The herald Idaeus runs to Troy with the news that Hector and his companions are bringing the bright-eyed Andromache by ship from holy Thebe and the ever-flowing Placia, with golden bracelets and purple robes, and the city comes out to meet them (fr. 44 Voigt; see [[sappho]]).

The Iliad does not tell what became of Andromache and her son. In the *Iliupersis*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, Odysseus killed Astyanax, and Neoptolemus took Andromache as his prize. [[euripides|Euripides]] told her fate in two plays. In the *Trojan Women* (415 BC) the Greeks decide on Odysseus' advice to throw the child from the walls, and his body is brought back to Hecuba on Hector's shield; in the *Andromache* she is Neoptolemus' concubine in Phthia, threatened with death by his wife Hermione. [[virgil|Virgil]]'s Aeneas finds her at Buthrotum in Epirus, married to Helenus, pouring offerings at an empty tomb of Hector (*Aeneid* 3.294–505). Sophocles gave the scene to Ajax and his captive wife Tecmessa, who pleads that she has no one but him (*Ajax* 485–524), and to Ajax and his son: “Boy, may you be luckier than your father, but in all else like him” (*Ajax* 550–551). Racine's *Andromaque* (1667) sets her, a widow still, at the court of Pyrrhus.
