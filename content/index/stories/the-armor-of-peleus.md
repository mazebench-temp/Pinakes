## The story

{{quote:35c4ed99-f1a4-528c-a5d5-b53cf90e47fe}}

**The gift.** The gods gave the armor to Peleus. The narrator says so as Hector puts it on, and Achilles, when he has lost it, names the day: he tells his mother that the gods gave it to Peleus as splendid gifts on the day they put her in the bed of a mortal man (θεοὶ δόσαν ἀγλαὰ δῶρα, 18.84–85; see [[peleus]], [[thetis]] and [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]]). The same words describe the horses that Achilles brought to Troy, “immortal, the splendid gifts that the gods gave to Peleus” (16.381 = 16.867; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]), and Achilles uses them again to Priam: the gods gave Peleus splendid gifts from his birth, wealth and rule over the Myrmidons, and made a goddess the wife of a mortal man (24.534–537). The poem does not say who made the armor.

**From father to son.** Peleus “granted it to his son / when he grew old” (ὄπασσε, 17.196–197), and Achilles brought it to Troy. The spear came to him the same way: it is “the Pelian ash, which Chiron gave to his dear father” (16.143), and the two gifts are told in the same words, πατρὶ φίλῳ πόρε and πατρὶ φίλῳ ἔπορον (16.143, 17.196; see [[the-pelian-ash]] and [[chiron]]).

**Lent.** Nestor's plan was that Patroclus should go out in it, “in the hope that, taking you for him, they may hold off from the war” (11.798–799). Patroclus asks for it in nearly the same words (16.40–41), and Achilles grants it: “So put my glorious armor on your shoulders” (16.64). Patroclus arms in all of it but the spear, the corselet “of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus” among the rest (16.130–144; see [[arming-scene]]), and the Trojans, seeing him, think that Achilles has thrown away his wrath (16.278–283; see [[exchange-of-armor]]).

**Lost.** At Patroclus' last charge Apollo strikes the helmet from his head, the helmet that “guarded the head and the graceful brow of a divine man, / Achilles; but then Zeus gave it to Hector / to wear on his head, and his destruction was near him” (16.798–800). The shield falls, and the god loosens the corselet (16.801–804; see [[helmet]] and [[corselet]]). Patroclus, dying, says that the gods themselves took the armor from his shoulders (16.846). Hector strips it from the body and sends it to the city, “to be great fame for him” (17.125–131); then he runs after the men who carry it, puts it on in place of his own, and Zeus fits it to his body (17.183–214; see [[hector]] and [[zeus]]).

**Worn by Hector.** Hector wears it through the fight for Patroclus' body (17.450, 17.472–473; see [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]]). Thetis tells Achilles that Hector glories in it on his own shoulders, but not for long, for his death is near him (18.130–133), and she promises to bring him new armor from Hephaestus (18.136–137; see [[the-making-of-the-arms]]). Until she comes he cannot go into battle: the Trojans have his armor, and he knows no other man's that he could put on, except the shield of Ajax (18.188–193). She brings the new armor at dawn (19.10–12).

**Taken back.** When Achilles meets Hector at the Scaean gates, the armor that Hector stripped from Patroclus covers his whole body but the throat, where the collarbones part the neck from the shoulders, and there Achilles drives his spear (22.321–327). He strips it, bloody, from the dead man's shoulders and lifts it into his chariot (22.367–369, 22.399). The poem says no more of it.

## In the poem

**The son did not grow old.** The history is told in a few verses at the moment Hector puts the armor on, and its last verse turns on one verb: Peleus gave it to his son γηράς, “when he grew old”, “but the son did not grow old (ἐγήρα) in his father's armor” (17.196–197). Walter Leaf takes γηράς as a present participle of γηράω, of which ἐγήρα is the imperfect (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.197). The father's old age and the son's short life are one story (see [[the-short-life-of-achilles]], [[death-of-achilles-foretold]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). Thetis tells Hephaestus that Peleus lies in his halls, worn out by grievous old age (18.434–435), and Achilles tells Priam that Peleus had one son, born to die untimely (παναώριον), and that the son does not care for his father as he grows old, sitting far from home at Troy (24.538–542).

**Immortal things in a mortal house.** ἄμβροτα τεύχεα, “immortal armor” (17.194, 17.202), is said of no other armor in Homer. In the Iliad ἄμβροτος, “immortal”, is said of gods and of their blood (5.339, 5.870; 20.358, 22.9, 24.460), of the garments in which Apollo dresses the body of Sarpedon (16.670, 16.680), and of the horses and the armor that the gods gave Peleus (16.381, 17.194). Zeus asks the horses why the gods gave them “to lord Peleus, / a mortal, while you are ageless and immortal” (17.443–444), and the question fits the armor as well: what the gods gave the father, the son loses with his friend (see [[immortal-and-ageless]]).

**The look of Achilles.** The armor carries its owner's look. In it the Trojans take Patroclus for Achilles (16.278–283), and Hector, putting it on, appears to the allies “flashing in the armor of the great-hearted son of Peleus” (17.214; see [[exchange-of-armor]]). The gods are not deceived: Apollo strikes it from Patroclus, and Zeus, watching Hector arm, sees a man near his death (16.793–800, 17.198–208).

**No homecoming.** Zeus gives Hector great power in recompense for a journey the armor will not make, “since you will not come home from the battle / for Andromache to receive from you the glorious armor of the son of Peleus” (17.207–208; see [[poine]], [[nostos]] and [[hector-and-andromache]]). μάχης ἐκνοστήσαντι, “come home from the battle”, is said three times in the Iliad, always of Hector: by Zeus here; of the bath that Andromache, not knowing that he is dead, has her handmaids heat for him (22.442–446); and by Cassandra, when she calls the Trojans to see him brought home dead, if ever they rejoiced in him when he came home alive from battle (24.704–706). The phrase was used before in the dual, with the same verb “receive”, of the two sons of old Phaenops, whom their father “did not receive … back alive, returned from battle” (5.157–158; see [[phaenops]]).

## Outside Homer

Apollodorus, telling the wedding of Peleus and Thetis on Pelion, names two gifts: Chiron gave Peleus an ash spear, and Poseidon the immortal horses Balius and Xanthus. He says nothing of armor (*Library* 3.13.5; see [[apollodorus]] and [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]]).
