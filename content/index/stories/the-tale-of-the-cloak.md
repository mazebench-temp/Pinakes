## The story

**The night.** After supper in the hut “an evil night came on, a night without a moon”, with rain all night and a great West Wind, and Odysseus spoke to Eumaeus and his men, “testing the swineherd, / whether he would take off his cloak and give it to him, or urge one of his companions / to do so” (*Odyssey* 14.457–461; see [[peira]] and [[eumaeus]]). He blames the wine, which “sets even a man of much wisdom to sing / and to laugh softly, and stirs him up to dance, / and brings out a word that is better left unsaid” (*Odyssey* 14.462–467; see [[the-mad-wine]]), and wishes that he were young again, as he was when he lay in ambush at Troy (*Odyssey* 14.468–469; see [[if-only-i-were-young]]).

**The ambush.** Odysseus and Menelaus led the ambush, and the teller was third in command. They lay around the city in the thick brushwood, among the reeds and the marsh, crouching under their armor, when a freezing night came on with the North Wind; snow fell like hoarfrost, and ice grew thick on their shields. The others slept wrapped in their cloaks, but he had left his with his companions, “in my folly”, and had come with only his shield and his kilt (*Odyssey* 14.469–482; see [[ambush]], [[odysseus]], [[menelaus]], [[snow-like-hoarfrost]] and [[kilt]]). In the third part of the night he nudged Odysseus with his elbow: the cold was killing him, “for I have no cloak” (*Odyssey* 14.483–489). Then:

**The point.** “If only I were young like that now, and my strength were steadfast; / then someone in the farmstead of the swineherds would give me a cloak, / for both reasons, out of friendship and respect for a good man; / but now they dishonor me, because I have wretched clothes on my body” (*Odyssey* 14.503–506; see [[if-only-i-were-young]]). Eumaeus answers, “Old man, the tale you have told is flawless”, and promises him clothing for the night, though he must go back to his rags at dawn, since each man in the hut has only one cloak, and Telemachus will clothe him when he comes. He makes him a bed by the fire and throws over him the thick cloak he keeps for storms, and goes out himself to sleep beside the boars (*Odyssey* 14.507–533; see [[ainos]], [[rags]] and [[cloak]]).

## In the poem

**A hint, not a request.** The tale asks for a cloak without asking. Its last three verses say what it means, and ancient critics rejected them for that reason: D. B. Monro finds them “weak in themselves” and thinks that they “spoil the preceding story, the point of which is that it hints at the need of a cloak without directly asking for one” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 14.504–506). Eumaeus takes the hint, and calls the story an αἶνος (*Odyssey* 14.508; see [[ainos]]).

**A trick about a trick.** The beggar's story is a device to get a cloak from his host, and its subject is a device to get a cloak at Troy. In the tale Odysseus, “such a man was he at planning and at fighting”, has “this thought” at once (*Odyssey* 14.490–491), which Monro explains as “the device of sending Thoas” (on *Odyssey* 14.490): a dream that never came, a call for help from the ships, and a comrade who leaves his cloak behind as he runs. The teller is Odysseus himself, praising his own quickness under his own name to a man who does not know him, and the story, like the Cretan tale he told earlier in the day, is set in the Trojan war, as all his false stories are, Monro observes, citing this one among them (Appendix I §3 and note; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]).

**The night of the Iliad.** The tale is set on a night like that of *Iliad* 10: an ambush near the enemy, the cold, the chiefs woken in the dark, who dress in cloaks and skins, and Nestor's “crimson cloak, / double and wide” (*Iliad* 10.21–24, 10.131–136, 10.177–178; see [[the-doloneia]] and [[the-doloneia-question]]). The third part of the night is reckoned as Odysseus reckons it there, when two parts of it are gone (*Odyssey* 14.483; *Iliad* 10.251–253). Odysseus' dream is announced in the verse in which Agamemnon tells the Achaeans of the Dream that Zeus sent him (*Odyssey* 14.495 = *Iliad* 2.56; see [[agamemnons-dream]] and [[dream]]), and his first words, “Be silent now, so that no other man of the Achaeans hears you” (*Odyssey* 14.493), are nearly those with which he silenced Agamemnon's talk of flight (*Iliad* 14.90). Thoas rises as he rose with Odysseus to face Hector in the duel (*Odyssey* 14.499; *Iliad* 7.161–168; see [[thoas]] and [[contest-scene]]), and runs to the ships, leaving his cloak, as Odysseus once ran through the camp and threw off his own (*Odyssey* 14.500–501; *Iliad* 2.182–184; see [[crimson]]).

## Outside Homer

**Strabo.** Strabo, following Demetrius of Scepsis, argues that the Ilium of his own day cannot stand on the site of Homer's Troy, since it lies so near the old naval station of the Achaeans, while the poet puts a great distance between the ships and the city. Among his proofs is this tale: “the narrative to Eumaeus, contrived by Odysseus, shows the distance from the naval station to the city to be great”, ἡ διήγησις … ἡ πρὸς τὸν Εὔμαιον ὑπὸ τοῦ Ὀδυσσέως διασκευασθεῖσα. He quotes the verse of the ambush beneath Troy, and then, “for he says further on”, “For we have come very far from the ships” (*Geography* 13.1.36, quoting *Odyssey* 14.469 and 14.496; see [[strabo]] and [[troy]]). Beside it he sets the spies sent out in *Iliad* 10 to learn whether the Trojans mean to stay by the ships or go back to the city, and Polydamas' warning, “we are far from the wall” (*Iliad* 10.208–210, 18.254–256).

**Hesiod's winter.** Hesiod's advice for the winter month has the tale's needs and its words. Against the North Wind a man must put on “a protection for the body, a soft cloak and a tunic reaching to the feet”, χλαῖνάν τε μαλακὴν καὶ τερμιόεντα χιτῶνα, “for cold is the dawn when the North Wind has fallen”, ψυχρὴ γάρ τʼ ἠὼς πέλεται Βορέαο πεσόντος (*Works and Days* 536–547; see [[hesiod]]). The tale's night comes on Βορέαο πεσόντος, “when the North Wind fell” (*Odyssey* 14.475), the words in which Hesiod describes the cold of dawn, and its want is the cloak and tunic that all the others had (*Odyssey* 14.478).
