## The story

{{quote:e520811a-5314-54b3-8de3-4c652681acbc}}

She finds the suitors at their game pieces in front of the doors, sitting on the hides of oxen they have killed, while heralds and attendants mix wine and set out tables (*Odyssey* 1.106–112; see [[the-suitors]], [[game-pieces]] and [[mentes-the-taphian]]). Telemachus, sitting among them and seeing his father in his mind, is the first to see her. Indignant that a stranger should stand long at the doors, he takes her right hand and her spear and welcomes her, meal first and questions after (*Odyssey* 1.113–124; see [[xenia]]). He sets the spear in the rack among the spears of Odysseus, seats the guest on a fine chair with a footstool, apart from the suitors, so that the stranger should not loathe the meal among overbearing men and so that he may ask about his father; a handmaid brings water for the hands, the housekeeper bread, a carver meat, and a herald pours wine (*Odyssey* 1.125–143; see [[spear-rack]] and [[hand-washing]]). The suitors come in to their meal, and afterwards the herald gives the lyre to Phemius, who sings among them under compulsion (*Odyssey* 1.144–155; see [[phemius]]).

Telemachus leans close and speaks of the suitors, who eat another man's substance while that man's bones rot in the rain or roll in the sea, and asks the stranger who he is, and whether he is a new visitor or a guest-friend of the house (*Odyssey* 1.156–177). The goddess answers with a tale. She is Mentes, son of Anchialus, lord of the Taphians, sailing to Temese with iron to trade for bronze; her ship lies in the harbor of Rheithron, under wooded Neion; the two houses have been guest-friends since their fathers' time, as old Laertes, now living on his farm, could tell. She came because they said Odysseus was home; the gods are hindering him, but he is alive, held on a sea-washed island, and she prophesies that he will not be away much longer, “since he is a man of many devices” (*Odyssey* 1.179–205; see [[taphians]], [[temese]], [[rheithron]], [[laertes]] and [[divination]]). Then she asks whether this tall young man is truly the son of Odysseus, so like him in his head and his fine eyes (*Odyssey* 1.206–212). Telemachus answers:

{{quote:f79afc23-3e13-5a0b-a4fd-9dc4d1950199}}

She tells him that the gods have not made his line nameless, since Penelope bore such a son, and asks what feast this is, “A banquet, or a wedding? For this is surely no shared feast” (*Odyssey* 1.222–229). Telemachus answers with his grief. The house was likely to be rich and flawless while his father was at home; had he died at Troy, the Achaeans would have made a tomb for him, and he would have won great fame for his son, but the Harpies have snatched him away without report; and the best men of Dulichium, Same, Zacynthus and Ithaca woo his mother and wear down his house, while she neither refuses the marriage nor can make an end (*Odyssey* 1.231–251; see [[kleos]] and [[penelope]]).

Athena chafes. If only Odysseus would come and stand at the doors with helmet, shield and two spears, as she first saw him in her father's house on his way back from Ephyra, “they would all be swift-fated, and bitter would their wedding be” (*Odyssey* 1.253–266; see [[odysseus-seeks-poison]]). But that lies on the knees of the gods (*Odyssey* 1.267; see [[on-the-knees-of-the-gods]]). Her counsel follows. Tomorrow he is to call the Achaeans to an assembly and bid the suitors scatter to their homes, and his mother, if she wants to marry, is to go back to her father's hall; he himself is to fit out a ship with twenty oarsmen and go to Pylos and to Sparta for news of his father. If he hears that Odysseus is alive, he can endure another year; if he hears that he is dead, he is to come home, heap up a mound, give him funeral rites and give his mother to a husband (*Odyssey* 1.269–292; see [[assembly]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). Then:

{{quote:18798678-88e6-56ef-a32a-1cca9cec6616}}

Orestes won fame among all men when he killed his father's slayer: let Telemachus, tall and handsome as he is, be valiant too (*Odyssey* 1.298–302; see [[orestes]] and [[aegisthus]]). She must go down to her ship and her companions (*Odyssey* 1.303–305). Telemachus thanks the stranger, who has spoken “as a father to his son”, and presses him to stay, bathe, and take a gift home, “such as dear guest-friends give to guest-friends”; the goddess asks to receive it on her way back (*Odyssey* 1.307–318; see [[doron]] and [[guest-gifts]]). So saying she goes away, “and like a bird she flew upward and away”, and puts might and daring in his heart; he perceives it, is amazed, and thinks that it was a god (*Odyssey* 1.319–323; see [[like-a-bird]] and [[divine-epiphany]]). “And at once he went among the suitors, a man equal to the gods” (*Odyssey* 1.324; see [[equal-to-the-gods]]).

## In the poem

**The plan carried out.** In the council Athena said that she would go to Ithaca “so that I may urge his son on / the more, and put might in his heart” (μένος ἐν φρεσὶ θείω, *Odyssey* 1.88–89); when she leaves, “in his heart / she put might and daring” (θῆκε μένος καὶ θάρσος, *Odyssey* 1.320–321; see [[menos]], [[tharsos]] and [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). The rest of the book shows the change. He sends his mother back to her rooms, to her amazement, and tells the suitors that in the assembly he will order them out of his house (*Odyssey* 1.345–380; see [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]).

**The stranger's tale.** The goddess's account of herself is a false tale, made of the facts of a sea trader's life and of the forms of guest-friendship: a name, a father, a people, a cargo, a harbor and an old bond between the houses (see [[truth-and-deception]] and [[xenia]]). It also makes room for the truth. In Mentes' person she says that Odysseus is alive and held on an island, and gives it as a prophecy, “though I am no seer and have no clear knowledge of birds” (*Odyssey* 1.196–205). Telemachus repeats her tale to Eurymachus, “but in his heart he knew the immortal goddess” (*Odyssey* 1.417–420; see [[taphos]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]).

**Two counsels.** Athena's advice has been found hard to reconcile with itself. She bids Telemachus send the suitors home and his mother to her father (*Odyssey* 1.274–278), then gives him a second course for the case that his father is dead: give his mother to a husband, and then plan how to kill the suitors (*Odyssey* 1.289–296). Merry reports that Nauck marks *Odyssey* 1.293–302 as spurious, and Hentze and Ameis with him, as inconsistent with the advice of *Odyssey* 1.274 and the lines after it (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.293; see the translation's note on 1.293–302). The mother's going back to her father is itself hard to construe: Merry explains the anacoluthon of μητέρα δʼ … ἂψ ἴτω, and takes the men who will make ready the bride-price to be the suitors, where Nitzsch, after Eustathius, took them to be the bride's family (on 1.275 and 1.277; see the translation's note on 1.275–278).

**The departure.** ὄρνις δʼ ὣς ἀνόπαια διέπτατο (*Odyssey* 1.320) was disputed in antiquity: ἀνόπαια was taken as the name of a bird, as “unseen”, as “through the smoke-vent”, or as “upward”, which Merry prefers (on 1.320; see the translation's note on 1.320 and [[like-a-bird]]). He notes that at Pylos Athena leaves in the likeness of a bird, φήνῃ εἰδομένη, and all who see it are amazed (*Odyssey* 3.371–373), while here Telemachus only ponders her sudden going.
