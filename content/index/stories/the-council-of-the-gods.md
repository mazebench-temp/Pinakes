## The story

Poseidon has gone to the far-off Ethiopians, “who are divided in two, the farthest of men”, to share in a hecatomb of bulls and rams, and sits at their feast (*Odyssey* 1.22–26; see [[poseidon]], [[ethiopians]] and [[zeus-among-the-ethiopians]]). The other gods are gathered “in the halls of Olympian Zeus”, and the father of men and gods speaks first, for he remembers Aegisthus, whom Orestes, the son of Agamemnon, has killed (*Odyssey* 1.26–31; see [[zeus]], [[olympians]], [[aegisthus]] and [[orestes]]):

{{quote:1645d478-ddf8-5153-88a0-08b2242fcf43}}

Aegisthus is his example. He married the wife of the son of Atreus and killed him on his return, “knowing it was sheer destruction”, since the gods had sent Hermes to warn him “neither to kill the man himself nor to woo his wife”: vengeance would come from Orestes. Hermes did not persuade him, “and now he has paid back all at once” (*Odyssey* 1.35–43; see [[hermes]]).

Athena takes up the example and turns it. Aegisthus “lies fallen in a fitting destruction; / so may any other perish too who did such things”; but her heart is torn for Odysseus, held far from his friends on a sea-washed island by the daughter of Atlas, who keeps him with gentle and wheedling talk so that he may forget Ithaca, while he longs to die (*Odyssey* 1.45–59; see [[calypso]], [[atlas]] and [[ogygia]]). Did he not please Zeus with sacrifices at Troy? “Why are you so angry with him, Zeus?” (*Odyssey* 1.60–62). Zeus answers that he could not forget Odysseus, “who is first among mortals in mind, and first in sacrifices / given to the immortal gods” (*Odyssey* 1.64–67). The anger is Poseidon's:

{{quote:ad8a8a30-fde5-5e81-be18-d99f13a91350}}

Since then Poseidon does not kill Odysseus, but drives him wandering from his native land. Let all of them take thought for his homecoming: Poseidon will let go his anger, for he will not be able to strive alone against all the immortals (*Odyssey* 1.74–79; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]] and [[nostos]]).

Athena sets out the plan. Hermes is to go to the island of Ogygia and tell the nymph “our unerring counsel, / the homecoming of stout-hearted Odysseus”; she herself will go to Ithaca to rouse his son, so that he calls the Achaeans to an assembly and speaks out to the suitors, and she will send him to Sparta and to sandy Pylos to learn of his father's homecoming, “and so that noble fame may be his among men” (*Odyssey* 1.81–95; see [[telemachus]], [[the-suitors]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). Then she binds on her golden sandals, takes her spear, and goes darting down from the peaks of Olympus (*Odyssey* 1.96–102; see [[athena-visits-telemachus]]).

## In the poem

**While Poseidon is away.** All the gods pity Odysseus “except Poseidon” (*Odyssey* 1.19–20), and the council meets while he is feasting at the ends of the earth. The Iliad's first book sends Zeus to the Ethiopians, and all the gods with him, so that Thetis must wait twelve days before she can plead for her son (*Iliad* 1.423–427, 1.493–495). Here one god is away, and the others decide without him.

**Mortals and the gods.** Zeus' complaint takes up the word of the proem. The companions of Odysseus perished “through their own recklessness” (σφετέρῃσιν ἀτασθαλίῃσιν, *Odyssey* 1.7), and mortals, Zeus says, “through their own recklessness have sorrows against fate” (σφῇσιν ἀτασθαλίῃσιν, *Odyssey* 1.34; see [[atasthalie]], [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]). Merry calls ὑπὲρ μόρον a popular solution of the difficulty of reconciling divine power with human free will: a certain amount of fate is ordained as each man's lot, but his own misconduct may aggravate or hasten it (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.34; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 1.34). He takes ἡμεῖς, “we ourselves”, as emphatic: the gods, in spite of the reproaches of mortals, were not the cause of Aegisthus' end, since they had warned him (on 1.37).

**Aegisthus and the house of Odysseus.** The example bears on the story that follows. Aegisthus was warned not to woo the wife of an absent king, and μνάασθαι, “to woo” (*Odyssey* 1.39), is the verb that gives the suitors their name, μνηστῆρες (see [[the-suitors]]). Athena will hold up the other side of the story to Telemachus, the fame that Orestes won “among all men, when he killed his father's slayer” (*Odyssey* 1.298–302; see [[athena-visits-telemachus]]).

**Two councils.** The plan is carried out in two stages, and the second waits four books. At the opening of *Odyssey* 5 the gods sit in council again; Athena recounts Odysseus' sorrows and adds that the suitors are plotting to kill his son on his way home (*Odyssey* 5.3–20). Zeus answers with the verse of the first council, “My child, what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!” (*Odyssey* 5.22 = 1.64), reminds her that the plan was her own, that Odysseus should come home and take vengeance on the suitors (*Odyssey* 5.23–24, repeated at 24.479–480), and sends Hermes to the nymph with the words Athena had proposed (*Odyssey* 5.28–31, as *Odyssey* 1.86–87; see [[hermes]]). Telemachus' journey fills the time between the two (see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).
