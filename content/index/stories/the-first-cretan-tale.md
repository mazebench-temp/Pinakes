## The story

On the shore of Ithaca Athena, in the likeness of a young herdsman, has just told Odysseus the name of his island (see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). He is glad, “yet he did not speak the truth, but took back his word, / always turning a very crafty mind in his breast” (*Odyssey* 13.250–255):

{{quote:3f2771df-f9e7-587f-8c97-dc0431d7de32}}

After the killing, he says, he went at once to a ship of noble Phoenicians, begged them to take him on board and gave them spoil to satisfy their hearts, and bade them set him down at Pylos or at shining Elis, where the Epeians rule; but the force of the wind drove them away from there, against their will, for they did not wish to deceive him, and they came to this harbor by night. No one thought of supper; they all went ashore and lay down, and sweet sleep came upon him, worn out as he was. Then the Phoenicians took his goods out of the ship, set them down where he lay on the sands, and went aboard for well-peopled Sidon; and he was left behind, grieving at heart (*Odyssey* 13.271–286; see [[phoenicians]], [[pylos]], [[elis]] and [[sidon]]).

“So he spoke, and the gray-eyed goddess Athena smiled” (*Odyssey* 13.287). She strokes him with her hand, takes the likeness of a tall and beautiful woman, and tells him that he would be a crafty cheat who could get past him in tricks, even a god; and he, even in his own land, will not leave off his deceits and thievish words, “which are dear to you from the ground up” (*Odyssey* 13.288–295; see [[crafty-would-he-be]], [[athena]] and [[dolos]]).

## In the poem

**Made of the truth.** The lie is built of what the stranger can see and of what has just happened. A man alone on the shore with a heap of goods and no ship needs a story; Odysseus gives himself a ship that has gone, sailors who left him asleep on the sand with his goods beside him (*Odyssey* 13.282–284), as the Phaeacians did a few hours before (*Odyssey* 13.116–123). Its verses are borrowed from his own story: “Then sweet sleep came upon me, worn out as I was” is the verse of the sleep in which the companions opened the skin of the winds (*Odyssey* 13.282 = 10.31; see [[odysseus-and-aeolus]]), and “passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves” is the verse in which the poet, a few hours before, described the sleeper on the Phaeacian ship (*Odyssey* 13.264 = 13.91; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). It caps Athena's last words: she said that the name of Ithaca had reached even to Troy; he answers that he heard of it even in wide Crete (*Odyssey* 13.248, 13.256).

**Troy and Crete.** D. B. Monro observes that the false stories of Odysseus all turn upon events and characters of the Trojan war, and he lists this tale first among them, with the two tales to Eumaeus and the tale to Penelope (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix I §3 and note, citing *Odyssey* 13.256, 14.235, 14.468, 19.172). Here the quarrel is over spoil from Troy, and its cause is a service refused at Troy: the Cretan would not serve the father as his attendant, but led companions of his own (*Odyssey* 13.262–266; see [[idomeneus]] and [[orsilochus-son-of-idomeneus]]). The Cretan of the next book, telling his tale to Eumaeus, was a bastard son of [[castor-son-of-hylax|Castor, son of Hylax]], who led the Cretan ships to Ilios with Idomeneus, and after the war came to grief in Egypt, and was carried off by a Phoenician “skilled in deceits” who meant to sell him in Libya (*Odyssey* 14.199–359; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]), where the Phoenicians of this tale “did not wish to deceive me” (*Odyssey* 13.277). To Penelope he will be Aethon, the younger brother of Idomeneus, who entertained Odysseus in Crete on his way to Troy (*Odyssey* 19.172–202, a plain rendering), and the poet will say of that tale, “He made the many lies he told like the truth” (*Odyssey* 19.203, a plain rendering). Eumaeus repeats the Cretan's claim to Telemachus, “He claims that his line is from wide Crete” (*Odyssey* 16.62, a plain rendering).

**A tale for a god.** The tale is told to the one hearer who knows it to be false, and it fails. The goddess smiles and answers with praise of the liar: “Crafty would he be, and a cheat, who should get past you / in tricks of every kind, even if it were a god who met you” (*Odyssey* 13.291–292). The contest ends in a truce between equals, “since we both know / crafty ways” (*Odyssey* 13.296–297; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]), and the disguised beggar's later tales are told to mortals who believe them, or half believe (see [[truth-and-deception]]).

## Outside Homer

**The Hymn to Apollo.** The *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* has the tale's elements in a meeting of its own. Cretans “from Minoan Cnossus”, sailing in a black ship to sandy Pylos, are carried past it against their will by the god himself, past Elis, “where the Epeians rule”, and within sight of the steep mountain of Ithaca, to Crisa (*Hymn to Apollo* 391–439); there Apollo, in the likeness of a young man in his first youth, asks them who they are, and their leader answers that they were sailing to Pylos from Crete, “from where we claim our line”, and came to this place against their will (*Hymn to Apollo* 448–473; see [[strangers-who-are-you]]). The verse of Elis, “where the Epeians rule”, is the tale's (*Hymn to Apollo* 426; *Odyssey* 13.275), and the Cretan's question to the god is Odysseus' question to the disguised Athena (*Hymn to Apollo* 467–468; *Odyssey* 13.232–233).

**Cretans and liars.** The Suda explains κρητίζειν, “to play the Cretan”, as “to lie”, from a story that Idomeneus, entrusted with dividing the bronze from the spoils, took the best for himself, and gives another proverb, “to play the Cretan against Cretans”, since they are liars and cheats (Suda κ 2407). In Odysseus' tale the son of Idomeneus tries to rob the Cretan of all his spoil from Troy (*Odyssey* 13.262–263).

**On the stage.** A single line survives from Aeschylus' *Penelope*: ἐγὼ γένος μέν εἰμι Κρὴς ἀρχέστατον, “By birth I am a Cretan, of the first stock” (Aeschylus, *Penelope*, fr. 187 Nauck; see [[aeschylus]]). Its speaker is not named; the disguised Odysseus, telling his Cretan tale to Penelope, is the likeliest.

**Lies like the truth.** Strabo, arguing that Homer built his fictions on facts, quotes the verse on the tale to Penelope, ἴσκε ψεύδεα πολλὰ λέγων ἐτύμοισιν ὁμοῖα, and adds, “not all, but many; for otherwise they would not have been like the truth”: a man lies more persuasively, as Polybius says of the wanderings of Odysseus, if he mixes in some of the truth (*Geography* 1.2.9; see [[strabo]]). Plato's Hippias holds that Homer made Odysseus πολύτροπός τε καὶ ψευδής, “of many turns and false”, and made him so often, in the Iliad and in the Odyssey (*Hippias Minor* 365b–c; see [[plato-hippias-minor]]). For Aristotle's praise of Homer's art of lying, see [[homer-taught-lies]].

**A true story from the lie.** In a rationalized account of the return preserved in the Suda, the persons of the lie become the facts: Phoenicians find Odysseus adrift on a plank after Charybdis and bring him to Idomeneus in Crete, who keeps him through the winter and sends him on to Phaeacia (Suda χ 144; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]).
