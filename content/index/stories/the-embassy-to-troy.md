## The story

Helen has just named Odysseus for Priam as a man who “knows all kinds of tricks and shrewd counsels” (3.202). Antenor, sitting among the elders, confirms it from what he saw himself.

{{quote:44f89616-789d-51d9-8c87-874d535a7106}}

σεῦ ἕνεκʼ ἀγγελίης is “on an embassy about you”; Aristarchus took ἀγγελίης as a masculine noun, “as a messenger about you”. The envoys stayed in Antenor's house as his guests (ἐξείνισσα, “I entertained them”; see [[xenia]]), and he came to know “the build of both of them and their shrewd counsels”. Then they spoke before the Trojans in assembly. Standing, Menelaus was taller by his broad shoulders; seated, Odysseus was the more stately. Menelaus spoke fluently, “few words but very clear”. Odysseus stood with his eyes on the ground and the scepter held stiff, like a man who knew nothing, until he let the great voice go from his chest and words like the snowflakes of winter, and then no mortal could rival him (3.209–224; see [[like-a-man-who-knows-nothing]] and [[snowflakes-of-winter]]).

Antenor does not say how the embassy ended, and Book 3 does not need him to: Helen is standing beside him on the wall. Book 11 adds the Trojan side. Antimachus, who had taken gold from Alexander, would not let Helen be given back to Menelaus (11.123–125), and he urged the Trojan assembly to kill Menelaus on the spot when he came as an envoy with Odysseus, and not let him go back to the Achaeans (11.138–142). Agamemnon kills his two sons for it.

## In the poem

The embassy belongs to the time before the fighting, when the quarrel was still a matter for words. The envoys were received as guests, as Paris had once been received by Menelaus, and a Trojan assembly heard them and chose war. The duel of Book 3 is a second attempt to settle the same claim in a single encounter, and it fails as the first did.

The story is told for Odysseus' sake. Priam has asked who the broad-shouldered man is, and Helen has named him; Antenor turns the name into a portrait, and the Trojans' first memory of the man who will one day take their city is of a speaker they underrated (see [[odysseus]]). It also tells something about Antenor. The elder who entertained the envoys is the Trojan who later proposes to give Helen back, because Troy is fighting after breaking its oaths (7.347–353; see [[antenor]]).

## Outside Homer

The *Cypria*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, placed the embassy after the landing at Troy. The Trojans opposed the landing, Protesilaus was killed by Hector, and Achilles routed them by killing Cycnus, son of Poseidon; the Achaeans took up their dead and sent envoys to the Trojans demanding Helen and the possessions, and when the Trojans refused, they laid siege to the city. The summary does not name the envoys.

Bacchylides made the embassy the subject of a dithyramb, *The Sons of Antenor, or The Demand for Helen's Return*, in which Antenor's wife Theano receives the envoys and Menelaus warns the Trojans against insolence (Bacchylides 15; see [[bacchylides-embassy]]). Herodotus' Egyptian priests told him that the envoys, with Menelaus among them, were told that Helen was not in Troy at all but in Egypt (2.118; see [[helen-in-egypt]]).

Antenor's hospitality was remembered at the sack. Strabo says that the Greeks spared Antenor and his sons because Menelaus had been his guest, and that Sophocles, in his play on the capture of Troy, had a leopard's skin hung before Antenor's door as a sign that his house was not to be plundered (13.1.53). Livy begins his history with the same tradition: the Greeks spared Aeneas and Antenor because of an old tie of hospitality and because both had always urged peace and the return of Helen (1.1.1).
