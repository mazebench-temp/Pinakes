## The story

{{quote:f27e722e-0793-5781-bd01-1ef76081bef8}}

The gods and men are asleep, but Zeus is awake, pondering how to honor Achilles and destroy many Achaeans beside the ships, as he promised Thetis (1.508–510, 1.524–530; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). The best plan is to send a baneful Dream (οὖλον ὄνειρον; see [[dream]] and [[baneful]]). He gives it the message: Agamemnon is to arm the long-haired Achaeans with all speed, for now he may take the city; the gods are no longer divided, since Hera has bent them all by her pleading (2.8–15).

The Dream finds Agamemnon asleep in his hut and stands above his head in the likeness of [[Nestor]], “whom Agamemnon honored most of all the elders”. It rebukes him for sleeping (see [[a-man-of-counsel-should-not-sleep]]), gives Zeus' message word for word, and adds two things of its own: the sorrows hanging over the Trojans come “from Zeus”, and he must not forget (2.23–34; see [[dream-scene]] and [[message-repeated]]). Then it is gone.

{{quote:130425b2-40e3-5355-8921-905c49fc817a}}

Agamemnon wakes with the divine voice poured around him, dresses, takes up the scepter of his fathers, and at dawn calls the elders to Nestor's ship. He tells them the dream, repeating it almost line for line, and announces that he will first test the army (2.56–75; see [[the-test-of-the-army]]). Nestor answers that from any other man they would call the dream a lie, but the man who saw it claims to be the best of the Achaeans (2.79–82).

## In the poem

The Dream sets the war going again. Zeus' plan requires Achaean defeats, and a battle is needed for that; the promise of a quick victory brings Agamemnon out to fight. The narrator says at once where it leads. Agamemnon is νήπιος, “the fool” (see [[nepios]]), who thinks he will take Priam's city that very day and does not know that Zeus means to lay sorrows and groaning on Trojans and Danaans alike.

Strictly, the message is less than a lie and more than one. “Now he may take the city” (νῦν γάρ κεν ἕλοι) promises a possibility, and Agamemnon hears “that very day” (2.37). But the reason the Dream gives, that the gods are now of one mind, is the opposite of the truth: Zeus has just agreed to help the Trojans. Achilles had said in Book 1 that “a dream, too, comes from Zeus” (see [[a-dream-comes-from-zeus]]). This one does, and it deceives. Agamemnon's own words in the test are truer than he knows: Zeus “has devised an evil deceit” (κακὴν ἀπάτην, 2.114; see [[apate]] and [[truth-and-deception]]).

The Dream borrows the shape of the elder Agamemnon honors most, and Nestor himself then vouches for the dream, because of who saw it. The poem's first deception by a god works through the best counselor in the army.

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates will not praise the dream Zeus sent to Agamemnon, since a god does not lie (*Republic* 2, 383a; see [[plato-lying-dream]]).

Aristotle quotes the opening line with “all the gods” for “the other gods”, and defends “all” as a metaphor for “many” (*Poetics* 25, 1461a16–20; see [[all-the-gods-slept]]).

Aristotle reports that Hippias of Thasos met the charge of a lying Zeus by changing an accent in the line Aristotle read in the Dream's message, so that the words became an order to the Dream and not a promise by Zeus (*Poetics* 25, 1461a21–23; *Sophistical Refutations* 4, 166b6–8; see [[aristotle-and-hippias]]).
