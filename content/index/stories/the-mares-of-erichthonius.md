## The story

{{quote:848117bf-f5e4-562d-8f08-1bfec6d53990}}

[[Erichthonius]], son of Dardanus, was “the richest of mortal men” (20.220), and his wealth was in horses: three thousand mares, “glorying in their tender foals”, were pastured along the marshland. The North Wind fell in love with them as they grazed and lay with them in the likeness of a stallion, and they conceived and bore twelve foals. When the foals bounded over the earth they ran on the tips of the ripe ears and did not break them, and when they bounded over the sea they ran on the crests of the breakers (see [[boreas]] and [[horses]]).

## In the poem

**In Aeneas' genealogy.** Aeneas tells the story as he recites his lineage to Achilles, between the birth of Erichthonius and the birth of his son Tros (20.219–230; see [[genealogy-in-battle]] and [[dardanus]]). Apart from the carrying off of Ganymede (20.232–235; see [[ganymede]]) it is the only story in the genealogy, and it is a story of horses. The house of Troy has horses of divine stock: Zeus later gave Tros a breed, “the best / of all horses under the dawn and the sun”, in recompense for Ganymede, and Anchises bred from it in secret (5.265–272; see [[ganymede-and-the-horses-of-tros]] and [[anchises-steals-the-horses]]). The horses of Achilles were foaled in the same way, by the Harpy Podarge to the West Wind as she grazed in a meadow beside the stream of Oceanus (16.149–151; see [[podarge]], [[zephyrus]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Walter Leaf notes that the belief that mares could conceive by the wind was widespread in antiquity, but thinks that here it need be no more than a mythological way of expressing extreme speed (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.223).

**A god's love.** The verses tell the North Wind's love in the poem's language for a god's love of a mortal woman. The North Wind ἠράσσατο, “fell in love” (20.223), the verb of Hermes' love for Polymele (16.182; see [[eros]]). ἵππῳ δʼ εἰσάμενος παρελέξατο κυανοχαίτῃ, “and in the likeness of a dark-maned stallion he lay with them” (20.224): so in the Odyssey Poseidon, in the likeness of the river Enipeus, lay with Tyro, who had loved the river, and she conceived (ὑποκυσαμένη) and bore Pelias and Neleus (*Odyssey* 11.238–254). The mares too ὑποκυσάμεναι, “conceived” (20.225), the word used of the Naiad who bore twins to Bucolion, Laomedon's son (6.22–26; see [[bucolion]]). κυανοχαίτης, “dark-maned”, is elsewhere Poseidon's epithet, “dark-haired” (13.563, 15.174; 20.144); Leaf sees no special point in it for a horse (on 20.224; see [[dark-haired]]). The mares “were pastured”, βουκολέοντο, with the verb of a herdsman tending cattle, used of Anchises when Aphrodite bore him Aeneas (5.313) and of Apollo herding Laomedon's cattle (21.448; see [[cattle]]); Leaf compares the “horse-herdsmen”, ἱπποβουκόλοι, of Euripides (*Phoenician Women* 28; on 20.221).

**Over grain and sea.** Two pairs of verses, built alike, set the land against the sea: αἳ δʼ ὅτε μὲν σκιρτῷεν ἐπὶ ζείδωρον ἄρουραν, “when they bounded over the grain-giving earth” (20.226), and ἀλλʼ ὅτε δὴ σκιρτῷεν ἐπʼ εὐρέα νῶτα θαλάσσης, “but when they bounded over the broad back of the sea” (20.228), each followed by ἄκρον ἐπί, “on the very tips” of the ears and “on the very crests” of the breakers (20.227, 20.229; see [[grain-giving]]). σκιρτάω, “bound”, occurs only in these two verses in Homer. Leaf reports that the A scholia explain ἀνθέρικος as the “ears” of grain; in later Greek it is the stalk of the asphodel, and he sees no reason why it should not be so here (on 20.227; see the translation's note on 20.227).

## Outside Homer

A fragment of the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* says the same of Iphiclus, son of Phylacus, in nearly the same verse: ἄκρον ἐπʼ ἀνθερίκων καρπὸν θέεν οὐδὲ κατέκλα, “he ran on the topmost fruit of the stalks and did not break it”, and raced over the ears of wheat without harming the crop (fr. 84 Evelyn-White, from a scholium on *Odyssey* 11.326; see [[iphiclus]]). [[apollonius|Apollonius of Rhodes]] gives the gift to Euphemus, a son of Poseidon, who would run even over the swell of the gray sea and did not dip his swift feet (*Argonautica* 1.182–184), with the verb of 20.229, θέεσκεν; Leaf takes his genitive, πόντου … οἴδματος, as a sign that Apollonius read ῥηγμῖνος here (on 20.229). [[virgil|Virgil]] gives it to Camilla: she could have flown over the topmost blades of an uncut crop without harming the tender ears, or made her way through the midst of the sea, borne up on the swelling wave, without wetting her swift feet (*Aeneid* 7.808–811).
