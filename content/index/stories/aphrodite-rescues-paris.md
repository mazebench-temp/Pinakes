## The story

Menelaus' spear has gone through Alexander's shield and corselet without wounding him, and his sword has shattered on Alexander's helmet. He cries out against Zeus: “Father Zeus, no other god is more baneful than you” (3.365). Then he leaps on him, seizes the helmet by its horsehair crest, and drags him toward the Achaeans, while the richly worked strap under his chin chokes him (3.369–372).

{{quote:88c3bd5a-ac1a-5264-a834-1d5b62f3b533}}

The strap was made from the hide of an ox “slain by force”, which is usually taken to mean the toughest leather, and the goddess breaks it. Menelaus is left holding an empty helmet, which he whirls and flings among the Achaeans. He comes on again with his spear, and finds nothing to strike. Alexander is set down “in his fragrant, perfumed chamber” (ἐν θαλάμῳ εὐώδεϊ κηώεντι, 3.382). Aphrodite goes at once to fetch Helen to him (see [[helen-and-aphrodite]]).

## In the poem

**Whose gifts.** Hector had mocked his brother's lyre, his hair and his looks as “the gifts of Aphrodite”, which would not help him when he lay in the dust (3.54–55). Paris answered that the gifts of the gods are not to be cast aside (3.64–66; see [[gifts-of-the-gods]]). The duel proves them both right. The gifts do not help Paris against Menelaus; the goddess who gave them does. In the next book Zeus teases Hera and Athena, who sit apart and watch while “laughter-loving Aphrodite is always at his side and wards off doom from him, and now she has saved him, when he thought that he would die” (4.10–12).

**The glory lost.** The rescue is told in the pattern the Iliad uses for a god's intervention: what would have happened, “had not” the god been quick to see it (3.373–374; see [[divine-rescue]]). Menelaus would have won “untold glory” (ἄσπετον … κῦδος; see [[kydos]]). He blamed Zeus for his failure; the audience knows it was Aphrodite. Paris, in the bedroom, will claim that Menelaus won “with Athena's help” (3.439), although the narrator has shown no god helping him.

**From battle to bed.** In three lines Paris passes from being choked in the middle of the two armies to a perfumed room. The next scene makes the contrast explicit. Aphrodite tells Helen that he does not look as if he had come from fighting a man, but as if he were going to a dance (3.391–394). Menelaus, meanwhile, ranges the field like a wild beast that has lost its prey (3.449; see [[like-a-wild-beast]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Aphrodite rescues her son Aeneas from Diomedes in Book 5 with the same verse, “had not Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, been quick to see it” (5.311–312); this time she is wounded by Diomedes and drops him, and Apollo carries him off in a dark cloud (5.334–346). Apollo saves Hector from Achilles in the words used here, “very easily, as a god can, and hid him in thick mist” (20.443–444), and Agenor too (21.596–598). No god saves Hector in the end: when the scales of Zeus sink, Apollo leaves him (22.208–213).
