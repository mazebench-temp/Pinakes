## The story

Homer tells only the end of it, and in a single sentence. When the gods pity the dead Hector and would have Hermes steal his body away, three refuse:

{{quote:29593681-b1e2-5de5-897b-dc7152a82239}}

The goddesses come to Alexander's farmyard (μέσσαυλον); he insults two of them and praises the third, and her gift to him is desire. The poem names neither the contest nor the prize, nor the goddess he praised. Hera and Athena are the two who keep their hatred of Troy, with Poseidon, and the one who gave Paris his lust is Aphrodite, whose gifts Hector throws in his face in Book 3 (3.54–55, 3.64–66; see [[paris]], [[hera]], [[athena]] and [[aphrodite]]). For ἄτης, “blind folly”, Walter Leaf would adopt the variant ἀρχῆς, “beginning”, were he sure of the line's antiquity (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.28; see [[arche-or-ate]]).

The rest is told by later writers. In the *Library* of Apollodorus, Strife threw an apple among Hera, Athena and Aphrodite as a prize for beauty, and Zeus ordered Hermes to lead them to Alexander on Ida, to be judged by him. Each promised him a gift: Hera, if she were preferred to all, kingship over all men; Athena, victory in war; Aphrodite, marriage with Helen. He chose Aphrodite, and sailed to Sparta in ships that Phereclus built (*Epitome* 3.2; see [[apollodorus]], [[strife]] and [[hermes]]). The shipbuilder is the Iliad's: Meriones kills Phereclus, “who built for Alexander the balanced ships / that began the evil” (5.62–63). Just before, Apollodorus puts the abduction of Helen, as some told it, down to the will of Zeus: that his daughter might become famous when Europe and Asia went to war, or, as others said, that the race of the demigods might be “lifted” (ἀρθῇ), a word that can mean exalted or taken away (*Epitome* 3.1; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[the-abduction-of-helen]]). In his note on the Judgment J. G. Frazer reports that Proclus' summary of the *Cypria* mentioned the intervention of Strife but not the golden apple, which first appears in late writers such as Lucian and Hyginus; the story ran, he says, that Strife alone was not invited to the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, and in spite threw among the guests a golden apple inscribed for the fair one (*Apollodorus: The Library*, 1921, on *Epitome* 3.2; see [[cypria]] and [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]]).

## Outside Homer

The tragedians tell the Judgment again and again as the beginning of Troy's sorrows. In Euripides' *Andromache* the chorus sings that the great woes began when the son of Maia and Zeus came to the glen of Ida, driving the goddesses' team of three, armed with hateful strife over beauty, to the herdsman's steading and the lonely youth at his hearth; the goddesses bathed in the mountain springs, came to the son of Priam vying in excesses of spiteful speech, and Cypris won with deceitful words, sweet to hear but a bitter ruin for the city of the Phrygians (*Andromache* 274–292). In the *Hecuba* the chorus dates its own sorrow from the day Alexander cut the pines of Ida for his ships, and from the strife “that a herdsman judges on Ida among the three daughters of the blessed gods” (*Hecuba* 629–648). Iphigenia, at Aulis, sings of the meadow on Ida by the springs of the Nymphs, where Pallas, guileful Cypris, Hera and Hermes the messenger of Zeus came to the hateful judgment of beauty that means death for her (*Iphigenia at Aulis* 1283–1309). In the prologue of the *Helen*, Helen says that Cypris won by offering her beauty to Alexander, and that Hera, angry at her defeat, gave him a phantom in her place (*Helen* 23–36; see [[helen-in-egypt]]).

In the *Trojan Women* the story is argued. Helen, on trial before Menelaus, makes the Judgment her defense: Pallas offered Paris command of the Phrygians and the conquest of Greece, Hera the rule of Asia and the bounds of Europe, Cypris Helen herself, and Greece owes its freedom to Cypris' victory (*Trojan Women* 924–937). Hecuba will not believe that goddesses went to Ida in childish vanity over their looks: Helen's own mind, seeing Paris, became her Cypris, since all follies are Aphrodite to mortals, and the goddess's name rightly begins as ἀφροσύνη, folly, begins (*Trojan Women* 969–990; see [[euripides]] and [[euripides-trojan-women]]).
