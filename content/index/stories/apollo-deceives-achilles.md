## The story

**Apollo goes into Ilios.** The battle of the gods is over. The others go back to Olympus, some angry and some exulting (21.518–520; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]), but Apollo goes into the city:

{{quote:0eed2a5b-5c74-5a61-9d93-7202787eab68}}

On the plain Achilles is killing the Trojans and their horses alike, and he brings toil and sorrows on them like the smoke that goes up from a burning city, sent up by the wrath of the gods (21.520–525; see [[smoke-from-a-burning-city]]).

**The gates.** Old Priam, on the divine tower, sees the Trojans driven in confusion before Achilles. He comes down groaning and orders the gatekeepers to hold the gates wide open until the army is inside, and then to shut them, “for I fear that this baneful man may leap inside the wall” (21.526–536; see [[priam]]). The gates are opened, “and the gates, thrown open, made a light for them; and Apollo / leapt out in front, to ward off ruin from the Trojans” (21.538–539). The Trojans flee “straight for the city and the high wall, / parched with thirst and grimed with dust”, and Achilles follows with his spear, eager to win glory (21.540–543; see [[the-walls-of-troy]] and [[kydos]]).

**Agenor.** The city would have fallen then:

{{quote:d800bf21-e6a2-5363-8137-9948fdde699d}}

Alone before the city, Agenor weighs flight against standing his ground. He could run with the others, or run the other way, over the plain to the slopes of Ida, and come back at evening; but Achilles would see him and catch him. He decides to meet him, since Achilles too is mortal, “and there is one life in him” (21.552–570; see [[pondering-two-ways]] and [[the-ilean-plain]]). He waits like a leopard that comes out of a thicket to face the hunter (21.571–580; see [[the-leopard-and-the-hunter]]), challenges Achilles, and hits him on the shin below the knee, but the greave of newly wrought tin, the god's gift, turns the bronze (21.581–594; see [[agenor]] and [[greaves]]).

**The rescue.** Achilles rushes at him:

{{quote:01f47685-93e1-586e-8a53-afc1ccdd189b}}

**The trick.** Then the god takes the rescued man's place:

{{quote:58e75549-35bd-5ae4-b5c4-1a093338e8f1}}

δόλῳ, “by a trick”, is said twice (21.599, 21.604). The god runs “a little way ahead”, close enough to keep Achilles running, over the wheat-bearing plain and along the Scamander, away from the city (see [[scamander]]). Meanwhile the army is saved:

{{quote:1d16a02f-c705-5940-89da-a643a44f6c7b}}

**The god revealed.** At the start of the next book the Trojans in the city cool their sweat, drink and quench their thirst, leaning on the battlements, while the Achaeans come up to the wall and Hector alone stays outside, held there by his fate before the Scaean gates (22.1–6). Then Apollo speaks to Achilles: why does the son of Peleus, a mortal, chase him, an immortal god, with his swift feet? He has not yet known him for a god. He has forgotten his work against the Trojans whom he put to flight, who have crowded into the city while he turned aside here; and he will not kill him, since he is not fated to die (22.7–13). Walter Leaf notes that Apollo is still in the guise of Agenor when he speaks, and takes Τρώων πόνος as the labor of killing the Trojans, not their distress (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 22.7, 22.11). Achilles, deeply troubled, answers: “You have foiled me, Far-Worker, most baneful of all the gods” (22.15), turning him away from the wall, when many more Trojans would have bitten the earth before they reached Ilios; the god has robbed him of great glory and saved them easily, since he feared no vengeance hereafter: “Truly I would take vengeance on you, if I had the strength” (22.14–20). Leaf takes ἔβλαψας as “you have foiled me”, a sense used in Homer only of what gods do to men, apart from wine in the Odyssey (on 22.15). Then Achilles makes for the city like a prize-winning horse (22.21–24; see [[achilles]]).

## In the poem

**The city saved for a day.** The gods went down to the battle because Zeus feared that Achilles might sack the wall “against fate” (20.30; see [[the-fourth-day-of-battle]]), and Apollo goes into the city for the same fear (21.516–517). The narrator's “would have … had not” is the one that stopped Patroclus at the same wall, when Apollo himself stood on the tower and drove him back three times (21.544–545 ≈ 16.698–700; see [[would-have-had-not]]). Here the god saves the city without showing himself. He sends one man out against Achilles, rescues him, and takes his shape, so that a rescue and a deception become one stratagem (see [[divine-rescue]] and [[truth-and-deception]]).

**Agenor's plan.** Agenor thought of running away from the wall over the plain toward Ida and coming back to the city at evening, “when I have bathed in the river, / and let the sweat dry from me” (21.560–561), and rejected the plan because Achilles would “dart after me and catch me with his swift feet” (21.564). What he feared happens to the god in his shape: Apollo runs from the wall across the plain and along the river with Achilles at his heels, and Achilles never catches him. The Trojans in the city do what Agenor meant to do. ἱδρῶ ἀπεψύχοντο, “let the sweat dry from them” (22.2), are the words of his hope, ἱδρῶ ἀποψυχθείς (21.561), and the army that fled “parched with thirst” (21.541) quenches its thirst inside the walls (22.2).

**Who escaped.** In the rout of *Iliad* 11 the Trojans halted at the Scaean gates and the oak “and waited for one another” (11.170–171; see [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). Now they do not dare “to wait for one another outside the city / and the wall” and learn who has escaped and who has died (21.608–610). In the next book Priam, looking at the army crowded inside, cannot see his two sons by Laothoe, Lycaon and Polydorus (22.46–48), whom Achilles has killed in this book and the last (21.34–135, 20.407–418; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]], [[polydorus-son-of-priam]] and [[laothoe]]).

**Achilles' feet.** The trick turns Achilles' speed against him. He runs after a man always a little ahead, “so that he always hoped to catch him with his own feet” (21.605), and the god asks him why he chases a god with his swift feet (22.8). The chase is a rehearsal for the next one. Achilles pursues Hector three times around the city, as in a dream, where one man cannot catch another who flees and the other cannot escape (22.199–201; see [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]), and Apollo comes to Hector one last time to rouse his might and his swift knees before he leaves him (22.202–204, 22.213).

**Two tricks.** Apollo's trick saves the Trojans for a day; Athena's kills Hector. In the likeness of Deiphobus she persuades him to stand and fight, and he knows the deceit only when he calls for Deiphobus to bring him a spear and no one is there (22.226–247, 22.294–299; see [[deiphobus]]). Apollo has used likenesses before: in the last book he spoke to Aeneas in the voice of Lycaon to send him against Achilles (20.79–82; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]]), and in *Iliad* 5, when he carried Aeneas away from Diomedes, he left a phantom of him on the field (5.449–453; see [[the-phantom-of-aeneas]] and [[eidolon]]).

**Achilles and Apollo.** Achilles calls Apollo the most baneful of the gods, and the poem has already named him as Achilles' killer. Thetis told her son that he would die under the wall of the Trojans by Apollo's arrows (21.277–278), and the dying Hector will tell him that Paris and Phoebus Apollo will kill him at the Scaean gates (22.359–360; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]] and [[apollo]]).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates, deciding what the young guardians may hear of the heroes, hesitates for Homer's sake to call it impious, but will not have it believed that Achilles spoke to Apollo as he does here, quoting 22.15 and 22.20, or that he was disobedient to the river, a god, and ready to fight it (*Republic* 3.391a–b; see [[plato-achilles-and-the-river]] and [[plato]]).

Virgil gives Juno a trick of the same kind to save Turnus. She makes a phantom of Aeneas out of hollow cloud, armed like him and walking like him, which challenges Turnus and then turns its back and flees; Turnus pursues it onto a ship moored by a rock, Juno breaks the cable, and the ship carries him away from the battle, *ignarus rerum ingratusque salutis*, “unaware of what had happened and ungrateful for his rescue” (*Aeneid* 10.636–688; see [[virgil]]). There the goddess draws the pursuer away to save him; here the god draws him away to save the city.
