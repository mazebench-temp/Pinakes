## The story

The suitors have wooed Penelope for three years and more, and she has given hope to all of them and made promises to each, sending out messages, while her mind has other things in view (*Odyssey* 2.89–92; see [[the-suitors]] and [[penelope]]). Then, as Antinous tells it:

{{quote:46eb195a-bc35-5c3e-8a2b-079a03e4728b}}

The cloth is a φᾶρος, “mantle”, the word for the mantle that the slave women put around Hector's body before Achilles lays it on the bier (*Iliad* 24.587–589), and Penelope calls it a ταφήιον, “shroud”, from τάφος, “burial”: without it the old man would lie “without a winding-sheet”, ἄτερ σπείρου (*Odyssey* 2.97–102; see the Odyssey translation's table of key words, [[shroud]] and [[laertes]]). Her reason is the women of the land, who would blame her if a man who “won great possessions” were to lie without one (see [[achaean-women]] and [[nemesis]]). The suitors agree:

## In the poem

**Three tellings.** The story is told three times, by three speakers, in nearly the same verses. In the assembly of Ithaca Antinous tells it to show that the suitors are not to blame for their long stay, but Penelope, “who knows crafty ways beyond all others”, and calls it her δόλος, “trick” (*Odyssey* 2.88, 2.93; see [[antinous]], [[dolos]] and [[the-assembly-of-the-ithacans]]). In the nineteenth book Penelope tells it herself to the beggar who is her husband: “they press on with the marriage; I wind up wiles”, and a god first breathed the mantle into her mind; her maids, “careless dogs”, betrayed her, and now she can neither escape the marriage nor find another device, for her parents urge her to marry and her son chafes at the wasting of his substance (*Odyssey* 19.137–161). In the last book the shade of the suitor Amphimedon tells it to the shade of Agamemnon, in the words of Antinous, as the beginning of the suitors' death: when she had finished the great web, washed it and shown it, shining like the sun or the moon, some evil god brought Odysseus to the swineherd's hut at the edge of the land (*Odyssey* 24.125–150; see [[odysseus]]). The renderings of the nineteenth and twenty-fourth books here are plain renderings, not the translation.

**The verses.** Penelope's words to the suitors are the same seven verses in all three tellings (*Odyssey* 2.96–102 = 19.141–147 = 24.131–137), and Amphimedon repeats the rest of Antinous' story almost word for word (*Odyssey* 24.128–146, as 2.93–110). The nineteenth and twenty-fourth books add a verse after the coming of the fourth year, “as the months waned, and many days were accomplished” (a plain rendering), which the second lacks (*Odyssey* 19.153, 24.143; see the translation's note on 2.107).

**Her fame.** Antinous grants that her purpose wins her fame, “Great fame she makes / for herself, but for you a longing for much substance” (*Odyssey* 2.125–126), and Eurymachus that the suitors strive “for the sake of her excellence” (*Odyssey* 2.206; see [[kleos]] and [[arete]]). After Amphimedon's story the shade of Agamemnon calls Odysseus blessed in a wife of great excellence: the fame of her excellence will never perish, and the gods will make a lovely song for her among men, not as for Clytemnestra, who killed her wedded husband (*Odyssey* 24.192–202; see [[clytemnestra]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]).

**The loom and the web.** ἱστός is both the loom, set up in the hall, and the web woven on it, which the translation keeps apart, and also the mast of a ship (*Odyssey* 2.94, 2.104, 2.109, 2.424; see [[loom]], [[mast]] and [[weaving]]). μέγαν ἱστὸν … ὕφαινε are the words the Iliad uses of Helen at her great purple web (*Iliad* 3.125; see [[helens-web]]).
