## The story

{{quote:84a00dac-6223-5807-b1eb-181ae4c95d79}}

Tlepolemus speaks first. Sarpedon, he says, cowers like a man who knows nothing of battle; they lie who call him the son of Zeus, for he falls far short of the sons Zeus fathered in the old days. What a man was Heracles, “my father, bold in endurance, lion-hearted”, who came for the horses of Laomedon with only six ships and fewer men, and sacked Ilios and widowed its streets. Sarpedon will be no defense to the Trojans; brought down by Tlepolemus, he will pass the gates of Hades (5.632–646; see [[heracles-sacks-troy]], [[bold-in-endurance]] and [[lion-hearted]]).

Sarpedon grants the fact and turns it round. Heracles destroyed sacred Ilios “through the folly of a man, noble Laomedon, who rebuked him with evil words though he had done him good, and did not give him the horses for whose sake he had come from far away” (5.649–651). As for Tlepolemus, he will give Sarpedon triumph, “and your soul to Hades of the famous horses” (5.652–654; see [[of-the-famous-horses]]).

They throw at the same moment. Sarpedon's spear goes through Tlepolemus' neck, and murky night covers his eyes. Tlepolemus' spear goes through Sarpedon's left thigh and grazes the bone, “but his father still kept ruin away” (5.655–662). His companions carry Sarpedon out of the battle with the long spear dragging from his thigh, for in their haste no one thinks to draw it out, and the Achaeans carry off Tlepolemus (5.663–669).

Odysseus sees it and ponders whether to go after the son of Zeus or to kill more Lycians. “But it was not fated for great-hearted Odysseus to kill the mighty son of Zeus with the sharp bronze” (5.674–675), and Athena turns his heart against the Lycians. He kills seven of them, Coeranus, Alastor, Chromius, Alcandrus, Halius, Noemon and Prytanis, and would have killed more if Hector had not come up (5.669–682; see [[pondering-two-ways]], [[odysseus]] and [[lycians]]). Sarpedon, glad to see him, begs Hector not to leave him as prey for the Danaans: let life leave him in Hector's city, since he was not to go home to gladden his wife and infant son (5.683–688). Hector says nothing and darts past (5.689–691).

## In the poem

**Son and grandson.** The narrator names the two men by their descent from Zeus, “the son and the grandson” (5.631), before either speaks. Tlepolemus' taunt turns on that descent: Sarpedon cannot be a son of Zeus, since he is no Heracles (5.635–639). The narrative proves the grandson wrong. Zeus “still kept ruin away” from his son (5.662), Odysseus is not fated to kill “the son of loud-thundering Zeus” (5.672–675; see [[loud-thundering]]), and it is the grandson who dies. See [[zeus]] and [[sarpedon]].

**Two versions of one story.** The taunt is a paradigm from the past, in the manner of Nestor's and Agamemnon's stories (see [[mythological-paradigm]]). Tlepolemus tells the sack of Troy as proof of his father's strength: six ships were enough. Sarpedon tells the same sack as proof of Laomedon's bad faith: the city fell because its king cheated a benefactor. Both are true, and each speaker takes the version he needs.

**Words of death.** The exchange is full of the language of dying. Tlepolemus promises Sarpedon the gates of Hades (5.646), which the ghost of Patroclus will long to pass (23.71); Sarpedon promises Tlepolemus that his soul will go to Hades of the famous horses (5.654). Night covers the eyes of the man who dies (5.659; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). The man who lives is given the same words: “his soul left him, and a mist was poured over his eyes” (5.696; see [[psyche]] and [[achlys]]). Then he breathes again, and the North Wind revives the spirit he had gasped away (5.697–698; see [[boreas]]). The North Wind was asleep in the simile of the still clouds earlier in the book (5.524; see [[clouds-on-the-mountains]]). The same language is used when Andromache faints on the wall at the sight of Hector's body dragged behind Achilles' chariot: dark night covers her eyes, she gasps out her soul, and then she breathes again (22.466–475).

**Fate.** “Mighty fate” (μοῖρα κραταιή) drives Tlepolemus against Sarpedon (5.629; see [[mighty-fate]] and [[moira]]), and fate keeps Odysseus from killing Sarpedon (5.674). The son of Zeus is saved here for a death that is still to come. In Book 16 he falls to Patroclus; Zeus weighs saving him and is dissuaded by Hera, sheds a rain of blood on the earth in his honor, and has Sleep and Death carry his body home to Lycia (16.431–461, 16.666–683). His plea to Hector, that he was not to go home and gladden his wife and son (5.686–688), looks ahead to that end. In Book 17 Glaucus reproaches Hector for leaving Sarpedon's body as the prey and spoil of the Argives (17.150–153; see [[the-net]]).

**The oak.** The companions lay Sarpedon under “the beautiful oak of aegis-bearing Zeus” (5.693), the oak that later books place by the Scaean gates (6.237, 11.170; see [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). The father who kept ruin away shelters him under his tree, and his dear companion Pelagon draws out the spear (5.694–695; see [[pelagon-of-lycia]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Tlepolemus, son of Heracles, appears in the Iliad only twice (another Tlepolemus, son of Damastor, is killed by Patroclus, 16.416): in the Catalogue, which tells how he killed his father's uncle Licymnius and fled with his people to Rhodes (2.653–670; see [[the-flight-of-tlepolemus]] and [[tlepolemus]]), and here, where he dies. Sarpedon's greatest speech comes in Book 12, when he tells Glaucus that the honors kings enjoy in Lycia bind them to fight among the foremost, and that since death comes in any case they should go forward (12.310–328). The duel of Hector and Ajax in Book 7 begins, as this one does, with a challenge and an answer before the throws (7.225–243).
