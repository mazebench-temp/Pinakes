## The story

**The threat.** When the companions had killed the cattle of the Sun on Thrinacia, the Sun demanded that they be punished, and Zeus promised to strike their swift ship with his gleaming thunderbolt and shatter it in the middle of the wine-dark sea (*Odyssey* 12.374–388; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[helios]]). Odysseus heard this, he says, from Calypso, who had it from Hermes (*Odyssey* 12.389–390). The threat is spoken in the words of the deed: νῆα θοὴν ἀργῆτι κεραυνῷ / τυτθὰ βαλὼν κεάσαιμι μέσῳ ἐνὶ οἴνοπι πόντῳ (*Odyssey* 12.387–388), and Calypso tells the wreck in the same words, νῆα θοὴν ἀργῆτι κεραυνῷ / Ζεὺς ἔλσας ἐκέασσε μέσῳ ἐνὶ οἴνοπι πόντῳ (*Odyssey* 5.131–132; see [[of-the-gleaming-thunderbolt]] and [[thunderbolt]]).

**The wreck.** On the seventh day the companions put to sea. When no land was in sight, Zeus set a dark cloud over the ship; the West Wind broke the forestays, the mast fell and crushed the helmsman's skull, and Zeus struck the ship with his thunderbolt. The companions fell overboard and were carried on the waves like sea crows, “and the god took away their homecoming” (*Odyssey* 12.399–419; see [[nostos]]). When the surge had torn the sides from the keel and broken off the mast, Odysseus lashed keel and mast together with the oxhide backstay, and sat on them, carried by the winds (*Odyssey* 12.420–425).

**Charybdis again.** The South Wind carried him back through the night to the strait he had passed, and at sunrise Charybdis sucked down the sea under him. He sprang up to the great fig tree above her and clung to it “like a bat”, unable to climb it or to set his feet, until late in the day she cast up the mast and keel; he dropped onto them and rowed away with his hands, and Zeus did not let Scylla see him (*Odyssey* 12.426–446; see [[charybdis]], [[the-fig-tree-of-charybdis]], [[odysseus-passes-scylla]] and [[like-a-bat]]).

**To Ogygia.** For nine days he drifted, and on the tenth night the gods brought him to the island of Ogygia, where Calypso lives (*Odyssey* 12.447–450; see [[ogygia]] and [[calypso]]). There his tale to the Phaeacians ends (see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).

**The other tellings.** To Arete, Odysseus speaks the verses of Calypso's account, with “my swift ship” for “his swift ship”, and adds that he clasped the keel in his arms and was carried for nine days (*Odyssey* 7.248–254). Disguised as a stranger, he tells Penelope that Odysseus lost his companions and his ship on the way from Thrinacia, because Zeus and the Sun were angry with him, and that the wave cast him on the keel onto the land of the Phaeacians; in that telling Calypso is left out (*Odyssey* 19.273–282). When at last he tells her his own story, Zeus who thunders on high strikes the ship with a smoky thunderbolt, and all the companions perish together (*Odyssey* 23.330–333). The wreck serves a lie as well: the Cretan of Odysseus' false tale to Eumaeus, carried off on a Phoenician ship bound for Libya, loses it when Crete is left behind, in the verses of the true wreck, and clings to the mast for nine days until a wave brings him to Thesprotia (*Odyssey* 14.301–315, after 12.403–406 and 12.415–419).

## In the poem

The wreck ends the wanderings that Odysseus tells and begins his seven years with Calypso (*Odyssey* 7.259). It is why the poem finds him alone, with “no ships with oars and no companions” (*Odyssey* 4.559 = 5.16), and it fulfills the prophecy of Teiresias, that if the cattle were harmed the ship and the companions would be destroyed, and Odysseus, if he escaped, would come home late and in misery, having lost all his companions, in another man's ship (*Odyssey* 11.112–115; see [[odysseus]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). Circe had warned him of Charybdis, “May you not be there when she sucks it down; / for not even the earth-shaker could save you from the evil” (*Odyssey* 12.106–107); he is there at that very moment, and the fig tree she pointed out saves him (*Odyssey* 12.103, 12.431–433).

## Outside Homer

**The Seventh Letter.** In the *Seventh Letter*, handed down among Plato's works, the writer, recalling how he was compelled to come to Sicily a third time, says that he had less reason to be angry with Dionysius than with himself and those who forced him to come “for the third time to the strait by Scylla, / that I might measure out again the way to deadly Charybdis” (*Seventh Letter* 345e, quoting *Odyssey* 12.428). Nauck, as Merry reports, thought the reading of Apollonius the Sophist, θάλασσαν, “the sea”, the right one, though the *Seventh Letter* and Plutarch quote the verse with Χάρυβδιν (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 12.428; see the translation's note on 12.428, [[plato]] and [[scylla-and-charybdis-in-later-writers]]).

**A tale of the whirlpool.** G. Gerland, as D. B. Monro reports, set beside this part of the Odyssey an Indian tale in the collection of Somadeva, whose hero, a Brahman named Saktideva, is saved from a great whirlpool, like Odysseus, by climbing into the branches of a fig tree that overhangs it, and is then carried through the air to a golden city whose fairy queen is destined to marry a mortal (Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix I §2, note 7).
