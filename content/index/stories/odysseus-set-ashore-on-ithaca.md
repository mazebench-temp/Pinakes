## The story

**The voyage.** When Odysseus has ended his tale, Alcinous orders new gifts for him, and through the feast of the next day Odysseus keeps turning his head toward the sun, eager for it to set (*Odyssey* 13.1–35; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]] and [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]). At sunset he takes leave of the king and queen and goes down to the ship, where the Phaeacians spread a rug and a linen sheet for him on the deck at the stern, “so that he might sleep unwaking” (*Odyssey* 13.36–75). As soon as the rowers lean back and toss up the sea with the oar-blade, “sweet sleep fell upon his eyelids, / unwaking, sweetest, most like death” (*Odyssey* 13.76–80; see [[sweet-sleep]] and [[sleep]]). The ship runs through the night like four stallions under the whip, and so surely that not even a falcon could keep pace with her (*Odyssey* 13.81–87; see [[the-four-stallions]] and [[falcon]]):

{{quote:87f9dbaa-8f95-50db-a37c-19058baded4e}}

**The landing.** When the brightest star rises, the one that comes to announce the light of Dawn, the ship draws near to the island (*Odyssey* 13.93–95; see [[the-morning-star]]). “There is a harbor of Phorcys, the Old Man of the Sea, / in the land of Ithaca”: two sheer headlands keep off the waves, so that ships stay inside without a mooring rope, and at its head stand a long-leaved olive and a misty cave sacred to the nymphs who are called Naiads (*Odyssey* 13.96–112; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]], [[phorcys-the-sea-god]], [[olive]] and [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]). The Phaeacians, who know the place, row in and run the ship up on the land half her length; they lift Odysseus out with his linen sheet and his rug, lay him on the sand still overcome by sleep, set his gifts all together by the trunk of the olive, away from the road, and go back home (*Odyssey* 13.113–125; see [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]). On the way Poseidon turns their ship to stone within sight of their city (*Odyssey* 13.125–187; see [[the-ship-turned-to-stone]]).

**The waking.** Odysseus wakes in his own land and does not know it, for Athena has poured a mist around him, “so that she might make him / unknown, and tell him each thing”, and everything looks strange to him, the long paths, the harbors, the rocks and the trees (*Odyssey* 13.187–196; see [[athena]]). He laments in the words with which he woke on the shore of Scheria, “Ah me, to the land of what mortals have I come this time?” (*Odyssey* 13.200–202 = 6.119–121; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]), wishes that he had stayed among the Phaeacians, accuses them of bringing him to a strange land, and prays that Zeus, the god of suppliants, may punish them. Then he counts his gifts. Nothing is missing, and he drags himself along the shore of the loud-roaring sea, mourning for his native land (*Odyssey* 13.197–221; see [[zeus]] and [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**The herdsman.** Athena comes to him “in form like a young man, a herdsman of sheep”, delicate as the sons of lords, with a cape folded double around her shoulders, sandals on her feet and a javelin in her hands (*Odyssey* 13.221–225; see [[shape-changing]]). Odysseus prays to the stranger as to a god, comes to his knees, and asks what land this is (*Odyssey* 13.226–235; see [[supplication]] and [[tell-me-this-truly]]). The answer opens with the Cyclops' scorn, “You are a fool, stranger, or you have come from far away” (*Odyssey* 13.237 = 9.273), and describes the island, rugged and not good for driving horses, but rich in grain and wine, rain and dew, with pasture for goats and cattle, forest of every kind and unfailing water: “Therefore, stranger, the name of Ithaca has reached even to Troy” (*Odyssey* 13.237–249; see [[ithaca]]). Odysseus is glad, but he does not speak the truth. He has heard of Ithaca in Crete, he says, and he is a Cretan in exile for killing the son of Idomeneus (*Odyssey* 13.250–286; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]).

**The goddess.** Athena smiles, strokes him with her hand and takes the likeness of a woman, beautiful and tall and skilled in splendid handiwork (*Odyssey* 13.287–290):

{{quote:13c7e3ab-bf45-517a-92bc-9a05db7f64d7}}

She has come to weave a plan with him, to hide his goods, and to tell him what he must endure in his house, in silence, submitting to the violence of men (*Odyssey* 13.303–310). “It is hard, goddess, for a mortal who meets you to know you, / even one who knows a great deal; for you make yourself like anything”, he answers, and he asks her, by her father, whether he has truly come to his native land (*Odyssey* 13.311–328; see [[hard-for-a-mortal-to-know-you]]). She cannot abandon him, she says, “because you are courteous, and quick-witted, and sensible”; she always knew that he would come home, but she would not fight with Poseidon, her father's brother (*Odyssey* 13.329–343; see [[poseidon]]). Then she shows him the harbor of Phorcys, the olive, the cave where he used to offer hecatombs to the nymphs, and Mount Neritum, and scatters the mist (*Odyssey* 13.344–352; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]], [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]] and [[neritum]]). Odysseus kisses the grain-giving earth and prays to the Naiads (*Odyssey* 13.353–360; see [[prayer]]). Together they hide the gifts in the cave, and Athena sets a stone against the doors; then the two sit down by the trunk of the sacred olive and plan destruction for the overbearing suitors (*Odyssey* 13.361–373; see [[the-suitors]] and [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]).

## In the poem

**The promise kept.** In the council of the gods Zeus decreed that the Phaeacians would send Odysseus home in a ship, “giving him bronze and gold in plenty, and clothing, / a great store, more than Odysseus would ever have carried off from Troy” (*Odyssey* 5.36–40; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]), and Alcinous promised him that he would lie asleep while the young men rowed him home (*Odyssey* 7.317–320; see [[pompe]]). The landing fulfills both in their own words. Poseidon complains that the Phaeacians “carried him asleep in a swift ship over the sea / and set him down in Ithaca, and gave him untold gifts”, and goes on in the verses of the decree (*Odyssey* 13.134–138, after 5.38–40; see [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]); Odysseus, telling his son the truth, uses Poseidon's verses: the Phaeacians “carried me asleep in a swift ship over the sea / and set me down in Ithaca, and gave me splendid gifts”, and the gifts “lie in caves by the will of the gods” (*Odyssey* 16.227–232, a plain rendering; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]] and [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]). In the account of his wanderings that he gives Penelope, the Phaeacians are the last stage, and sleep comes upon him as he ends it (*Odyssey* 23.338–343).

**Asleep at the turning points.** Twice in his tale sleep came upon Odysseus at the moment of ruin: when his native fields were in sight and the companions opened the skin of the winds (*Odyssey* 10.31; see [[odysseus-and-aeolus]]), and on Thrinacia, while they killed the cattle of the Sun (*Odyssey* 12.338; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). Arete told him to tie a knot on his chest, “so that no one may do you harm on the way, when hereafter / you sleep sweet sleep as you go in the black ship” (*Odyssey* 8.443–445). The third sleep brings him home, and nothing is lost: on waking he counts the gifts, “to see whether they have gone off with anything in the hollow ship”, and misses nothing (*Odyssey* 13.215–219). In his lie to Athena the first of those sleeps comes back in the Cretan's mouth, “Then sweet sleep came upon me, worn out as I was” (*Odyssey* 13.282 = 10.31; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]).

**The poet speaks again.** After four books in which Odysseus has told his own story, the poet pauses over his sleeping hero, and his words look back to the opening of the poem. The ship carries a man “who before had suffered very many sorrows in his heart” (*Odyssey* 13.90), as the man of the proem “suffered many sorrows in his heart” on the sea (*Odyssey* 1.4), “passing through the wars of men and the grievous waves” (*Odyssey* 13.91), the verse in which Odysseus told the Phaeacians what he had endured (*Odyssey* 8.183) and in which he will tell Athena the sufferings of the Cretan (*Odyssey* 13.264). D. B. Monro finds the character of the narrative changing from this book on: “Like Ulysses after his landing in Ithaca, when Athene removed the mist from his eyes, we find ourselves in a familiar world—the world of the Trojan story”, after a Phaeacian episode that is “distinctly märchenhaft” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix I §§2–3; see [[phaeacians]]).

**Two wakings.** The waking on Ithaca repeats the waking on Scheria. There Odysseus woke on an unknown shore and asked what men lived in the land (*Odyssey* 6.117–126); here he asks the same of his own island (*Odyssey* 13.200–202). In Scheria Athena poured a mist around him so that no Phaeacian should question him on his way to the city (*Odyssey* 7.14–17); on Ithaca the mist hides the land from him. Monro takes its purpose to be preparation rather than concealment: Athena meant to consult with him and to change his looks, and “If Athene had not taken these measures, Ulysses would have gone straight to his palace, and all would have been lost” (on *Odyssey* 13.191). The strangeness of the land, he adds, “may be only the exaggeration of a natural effect”, and he quotes Wordsworth's *The Brothers*, in which a man come home after long absence sees “Strange alteration wrought on every side” (Monro, Additional Notes, on *Odyssey* 13.194).

**Athena face to face.** In Scheria the goddess came to Odysseus only in disguise, as a girl carrying a water jar (*Odyssey* 7.19–20), for “she did not yet appear to him face to face; for she respected / her father's brother” (*Odyssey* 6.329–330; see [[seeing-the-gods]]). Merry notes that she throws off this reserve as soon as Odysseus has landed in Ithaca and is beyond the power of Poseidon (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 6.330), and she gives Odysseus the same reason herself (*Odyssey* 13.341–343). She still comes first in disguise, and the meeting becomes a contest between a goddess who is “famed for cunning and crafty ways” among the gods and a man who is “by far the best of all mortals / in counsel and in words” (*Odyssey* 13.297–299; see [[crafty-would-he-be]], [[metis]], [[kerdos]] and [[dolos]]). In the Iliad Odysseus prayed to her, “you who always / stand by me in all my toils” (*Iliad* 10.278–279); on Ithaca she says it of herself, “who always / stand by you in all your toils and guard you” (*Odyssey* 13.300–301).

## Outside Homer

**Aristotle and the schools.** Aristotle takes the putting ashore, τὰ περὶ τὴν ἔκθεσιν, as his example of an irrational incident that a bad poet would have made intolerable and Homer conceals with his other virtues (*Poetics* 1460a35–b2; see [[alogon]]), and the *Divisions* that pass under his name make it a stock problem; for these readings, and Strabo's, see [[aristotle-the-putting-ashore]].

**Other returns.** In a rationalized account preserved in the Suda, Odysseus, after losing his companions and ships at Charybdis, floated on a plank until some Phoenicians picked him up and brought him naked to Idomeneus in Crete; Idomeneus kept him through the winter and sent him to Phaeacia, “now called Corcyra”, and the Phaeacians sent him on “with two ships and companions” (Suda χ 144). Its Phoenicians and its Idomeneus are the persons of Odysseus' lie to Athena (see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]). Hyginus' summary of the Odyssey has Alcinous receive him generously and send him home to Ithaca with gifts, but then “by the anger of Mercury he was shipwrecked again”, and he came home after the twentieth year, alone, his companions lost (Hyginus, *Fabulae* 125).

**The Hymn to Apollo.** In the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* the god, in the likeness of a young man in his first youth, meets Cretan sailors whom he has carried away from their course to Pylos, and their leader asks him what land this is, in nearly the verses with which Odysseus questions the disguised Athena: καί μοι τοῦτʼ ἀγόρευσον ἐτήτυμον, ὄφρʼ εὖ εἰδῶ· / τίς δῆμος; τίς γαῖα; τίνες βροτοὶ ἐγγεγάασιν; “and tell me this truly, so that I may know it well: / what people is this, what land, what mortals are born here?” (*Hymn to Apollo* 449–450, 467–473; *Odyssey* 13.222, 13.232–233; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]).

**Virgil.** Aeneas, cast ashore in Libya, goes out at first light to learn what shores the wind has brought him to and who holds them, “men or beasts” (*Aeneid* 1.305–309), and when his mother meets him in the likeness of a huntress he asks her under what sky and on what shores of the world they are tossed, ignorant of men and places (*Aeneid* 1.331–334). She leaves him revealed as a goddess by her step, and he cries after her, *quid natum totiens, crudelis tu quoque, falsis / ludis imaginibus?*, “why do you, cruel too, so often mock your son with false shapes?” (*Aeneid* 1.405–408), as Odysseus tells Athena that a mortal cannot know her, “for you make yourself like anything” (*Odyssey* 13.313). The harbor where Aeneas lands has the features of the harbor of Phorcys (see [[virgils-storm]] and [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]]). The sleep of the voyage has an echo of its own: Deiphobus remembers the sleep that held him on the last night of Troy as *dulcis et alta quies placidaeque simillima morti*, “a sweet and deep rest, most like a peaceful death” (*Aeneid* 6.520–522; see [[virgil]]).
