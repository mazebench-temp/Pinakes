## The story

The Iliad tells the abduction only in pieces, in the words of its characters. Paris gathered companions and sailed across the sea in seafaring ships, and “mingling with foreigners” brought back a beautiful woman from a distant land (3.46–49). He had been a guest in Menelaus' house, and took his host's wife and possessions with him (3.351–354, 13.624–627). Helen left, in her own words, “my bridal chamber and my kinsmen, and my darling daughter, and the lovely companions of my youth” (3.174–175). Paris “snatched” her away from Lacedaemon, and they first lay together on the island of Cranae (3.443–445; see [[cranae]]).

{{quote:0eb62c9a-d0ff-5e30-93d0-399938ae83ad}}

Other books add a few details. The craftsman Phereclus built Paris' ships, the ships “that began the evil” (5.62–63). On the voyage home Paris put in at Sidon and brought back Sidonian women, whose embroidered robes Hecuba keeps in her storeroom (6.289–292). He brought “possessions from Argos” to his house, and later offers to return them, though not the woman (7.362–364). In her lament for Hector, Helen says it is the twentieth year since she left her native land (24.765–766). Behind it all lies the Judgment of Paris, to which the Iliad alludes only once: he insulted Hera and Athena when they came to his farmstead and praised the goddess “who gave him grievous lust” (24.28–30).

## In the poem

Book 3 looks back on the abduction again and again, because the duel is meant to undo it. Each speaker sees it differently.

For Hector it is a disgrace: the champion who has nothing but his looks once sailed abroad and brought home “a great grief to your father and your city and all the people, a joy to your enemies, and a shame to yourself” (3.50–51). For Menelaus it is a crime against the host. He prays that Zeus will let him take vengeance on “the man who first wronged me”, so that men to come may shudder “to do evil to a host who has shown him friendship” (3.351–354; see [[xenia]]). For Helen it is a regret she cannot undo:

{{quote:327b71cb-428c-5699-9f2d-72d3c3c29fa7}}

For Paris it is the measure of desire, and he remembers it only to say that he wants her more now. At the end of the day of the duel he leads her to bed with the memory of Cranae (3.441–446; see [[helen-and-aphrodite]]).

The terms of the duel treat the abduction as a theft to be repaid: “Helen and all her possessions” go to the winner (3.70, 3.91), with a satisfaction on top if Menelaus wins (3.286–287; see [[war-for-menelaus]]). Menelaus names the cause of the war as “my quarrel, and because Alexander began it” (Ἀλεξάνδρου ἕνεκʼ ἀρχῆς, 3.100). An ancient variant read ἄτης, “Alexander's blind folly”, the ending of the same phrase at 6.356 and 24.28 (see [[arche-or-ate]]). On who is to blame, Book 3 gives every answer: the old men say there is no blame in fighting for such a woman, Priam blames the gods, Helen blames herself, and Hector his brother (see [[who-is-to-blame]]).

## Outside Homer

The *Cypria*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, told the whole story. After the Judgment, on Aphrodite's advice, Paris built ships, and Aeneas sailed with him. Helenus and Cassandra foretold what would happen. In Lacedaemon he was entertained by the sons of Tyndareus, and then in Sparta by Menelaus, and at the feast he gave Helen gifts. Menelaus sailed to Crete, telling Helen to look after the guests until they left. Aphrodite brought Helen and Alexander together, and after their union they loaded the ship with great possessions and sailed away by night. Hera sent a storm against them; Paris was carried to Sidon and took the city, and then sailed to Troy and celebrated his marriage. While he was away, Castor and Polydeuces were killed (see [[the-death-of-the-dioscuri]]), and Iris brought the news to Menelaus.

Herodotus read the *Cypria* differently. In his text Paris reached Troy from Sparta on the third day, with a fair wind and a smooth sea, while the Iliad makes him wander by Sidon; and so, Herodotus argued, the *Cypria* could not be Homer's (2.117). How his *Cypria* is to be squared with Proclus' storm and Sidon is not known. His Egyptian priests told him that Helen never reached Troy at all (see [[helen-in-egypt]]), and Stesichorus had already recanted the story in his palinode (see [[stesichorus-palinode]]). Pausanias saw the island of Cranae off Gytheum, with a sanctuary of Aphrodite that Paris was said to have founded on the shore opposite (3.22.1).
