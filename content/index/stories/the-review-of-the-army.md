## The story

While Machaon tends Menelaus, the Trojan ranks come on, and the Achaeans put on their armor again and remember the joy of battle (4.220–222; see [[pandarus-breaks-the-truce]] and [[charme]]).

{{quote:e54d1847-9556-5821-b657-133af625beab}}

He leaves his horses and chariot with his attendant Eurymedon, to be kept close for when weariness takes him, and goes on foot through the ranks (4.226–231; see [[eurymedon]]). Those he sees eager he encourages: father Zeus will be no helper to liars, and vultures will eat the flesh of those who broke the oaths (4.232–239; see [[vultures]]). Those he sees holding back he rebukes: why do they stand dazed like fawns (4.240–249; see [[like-fawns]])?

Then he goes from leader to leader. He finds the Cretans arming, Idomeneus among the champions “like a boar in courage” and Meriones urging on the rear, and honors Idomeneus above the Danaans; Idomeneus answers that he will be the trusty companion he promised at the start to be (4.251–272; see [[like-a-boar]]). He finds the two Ajaxes arming, with a cloud of foot soldiers dark as a storm cloud, and tells them they need no orders: if all had their spirit, Priam's city would soon fall (4.273–292; see [[storm-cloud]]). He finds Nestor setting the Pylians in order, horsemen in front, foot soldiers behind and cowards in the middle, and teaching his charioteers to fight as the men of old did (4.293–310; see [[battle-order]]). Agamemnon wishes that the old man's strength matched his spirit. So does Nestor, who remembers the day he killed Ereuthalion; but the gods never give men all things at once, and counsel is the prize of the old (4.311–325; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]).

Then praise turns to blame. Menestheus with his Athenians, and Odysseus with his Cephallenians, are standing still, because the war cry has not yet reached them. Agamemnon calls them the first to hear his call to the feast and the last to fight (4.327–348). Odysseus answers “looking at him darkly”: what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth! He will see the father of Telemachus among the champions (4.349–355; see [[looking-darkly]] and [[what-a-word]]).

{{quote:c6915534-a83a-59c7-9bf5-533b3ab46e6e}}

Last he finds Diomedes standing by his chariot with Sthenelus, and rebukes him at length with the story of his father. Tydeus went alone into Thebes as an envoy, beat the Cadmeians at every contest, and killed all but one of the fifty men who lay in ambush for him; “but the son he fathered is worse than he in battle, though better in assembly” (4.365–400; see [[tydeus-at-thebes]]). Diomedes says nothing, “in awe of the rebuke of the king he revered” (4.401–402). Sthenelus answers that the sons are far better than their fathers, for they took seven-gated Thebes, where their fathers died (4.403–410; see [[the-epigonoi]]). Diomedes silences him.

{{quote:3360dd1a-17c4-5c7e-b6da-e2253f32be96}}

He leaps from his chariot in his armor, and the bronze on his breast rings terribly as he moves (4.419–421). The armies advance to meet (see [[waves-on-the-shore]]).

## In the poem

**The king on foot.** The narrator introduces the review with three denials: you would not have seen Agamemnon drowsing, nor cowering, nor unwilling to fight (4.223–224). The king whom Achilles called a coward who never armed for battle (1.225–228), and who tested his army in Book 2 with a false order to go home (see [[the-test-of-the-army]]), now goes among his men on foot, eager for the fight. He has just seen his brother wounded and sworn that the Trojans will pay (4.155–168).

**Praise and blame.** The review is built of five visits, three that end in praise and two in rebuke (see [[the-review]], [[exhortation]] and [[praise-and-blame]]). The rebuked men are not failing. The Athenians and Cephallenians are waiting because the call to battle has not reached them (4.331–335), and Diomedes is standing ready by his chariot (4.366). Each answers in his own way. Odysseus answers back at once, and Agamemnon smiles and gives way (4.349–363; see [[smiling-at-him]]). Diomedes says nothing and defends the king against his own companion: the glory will be Agamemnon's if the Achaeans take Ilios, and the grief his if they are cut down (4.412–418). But he does not forget. In Book 9, when Agamemnon proposes to go home, Diomedes reminds him that he insulted his courage first among the Danaans, calling him unwarlike and weak (9.32–36). The silence of Book 4 is answered five books later in the assembly, the place where Agamemnon had allowed that Diomedes was better than his father.

**Fathers and the past.** The review is full of the past. Nestor's horsemen are to fight as “the men of old” did (4.308–309), and he remembers his own youth (4.318–325). Menestheus is named by his father, Odysseus by his son (4.338, 4.354). Diomedes is measured against Tydeus, and Sthenelus answers that the sons are better than the fathers (4.370–410). See [[fathers-and-sons]], [[old-age-and-memory]], [[tydeus]] and [[sthenelus]].

**Before the battle.** Like the Catalogue of Ships and Helen's naming of the leaders from the wall, the review shows the Achaean leaders one by one just before they fight (see [[catalogue-of-ships]] and [[the-view-from-the-wall]]). It gives each a few strokes of character, and it ends on the man who will lead the fighting of the next book: Diomedes, called worse than his father in battle, leaps down in ringing armor (4.419–421).

## Outside Homer

The traditional title of the second part of Book 4, Ἀγαμέμνονος ἐπιπώλησις, “Agamemnon's review”, is made from the verb of the scene, ἐπεπωλεῖτο, “he ranged through” (4.231, 4.250; see [[titles-of-book-4]]). Plato's Socrates quotes Diomedes' rebuke, “Friend, sit in silence, and obey my word” (4.412), as a lesson in self-control for the guardians of his city (*Republic* 3.389e; see [[plato-silent-achaeans]]).
