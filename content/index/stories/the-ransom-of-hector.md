## The story

**The twelfth dawn.** The games are over and the army goes to supper and sleep, but Achilles weeps for Patroclus and cannot sleep. At each dawn he yokes his horses, drags Hector three times around the mound of Patroclus and leaves him face down in the dust, and Apollo keeps all defilement from the body and covers it with the golden aegis (24.1–21; see [[achilles]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). The blessed gods pity Hector and urge the slayer of Argus to steal him away; all agree but Hera, Poseidon and Athena, who keep the hatred they conceived for Ilios when Alexander insulted the goddesses (24.22–30; see [[hermes]], [[the-judgment-of-paris]] and [[the-gods-debate]]). On the twelfth dawn after Hector's death Apollo reproaches the gods: Hector burned them offerings, and they will not save even his corpse for his family to burn, but help “deadly Achilles”, who has lost all pity and defiles the senseless earth (24.31–54; see [[apollo]] and [[the-senseless-earth]]). Hera answers that Achilles, a goddess' son, cannot have equal honor with a mortal (24.55–63; see [[hera]]). Zeus rules that the body shall not be stolen, but that Thetis shall bid her son take gifts from Priam and release it (24.64–76; see [[zeus]]).

**Thetis.** Iris plunges into the sea between Samos and Imbros and finds Thetis in her cave among the sea goddesses, weeping for her son's fate; veiled in black, Thetis goes up to Olympus, where Hera puts a golden cup in her hand (24.77–102; see [[iris]] and [[thetis]]). Zeus tells her of the nine days' quarrel: he bestows this glory on Achilles, and she is to tell her son that the gods are angry with him, Zeus above all, because he keeps Hector by the ships; Iris will go to Priam, “to ransom his dear son, going to the ships of the Achaeans, / and bring gifts to Achilles that may warm his heart” (24.103–119; see [[kydos]] and [[doron]]). Thetis finds Achilles groaning in his hut, bids him remember food and bed, since his death is near, and gives him the message; he consents at once: “So be it: let him who brings the ransom carry off the body, / if the Olympian himself so bids with a ready heart” (24.120–142; see [[apoina]]).

**Iris and Priam.** Zeus sends Iris with the message and its conditions: Priam is to go alone, with one older herald to drive the mules and the wagon; the slayer of Argus will escort him; and Achilles will neither kill him nor let others do so, since he is “not a fool, nor heedless, nor an offender” (24.143–158). Iris finds Priam in his courtyard, wrapped in his cloak, the dung he rolled in heaped on his head and neck, his sons weeping around him and his daughters and his sons' wives mourning through the house, and she gives him the message (24.159–187; see [[priam]], [[sons-of-priam]] and [[priams-palace]]).

**Hecuba and the ransom.** Priam has the mule wagon made ready and asks Hecuba's counsel. She cries out against his going to “that eater of raw flesh, that faithless man”, and wishes she could fix her teeth in Achilles' liver; he answers that he heard the goddess himself, and will go even if Achilles kills him once he has taken his son in his arms (24.188–227; see [[hecuba]]). From his chests he takes twelve robes, twelve single cloaks, twelve rugs, twelve white mantles and twelve tunics, ten talents of gold, two tripods, four cauldrons and the beautiful cup that the Thracians gave him on an embassy (24.228–237; see [[robe]], [[cloak]], [[tunic]], [[talent]], [[tripod]], [[cauldron]] and [[cup]]).

**The wagon.** He drives the Trojans from his colonnade with his staff and rebukes his nine surviving sons as liars and dancers, wishing that they had all been killed in Hector's place; they bring out the wagon, lash the yoke to the pole, load “the boundless ransom for Hector's head”, and yoke the mules that the Mysians once gave him, and the horses he kept for himself (24.237–280; see [[boundless-ransom]], [[mules]] and [[mysians]]). Hecuba brings wine in a golden cup; Priam washes his hands, pours a libation and prays to Zeus of Ida to let him come to Achilles “as one welcomed and pitied”, and Zeus sends an eagle on the right (24.281–321; see [[libation]], [[prayer]] and [[eagle]]).

**Hermes.** Priam drives out with Idaeus in the wagon before him, and his sons and sons-in-law follow him to the plain, “lamenting much, as if he were going to his death” (24.322–331; see [[idaeus]]). Zeus pities the old man and sends Hermes, who binds on his golden sandals, takes his wand and goes in the likeness of a young prince (24.331–348). At nightfall, while the mules and horses drink at the river beyond the mound of Ilus, Hermes meets them, takes Priam's hand, calls himself a Myrmidon, the son of Polyctor and an attendant of Achilles, and tells him that the body lies whole and fresh as dew, because the gods care for his son; he refuses Priam's gift of a goblet, drives the chariot to the ships, puts the sentries to sleep, opens the gate of Achilles' courtyard, reveals himself and goes back to Olympus (24.349–467; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]], [[polyctor]], [[truth-and-deception]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]).

**The supplication.** Priam goes into the hut unseen, clasps Achilles' knees and kisses his hands, and Achilles and his companions look at him in amazement, as men look at an exile who has killed a man in his own land and comes to a rich man's house (24.468–484; see [[hut]] and [[thambos]]). He bids Achilles remember his father, an old man like himself, who still hopes to see his son come home; of his own fifty sons Ares has taken most, and Achilles killed Hector, for whom he has come with a boundless ransom: “But respect the gods, Achilles, and pity me, / remembering your own father” (24.485–506; see [[supplication]]). The two men weep, Priam for Hector and Achilles for his father and for Patroclus. Then Achilles raises the old man by the hand and bids him sit: the gods have spun the thread for mortals to live in grief; Zeus gives from two jars, of evils and of blessings; Peleus had every gift but one doomed son, who is far away, and Priam was blessed once in wealth and sons (24.507–551; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]] and [[peleus]]).

**The ransom taken.** Priam will not sit while Hector lies uncared for, and Achilles flares up: he means to release Hector himself, since his mother brought him the message of Zeus, and he knows that a god led Priam to the ships; let him not stir his heart again, lest he not spare him, suppliant though he is (24.552–571). He goes out like a lion with Automedon and Alcimus, unyokes the mules and horses, brings in the herald and takes the ransom from the wagon, leaving two mantles and a tunic; he has the slave women wash and anoint the body out of Priam's sight, lifts it onto a bier himself, and asks Patroclus not to be angry, since the ransom is not unseemly and he will have his share of it (24.572–595; see [[automedon]], [[alcimedon]], [[hector]] and [[patroclus]]).

**Niobe and the meal.** “Your son is released to you, old man, as you asked” (24.599): Priam will see him at dawn, and now they must think of supper, as even Niobe remembered food when her twelve children had been killed by Apollo and Artemis (24.596–620; see [[the-children-of-niobe]] and [[niobe]]). Achilles slaughters a sheep, his companions roast it, Automedon sets out the bread and Achilles serves the meat; when they have eaten, Priam marvels at Achilles, how tall he was and how fair, and Achilles marvels at Priam (24.621–633; see [[feast-type-scene]]).

**The truce.** Priam asks to be put to bed, for he has not slept since Hector died, and has only now eaten and drunk (24.634–642; see [[fasting]]). Beds are made for him and the herald beneath the colonnade, outside, lest a counselor see him and tell Agamemnon; and Achilles asks how many days the funeral will take. Nine days of lament, Priam answers, the burial and the feast on the tenth, the tomb on the eleventh, “and on the twelfth we will fight, if we must”; Achilles promises to hold back the war, and takes the old man's right hand at the wrist, so that he may feel no fear (24.643–672; see [[truce]] and [[bed]]). Priam and the herald sleep in the porch, Achilles in the inner part of the hut with Briseis beside him (24.673–676; see [[briseis]]).

**Home.** Hermes wakes Priam before dawn: his sons would pay three times as much to ransom him alive, if Agamemnon found him; he yokes the horses and mules, drives them through the camp unseen, and leaves them at the ford of the Xanthus as Dawn spreads over the earth (24.677–697; see [[scamander]]). Cassandra sees her father from Pergamus and cries out to the city; everyone comes to meet the body at the gates, and Andromache and Hecuba tear their hair at the wagon, until Priam bids the crowd make way, and in the house Hector is laid on a corded bed with singers beside him (24.697–722; see [[cassandra]], [[pergamus]] and [[lament]]). Andromache, Hecuba and Helen lead the laments; Priam sends the Trojans for wood, since Achilles has promised that they will not be harmed until the twelfth dawn, and for nine days they gather it; on the tenth they burn Hector, and at the next dawn they gather his bones into a golden chest, heap up his mound and feast in Priam's house (24.723–804; see [[the-laments-for-hector]] and [[the-funeral-of-hector]]).

## In the poem

**Asked and refused.** The ransom was asked for once before. Hector, dying, begged Achilles to take “bronze and gold in plenty, / the gifts that my father and my lady mother will give you”, and give back his body for the fire (22.338–343). Achilles refused: not even if they brought ten and twenty times the ransom, “not even if he should bid them weigh out your own body in gold, / Priam, descendant of Dardanus”, would his mother lay him on a bier and lament him (22.345–354; see [[the-death-of-hector]]). When the body was dragged before the city, Priam begged the people to let him go alone to the ships and beg Achilles by his father, “for he too has a father like me, / Peleus” (22.416–422); that is the plea he makes in the hut (24.486–492). The refusal is undone point by point: Achilles takes the ransom, lifts the body onto a bier with his own hands (24.589), and lets it go home, where his mother laments him (24.747–759).

**The will of Zeus.** Zeus himself joins the ransom to Achilles' honor: “I bestow this glory on Achilles” (24.110). Walter Leaf takes the κῦδος to be the gifts, a solace to his honor, since he will not have to give the body up unpaid; in the heroic code, as Book 9 shows, honor lay in a return received, as when Phoenix tells Achilles that he would not bid him give up his wrath if Agamemnon were not bringing gifts, and that if he goes into the war without the gifts he will not be held in the same honor (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.110; 9.515–519, 9.602–605; see [[time]] and [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). The Alexandrians athetized Achilles' promise to give Patroclus a share of the ransom (24.594–595), since he releases the body at Zeus' command and not for the gifts; Leaf answers that the command itself is conditional on the ransom (24.119, 24.137–139), and that only the ransom lets Achilles say that the surrender is not dishonorable (on 24.594; see the translation's note on 24.594). Thetis gives her son the end of Zeus' message but not the means (24.133–137 against 24.117–119), and so Achilles, who consented to give the body to “him who brings the ransom”, is amazed when Priam himself appears (24.139, 24.483; Leaf on 24.181).

**The first book and the last.** Book 1 began with an old father who came to the ships with a boundless ransom and was driven away (1.12–33), and the last book ends with an old father who comes with a boundless ransom and is received (24.502 ≈ 1.13; see [[book-1-and-book-24]], [[chryses]] and [[boundless-ransom]]). Even Achilles' threat takes up Agamemnon's words to Chryses, “do not provoke me” and μή σε γέρον (24.560, 24.569; 1.26, 1.32). Thetis goes up to Olympus in both books (1.493–530, 24.97–119), and both count nine days, of the plague before the assembly and of the gods' quarrel before Zeus decides (1.53–54; 24.107).

**Two fathers.** The story turns on Achilles' father. Priam's first words are “Remember your father, Achilles” (24.486), and Hermes had told him to beg Achilles by his father, his mother and his child (24.465–467); Achilles weeps for his father beside the father whose son he killed, and his consolation turns to Peleus, given everything by the gods but a son to care for him in his old age (24.507–512, 24.534–542; see [[fathers-and-sons]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]). The mother's part is Thetis', who carries the message and is the reason, as Zeus says, that the body cannot simply be stolen (24.71–73; see [[mothers-and-sons]]).

**An Odyssean book.** Walter Leaf notes again and again the words and verses that the book shares with the Odyssey, and takes them as signs of its affinity with that poem: the hardships of the sea in Achilles' memories of Patroclus (on 24.6), the beds made for guests as in a palace (on 24.643). Bergk, he reports, observed that the Iliad knows only omens that come unasked, and that praying for one, as Hecuba bids Priam do, belongs elsewhere to the Odyssey (on 24.290). He finds the hut of Achilles drawn as a palace, unlike anywhere else in the Iliad (on 24.448; see [[hut]]).

## Outside Homer

The book's traditional title is Ἕκτορος λύτρα, “the Ransom of Hector” (see [[title-of-book-24]]). Aeschylus put the story on the stage in his lost *Phrygians*, which was also called *The Ransom of Hector* (see [[aeschylus-phrygians]]). Plato's Socrates will not have it believed that Achilles was so fond of money that he took gifts from Agamemnon, and released a corpse when he had been paid its price but not otherwise (*Republic* 3.390e–391a; see [[plato-the-price-of-hectors-body]]). Virgil's Aeneas finds the story painted on the walls of Juno's temple at Carthage: Achilles has dragged Hector three times around the walls of Ilium and is selling the lifeless body for gold, and Priam stretches out unarmed hands (*Aeneid* 1.483–487; see [[virgils-hector]]). On the night Troy falls Priam reproaches Pyrrhus, the son of Achilles, who has made him see his son killed before his eyes: Achilles, whom he falsely claims as his father, was not such to his enemy Priam, but respected the rights and the trust of a suppliant, gave back Hector's bloodless body for burial and sent Priam home to his kingdom (*at non ille, satum quo te mentiris, Achilles / talis in hoste fuit Priamo; sed iura fidemque / supplicis erubuit corpusque exsangue sepulcro / reddidit Hectoreum meque in mea regna remisit*, *Aeneid* 2.540–543; see [[virgils-priam]]). Horace counts the journey among the deeds of Mercury: with him as guide, rich Priam left Ilium and slipped past the proud sons of Atreus, the Thessalian fires and the camp hostile to Troy (*Quin et Atridas duce te superbos / Ilio dives Priamus relicto / Thessalosque ignis et iniqua Troiae / castra fefellit*, *Odes* 1.10.13–16; see [[horace-mercury-and-priam]]).
