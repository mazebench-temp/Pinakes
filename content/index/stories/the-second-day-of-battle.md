## The story

**Dawn on Olympus.** At dawn Zeus holds an assembly of the gods on the highest peak of Olympus and forbids any of them, god or goddess, to help either side, on pain of a beating or of being hurled into Tartarus; let them hang a golden rope from the sky and all pull on it, they could not drag him down (8.1–27; see [[tartarus]] and [[the-golden-rope]]). Athena alone answers: the gods will keep out of the war but will give the Argives counsel, and Zeus smiles at her (8.28–40). He drives his bronze-footed horses to Gargarus on Ida and sits there, looking out on the city and the ships (8.41–52; see [[gargarus]], [[ida]] and [[watching-the-war]]).

**The scales.** The Achaeans eat and arm in their huts, the Trojans, fewer but driven by necessity, in their city, and the armies meet in the six verses of their first meeting in Book 4 (8.53–65; see [[the-armies-clash]]). The fighting is even through the morning. At midday Zeus holds out his golden scales with two fates of death in them, and the Achaeans' day sinks; he thunders from Ida and sends a blazing flash among them, and pale fear takes hold of them all (8.66–77; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]).

**Diomedes and Nestor.** Idomeneus, Agamemnon and the two Ajaxes give way. Only Nestor is left, held by a trace horse that Paris has shot in the head, and Hector is bearing down on him when Diomedes sees it (8.78–91; see [[nestor]] and [[paris]]). Odysseus does not hear his call and runs past to the ships (8.92–98; see [[odysseus]]). Diomedes takes the old man into his own chariot and drives at Hector; his spear kills Hector's charioteer Eniopeus, and Hector finds a new one, Archeptolemus (8.99–129; see [[diomedes]], [[eniopeus]] and [[archeptolemus]]). The Trojans would have been penned in their city like lambs, had not Zeus thrown his thunderbolt before Diomedes' horses (8.130–136; see [[penned-like-lambs]] and [[thunderbolt]]). Nestor turns the chariot: Zeus is giving the glory to Hector today, and no man can hold back the mind of Zeus (see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). Diomedes fears what Hector will say, and when Hector taunts him, he three times thinks of turning, and three times Zeus thunders from Ida (8.137–171).

**Hector's boast.** Hector rouses the Trojans: Zeus has nodded him victory, the wall is a feeble thing, and his horses will leap the ditch; he will burn the ships. He calls on his four horses to carry him to Nestor's golden shield and Diomedes' corselet (8.172–197; see [[hector]] and [[the-horses-of-hector]]). Hera, shaking Olympus in her anger, asks Poseidon to join the gods who favor the Danaans in holding off Zeus, and he refuses (8.198–211; see [[hera]] and [[poseidon]]). The Achaeans are hemmed in between the ditch and the wall, and Hector would have burned the ships, had Hera not put it into Agamemnon's mind to rouse them (8.212–219; see [[the-achaean-wall]]).

**Agamemnon and the eagle.** Agamemnon goes to the ship of Odysseus in the middle of the line, a purple cloak in his hand, and shouts to the army: where are the boasts they made on Lemnos (see [[where-have-our-boasts-gone]])? Then he prays with tears that the Achaeans may at least escape. Zeus pities him, nods that the army will be saved, and sends an eagle that drops a fawn beside his altar (8.220–252; see [[agamemnon]] and [[eagle]]).

**Teucer.** Diomedes is first across the ditch and kills Agelaus, and the other leaders follow (8.253–265; see [[agelaus]]). Teucer shoots from under the shield of his brother Ajax, going back to him after each shot “like a child under its mother”, and kills eight men; Agamemnon promises him a gift of honor (8.266–299; see [[teucer]], [[ajax]] and [[like-a-child-under-its-mother]]). His arrows meant for Hector kill Gorgythion, son of Priam, who droops like a poppy, and the charioteer Archeptolemus; Hector takes his brother Cebriones as his driver, leaps down, and strikes Teucer with a stone; Ajax covers him, and he is carried groaning to the ships (8.300–334; see [[gorgythion]], [[the-poppy]] and [[cebriones]]).

**To the ships.** Zeus rouses the Trojans again, and Hector drives the Achaeans back across the ditch like a hound at the heels of a boar or a lion, always killing the hindmost. They halt by the ships and pray, while Hector wheels his horses this way and that with the eyes of the Gorgon or of Ares (8.335–349; see [[the-hound-at-the-heels]] and [[gorgon]]).

**The goddesses.** Hera pities the Achaeans. Athena rails against her father, who has forgotten how she saved his son Heracles in his labors and has fulfilled the plans of Thetis, and she arms in Zeus' own tunic while Hera harnesses the horses; the gates of heaven, kept by the Hours, open of themselves (8.350–396; see [[athena]], [[heracles-and-cerberus]], [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]] and [[hours]]). Zeus sees them from Ida and sends Iris with his threat: he will lame their horses, hurl them from the chariot and smash it, and their wounds will not heal in ten years. Iris meets them at the gates of Olympus and adds an insult of her own, and Hera turns back (8.397–437; see [[iris]]). Zeus drives home to Olympus and mocks the two goddesses. Hera answers in the words of Athena's plea at dawn, and Zeus foretells that Hector will not cease from war until Achilles is roused beside the ships, over the dead Patroclus (8.438–484; see [[quarrel-on-olympus]], [[the-death-of-patroclus]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**Night.** The sun sets, against the Trojans' will and to the Achaeans' relief:

{{quote:afbb3fe7-2380-54db-a972-5bc74db915bc}}

Hector holds an assembly by the river, leaning on his spear of eleven cubits. The Trojans are to camp on the plain, feed their horses, bring food and wine from the city and burn fires all night, so that the Achaeans cannot slip away; the city is to be watched; at dawn they will fight at the ships, and Diomedes, he boasts, will lie wounded among the foremost. “If only I were immortal and ageless all my days” (8.489–541; see [[hector]], [[immortal-and-ageless]] and [[war-and-night]]). The Trojans sit all night on the bridges of war, and their thousand fires shine between the ships and the Xanthus like the stars around the moon, while the horses wait for the dawn (8.542–565; see [[stars-around-the-moon]] and [[night-watch]]).

## In the poem

**The plan at work.** This is the first day on which the promise that Zeus made to Thetis is kept in battle. The Dream of Book 2 brought the armies out, but through the long first day of fighting, from Book 2 to Book 7, the Achaeans held their own without Achilles, and Zeus stayed in the background (see [[the-first-battle]] and [[the-aristeia-of-diomedes]]). In Book 8 he acts himself, and every turn of the day is his. He clears the field of gods, weighs the fates, puts the Achaeans to flight with his thunder, stops Diomedes with a thunderbolt, sends the eagle that lets them rally, rouses the Trojans again, and turns back the goddesses. Nestor says what the army must learn: “No man can hold back the mind of Zeus, however mighty he is, since he is far the stronger” (8.143–144; see [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**Turns.** The narrator marks the day's turning points with his figure of the outcome that almost happened. Nestor would have lost his life, had not Diomedes seen it (8.90–91); the Trojans would have been penned in Ilios, had not Zeus seen it (8.130–132); Hector would have burned the ships, had not Hera prompted Agamemnon (8.217–219; see [[would-have-had-not]]). Each time the story turns, and the day swings between Achaean and Trojan advance until the last rout.

**Answering scenes.** The book is built of pairs. The day opens with an assembly of the gods, at which Zeus speaks, and closes with an assembly of the Trojans on the plain, at which Hector speaks; both are told with the same words, ἀγορὴν ποιήσατο, “held an assembly” (8.2, 8.489; see [[assembly]]). Zeus drives out to Ida and back to Olympus, and Hera and Athena drive out to the gates of Olympus and back, the god's journeys bracketing the goddesses' failed one (8.41–52, 8.374–396, 8.432–443; see [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]]). Athena's plea at dawn is Hera's at nightfall (8.32–37 ≈ 8.463–468). Hector loses two charioteers, and the same four verses tell how each fell and how Hector grieved and left him (8.122–125 = 8.314–317). The day begins and ends with Dawn (8.1, 8.565).

**The first test of the wall.** The Achaeans built their wall and ditch the day before, without offering hecatombs to the gods, as Poseidon complained (7.433–464; see [[the-building-of-the-wall]]). In Book 8 it is tried for the first time, and it holds only as a refuge. Hector calls it a feeble thing and says his horses will leap the ditch (8.177–179); the Achaeans are hemmed in between ditch and wall (8.213–216), cross back over the stakes and the ditch in their flight (8.343–345), and spend the night behind it while the Trojans camp outside (see [[the-achaean-wall]]).

**Hector's day.** The day is Hector's first great success, and Zeus gives it to him. He rises from the near rout of the early afternoon, when Diomedes almost drove the Trojans back into their city, to the camp on the plain at nightfall, and his confidence rises with it, from the taunt of Diomedes to the boast of the ships' burning to the wish to be immortal and ageless. The prophecy that Zeus speaks at the same hour sets its term: Hector will fight on only until Achilles is roused (8.473–477).

**The interrupted battle.** The ancient title of the book is Κόλος μάχη, “the interrupted battle”. κόλος is “docked, cut short”, the word for the spear that Hector's sword cuts off in Ajax's hand (κόλον δόρυ, 16.117; see [[title-of-book-8]]). The battle of Book 8 is cut short twice over, by Zeus' interventions and by the night, which saves the Achaeans before Hector can reach the ships (8.497–501). The day is Day 25 in this index's count of the poem's days. The night that follows lasts through the embassy to Achilles in Book 9 and the night raid of Book 10, and [[the-third-day-of-battle|the third day of battle]] begins at the dawn of Book 11 (11.1–2).
