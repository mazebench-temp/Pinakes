## The story

**At the ford.** The Trojans flee from Achilles to the ford of “the eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot” (21.2), and there he splits them. Half run across the plain toward the city, where Hera spreads a mist before them; half are penned into the river and swim this way and that among the eddies, like locusts that flee from a fire into a river (21.1–16; see [[the-locusts-and-the-fire]]). Achilles leaves his spear leaning against the tamarisks on the bank and leaps in with his sword, striking on every side, and the water grows red with blood; the Trojans cower under the banks like small fish fleeing a dolphin (21.17–26; see [[tamarisk]] and [[the-dolphin-and-the-fishes]]). When his hands are weary he takes twelve young men alive out of the river as the penalty for Patroclus and sends them, bound with their own belts, to the ships (21.26–32; see [[human-sacrifice]] and [[achilles]]).

**The river's anger.** By the river he meets Lycaon, son of Priam, and kills him; he throws the body into the stream, to be carried out to sea and eaten by the fish, and he tells the Trojans that the river to which they sacrifice bulls and throw live horses will not save them (21.34–135; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]], [[rivers]] and [[fish]]). “So he spoke, and the river grew all the more angry in his heart” (21.136). The river ponders how to stop Achilles, and puts might into Asteropaeus, the grandson of another river, the Axius, who comes out of the water to face Achilles with two spears. Achilles kills him and boasts that the lineage of Zeus is mightier than any river's, and the eels and the fishes feed on the body (21.136–204; see [[asteropaeus]], [[zeus-is-mightier-than-rivers]] and [[eels]]).

**The river protests.** Achilles kills seven of the Paeonians who flee along the river (21.205–210; see [[paeonians]]), and the river speaks:

{{quote:db5abc47-d418-5c3d-9d4d-bc55127b8d9d}}

Achilles agrees, “So it shall be, Scamander, nurtured by Zeus, as you bid”, but he will not stop killing Trojans until he has penned them in the city and met Hector (21.222–226). He rushes on the Trojans again, and the river reproaches Apollo for not standing by them as Zeus had charged him (21.227–232; see [[apollo]]).

**The river rises.** Achilles leaps from the bank into the middle of the stream:

{{quote:4b49e0dc-c977-5b51-9649-bf8a332006e9}}

The wave rises around him and beats on his shield, and he cannot keep his footing. He catches hold of an elm, which falls uprooted, tears away the bank and bridges the river; he leaps out of the eddy and runs over the plain in fear. The river rises after him, darkening at the crest, to stop him and save the Trojans (21.240–250; see [[elm]]). Achilles springs away as far as a spear's cast, with the swoop of an eagle, the black hunter, and the river comes flowing behind him with a great din (21.251–256; see [[like-an-eagle]]); as water led along a channel outruns the man who leads it, the wave of the stream keeps overtaking him, “nimble though he was; for the gods are stronger than men” (21.257–264; see [[the-channel-and-the-gardener]]). As often as he turns to stand against it, the great wave beats upon his shoulders, and the current eats the ground from under his feet (21.265–271).

**Achilles' prayer.** Achilles looks up to the sky and groans. No god will save him from the river: his mother bewitched him with lies when she told him that he would die under the wall of Troy by the arrows of Apollo. Would that Hector had killed him, a brave man killing a brave; “But now it was fated that I be caught by a wretched death, / trapped in a great river, like a swineherd boy / whom a torrent sweeps away as he crosses it in winter” (21.272–283; see [[the-swineherd-boy]], [[thetis]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). Poseidon and Athena come at once in the likeness of men and take his hand. With the consent of Zeus they are his helpers: “so it is not your fate to be brought down by a river, / but he will soon subside, and you yourself will see it” (21.284–292). He is not to stop until he has penned the Trojans in the city and killed Hector; then he will go back to the ships, and they grant him the triumph (21.293–298; see [[poseidon]] and [[athena]]). Achilles goes on toward the plain, now all under water, with fine armor and corpses floating in it, and the river can no longer hold him back, for Athena has put great strength in him (21.299–304).

**The Simoeis.** The river does not give up. Rearing his wave on high, he calls on his brother the Simoeis to help him check this man before he sacks the city of Priam: let him fill his streams from his springs, spur on all his torrents, and raise a great wave with a din of logs and stones. Neither strength nor looks nor fine armor will save Achilles; the river will bury him in sand and silt so deep that the Achaeans will not find his bones, and he will need no tomb (21.305–323; see [[simoeis]] and [[scamander]]). The Simoeis does not answer. The river rushes on Achilles, seething with foam and blood and corpses, and the surging wave stands over him and is bringing him down (21.324–327).

**Hephaestus.** Hera cries out in fear for Achilles and sends her son: he is to burn the trees along the banks and set the river himself on fire, while she raises the West and South Winds to carry the blaze to the Trojans, and he is not to stop until she calls (21.328–341; see [[hera]] and [[hephaestus]]). The fire burns the dead on the plain and dries it, as the North Wind of late summer dries a newly watered orchard; then it turns on the river (21.342–349; see [[the-north-wind-dries-the-orchard]]). The elms and the willows and the tamarisks burn, the lotus and the rushes and the galingale, and the eels and the fishes tumble in distress (21.350–355; see [[fire]]). The burning river gives way: no god can match Hephaestus, let Achilles drive the Trojans out of their city, what has he to do with strife? His streams boil like a cauldron melting the fat of a hog (21.356–365; see [[the-boiling-cauldron]]). He begs Hera to call off her son, and swears an oath:

{{quote:ea279537-4176-5c87-8e7b-f8cccf95cbd4}}

Hera bids Hephaestus hold back, for it is not fitting to batter an immortal god like this for the sake of mortals. He quenches the fire, the wave rushes back into its fair streams, and the two gods cease (21.377–384). Then strife falls among the other gods (21.385; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]).

## In the poem

**Foretold.** The Catalogue had said that Achilles would kill two of the Trojan allies, Ennomus the augur and the Carian who came to war wearing gold, “in the river” (2.858–861, 2.870–875; see [[the-river-battle-foretold]]), and when the gods went to war in Book 20 they were set in pairs, “and against Hephaestus the great deep-eddying river” (20.73). This book carries out both, and its battle in the river and its battle of fire against water are one story: the river's fight with Achilles becomes the first of the fights of the gods. The book's traditional title, Μάχη παραποτάμιος, “the Battle by the River”, is named for it (see [[title-of-book-21]]).

**The river as enemy.** The similes of the earlier books likened a hero who cannot be stopped to a river in flood, Diomedes sweeping the dikes away (5.87–92; see [[river-in-flood]]) and Ajax driving the Trojans as a winter torrent comes down from the mountains (11.492–495). Here the river in flood is real, and it is the hero's enemy. The river fights with what he has, his streams and eddies, the dead he casts out, a wave that breaks over Achilles' shoulders and a current that eats the ground from under his feet, the logs and stones he calls for, and the sand and silt in which he means to bury him. Achilles cannot strike back. The killer of the book runs from the water in fear (21.247–248), and his prayer is not for victory but against a death without glory, like a boy's in a winter torrent.

**A god and a stream.** The river is a god who takes a man's likeness to speak (21.213), the son of Zeus and “nurtured by Zeus” (21.2, 21.223), a god with a cult among the Trojans (21.130–132; see [[dolopion]]), and a brother to the Simoeis (21.308). It is also water. Hephaestus does not fight him with spears but burns his trees and plants, his fish and his water, until the river boils; the river yields not to a blow but to heat. Walter Leaf calls the symbolic allusion to the enmity of fire and water obvious (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.332). Hera stops her son before the river is destroyed, “for the sake of mortals” (21.380), and the river's oath turns the fight toward the end of Troy, which he will not save “even when all Troy blazes with ravening fire” (21.375; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).

**Achilles' death.** In his fear Achilles remembers his mother's word that he would die under the wall by the arrows of Apollo (21.277–278), and Poseidon assures him that he is not fated to be overcome by a river (21.291). The book thus names, in the midst of Achilles' greatest killing, the death that awaits him; Hector will foretell it again, dying, at the Scaean gate (22.359–360; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).

**The critics.** Aristophanes athetized Achilles' taunt about the bulls and horses given to the river (21.130–135), as added by readers who did not see why the river was angry; the speech to Apollo (21.229–232) has been condemned because Apollo is not there to hear it, and Poseidon's naming of himself and Athena (21.290) because a god in a man's shape should not name himself; Leaf defends both speeches (on 21.229, 21.290; see the translation's notes on 21.130–135, 21.229–232 and 21.290). He finds it hard to say what Achilles grants when he answers “So it shall be” (on 21.223), and he notes that the appeals to Apollo and to the Simoeis are both left unanswered (on 21.307).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates names Achilles' fight with the river among the things that must not be believed of him: that he was “disobedient toward the river, which was a god, and ready to fight it” (*Republic* 3.391b; see [[plato]] and [[plato-achilles-and-the-river]]). Proclus answers that Achilles did not disobey the god himself, but fought the visible water that hindered his attack, or one of the local powers, with Athena and Poseidon on his side (*On the Republic* I 148–149 Kroll), and he reads the pair of Hephaestus and the river as heat and dryness against cold and wet (I 95 Kroll; see [[proclus-on-the-battle-of-the-gods]]).

Virgil's Neptune, assuring Venus of his care for Aeneas, calls the Xanthus and the Simois to witness, and remembers the day when Achilles drove the Trojans against their walls and sent thousands to their death, *gemerentque repleti / amnes nec reperire viam atque evolvere posset / in mare se Xanthus*, “and the rivers groaned, choked full, and the Xanthus could not find its way and roll itself out into the sea” (*Aeneid* 5.803–808; see [[virgil]] and [[virgil-the-house-of-aeneas]]): the river's own complaint to Achilles, that he can find no way to pour his stream into the sea (21.218–220).
