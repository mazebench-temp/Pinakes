## The story

Homer never tells the war of the Seven, and he never counts them. What the Iliad says of it comes in pieces, most of them in Book 4.

{{quote:930cc724-b8b6-5776-918d-ad9c4cce47aa}}

The army marched to the Asopus, and from there sent Tydeus ahead as an envoy to the Cadmeians (4.382–384; see [[tydeus-at-thebes]]). Homer calls the attackers Achaeans (4.384). Their leaders “perished through their own recklessness”, Sthenelus says, and their sons took the city afterward (4.409; see [[the-epigonoi]]). Thebes is “seven-gated” (ἑπταπύλοιο, 4.406; see [[seven-gated]]); the seven attackers, one for each gate, are known from later poets.

The rest of Homer adds a little. Tydeus lies buried at Thebes (14.114), and Diomedes was a small child “when the army of the Achaeans perished at Thebes” (6.222–223). Tydeus had come to Argos from Calydon after wandering, and married a daughter of Adrastus (14.119–121), whose divine horse Arion is remembered in Book 23 (23.346–347; see [[adrastus]]). Mecisteus, father of Euryalus, once went to Thebes for the funeral games of Oedipus and beat all the Cadmeians (23.677–680; see [[mecisteus]]). In the Odyssey Odysseus sees in the underworld the mother of Oedipus, Epicaste, who married her son unknowing and hanged herself (Odyssey 11.271–280), and Eriphyle, “who took precious gold for her own husband” (Odyssey 11.326–327). The seer Amphiaraus, dear to Zeus and Apollo, “died at Thebes because of a woman's gifts” (15.244–247).

## In the poem

The war of the Seven is the great war before Troy, and its sons fight at Troy. Diomedes and Sthenelus, who lead the men of Argos and Tiryns with Euryalus son of Mecisteus (2.559–568), are the sons of Tydeus and Capaneus (see [[diomedes]], [[sthenelus]], [[tydeus]] and [[capaneus]]). Book 4 uses the older war to set the fathers against the sons. Agamemnon holds up Tydeus to Diomedes as the better man (4.370–400). Sthenelus answers that the sons did better: they took, with a smaller army, the city under whose walls their fathers had died (4.404–410).

In Agamemnon's story the war is fought against the warnings of the gods. The Mycenaeans were willing to join, but Zeus turned them back with ill-omened signs (4.380–381), and Sthenelus lays the fathers' deaths to their ἀτασθαλίαι, their reckless folly (4.409; see [[atasthalie]]). The sons won because they trusted the portents of the gods and the help of Zeus (4.408). The Theban war gives Book 4 a model of what happens to an army that ignores the gods' signs, told on the day the Trojans have broken an oath.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod names the war of Thebes beside the war of Troy as the two wars that destroyed the race of heroes. Evil war and the dread din of battle destroyed some of them “under seven-gated Thebes, in the land of Cadmus, fighting for the flocks of Oedipus”, and others at Troy, for the sake of Helen (*Works and Days* 161–165). His opening words, πόλεμός τε κακὸς καὶ φύλοπις αἰνή, are the words of the question Zeus puts to the gods in Book 4 (4.15), which the watching soldiers repeat when they see Athena come down (4.82).

An epic of the war, the *Thebaid*, was known in antiquity and is lost. Some ascribed it to Homer, and Pausanias, who reports this, ranked it next after the Iliad and the Odyssey (9.9.5). Its first line survives, quoted in the *Contest of Homer and Hesiod*: “Sing, goddess, of thirsty Argos, from which the lords…”, with the epithet πολυδίψιον (“thirsty”) that Agamemnon gives Argos in Book 4 (4.171; see [[thirsty]]). The Iliad's allusions show that a Theban epic tradition was well known to its poet and audience; whether it had the form of the *Thebaid* that later readers knew cannot be told.

Aeschylus' *Seven against Thebes* (467 BCE) sets seven champions at the seven gates, Tydeus, Capaneus, Eteoclus, Hippomedon, Parthenopaeus, Amphiaraus and Polynices, and ends with the brothers killing each other (see [[aeschylus-seven]]). Pindar has Adrastus, when the seven pyres of the dead had burned, mourn Amphiaraus, swallowed by the earth with his chariot, as “the eye of my army” (*Olympian* 6.12–17). Euripides retold the war in the *Phoenician Women* and its aftermath in the *Suppliant Women*, where Athens wins burial for the dead. Apollodorus gives the fullest summary: the brothers' quarrel over the kingship, Adrastus' army, Amphiaraus forced to go to a death he foresaw because his wife Eriphyle took a bribe, the deaths of the champions, and Adrastus alone saved by his horse Arion (3.6.1–3.7.1). He counts Adrastus among the Seven and notes that some lists omit Tydeus and Polynices and give Eteoclus and Mecisteus instead (3.6.3). Statius' Latin *Thebaid* (first century CE) told the whole war in twelve books (see [[statius-tydeus]]).
