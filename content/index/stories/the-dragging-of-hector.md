## The story

**The body.** Hector dies foretelling Achilles' own death, and Achilles draws the bronze spear out of the body, lays it aside and strips the bloody armor from his shoulders (22.355–369; see [[the-death-of-hector]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]). The other Achaeans run up, “and gazed at the stature and admirable looks / of Hector; and not one stood beside him without stabbing him” (22.370–371). ἀνουτητί, “without stabbing him”, is found only here, and οὐτάω, “stab”, is a thrust with a weapon held in the hand, kept apart from βάλλω, “hit, shoot” (see the translation's row for βάλλω / οὐτάω). They speak as they stab:

{{quote:a7257c43-015b-5b37-9e4c-fb182537e31e}}

μαλακώτερος, “softer”, is found only here. The fire was Hector's. He took hold of the stern of the ship that had brought Protesilaus to Troy and called for fire, and the Trojans threw it on the ship (15.704–718; 16.112–123; see [[fire]]); “And now he would have burned the balanced ships with blazing fire” is the narrator's verse of *Iliad* 8 (8.217; see [[blazing-fire]]).

**To the ships.** Achilles stands among the Achaeans and thinks of making trial of the city, then breaks off: Patroclus lies by the ships “a corpse unwept, unburied” (22.378–390; see [[patroclus]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). “But now come, young men of the Achaeans, singing the paean, / let us go back to the hollow ships, and take this man with us” (22.391–392; see [[paean]]). Then he devises his shameful deeds. He pierces the tendons of both feet behind, “from heel to ankle”, fastens straps of oxhide to them, ties them to the chariot and lets the head drag; he mounts, lifts the glorious armor in, and whips the horses, “and the pair flew on not unwilling” (22.396–400), the verse of eager horses since *Iliad* 5 (5.366, 8.45; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[chariot]]). The dust rises around the body, the dark hair spreads out, and the head lies in the dust, “that once was handsome” (22.401–403).

**Seen from the city.** His mother tears her hair, flings her head covering far from her and wails; his father groans, and the people wail through the city, as if all Ilios were smoldering with fire from top to bottom (22.405–411; see [[hecuba]], [[priam]], [[lament]] and [[as-if-ilios-were-smoldering]]). The people can hardly hold back Priam, who would go out through the Dardanian gates to the ships, to beg “this reckless man, this doer of violent deeds” (22.412–428; see [[supplication]]). Andromache, at her loom, hears the wailing from the tower, runs to the wall, and sees him “being dragged in front of the city; and the swift horses / were dragging him ruthlessly toward the hollow ships of the Achaeans”, and murky night covers her eyes (22.437–467; see [[andromache]]). ἀκηδέστως, “ruthlessly”, is said again when Hermes tells Priam how Achilles drags his son each dawn (24.417).

**At the ships.** Back at the ships, Achilles tells the dead Patroclus that he is fulfilling his promises: he has dragged Hector here to give him to the dogs to tear raw, and he will cut the throats of twelve young Trojans before the pyre (23.19–23; see [[human-sacrifice]]). The verse of the dragging is said again, “He spoke, and devised shameful deeds against brilliant Hector” (23.24 = 22.395), and he stretches the body face down in the dust beside Patroclus' bier (23.24–26). At the pyre he says that he will give Hector not to the fire but to the dogs; but Aphrodite keeps the dogs from him day and night and anoints him with ambrosial oil of roses, so that the dragging may not tear his skin, and Apollo covers the ground where he lies with a dark cloud, so that the sun may not wither his flesh (23.182–191; see [[aphrodite]] and [[apollo]]).

**Around the tomb.** When the funeral games are over, Achilles cannot sleep for grief. Each dawn he yokes his horses, ties Hector behind the chariot, drags him three times around the tomb of Patroclus, and leaves him stretched face down in the dust; Apollo, pitying the man even in death, keeps all disfigurement from his flesh and covers him with the golden aegis, so that the dragging may not tear him (24.1–21; see [[aegis]]). The gods pity Hector as they watch, and on the twelfth dawn Apollo rebukes them for letting Achilles defile the dead man (24.22–54; see [[the-senseless-earth]]). Zeus sends Thetis to tell her son that the gods are angry, and Achilles agrees to give the body back for a ransom (24.104–140; see [[thetis]] and [[the-ransom-of-hector]]). Hermes, guiding Priam to the ships, tells him that neither dogs nor birds have eaten his son and that he has not decayed; though he is dragged each dawn he is not disfigured, the blood is washed from him, and all the wounds have closed where he was struck, though many drove their bronze into him (24.411–421; see [[hermes]]).

## In the poem

**Foretold.** *Iliad* 22 announces the outrage before it happens. Priam pictures his own body torn by the dogs of his house at his doors, the most pitiable thing that comes to wretched mortals (22.66–76; see [[all-is-fitting-for-the-young]]), and Hecuba warns Hector that “beside the ships of the Argives, the swift dogs will devour you” (22.89; see [[mothers-and-sons]]). Hector, proposing that the victor give back the other's body, promises, “for I will not defile you terribly, if Zeus grants me / to outlast you” (22.256–257), and Achilles refuses any agreement (22.261–267; see [[no-oaths-between-lions-and-men]]). Over the dying man he says, “and you the dogs and the birds / will tear shamefully, but him the Achaeans will give burial rites” (22.335–336), and he refuses all ransom: “the dogs and the birds will divide you all among them” (22.345–354; see [[dogs]], [[birds]] and [[apoina]]). The proem promised that fate to many heroes, made “the spoil of dogs / and of every bird” (1.4–5); Achilles promises it to Hector by name. The dogs never touch him (23.184–187, 24.411).

**Hector's purpose for Patroclus.** The outrage answers one that Hector meant to commit. When he had stripped Patroclus, Hector “was dragging him, to cut the head from his shoulders with the sharp bronze, / and drag off the corpse and give it to the Trojan dogs” (17.126–127), and Iris told Achilles that Hector “is bent on dragging it; and his heart bids him fix the head / on the stakes” (18.175–177; see [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]]). Hippothous dragged the body by the foot, “having tied a strap around the tendons by the ankle” (17.289–290; see [[hippothous]]), where Achilles now pierces Hector's tendons “from heel to ankle” (22.397). Achilles answered with a vow: he would not give Patroclus burial rites until he brought “Hector's / armor and head” (18.334–335). He carries the armor in the chariot and lets the head drag in the dust (22.398–402). Homer's ancient defenders answered Plato from these passages (see [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]]).

**The head in the dust.** The verses of Hector's head are made from those of the helmet that Apollo struck from Patroclus. Then the horsehair “was stained / with blood and dust; before that it had not been lawful / for the horsehair-crested helmet to be stained with dust, / but it guarded the head and the graceful brow of a divine man, / Achilles; but then Zeus gave it to Hector / to wear on his head” (16.795–800; see [[helmet]]). Now the man who wore that helmet lies in the dust, and his head, κάρη … πάρος χαρίεν, “that once was handsome”, is given to his enemies by the same god: τότε δὲ Ζεὺς δυσμενέεσσι / δῶκεν ἀεικίσσασθαι, “but then Zeus gave him to his enemies / to defile in his own native land” (22.402–404; see [[zeus]] and [[the-armor-of-peleus]]).

**Shameful deeds.** The poem names the act. ἀεικέα … ἔργα, “shameful deeds” (22.395 = 23.24), and ἀεικίσσασθαι, “to defile” (22.404), take up Hector's promise, οὐ γὰρ ἐγώ σʼ ἔκπαγλον ἀεικιῶ, “for I will not defile you terribly” (22.256), and Achilles' own ἀϊκῶς, “shamefully” (22.336), is the adverb of the same adjective (see the translation's row for ἀϊκῶς). The verb was used of what the Myrmidons might do to Sarpedon's body, “lest they take away his armor and defile his body” (16.545, 16.559), and of the flies that might “defile the body” of Patroclus (19.26–27); Apollo tells the gods of Achilles, “for in his rage he defiles the senseless earth” (24.54). The last word of it is Achilles' own. When he has given the body back, he asks Patroclus not to be angry, since Priam gave him a ransom that was οὐ … ἀεικέα, “not unseemly” (24.592–594).

**Admired and stabbed.** The Achaeans gaze at Hector's “stature and admirable looks” before each of them stabs him (22.370–371), and the narrator says of his head, as it drags, “that once was handsome” (22.403). Priam had said that for a young man killed in battle “all that is seen of him is fair, dead though he is” (22.71–73; see [[death-in-battle]] and [[beauty-and-its-price]]). What Priam feared for his own body, that the dogs “disfigure” it (αἰσχύνωσι, 22.75), does not happen to Hector's: Hermes tells Priam that Achilles drags it but does not disfigure it (οὐδέ μιν αἰσχύνει, 24.418; see [[aischros]]).

**In his own native land.** ἑῇ ἐν πατρίδι γαίῃ, “in his own native land” (22.404): Hector, who alone guarded the gates and the long walls (22.507), is dragged before them in the sight of his father, his mother and his wife (22.405–467; see [[the-walls-of-troy]] and [[hector]]).

## Outside Homer

Later poets moved the dragging to the walls. Euripides' Andromache remembers Hector, “whom the son of sea-born Thetis dragged around the walls, driving his chariot” (τὸν περὶ τείχη / εἵλκυσε διφρεύων παῖς ἁλίας Θέτιδος, *Andromache* 107–108; see [[euripides-andromache]]), and in Virgil Achilles has dragged Hector three times around the walls of Ilium and is selling the lifeless body for gold (*Ter circum Iliacos raptaverat Hectora muros, / exanimumque auro corpus vendebat Achilles*, *Aeneid* 1.483–484); on the night Troy falls Hector comes to Aeneas in a dream as he was when the chariot dragged him, black with bloody dust and pierced through his swollen feet with thongs (2.270–273; see [[virgils-hector]]). In Homer the three circuits of the walls belong to the chase (22.165), and the three circuits behind the chariot are around Patroclus' tomb (24.16). In Sophocles' *Ajax* the dragging kills him: Teucer says that Hector, held fast to the chariot rail by the belt that Ajax had given him, was torn without end until he breathed out his life (ζωστῆρι πρισθεὶς ἱππικῶν ἐξ ἀντύγων / ἐκνάπτετʼ αἰέν, ἔστʼ ἀπέψυξεν βίον, *Ajax* 1029–1031; see [[sophocles-sword-and-belt]]). Plato's Socrates will not let his citizens believe in “the draggings of Hector around the tomb of Patroclus” (τὰς … Ἕκτορος ἕλξεις περὶ τὸ σῆμα τὸ Πατρόκλου, *Republic* 3.391b), which are those of *Iliad* 24, not the dragging from the walls to the ships (24.14–18; see [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]]). Aristotle cites Apollo's complaint that it is senseless earth that Achilles maltreats to show that anger ends with the death of its object (*Rhetoric* 2.3, 1380b28–30; see [[the-senseless-earth]]). For Cicero's use of the dragging, see [[cicero-the-body-of-hector]].
