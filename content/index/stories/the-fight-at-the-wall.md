## The story

**The wall's fate.** While Patroclus tends Eurypylus (12.1–2), the poet looks past the war. The wall was built without hecatombs, “against the will of the gods” (12.8), and after the sack Poseidon and Apollo will turn the rivers of Ida against it and sweep it into the sea (12.3–34; see [[the-building-of-the-wall]]).

**The ditch.** Beaten down by “the lash of Zeus”, the Argives are penned among their ships (12.37–40). Hector, like a boar or a lion among hunters, urges his men across the ditch, but the horses balk at its edge, for it is wide, steep-banked and staked (12.41–59; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[the-boar-or-lion-at-bay]]). On Polydamas' advice the Trojans leave their chariots and form five companies on foot (12.60–107; see [[polydamas]] and [[battle-order]]).

**Asius at the gate.** Asius alone keeps his chariot and drives for a gate that stands open for fugitives; the poet says that Idomeneus' spear awaits him (12.108–126; see [[asius]]). The Lapiths Polypoetes and Leonteus hold the gate like oaks and fight like wild boars, while stones fall from the towers like snow. Asius calls Zeus “a lover of lies”, and the Lapiths kill eight Trojans (12.127–194; see [[polypoetes]] and [[leonteus]]). Others fight at the other gates, “and it is hard for me to tell all these things like a god” (12.175–176).

**The omen.** An eagle flying on the left drops a wounded serpent among Hector's men. Polydamas reads it as a warning, and Hector answers: “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (12.195–250; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]] and [[one-omen-is-best]]).

**The assault.** Zeus sends a wind of dust from Ida against the ships. The Trojans tear at the battlements, the two Ajaxes range along the towers rousing every man, “outstanding, or middling, or worse” (12.269–270), and the stones fly as thick as snow (12.251–289; see [[battlements]] and [[the-two-ajaxes]]).

**Sarpedon.** Zeus rouses his son Sarpedon, who tells Glaucus why the kings of the Lycians must fight among the foremost (12.290–330; see [[sarpedon]] and [[sarpedons-speech]]). They attack Menestheus' tower, and Menestheus sends for Telamonian Ajax and Teucer (12.331–377; see [[menestheus]]). Ajax kills Epicles, Teucer wounds Glaucus, and Sarpedon pulls down a battlement and lays the wall bare (12.378–412). The lines hold level, like two men disputing a boundary, like the scales of an honest working woman (12.413–436; see [[the-boundary-dispute]] and [[the-working-womans-scales]]).

**The gate.** Zeus gives the higher glory to Hector. With a stone that Zeus makes light he breaks the doors of a gate and leaps in, “his face like swift night” (12.437–466; see [[hector]] and [[the-gates-of-the-wall]]):

## In the poem

**The wall.** Since Book 7 (7.327–343, 7.433–441) the wall has been the line between the Achaeans and defeat. Hector scorned it (8.177–179), the Achaeans were twice driven back inside it (8.213–216, 8.335–345), and Achilles mocked it (9.348–352). Its end is told before its battle. Its fall opens the fighting at the ships. While Zeus looks away, Poseidon rallies the Achaeans around the two Ajaxes (13.1–135); Nestor finds the wall thrown down (14.13–15); Apollo tramples the ditch into a causeway and knocks the wall down, and the Trojans drive in their horses and fight at the sterns (15.343–389). When Patroclus drives them back, the ditch holds them against their will and wrecks their chariots (16.366–371).

**Zeus and his son.** Zeus has promised Hector power to kill until he reaches the ships (11.191–194). His heart “wished to hold out glory to Hector” (12.174), his wind bewitches the Achaeans (12.252–255), and he makes the stone light (12.450). Yet the gates would not have been broken “had not Zeus the counselor roused his own son, Sarpedon” (12.292), and Zeus keeps that son from dying “at the sterns of the ships” (12.403); his death waits for Book 16 (see [[zeus]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**Counsel and foresight.** Hector takes Polydamas' first counsel (12.80) and rejects the second (12.230–250). The pattern recurs: he takes Polydamas' advice in Book 13 (13.748 = 12.80) and rejects it in Book 18 (18.284–313), and before he dies he regrets it (22.99–103). The narrator knows the end of what he tells: the wall's destruction, and Asius' death (12.113–117, fulfilled at 13.384–393). Once he speaks of himself, as in his appeals to the Muses (12.176; 2.484–493; see [[invocation-of-the-muses]]).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates names the episode when he quotes the eagle and the serpent as a passage for a seer to judge: “in many places in the Iliad too, as at the Teichomachia” (ἐπὶ τειχομαχίᾳ, *Ion* 539b–d; see [[title-of-book-12]]). [[strabo|Strabo]], arguing that Homer's Ilium stood farther inland than the Ilium of his day, uses the verb: it would have been cowardice in the Trojans to “fight at the wall” (ἐτειχομάχουν) and break into the naval station once it was walled, and yet not dare to attack it while it lay close by without a wall (13.1.36; see [[aristotle-and-the-wall]]).
