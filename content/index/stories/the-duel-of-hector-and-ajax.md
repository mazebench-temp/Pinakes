## The story

**The gods' plan.** Athena sees the Argives dying and darts down from Olympus, and Apollo, who wants victory for the Trojans, meets her by the oak. They agree to stop the battle for the day by rousing Hector to challenge one of the Danaans to fight him alone (7.17–43; see [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). Helenus perceives their plan and tells his brother to issue the challenge, “for it is not yet your fate to die and meet your end” (7.44–53; see [[helenus]]). Hector holds back the Trojans, Agamemnon makes the Achaeans sit, and the two gods sit on the oak “like birds, like vultures” above ranks as dark as a rippled sea (7.54–66; see [[like-vultures]] and [[the-ripple-of-the-west-wind]]).

**The challenge.** Hector says that Zeus has not fulfilled the oaths of the truce but means evil for both sides (7.69–72). Let the best of the Achaeans come forward. If the other man kills him, he may strip the armor but must give the body back to be burned; if Hector kills him, he will hang the armor in Apollo's temple and give the body back for burial (7.73–86). Then he imagines the grave:

{{quote:49b62789-a319-5614-b469-0ea22b08bbda}}

**Silence.** “So he spoke, and they all fell hushed in silence; they were ashamed to refuse him, and afraid to accept” (7.92–93; see [[silence]] and [[aidos]]). Menelaus rises, calls them “Achaean women, Achaean men no longer” (7.96; see [[achaean-women-no-longer-men]]), and arms. Agamemnon takes him by the hand and holds him back: even Achilles “shudders to meet this man” (7.104–119; see [[menelaus]] and [[agamemnon]]). Then Nestor shames the army. Peleus would groan to hear of it, and he himself, the youngest of all, once faced the champion Ereuthalion when no one else dared (7.123–160; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]). Nine men stand up: Agamemnon, Diomedes, the two Ajaxes, Idomeneus, Meriones, Eurypylus, Thoas and Odysseus (7.161–169).

**The lot.** Each man marks a lot, and they throw them into Agamemnon's helmet. The army prays that the lot may fall to Ajax, to Diomedes or to Agamemnon (7.175–180). Nestor shakes the helmet, “and out of the helmet leapt the lot that they themselves wanted, the lot of Ajax” (7.181–183). The herald carries it around the circle, and no one knows it until Ajax sees his own mark and rejoices (7.183–192; see [[lots]]). While he arms, the army prays to Zeus of Ida for his victory, or, if Zeus loves Hector too, for “equal strength and glory to them both” (7.200–205; see [[prayer-type-scene]]).

**The fight.** Ajax comes on like huge Ares, smiling with grim face, and the Trojans tremble. Hector's heart pounds, but he cannot draw back, since he made the challenge (7.206–218; see [[like-huge-ares]]). Ajax carries his shield of seven oxhides “like a tower” (7.219–223; see [[like-a-tower]] and [[the-shield-of-ajax]]) and threatens him: Hector will learn what chiefs the Danaans have “even after Achilles” (7.226–232). Hector answers that he is no feeble boy or woman, but knows every kind of fighting (7.234–243; see [[like-a-boy-or-a-woman]]). His spear goes through six layers of Ajax's shield and stops in the seventh. Ajax's goes through Hector's shield and corselet and cuts his tunic, and Hector swerves and escapes death (7.244–254). They close with spears, like lions or boars; Hector's point bends on the shield, and Ajax's drives through Hector's shield and draws blood from his neck (7.255–262; see [[lions-and-boars]]). Hector strikes Ajax's shield on the boss with a great black stone. Ajax lifts a far greater stone, “like a millstone”, breaks Hector's shield inward and knocks him flat, “but Apollo at once set him on his feet” (7.263–272; see [[like-a-millstone]]).

**The heralds.** They would have fought on with swords, had not the heralds come, Talthybius for the Achaeans and Idaeus for the Trojans, and held out their scepters between them (7.273–278; see [[heralds]], [[talthybius]] and [[idaeus]]).

Ajax replies that Hector made the challenge and must be the one to end it (7.283–286). Hector praises him for the stature, strength and sense a god has given him, and proposes that they stop for today and fight again another day, “until a god decides between us”. And he proposes gifts:

{{quote:55017447-7604-5bda-a664-2dcf193d8463}}

The Trojans rejoice to see Hector “alive and unhurt” and take him to the city, though they had not hoped to see him safe (7.306–310). The Achaeans lead Ajax, “exulting in his victory”, to Agamemnon, who sacrifices a five-year-old ox to Zeus and honors Ajax with the long chine at the feast (7.311–322; see [[ajax]] and [[geras]]).

## In the poem

**The second duel.** The Iliad has two duels arranged between the armies, and Book 7 tells its second with the materials of the first (see [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]] and [[single-combat]]). In both the battle stops, the armies sit, a challenge is made, lots are shaken in a helmet (3.316, 3.324–325; 7.175–183), and the army prays to Zeus “who rule from Ida” (3.320 = 7.202). The spear casts are told in the same words. Ajax's spear goes through Hector's shield, corselet and tunic as Menelaus' went through Paris' (7.250–254 = 3.356–360), and Hector's point bends on Ajax's shield as Paris' did on Menelaus' (7.259 = 3.348). Each time a god saves the Trojan: Aphrodite snatches Paris away (3.380–382), and Apollo sets Hector on his feet (7.272). The differences are as plain. Paris offered to fight for Helen and the war, and the terms were sworn over victims. Hector only calls Zeus to witness his terms (7.76), and the stake is the body of the loser and the fame of the winner. Paris shrank back from his opponent before he offered to fight (3.30–37); Hector cannot shrink back from his (7.217–218). The first duel ended in a rescue and a broken truce, the second in gifts and friendship.

**Why here.** The gods want the day's fighting stopped (7.29–32), and the duel closes the battle that began in Book 4 (see [[the-first-battle]]). It gives Hector, back from the city, his first great moment as champion of the Trojans. It matches him against Ajax, who is the best of the Achaeans while Achilles keeps his wrath (2.768–769; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). Achilles is named in it twice, by Agamemnon, who says that even he “shudders” to meet Hector (7.113–114), and by Ajax, who fights “even after Achilles” (7.228; see [[achilles-withdraws]] and the translation's notes on 7.114 and 7.228). Hector and Ajax meet again. Ajax fells Hector with a stone (14.409–420), Hector lops the point off Ajax's spear and Ajax knows that Zeus is against him (16.114–121), and Hector draws back when Ajax comes over Patroclus' body with his tower of a shield (17.128–129).

**Bodies and fame.** Hector's terms concern the body of the man who loses, and the book ends with a truce to burn the dead (see [[the-truce-for-the-dead]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). He will offer the same terms to Achilles before their duel, and Achilles will refuse them: there are no oaths between lions and men (22.254–267). The mound Hector imagines is a monument to his own fame, which “will never perish” (7.91; see [[kleos]], [[fame-and-the-tomb]] and [[tis-speech]]). Two days later the Achaeans heap a real mound over all their dead, “one for all alike” (7.435–436), and build a wall against it, which the gods will wash away (see [[the-building-of-the-wall]]).

**A drawn fight.** Hector's challenge did not say what would happen if neither man died. The army's prayer had asked for “equal strength and glory to them both” (7.205), and the heralds stop the fight with night coming on (see [[yield-to-night]] and [[war-and-night]]). Ajax had the better of it: he drew Hector's blood and knocked him down (7.260–272). The Achaeans treat him as the winner and lead him back “exulting in his victory” (κεχαρηότα νίκῃ, 7.312). The Trojans are glad that Hector is alive, in the words used of Aeneas' return in Book 5 (7.308 = 5.515).

**Gifts between enemies.** Like Glaucus and Diomedes in Book 6, the two men end with gifts (see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]] and [[guest-gifts]]). There the gifts proved an old guest-friendship. Here they are meant for what men will say: “They fought in heart-devouring strife, and then parted in friendship, reconciled” (7.301–302; see [[parted-in-friendship]] and [[philotes]]). Hector gives a silver-studded sword with its sheath and “well-cut strap” (τελαμῶνι, the word that is also the name of Ajax's father Telamon), and Ajax a belt “bright with purple”, in the words of the belt Oeneus gave Bellerophon (7.303–305; 6.219; see [[sword]] and [[belt]]).

## Outside Homer

An ancient title of Book 7 was Ἕκτορος καὶ Αἴαντος μονομαχία, “the single combat of Hector and Ajax” (see [[title-of-book-7]]). The gifts had a dark later history. In Sophocles' *Ajax* the hero says of the sword Hector gave him that the gifts of enemies are no gifts (*Ajax* 654–665), and he falls on it. His brother Teucer tells how Hector was bound to the chariot with the belt Ajax gave him and dragged until he died, and he reminds Agamemnon that Ajax's lot was the one that leapt first from the helmet (see [[sophocles-sword-and-belt]]). In the Iliad Achilles drags Hector's body by thongs drawn through his ankles, and the belt plays no part (22.395–400).
