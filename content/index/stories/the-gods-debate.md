## The story

At each dawn Achilles drags the body of Hector three times around the mound of Patroclus, and Apollo keeps it from all defilement (24.14–21; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]] and [[apollo]]). The gods look on:

{{quote:fa225b84-b1a4-5921-a457-de417e5eecf3}}

All agree but Hera, Poseidon and the gray-eyed maiden, who keep the hatred of Ilios they have held since Alexander insulted the goddesses in his farmyard (24.25–30; see [[the-judgment-of-paris]]). On the twelfth dawn Apollo speaks among the immortals. They are hard and destructive: Hector burned them thighs of oxen and goats, and now they will not save even his corpse for his wife and mother, his child, his father and his people to see and to burn; they help deadly Achilles, who has lost all pity and has no shame. Other men lose a brother or a son, weep and leave off; this man drags Hector around the mound of his companion, “for in his rage he defiles the senseless earth” (24.31–54; see [[pity]] and [[the-senseless-earth]]). Hera answers in anger. The gods cannot give Achilles and Hector equal honor: Hector is a mortal and was suckled at a woman's breast, Achilles is the son of a goddess whom she herself reared and gave to Peleus, and all the gods came to the wedding, Apollo with his lyre, “friend of the wicked, faithless always” (24.55–63; see [[hera]] and [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]]).

Zeus grants each of them something. Their honor will not be the same, but Hector was the dearest to the gods of all the mortals in Ilios, for he never failed in the gifts Zeus loves (24.65–70; see [[zeus]]). Then he decides:

{{quote:d93a9831-4e7b-5c70-bc9f-84dd37297255}}

Iris brings Thetis up from the sea, and on Olympus, among all the gods, Zeus tells her: “For nine days a quarrel has arisen among the immortals / about Hector's corpse and Achilles, sacker of cities” (24.107–108). He gives her his message for her son, that the gods are angry and he above all, and sends Iris to Priam (24.77–119; see [[thetis]] and [[iris]]). Achilles consents: “So be it: let him who brings the ransom carry off the body” (24.139).

## In the poem

**The second council over Hector.** In *Iliad* 22 Zeus asked the gods whether to save Hector alive, and Athena answered him with Hera's words over Sarpedon, “Do it; but not all of us other gods approve” (22.181 = 16.443; see [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]). Zeus gave way, and Hector died. Now the question is his body, and the majority is with him: “This pleased all the others” (24.25). In both scenes Zeus remembers Hector's sacrifices (22.170–172, 24.66–70; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). The verses of the altar that never lacked its share are those with which he gave up Troy to Hera in Book 4 (24.69–70 = 4.48–49; see [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]]): there they surrendered the city, here they save the body of its defender.

**Theft or ransom.** The gods' first plan is to have “the keen-sighted slayer of Argus” steal the body (κλέψαι, 24.24, 24.109; see [[keen-sighted]]), and Zeus refuses it. Aristarchus athetized his reason, that Thetis is always beside her son (24.71–73), since she is not; Walter Leaf thinks the objection futile, since a goddess who hears from afar and comes in a moment may well be said always to stand beside him (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.71). He reports that Aristarchus seems to have condemned the stealing of 24.24 along with the Judgment of Paris (25–30), and finds the case against that verse weaker: Homer does not know Hermes as the thievish god, but the Hermes of this book, the escort of Priam, is already unlike the Hermes of the rest of the Iliad (on 24.23; see [[hermes]]). In place of theft Zeus gives Achilles glory: “but I bestow this glory on Achilles” (24.110). Leaf takes the glory to be the gifts received for the body, instead of yielding it unransomed, the point of honor on which *Iliad* 9 turns (on 24.110; see [[kydos]], [[apoina]] and [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).

**From the first book to the last.** The scene opens with the verse of the twelfth dawn on which the gods came back to Olympus and Thetis went up to Zeus to ask honor for her son (24.31 = 1.493; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). Now Zeus summons her to Olympus, and the honor he gives her son is the ransom. Leaf counts the days from Hector's death: three for the funeral of Patroclus and nine for the quarrel in heaven (on 24.31). Zeus had foretold Hector's death in *Iliad* 15, “And in anger for him brilliant Achilles will kill Hector” (15.68; see [[plan-of-zeus]]); his will in the last book turns Achilles' anger to giving the body back.

**Pity.** The blessed gods pity the dead man (ἐλεαίρεσκον, 24.23), as Apollo does (24.19), and Iris tells Priam that Zeus “cares greatly for you and pities you” (24.174). Apollo's charge against Achilles is that he “has lost all pity” (24.44), and pity is what Priam will ask of him (24.503).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle quotes the last verse of Apollo's speech in his account of calming anger. Men are not angry with the dead, who feel nothing, and that, he says, is why the poet spoke well about Hector, “when he wished to stop Achilles' anger against the dead man” (*Rhetoric* 2.3, 1380b28–30; see [[the-senseless-earth]] and [[aristotle]]).
