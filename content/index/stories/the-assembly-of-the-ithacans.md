## The story

At dawn Telemachus rises, dresses, slings his sword about his shoulder and orders the clear-voiced heralds to summon the long-haired Achaeans to the assembly. When they have gathered he goes to the place of assembly with a bronze spear in his hand and two swift dogs at his heels; Athena pours a wondrous grace upon him, the people gaze at him as he comes, and he sits in his father's seat while the elders make way (*Odyssey* 2.1–14; see [[dressing-scene]], [[heralds]], [[dogs]] and [[assembly-type-scene]]).

The old hero Aegyptius, whose son Antiphus sailed with Odysseus and was eaten by the Cyclops, speaks first: there has been no assembly since Odysseus went away; who has called this one, and why? Has he news of the army, or some other public matter? May Zeus bring him good (*Odyssey* 2.15–34; see [[aegyptius]] and [[antiphus-son-of-aegyptius]]). Telemachus rejoices at the word of omen and stands in the middle, and the herald Peisenor puts the scepter in his hand (*Odyssey* 2.35–38; see [[peisenor-the-herald]] and [[scepter]]). It was he who called them, he says, on a business of his own: a twofold evil has fallen on his house, the loss of his father, who was king among them and kind, like a father, and the suitors who devour his substance. He calls on the people to feel indignation and shame, to fear the wrath of the gods, and to leave him to his grief; it would be better for him if they ate up his goods themselves, for then he could ask them back (*Odyssey* 2.40–79; see [[nemesis]], [[aidos]] and [[menis]]). Then:

Antinous answers that the fault is not the suitors' but Penelope's, who has cheated them for three years with promises and with the trick of the web, a shroud for Laertes woven by day and unraveled by night, until one of her women betrayed her. Let Telemachus send her back to her father to marry; until she marries, the suitors will not leave (*Odyssey* 2.85–128; see [[antinous]], [[penelope]] and [[the-web-of-penelope]]). Telemachus will not thrust out the mother who bore him and raised him: her father would make him pay, a god would send evil, she would call down the Erinyes, and men would blame him. If the suitors are indignant, let them leave his halls; if not, he will call on the gods for requital (*Odyssey* 2.129–145; see [[mothers-and-sons]] and [[icarius]]).

At his words Zeus sends two eagles from a mountain peak. They fly side by side on the wind, wheel over the middle of the assembly, look down on the heads of all, tear each other's cheeks and necks with their talons, and dart away to the right over the city (*Odyssey* 2.146–156; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagles]], [[zeus]] and [[eagle]]). The old Halitherses, son of Mastor, who surpassed the men of his age in reading birds, declares that Odysseus is near and planting slaughter for the suitors, as he foretold when the Argives sailed for Ilios: after suffering many evils and losing all his companions, he would come home unknown to all in the twentieth year (*Odyssey* 2.157–176; see [[halitherses]], [[the-prophecy-of-halitherses]] and [[divination]]). Eurymachus tells the old man to go home and prophesy to his own children: many birds fly under the sun, and not all are birds of fate; Odysseus is dead, and the old man will pay a fine if he stirs the young man up. Let Telemachus send his mother to her father; the suitors fear no one and heed no oracle (*Odyssey* 2.177–207; see [[eurymachus]] and [[many-birds-not-all-of-fate]]).

Telemachus will plead no more. He asks for a swift ship and twenty companions to go to Sparta and Pylos for news of his father: if he is alive, Telemachus will endure another year; if he is dead, he will come home, heap up a mound for him and give his mother to a husband (*Odyssey* 2.208–223; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). Mentor, to whom Odysseus entrusted his house, rebukes the people: no one remembers the king who was kind as a father, and they sit silent, many as they are, while a few suitors devour his house (*Odyssey* 2.224–241; see [[mentor]]). Leiocritus, son of Euenor, answers that even Odysseus, if he came, would die fighting so many; let the people go back to their work, and Mentor and Halitherses help Telemachus on his way, a journey he will never make (*Odyssey* 2.242–256; see [[leiocritus-son-of-euenor]]). So he dissolves the assembly. The people scatter to their homes, and the suitors go to the house of Odysseus (*Odyssey* 2.257–259; see [[the-suitors]]).

## In the poem

**The first assembly in twenty years.** “Never has there been an assembly of ours or a session / since brilliant Odysseus went away in the hollow ships” (*Odyssey* 2.26–27). Merry distinguishes the council of the chiefs, the βουλή or θῶκος, from the ἀγορή, where the people attended and assented or dissented, and compares Aristotle's account of the Cretan constitution, in which all share in the assembly but it decides nothing except to ratify what the elders and the magistrates have resolved (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 2.7; see the translation's note on 2.7, 2.26, and [[assembly]]). Telemachus begs the people by Zeus and by Themis, “who dissolves the assemblies of men and seats them” (*Odyssey* 2.68–69; see [[themis-the-goddess]]).

**Athena's plan.** The assembly is the first step of the plan Athena laid before the gods, to rouse Telemachus “to call the long-haired Achaeans to an assembly” (*Odyssey* 1.88–91), which she gave him as counsel in the likeness of Mentes and which he announced to the suitors that night (*Odyssey* 1.272–274, 1.372–375; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]], [[athena-visits-telemachus]] and [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]). He bids the suitors leave his halls, as she told him, and the suitors take up the other half of her counsel, that his mother go back to her father's house, as their own demand, Eurymachus in her very words (*Odyssey* 2.113–114, 2.195–197, where 2.196–197 = 1.277–278; see [[telemachus]] and [[icarius]]). The assembly does not give him the ship he asks for; Athena finds one (*Odyssey* 2.287, 2.386–387).

**The young man's assembly.** Telemachus' first public speech ends with the gesture of the angry Achilles in the Iliad's first assembly, “and flung the scepter to the ground” (*Odyssey* 2.80, as *Iliad* 1.245; see [[scepter]]), and the assembly ends with the verses that end the assembly of *Iliad* 19, “So he spoke, and quickly dissolved the assembly”, with the people scattering to their houses instead of their ships (*Odyssey* 2.257–258, as *Iliad* 19.276–277). Here it is a suitor who dissolves it. The people pity Telemachus but say nothing (*Odyssey* 2.81–82), and Mentor's rebuke is for their silence (*Odyssey* 2.239–241; see [[demos]] and [[laos]]).

**The second assembly.** The poem has one more assembly of Ithaca, after the slaughter of the suitors, and it is built on this one. A father grieving for his son speaks first, Eupeithes for Antinous, with the verse of Aegyptius' tears, “Shedding tears for him, he addressed them and spoke” (*Odyssey* 24.425 = 2.24); Halitherses speaks again, with the address “Hear me now, men of Ithaca, in what I say”, and reminds the people that they would not listen to him or to Mentor and stop their sons from their folly (*Odyssey* 24.420–462, where 24.454 = 2.161; see [[eupeithes]], [[halitherses]], [[hear-me]], [[ithacans]] and [[mentor]]). The renderings of the last book here are plain renderings, not the translation.
