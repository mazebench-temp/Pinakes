## The story

**Ismarus.** Odysseus begins the tale of his homecoming, “of many sorrows, / which Zeus sent upon me as I came from Troy” (*Odyssey* 9.37–38; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]), with the first landfall after Troy:

{{quote:71e76b53-aa60-587d-a68a-9447c65e9833}}

The Cicones had fought for Troy: the Catalogue names them among the Trojans' allies, spearmen led by Euphemus (*Iliad* 2.846; see [[cicones]], [[ismarus]] and [[thrace]]). One household was spared in the sack. Maron, son of Euanthes, the priest of Apollo, lived in the god's grove at Ismarus, and Odysseus and his men protected him, his child and his wife “in reverence”; in return he gave Odysseus seven talents of gold, a mixing bowl all of silver and twelve jars of sweet unmixed wine, the wine with which Odysseus later makes the Cyclops drunk (*Odyssey* 9.196–211; see [[maron]], [[apollo]], [[wine]] and [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). The ships also carried off much red wine in two-handled jars “when we took the sacred citadel of the Cicones” (*Odyssey* 9.163–165).

**The feast on the shore.** The men stayed to drink the wine and slaughter sheep and cattle beside the shore, while the Cicones went off and called to other Cicones, their neighbors, “more numerous and braver too, / living inland, who knew how to fight with men from chariots, / and on foot too, where there was need” (*Odyssey* 9.45–50).

**The battle.** They came “as many as the leaves and flowers that grow in their season, / early in the morning”, and “an evil fate from Zeus stood beside us” (*Odyssey* 9.51–52; see [[leaves-and-flowers]], [[aisa]] and [[zeus]]). The two sides fought beside the ships with bronze-fitted spears. As long as it was morning the Achaeans held their ground, though the Cicones were more, but in the afternoon, “when the sun moved on toward the time for unyoking oxen”, the Cicones overpowered them and turned them back, and six of the companions perished from each ship (*Odyssey* 9.54–61; see [[achaeans]] and [[hetairos]]).

**The dead.** “From there we sailed on, grieving at heart, / glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions” (*Odyssey* 9.62–63; see [[from-there-we-sailed-on]]). The ships did not go farther until they had called three times on each of the companions who had died on the plain (*Odyssey* 9.64–66; see [[the-call-to-the-dead]]). Then Zeus roused the North Wind against them (*Odyssey* 9.67; see [[the-lotus-eaters]]).

## In the poem

**The first adventure.** The raid stands first in the tale, as it does in the summary that Odysseus later gives Penelope, which begins with “how he first overcame the Cicones” (*Odyssey* 23.310, a plain rendering). The verb of that summary, Κίκονας δάμασʼ, “overcame the Cicones”, is the verb of the battle's end in the tale, told the other way round: the Cicones κλῖναν δαμάσαντες Ἀχαιούς, “overpowered the Achaeans and turned them back” (*Odyssey* 9.59). It is also the one adventure of the tale set on a coast that the Greeks knew: the next takes the ships past Malea and out of the known world (see [[the-lotus-eaters]] and [[malea]]).

**The Iliad's battle.** The fight is told in the Iliad's verses of battle. The Cicones come in the numbers of the Achaeans who filled the meadow of the Scamander, “as the leaves and flowers that grow in their season” (*Iliad* 2.468). They set their battle in array and strike at one another with bronze-fitted spears, as do, on the shield of Achilles, the men who have carried off the herds and the army that rides out after them, “beside the swift ships” where the shield has “beside the river's banks” (*Odyssey* 9.54–55, after *Iliad* 18.527–534). The morning verse is the Iliad's (*Odyssey* 9.56 = *Iliad* 8.66, 11.84), and so is the hour “when the sun moved on toward the time for unyoking oxen”, at which, in the fight over Cebriones, “the Achaeans were stronger beyond their portion” (*Iliad* 16.779–780). In the tale the same hour turns the battle against them.

**The companions.** The companions bring the disaster on themselves. Odysseus bade them flee, “but they, the great fools, would not obey” (*Odyssey* 9.44): the word is νήπιοι, the proem's word for the companions who ate the cattle of the Sun, the “fools” who “perished through their own recklessness” (*Odyssey* 1.7–8; see [[nepios]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). The losses at Ismarus are the first of those that leave Odysseus, at the end of the tale, without a single companion (see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).
