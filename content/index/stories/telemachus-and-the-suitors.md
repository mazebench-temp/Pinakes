## The story

When Penelope has gone up to her room in tears, “the suitors raised a din throughout the shadowy halls, / and they all prayed to lie beside her in bed” (*Odyssey* 1.365–366; see [[the-suitors]] and [[penelope]]). Telemachus speaks to them:

{{quote:8f7461a6-edee-595c-a7da-38411f8cc76f}}

They bite their lips and marvel at Telemachus, “that he spoke so boldly” (*Odyssey* 1.381–382). Antinous, son of Eupeithes, answers with a sneer. The gods themselves must be teaching him “to be a man of lofty speech and to speak boldly”; may the son of Cronus never make him king in sea-girt Ithaca, “which is yours by birth from your father” (*Odyssey* 1.383–387; see [[antinous]]). Telemachus would be willing to win that too, if Zeus should grant it: it is no bad thing to be a king, whose house grows rich and who is himself more honored. But there are many other kings of the Achaeans in Ithaca, young and old; let one of them have the kingship, since Odysseus is dead, and Telemachus will be lord of his own house and of the slaves his father won for him (*Odyssey* 1.389–398; see [[basileus]] and [[anax]]).

Eurymachus, son of Polybus, leaves the kingship on the knees of the gods and wishes Telemachus to keep his possessions; may no man come to rob him of them “while Ithaca is still inhabited”. Then he asks about the stranger: where he came from, whether he brought news of Odysseus' coming or came on business of his own, and why he left so quickly, “for he was not like a base man to look on” (*Odyssey* 1.399–411; see [[eurymachus]] and [[on-the-knees-of-the-gods]]). Telemachus answers that his father's homecoming is lost: he no longer trusts in news, nor heeds the oracles his mother asks of prophets. The stranger is Mentes of Taphos, a guest-friend of his house; “So Telemachus spoke, but in his heart he knew the immortal goddess” (*Odyssey* 1.413–420; see [[taphos]] and [[mentes-the-taphian]]). The suitors turn to dancing and song until black evening, and go home to sleep (*Odyssey* 1.421–424).

## In the poem

**The plan kept.** In the council Athena said that she would rouse Telemachus to call the Achaeans to an assembly “and speak out to all the suitors” (πᾶσι μνηστήρεσσιν ἀπειπέμεν, *Odyssey* 1.90–91), and she has just put might and daring in his heart (*Odyssey* 1.320–321; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]] and [[athena-visits-telemachus]]). He addresses them by what they want, “Suitors of my mother” (*Odyssey* 1.368; see [[mothers-and-sons]]), and announces the assembly, where he will “speak out my word to you without regard for anyone” (μῦθον ἀπηλεγέως ἀποείπω, *Odyssey* 1.373). Merry takes ἀπειπεῖν in both places as “speak out”, not, with Nitzsch, “renounce” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.91 and 1.373; see the translation's note on 1.91).

**The warning.** The speech ends with a threat that the poem keeps. If the suitors go on eating “without payment”, they will perish in his house “unavenged”, νήποινοι (*Odyssey* 1.377–380). Merry explains the second word from the blood-price: Telemachus would have been liable to vengeance or compensation had he killed the suitors under his own roof without warning, but once he has formally told them to leave, they will perish with no compensation owed for them (on 1.377; see the translation's note on 1.377, 1.380). In the assembly next day he repeats the last seven lines (*Odyssey* 2.139–145 = 1.374–380, with ἔξιτέ μοι μεγάρων, “go out of my halls”, for ἐξιέναι μεγάρων), and Kirchhoff took the lines in Book 1 for an awkward imitation of those in Book 2 (Merry on 1.374; see the translation's note on 1.374–375). The gods, too, had warned Aegisthus before he died (*Odyssey* 1.37–43).

**The kingship.** Antinous prays that Telemachus may never be king, and Merry hears the emphasis of σέ γε: it is not Telemachus whom Antinous would wish to see king, but himself, unless the words are a sneer at the young man (on 1.386). Telemachus, he notes, answers as if Antinous were kindly sparing him the cares of a throne that Antinous himself wants (on 1.391). Later Eurymachus confirms it: Antinous wanted not so much the marriage as to be king of Ithaca and to kill Telemachus (*Odyssey* 22.48–53; see [[antinous]]). In Telemachus' answer the kingship of Ithaca does not pass of itself from father to son. There are “other kings of the Achaeans, / many in sea-girt Ithaca” (*Odyssey* 1.394–395), and Merry compares the twelve other kings among the Phaeacians beside Alcinous, and the title of king given to Antinous and Eurymachus (on 1.394; see [[kings]] and [[kingship-and-authority]]). What Telemachus claims is the house.

**What he keeps back.** To Eurymachus' questions Telemachus answers with the words the goddess gave him (*Odyssey* 1.417–419, as 1.180–181), and the poet adds that he knew the immortal goddess (*Odyssey* 1.420; see [[seeing-the-gods]]). His disbelief in oracles comes back in Eurymachus' mouth, when the suitor dismisses the prophecy of Halitherses in the assembly with the same words: “nor do we heed the prophecy” (οὔτε θεοπροπίης ἐμπαζόμεθʼ, *Odyssey* 2.201, a plain rendering; compare *Odyssey* 1.415; see [[divination]]).
