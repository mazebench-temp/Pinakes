## The story

The army is back in its seats. Only [[Thersites]] keeps up his brawling, a man of unmeasured words who says whatever he thinks will make the Argives laugh. The narrator describes him at a length he gives no one else's ugliness.

{{quote:993e67fc-8077-58b8-969b-3b3789d5e77c}}

He is most hateful to Achilles and Odysseus, whom he is always abusing, but now he screams his insults at Agamemnon. What does the king lack? His huts are full of bronze and of the choicest women, whom the Achaeans give him first whenever they take a town. Does he want more gold, or a young woman to keep for himself? Then to the army: “Weaklings, wretched disgraces, Achaean women, Achaean men no longer”, let us go home and leave him to stew over his prizes. He has dishonored Achilles, a far better man; but Achilles has no anger in him, or this insult would be Agamemnon's last (2.225–242).

[[Odysseus]] comes and stands beside him, looking at him darkly (see [[looking-darkly]]). There is no worse man among all who came to Troy; he should not take the kings into his mouth. If Odysseus finds him playing the fool again, may his own head no longer sit on his shoulders and may he no longer be called the father of Telemachus, if he does not strip Thersites and send him howling to the ships (2.246–264). Then he strikes him with the scepter.

## In the poem

Thersites says what Achilles said in Book 1, and much of it in Achilles' words: the king's greed, the unequal shares, the prize taken from a better man. Two of his lines are Achilles' own lines (2.240 = 1.356; 2.242 = 1.232). Spoken by the best of the Achaeans, the charges broke up the army's unity. Spoken by the ugliest man who came to Ilios, they make the army laugh. The narrator's verdict comes before a word is spoken: his words are ἄκοσμα, “disorderly”, and οὐ κατὰ κόσμον, “against all order”.

The episode ends the crisis of the test (see [[the-test-of-the-army]]). The men are “troubled” (ἀχνύμενοι), since the homecoming they ran for is gone, but they laugh together, and the anonymous voice of the crowd praises Odysseus (see [[tis-speech]]). He then rises with the scepter to speak, and the army listens.

Homer gives Thersites no father and no city. Odysseus treats him as he treated the men of the people, with the scepter rather than with gentle words, and readers have often taken him for the one commoner who speaks in the Iliad. Gregory Nagy read him as a figure of blame poetry, the opposite of epic praise (*The Best of the Achaeans*, 1979). W. G. Thalmann saw a scapegoat whose humiliation restores the army's unity (“Thersites: Comedy, Scapegoats, and Heroic Ideology in the *Iliad*”, *Transactions of the American Philological Association* 118, 1988). The laughter is the army's, and the poem shares it. See [[words-and-violence]], [[aischros]] and [[oneidos]].

## Outside Homer

In the *Aethiopis*, as Proclus summarizes it, Achilles kills Thersites for mocking him over his supposed love for the Amazon Penthesilea; the Achaeans quarrel over the killing, and Achilles sails to Lesbos to be purified.

In the myth of Er, the soul of Thersites the buffoon takes the form of an ape (Plato, *Republic* 10, 620c; see [[plato-thersites]]). In Lucian's *True Histories* Thersites sues Homer for outrage over his portrait, and loses, with Odysseus as Homer's advocate (2.20; see [[lucian-thersites]]). Shakespeare made him the railing chorus of *Troilus and Cressida* (see [[shakespeare-thersites]]).
