## The story

**Cassandra.** At dawn Priam and the herald drive toward the city “with cries of woe and groaning”, and the mules carry the corpse (24.695–697; see [[the-ransom-of-hector]]). No one else, “of the men or the lovely-girdled women”, knew them first, but Cassandra, who had gone up onto Pergamus, saw her father standing in the chariot with the herald, and Hector “lying on the bier, drawn by the mules” (24.697–702; see [[cassandra]], [[pergamus]] and [[idaeus]]). She wailed aloud and called out through all the city: “Go and see Hector, Trojans and Trojan women, / if ever too, when he came home alive from battle, / you rejoiced; for he was a great joy to the city and to all the people” (24.703–706).

**At the gates.** “not a man was left there in the city, / nor a woman; for uncontrollable grief came upon them all” (24.707–708; see [[penthos]]). They meet him near the gates, and first his wife and his mother tear their hair, rushing to the wagon and touching his head, while the throng stands around them weeping (24.709–712; see [[andromache]], [[hecuba]] and [[hair]]). They would have mourned him before the gates until the sun went down, had not Priam spoken from the chariot: “Make way for me to pass through with the mules; and afterwards / you shall have your fill of weeping, when I have brought him home” (24.713–718; see [[would-have-had-not]] and [[priam]]).

**The dirge.** In the house they lay him on a corded bed and set singers beside him to lead the dirges, and the women groan in answer (24.719–722; see [[lament]] and [[bed]]).

**Andromache.** “And among them white-armed Andromache led the lamentation, / holding in her hands the head of man-slaying Hector” (24.723–724). He has perished young and left her a widow, and their son a mere infant who will not reach his youth; for the city will be sacked now that its guardian is dead, its wives and children carried off in the ships, she among them, and the boy will follow her to shameful work, or some Achaean whose kin Hector killed will hurl him from the tower. He did not stretch out his hands to her from his bed as he died, nor leave her a word to remember (24.725–745; see [[astyanax]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). “So she spoke, weeping, and the women groaned with her” (24.746).

**Hecuba.** “And among them Hecuba in turn led the unceasing lamentation” (24.747). Her son was dear to the gods while he lived, and they have cared for him even in death. Achilles sold her other sons across the sea, but Hector he dragged many times around the mound of Patroclus, and now he lies in her halls fresh as dew, “like one whom Apollo, god of the silver bow, / has come upon and killed with his gentle arrows” (24.748–759; see [[hecuba]] and [[fresh-as-dew]]). She stirs up “relentless lamentation” (24.760).

**Helen.** “And then Helen, the third, led the lamentation among them” (24.761). Hector was the dearest of her brothers-in-law. In the twenty years since she came to Troy with Alexander she never heard an unkind word from him, and when the others of the house rebuked her he held them back with his gentleness; now there is no one in Troy who is kind to her, and all shudder at her (24.762–775; see [[helen]], [[paris]] and [[in-laws]]). “So she spoke, weeping, and the countless people groaned with her” (24.776). Then Priam turns to the people and sends them for wood (24.777–781; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]]).

## In the poem

**Refused and granted.** In *Iliad* 22 Hecuba begged Hector not to face Achilles: if he were killed, “I shall never / weep for you on a bier, dear scion, whom I bore myself, / nor will your richly dowered wife” (22.86–88; see [[mothers-and-sons]]). Achilles refused the dying Hector in the same words, “not even so shall your lady mother / lay you on a bier and lament for you, whom she bore herself” (22.352–353; see [[the-death-of-hector]]). In the last book the gods undo it. Apollo reproaches them for not saving the corpse “for his wife to see, and his mother and his child, / and his father Priam and the people” (24.35–37; see [[apollo]]), and when Achilles releases it he tells Priam that he may weep again for his son in Ilios: πολυδάκρυτος δέ τοι ἔσται, “he will be much wept for” (24.619–620; see [[achilles]]). Priam asks for nine days: “Nine days we would lament him in our halls” (24.664; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]]). The bier, λέχεα, that Hecuba and Achilles denied him is the bier on which he comes home and the bed on which he is laid (24.702, 24.720).

**Six laments.** The first laments for Hector were spoken in the city as he was dragged to the ships: his father's, his mother's and his wife's, in that order, and the citizens groaned with Priam as the women did with Andromache (22.416–436, 22.477–515; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]] and [[lament]]). Over the body the order changes. The wife speaks first, the mother second, and the third is not the father but Helen; Priam speaks only to send the people for wood. Andromache's second lament takes up her first only in its opening, the widow in the halls and the infant son (24.725–727 ≈ 22.483–485); Walter Leaf notes that otherwise it is quite independent of the lament of *Iliad* 22 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.726). He finds in the three a theme for each: Hector's valor, his piety and its reward, and his gentleness (on 24.723).

**Answered.** Each lament is answered as it ends. “So she spoke, weeping, and the women groaned with her” follows Andromache's, as it followed her lament in *Iliad* 22 and Briseis' over Patroclus (24.746 = 22.515 = 19.301; see [[briseis]]); Hecuba's ends in the lamentation that she stirs up, and Helen's in the groaning of “the countless people” (24.760, 24.776). δῆμος ἀπείρων is used of a multitude only here in Homer (Leaf on 24.776; see the translation's note on 24.776). The singers who lead the dirge are answered in the same way, “and the women groaned with them” (24.722). Leaf takes them for professional mourners singing a formal dirge, while the women keep up the keening (on 24.720; see the translation's note on 24.720–722); ἐξάρχω, “lead”, is the word for the leaders of a song (18.606; see [[dance]]).

**The last speeches.** The laments are the last speeches of the poem but one; the last is Priam's order for the wood (24.778–781). Helen, whose going to Troy began the war, speaks last of the women, as she was the first woman the poem shows in Troy, weaving the war into her web (3.125–128; see [[helens-web]]). The book that began with Achilles' grief for Patroclus ends with the grief of Hector's city, and Hecuba's lament recalls the dragging around Patroclus' mound (24.3–18, 24.755–756; see [[patroclus]]).

## Outside Homer

Walter Leaf reports the attempts, beginning with von Leutsch, to arrange the three laments in strophes, which he does not think successful, and Peppmüller's view that they follow the three parts of the hexameter νόμος, a beginning, a center and an epilogue, which he calls no more than a conjecture (on 24.723). To illustrate the dirge he quotes a description of hired mourners at a funeral on Mykonos, and von Hahn's account of an Albanian funeral, where a couplet sung by one voice is repeated by a chorus of women (on 24.720).
