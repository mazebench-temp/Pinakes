## The story

**The fire.** Athena gives Diomedes “might and daring, so that he would stand out among all the Argives and win noble fame” (5.2–3), kindles a tireless fire on his helmet and shield like the star of late summer, and drives him into the thick of the fighting (5.1–8; see [[the-autumn-star]]). His first victim is Phegeus, son of Dares, the priest of Hephaestus; the god hides the other son in darkness, so that the old man should not lose both (5.9–26; see [[dares]]). Athena leads Ares out of the battle, the Trojans give way, and each Achaean leader kills a man (5.27–84).

**The rampage and the wound.** Diomedes sweeps across the plain like a river in flood, and you could not have told which side he was on (5.85–94; see [[river-in-flood]]). Pandarus shoots him through the right shoulder and boasts that he will not last long (5.95–105). Sthenelus draws out the arrow, and Diomedes prays to Athena: as she once stood by his father, let her stand by him now, and bring the man who shot him within reach of his spear (5.106–120). She makes his limbs light, puts his father's might in his breast, takes the mist from his eyes so that he can tell god from man, and forbids him to fight any god but Aphrodite (5.121–132; see [[seeing-the-gods]] and [[achlys]]). He goes back like a wounded lion, three times as fierce, and kills pair after pair: Astynous and Hypeiron; Abas and Polyidus, the sons of the old dream-reader Eurydamas; Xanthus and Thoon, the only sons of old Phaenops; and Echemmon and Chromius, sons of Priam, whom he takes from one chariot like a lion breaking a heifer's neck (5.133–165; see [[wounded-lion]], [[lion-among-cattle]] and [[bereaved-parents]]).

**Pandarus and Aeneas.** Aeneas finds Pandarus, who regrets that he left his horses at home and trusted his bow, and takes him into his own chariot against Diomedes (5.166–240; see [[the-bow-of-pandarus]]). Sthenelus urges retreat. Diomedes refuses to flee or even to mount his chariot, and tells Sthenelus to seize Aeneas' horses if they win, for they are of the breed that Zeus gave to Tros (5.241–273; see [[ganymede-and-the-horses-of-tros]]). Pandarus' spear goes through Diomedes' shield and stops at his corselet; Diomedes' spear, guided by Athena, goes through Pandarus' face (5.274–296). Aeneas stands over the body like a lion, and Diomedes crushes his hip with a stone that two men of today could not lift (5.297–310; see [[like-a-lion]] and [[men-of-today]]).

**Aphrodite and Apollo.** Aphrodite carries her son out of the fighting, and Diomedes goes after her, knowing her for a weak god, and wounds her hand; she drops Aeneas, and Apollo hides him in a dark cloud (5.311–351; see [[diomedes-wounds-aphrodite]]). Meanwhile Sthenelus drives off the horses of Tros (5.319–327). Three times Diomedes springs at Aeneas, though he knows that Apollo is holding his hands over him, and three times Apollo beats back his shield. At the fourth the god shouts at him: “Take thought, son of Tydeus, and give way, and do not wish to think yourself equal to the gods” (5.431–442; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]] and [[gods-and-men-not-alike]]). He draws back a little. Apollo takes Aeneas to his temple on Pergamus, leaves a phantom for the armies to fight over, and sends Ares to rouse the Trojans (5.443–470; see [[the-phantom-of-aeneas]]).

**The retreat.** When Hector charges with Ares beside him, Diomedes, who can see the god, shudders like a traveler who stops at a flooded river, and orders the Achaeans to give ground with their faces to the enemy (5.590–606; see [[man-at-the-river]]).

**Ares.** Hera and Athena arm and come down to the plain (5.711–777). Athena finds Diomedes cooling his wound beside his chariot and taunts him: Tydeus fathered a son little like himself (5.793–813; see [[tydeus-at-thebes]]). Diomedes answers that he is only keeping her command not to fight the gods, and that he has recognized Ares (5.814–824). She releases him: let him strike Ares first. She pushes Sthenelus out of the chariot and takes the reins herself, and the oaken axle groans under the weight of a dread goddess and the best of men (5.825–841). Wearing the helmet of Hades, she turns aside Ares' spear and drives Diomedes' into the god's belly. Ares bellows like ten thousand men and goes up to the sky like a dark mist (5.842–867; see [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]).

## In the poem

**A hero's day.** The aristeia, a hero's great day in battle, has a recurring shape: the hero shines, breaks the enemy's ranks, is wounded, prays, is renewed by a god, kills a great opponent and fights over his body, and meets a god in the end (see [[the-aristeia]]). Diomedes' is the first in the Iliad and the fullest. Ancient readers named the book after it, and the episode they knew by that name ran on into Book 6 (see [[title-of-book-5]]).

**Without Achilles.** Book 5 is still the first day of fighting in the poem, and its hero is not the best of the Achaeans. Achilles sits by the ships, and the Iliad shows what the others can do without him. Pandarus calls Diomedes “the best of the Achaeans” (5.103), Dione calls him the same (5.414), and in Book 6 the seer Helenus says that the Trojans never feared Achilles as they fear him (6.96–101; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). Hera, in the voice of Stentor, measures the day against the absent hero: while Achilles fought, the Trojans never came out before the gates; now they are fighting by the ships (5.787–791).

**A mortal against gods.** Diomedes' day is the Iliad's fullest account of what a man may do against the gods, and it is carefully bounded. His sight is Athena's gift (5.127–128), and so is his permission: he may wound Aphrodite, and no other (5.130–132). He wounds her. He does not stop for Apollo until the god warns him, and when he sees Ares he gives ground and says why (5.600–606). He strikes Ares only when Athena drives the chariot and pushes the spear home (5.855–857). Dione's warning, that the man who fights the immortals does not live long, and his children do not climb on his knees when he comes home from the war (5.406–409; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]), is not fulfilled in the poem; in the Odyssey Nestor tells how Diomedes' ships came safely home to Argos (Odyssey 3.180–182). In Book 6 Diomedes himself tells Glaucus that he would not fight the gods of heaven, for Lycurgus, who did, did not live long (6.128–141). See [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]] and [[gods-and-mortals]].

**Father and son.** In Book 4 Agamemnon held up Tydeus to Diomedes as the better man (4.370–400), and Diomedes kept silent. Book 5 answers in deeds. Athena gives him “your father's might” (5.125), he prays to her as the goddess who stood by his father (5.116–117), and when she taunts him with Tydeus again, he answers her (5.815–824; see [[fathers-and-sons]] and [[tydeus]]).

**Pairs.** Much of the day is told in pairs. Diomedes kills in pairs: after Phegeus, whose brother a god saves, come Astynous and Hypeiron and then three pairs of brothers, each pair named with its father (5.9–26, 5.144–165; see [[bereaved-parents]]). The Trojans come against him two in one chariot, Aeneas and Pandarus. He wounds two gods, and each goes up to Olympus and is healed there, Aphrodite comforted by her mother, Ares rebuked by his father (5.370–430, 5.868–906). The god who was led out of the battle at the start of the book (5.29–36) is driven out of it at the end.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The day goes on in Book 6. Diomedes kills Axylus (6.12–19), the Trojan women pray to Athena to break his spear (6.305–307), and he meets Glaucus between the armies and exchanges armor with him (6.119–236). In Book 8 he takes Nestor into his chariot behind the horses of Tros (8.99–108). In Book 11 Paris shoots him through the foot, and he leaves the battle (11.369–400). In Book 21 Ares, attacking Athena, reminds her of the day she set Diomedes on to wound him and thrust the spear herself (21.394–399). In the funeral games of Patroclus Diomedes wins the chariot race with the horses he took from Aeneas (23.290–292, 23.499–513).

## Outside Homer

In Virgil, Diomedes has settled in Italy, and he will not fight the Trojans again. He tells the envoys of the Latins that he still pays for having attacked the bodies of the gods and wounded Venus in the hand, and that they should make peace with Aeneas (*Aeneid* 11.252–293).
