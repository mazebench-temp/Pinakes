## The story

**The mourning.** The Achaeans come back from the plain to the ships and scatter, each to his own ship, but Achilles keeps the Myrmidons with their horses and chariots: “let us go near / and weep for Patroclus; for that is the prize of the dead” (23.1–11; see [[the-prize-of-the-dead]] and [[myrmidons]]). Three times they drive their horses around the body, and Thetis rouses in them the longing for lament; the sands and their armor are wet with tears (23.12–16; see [[thetis]] and [[lament]]). Achilles leads the lament with his hands on his companion's breast and tells the dead man that he is fulfilling all he promised: he has dragged Hector here to give him raw to the dogs, and before the pyre he will cut the throats of twelve young Trojans (23.17–23; see [[human-sacrifice]]). He stretches Hector face down in the dust beside the bier (23.24–26; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). The Myrmidons unarm, and he gives them a funeral feast; oxen, sheep, goats and hogs are slaughtered, “and everywhere around the corpse the blood ran, enough to be drawn in cups” (23.26–34; see [[feast]]).

**No water for his head.** The kings bring Achilles to Agamemnon's hut and have a tripod set over the fire for his bath. He swears by Zeus that no water shall come near his head until he has laid Patroclus on the fire, heaped up a mound and cut his hair, and he asks Agamemnon to have wood brought at dawn, “so that the tireless fire may burn him away / the sooner from our eyes, and the army may turn to its tasks” (23.35–53; see [[bathing]] and [[agamemnon]]). After the meal he lies groaning on the shore among the Myrmidons until sleep takes him (23.54–64).

**The ghost.** In his sleep the soul of Patroclus comes to him, like him in all things, and asks to be buried quickly, so that he may pass the gates of Hades; it asks too that their bones lie together in one urn. Achilles reaches out to embrace it, and it goes beneath the earth like smoke (23.65–107; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]]). The Myrmidons weep with him until dawn (23.108–110).

**The wood.** At dawn Agamemnon sends mules and men from all the huts, with Meriones in charge. “Many a time they went uphill and downhill, sideways and aslant” (23.116) to the slopes of Ida, where they cut high-leafed oaks, split them and bind the logs behind the mules (23.110–124; see [[meriones]], [[mules]] and [[ida]]). They throw the wood down on the shore, where Achilles “had planned a great barrow for Patroclus and for himself” (23.125–126; see [[tomb]]).

**The procession.** The Myrmidons arm and mount their chariots, the horsemen in front and a cloud of foot soldiers behind, and the companions carry Patroclus in the middle. They cover the corpse with their hair, cut off and cast on it, and Achilles holds the head, grieving, “for he was sending his noble companion to Hades” (23.127–137; see [[hair]]). At the place he had shown them, he cuts off the fair hair that he had grown long for the river Spercheius, as his father vowed for his homecoming, and since he will not go home he puts it in his dead friend's hands (23.138–153; see [[spercheius]] and [[peleus]]).

**The pyre.** At Achilles' word Agamemnon sends the army to its meal, and “those who cared for the dead” stay, build the pyre and lay the body on its top (23.154–165; see [[pyres]]). Achilles covers the corpse with the fat of the sheep and cattle flayed before the pyre and heaps the carcasses around it; he sets jars of honey and ointment against the bier, throws on four horses and two of the nine dogs that fed at the lord's table, and kills twelve sons of the Trojans with the bronze, “and he devised evil deeds in his mind” (23.166–177; see [[cremation]], [[dogs]] and [[human-sacrifice]]). He greets the dead man again: the fire is eating the twelve with him, but Hector he will give to the dogs (23.178–183). Aphrodite keeps the dogs from Hector day and night and anoints him with oil, and Apollo covers the ground where he lies with a dark cloud (23.184–191; see [[aphrodite]] and [[apollo]]).

**The winds.** The pyre will not burn. Achilles prays to the North Wind and the West Wind, promising fine sacrifices and pouring libations from a golden cup; Iris finds the winds feasting in the West Wind's house and gives them his message, and they come over the sea with a wondrous din, fall on the pyre, and toss its flame all night (23.192–217; see [[boreas]], [[zephyrus]] and [[iris]]). All night Achilles draws wine from a golden mixing bowl and pours it on the ground, calling on the soul of Patroclus, and mourns as a father mourns who burns the bones of his son (23.218–225; see [[libation]] and [[the-father-burning-his-sons-bones]]).

**The bones and the mound.** When the morning star rises the pyre dies down, and the winds go home over the Thracian sea (23.226–230; see [[thrace]]). The leaders wake Achilles, and he tells them to quench the pyre with wine and to gather the bones of Patroclus, which lie apart in the middle of the pyre, into a golden bowl and a double fold of fat, to wait until he himself is hidden in Hades; the tomb is to be no greater than is fitting, for those who outlive him to make wide and high (23.231–248; see [[the-golden-urn]] and [[tomb]]). They do as he says, lay the bones in the huts under a soft linen cloth, mark out the circle of the mound around the pyre and heap up the earth:

## In the poem

**A vow kept.** Over the body in Book 18 Achilles vowed not to give Patroclus burial rites until he had brought back Hector's armor and head, and to cut the throats of twelve young Trojans before his pyre (18.333–337); he took the twelve alive in the river (21.26–32), told the dying Hector that the Achaeans would give Patroclus his rites (22.335–336), and remembered him by the ships as “a corpse unwept, unburied” (22.386–390; see [[patroclus]]). The funeral opens with the promise repeated, and Achilles repeats it again at the pyre (23.19–23, 23.179–183). Odysseus' rule, that the dead are buried after a single day of weeping so that the living may eat and fight (19.225–233; see [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]]), is not Achilles' rule: he has fasted and refused the bath, and he buries his companion only when the killer is dead.

**The funeral of Achilles.** The funeral of Patroclus is also the first part of Achilles' own. The ghost reminds him that he too is fated to die beneath the wall of Troy and asks that their bones lie in one urn (23.80–92); the barrow on the shore is planned “for Patroclus and for himself” (23.126); the hair vowed for his homecoming goes with Patroclus, since he will not go home (23.144–151); and the bones wait in the golden bowl until Achilles is hidden in Hades, under a mound that the survivors are to make wide and high (23.243–248; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]). The Odyssey tells the rest. The shade of Agamemnon tells Achilles how the Achaeans burned him on the eighteenth day with sheep and cattle, ointment and honey, and moved in armor around his pyre, footmen and horsemen; how they gathered his white bones at dawn in unmixed wine and ointment and laid them in a golden amphora, the gift of his mother, mingled with the bones of Patroclus; and how they heaped a great tomb over them on a headland by the Hellespont, to be seen far out at sea (*Odyssey* 24.63–84).

**The funeral of Hector.** The poem ends with the funeral the Trojans give Hector, told in some of the same verses: the body is laid on the top of the pyre (24.787 ≈ 23.165), the pyre is quenched with wine as far as the fire had reached (24.791–792 ≈ 23.237–238, 23.250), the white bones are gathered into a golden chest (24.793–795), and the burial ends with the half-verse that ends Patroclus' burial, χεύαντες δὲ τὸ σῆμα πάλιν κίον (24.801, 23.257; see [[hector]]). Patroclus is mourned far from home by his companions, Hector in his own city by his wife, his mother and Helen.

**Before the games.** Walter Leaf takes κίον in 23.257 as an imperfect: the Achaeans were going back when Achilles stopped them. He thinks it not unlikely that before the games were added the verse was followed by 24.3, which ends with the same αὐτὰρ Ἀχιλλεύς, so that the army went to supper and sleep and Achilles to his grief (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.257; 24.1–4; see the translation's note on 23.257).

For the rite itself, its parts and their parallels, see [[cremation]], [[pyres]] and [[tomb]].

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates will not have it believed that Achilles gave the hair consecrated to the Spercheius to the dead Patroclus, or that he slaughtered the captives at the pyre (*Republic* 3.391b; see [[plato]]). Strabo records a shrine and a memorial of Achilles near Sigeum and memorials of Patroclus and Antilochus, and says that the people of Ilium make offerings to them all and to Ajax (*Geography* 13.1.32; see [[strabo]]).
