## The story

**Sleep on the shore.** After the meal in Agamemnon's hut the others go to their own huts, but Achilles lies groaning on the shore among the Myrmidons, in a clear space where the waves wash up on the beach, until sleep takes him, “loosening the cares of his heart”, for his limbs are weary from chasing Hector toward windy Ilios (23.59–64; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]] and [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]).

**The likeness.** The soul of Patroclus comes to him “like him in all things, in stature and in the fair eyes / and the voice”, and in the clothes he used to wear, and stands above his head (23.65–68; see [[psyche]] and [[dream-scene]]). It begins with a reproach: “You are asleep, and you have forgotten me, Achilles. / You were not careless of me while I lived, but you are now that I am dead” (23.69–70).

**Bury me.** The soul asks to be buried as quickly as may be, so that it may pass the gates of Hades: the souls, “the phantoms of the dead”, keep it away and will not let it mingle with them beyond the river, and it wanders through the house of Hades. It asks for Achilles' hand, since once it has had its portion of fire it will never come back from Hades (23.71–76; see [[hades]], [[eidolon]] and [[cremation]]).

**Two deaths.** The two will no longer sit apart from their companions and take counsel together; the doom allotted at Patroclus' birth has gaped around him, “and it is your fate too, Achilles, like the gods, / to die beneath the wall of the wealthy Trojans” (23.77–81; see [[ker-doom]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). Achilles has said that his mother told him he would die beneath the wall of the Trojans by the arrows of Apollo, and the dying Hector named the Scaean gates, Paris and Apollo (21.277–278, 22.359–360).

**One urn.** Last comes a charge. Their bones are not to be laid apart, but together, as the two were reared together in Achilles' house, when Menoetius brought Patroclus there from Opus as a little boy, after he had killed the son of Amphidamas in anger over knucklebones, and Peleus received him, reared him with care and named him Achilles' attendant; “so too let one urn enclose the bones of us both, / the golden two-handled jar that your lady mother gave you” (23.82–92; see [[menoetius]], [[opus]], [[amphidamas-of-opus]], [[peleus]] and [[the-golden-urn]]).

**The embrace.** Achilles promises to do it all: “But stand nearer to me; let us put our arms around each other, if only for a little while, / and take our fill of deadly lament” (23.93–98). He reaches out with his hands and takes nothing, and the soul goes beneath the earth like smoke (see [[like-smoke]]):

{{quote:99962af3-b84d-52c3-a71a-320e97d226e2}}

He speaks to the Myrmidons, and his words rouse in them all the longing for lament, which lasts until dawn (23.108–110; see [[lament]] and [[the-longing-for-lament]]). In the morning he begins to do what the soul asked (see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]).

## In the poem

**A dream that is a visit.** The scene has the shape of a dream: the sleeper, the figure standing above his head, the first word εὕδεις, “You are asleep”, as from Zeus' Dream to Agamemnon (2.20–23; see [[dream-scene]]). But no god sends this figure, and it wears no one else's likeness: it is the dead man himself, as far as anything of him is left (see [[eidolon]]). When Achilles wakes he speaks of what he has seen as a fact about the dead: “Ah, truly even in the house of Hades there is some / soul and phantom, but there is no mind in it at all” (23.103–104). Monro–Allen print τίς, “there is some soul”; Walter Leaf prefers the variant τι, “the soul is something even in Hades”, which he thinks Propertius read when he wrote *sunt aliquid manes* (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.103; see the translation's note on 23.103–104 and [[propertius-the-ghost-of-cynthia]]).

**The Odyssey.** Several verses of the visit are Odyssean. ἦλθε δʼ ἐπὶ ψυχή, “there came to him the soul”, brings the shades up to Odysseus one after another (*Odyssey* 11.84, 11.90, 11.387, 11.467), and Leaf counts περὶ χροῒ εἵματα ἕστο, “it wore such clothes about its body”, as another Odyssean phrase (on 23.65, 23.67). The failed embrace has its double in the underworld. Three times Odysseus tries to take hold of his mother's soul, and three times it flies from his hands “like a shadow or a dream”; he asks her why she will not stay, so that even in Hades they may put their arms around each other and take their fill of chill lament, in the verse-end Achilles uses here (*Odyssey* 11.204–212; τεταρπώμεσθα γόοιο, 23.98). She answers that this is the way of mortals when they die: the might of the fire destroys flesh and bones, and the soul flies off like a dream (*Odyssey* 11.218–222). The thin voice of the vanishing soul, τετριγυῖα, “gibbering”, is the sound of the suitors' souls following Hermes down, like bats in a cave (*Odyssey* 24.6–9; Leaf on 23.101). The unburied Elpenor, on the other hand, is not kept from the shades (*Odyssey* 11.51–83), a difference Leaf puts down to the differing ages and places of the poems (on 23.72; see the translation's note on 23.71–74).

**The last request.** The bones are to lie together because the two were reared together, and the soul tells, as no one else in the poem does, how Patroclus came to Peleus' house: a killing in his boyhood, the exile it brought, and the welcome he found (see [[patroclus]] and [[exile-and-refuge]]). Achilles has the bones gathered into a golden bowl, to wait until he himself is hidden in Hades (23.243–244). The verse that names the jar as Thetis' gift was rejected in antiquity, and Leaf agrees (on 23.92; see [[the-golden-urn]] and the translation's note on 23.91–92). In the Odyssey the bones of the two lie mingled in the golden amphora that Thetis gave (*Odyssey* 24.73–77).

**Dear heart.** Achilles greets the soul as ἠθείη κεφαλή, “dear heart” (23.94; see [[dear-heart]]). Leaf reports the scholia's explanation that the word is the address of a younger man to an elder, and Patroclus was the elder (on 23.94; 11.786–787).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates strikes Achilles' cry and the soul that goes beneath the earth like smoke, gibbering, out of the poems his guardians are to hear, with the other verses that make death fearful (23.103–104, 23.100–101; *Republic* 3.386d–387a; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]). Aeschines had the ghost's speech read to the jury in his prosecution of Timarchus (see [[aeschines-on-achilles-and-patroclus]]); Leaf calls it the longest quotation from Homer in any classical writer, and notes its large variations from our text (on 23.77). Leaf also notes that Virgil follows the idea that the unburied cannot cross the river (on 23.72, citing *Aeneid* 6.327 ff.; see [[virgil-the-unburied]]).
