## The story

Homer tells it twice, both times in Poseidon's words.

**Book 7.** The Achaeans have built their wall without sacrifice, and Poseidon complains to Zeus that its fame will spread as far as the dawn, “and men will forget the wall that I and Phoebus Apollo toiled to build for the hero Laomedon” (7.452–453; see [[the-building-of-the-wall]]). Here the two gods build it together. ἀθλήσαντε, “the two of us, toiling”, is dual. ἀθλέω is the verb of hard labor, used of Heracles and his many toils (15.30) and of the servitude Andromache foresees for her son (ἀθλεύων, 24.734). πολίζω, “build a city, fortify”, occurs once more in the Iliad, of the founding of Ilios in the plain (πεπόλιστο, 20.217).

**Book 21.** In the battle of the gods Poseidon challenges Apollo and reminds him why he has no cause to help the Trojans. The two of them alone of the gods “came from Zeus” and served the lordly Laomedon for a year, for an agreed wage, and took his orders. Poseidon built a wall around the city for the Trojans, “broad and very fine, so that the city might be unbreakable”. Apollo herded the king's shambling, crumple-horned cattle in the glens of wooded Ida. When the seasons brought the end of the year, Laomedon robbed them of their whole wage and sent them away with threats: he would bind their hands and feet and sell them into distant islands, and he would cut off the ears of both with the bronze. They went away angry at heart over the wage he had promised and did not pay (21.441–457; see [[laomedon]]). Apollo will not fight his uncle over mortals, and turns away (21.461–469).

θητεύσαμεν, “we served for hire” (21.444), makes the gods θῆτες, landless laborers working for another man's pay. The shade of Achilles names the same station when he says he would rather serve for hire on earth than rule over all the dead (θητευέμεν, *Odyssey* 11.489–491). Why the gods took such work, Homer does not say. πὰρ Διὸς ἐλθόντες, “coming from Zeus”, may mean that Zeus sent them.

## In the poem

**Two versions.** The two accounts do not agree about Apollo. In Book 7 he builds the wall with Poseidon; in Book 21 he herds the cattle while Poseidon builds (see the translation's note on 7.443–464). Each version suits its occasion. In Book 7 Poseidon is jealous for the fame of a work, and he names Apollo as his partner in it. In Book 21 he is rebuking Apollo, and he recalls what each of them suffered.

**A king who cheats his creditors.** Laomedon is also the king who cheated Heracles of the horses promised for his service, so that Heracles sacked Ilios with six ships (5.640–651; see [[heracles-sacks-troy]]). The two stories show the same fault in the same reign. Poseidon's grievance is one reason the poem gives for his hostility to Troy. It gives another in its last book, where he, Hera and Athena go on hating Ilios for the folly of Paris (24.25–30). Apollo, cheated as well, still stands by the Trojans.

**The wall the gods built.** The walls of Troy keep the Achaeans out for the whole poem. When Patroclus three times climbs the angle of the high wall, Apollo pushes him back from the tower: the city is not fated to fall by his spear (16.698–711; see [[the-walls-of-troy]]). In Book 7 the god who built that wall wants the new Achaean wall destroyed, and in Book 12 he and Apollo destroy it together (12.17–33).

## Outside Homer

[[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] says that Apollo and Poseidon, wishing to test Laomedon's arrogance, took the likeness of men and undertook to fortify Pergamum for a wage. When Laomedon did not pay, Apollo sent a plague and Poseidon a sea monster, and an oracle said the city would be freed if Laomedon exposed his daughter Hesione to it. Heracles killed the monster for the promise of the horses Zeus had given for Ganymede, was cheated in his turn, and came back to take Troy (*Library* 2.5.9, 2.6.4; see [[ganymede-and-the-horses-of-tros]]). [[pindar|Pindar]] adds a mortal builder, Aeacus, and an omen that the city would be taken where his hands had worked (*Olympian* 8; see [[aeacus]]). Ovid tells the story of the walls, the broken promise, Hesione and Heracles in the *Metamorphoses* (Book 11; see [[ovid]]). For the Romans the cheat became the first of Troy's perjuries: “long enough have we paid with our blood for the perjuries of Laomedon's Troy” (Virgil, *Georgics* 1.501–502; see [[virgil]]), and Horace makes Juno date Troy's doom from the day Laomedon cheated the gods of their agreed pay (*Odes* 3.3; see [[horace]]).
