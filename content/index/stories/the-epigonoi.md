## The story

{{quote:962da432-0690-53a3-9676-5c0e0b672ce4}}

This is all the Iliad says of the war. ἕδος (4.406) is the “seat” of a city, its site and foundation, and Thebes is ἑπτάπυλος, “seven-gated” ([[seven-gated]]). The sons led a smaller army than their fathers against a stronger wall (4.407; on the reading see [[wall-of-ares]]), and they won because they trusted “the portents of the gods and the help of Zeus” (4.408). Their fathers “perished through their own recklessness” (4.409; see [[the-seven-against-thebes]]). Sthenelus says “we”, himself and Diomedes, and the Iliad names no other of the Epigonoi.

The Catalogue of Ships may show the result. Among the towns of the Boeotians it names no Thebes, only Hypothebae (2.505), whose name means “Lower Thebes”, and ancient readers took this for the lower town that was still lived in after the Epigonoi destroyed the citadel (see [[hypothebae]]). In the Odyssey the seer Amphiaraus, who died at Thebes, has two sons, Alcmaeon and Amphilochus (Odyssey 15.244–248); in later tradition Alcmaeon led the Epigonoi.

## In the poem

**Better than our fathers.** Sthenelus reverses the order that the Iliad's old men take for granted. Nestor says the men of his youth were stronger than any now alive (1.260–272), and Agamemnon has just told Diomedes that he is worse than his father in battle (4.399–400). Sthenelus claims the opposite, with evidence: the sons took the city that defeated the fathers. He ends by asking the king never to put the fathers “in equal honor” with them (4.410; see [[time]]). See [[fathers-and-sons]] and [[old-age-and-memory]].

**The words of the Odyssey.** κεῖνοι δὲ σφετέρῃσιν ἀτασθαλίῃσιν ὄλοντο, “but they perished through their own recklessness” (4.409), is nearly the line in which the proem of the Odyssey explains the loss of Odysseus' companions, who ate the cattle of the Sun: αὐτῶν γὰρ σφετέρῃσιν ἀτασθαλίῃσιν ὄλοντο, “for they perished by their own reckless acts” (Odyssey 1.7). In both poems ἀτασθαλίαι are the folly of men who ignore the gods' warnings and bring on their own ruin (see [[atasthalie]]).

**Silence.** Diomedes does not join his companion's boast. “Friend, sit in silence, and obey my word” (τέττα; see [[tetta]]): he does not blame Agamemnon for urging the Achaeans on, since the glory and the grief of the war will be his (4.412–418). The son of Tydeus leaves his own claim to be made in the next book, in battle. See [[the-review-of-the-army]] and [[diomedes]].

**The three leaders of Argos.** Diomedes, Sthenelus and Euryalus lead the men of Argos and Tiryns together (2.559–568). All three are sons of men who went to Thebes: Tydeus and Capaneus died there, and Mecisteus, Euryalus' father, once beat all the Cadmeians in the funeral games of Oedipus (23.677–680). Later lists of the Epigonoi include all three (Apollodorus 3.7.2).

## Outside Homer

An epic *Epigonoi* was known in antiquity. Herodotus names it as a poem of Homer's, and adds a doubt, “if Homer really composed this poem” (Herodotus 4.32). Its first line is quoted in the *Contest of Homer and Hesiod* and sung by a boy at the end of Aristophanes' *Peace*: “Now, Muses, let us begin on the younger men” (νῦν αὖθʼ ὁπλοτέρων ἀνδρῶν ἀρχώμεθα, Μοῦσαι; *Peace* 1270). ὁπλότεροι, “younger”, is Nestor's word for the men who now wield the spears (4.324–325).

In Apollodorus the Epigonoi march on Thebes ten years after the Seven, led by Alcmaeon son of Amphiaraus. Laodamas son of Eteocles kills Aegialeus son of Adrastus, and Alcmaeon kills Laodamas; on the advice of the seer Tiresias the Thebans abandon their city, and the Argives take it and pull down its walls (3.7.2–3). Pindar makes Amphiaraus, who died in the first war, foretell the victory of the sons as they stand before seven-gated Thebes: by nature the noble spirit of the fathers shines out in the sons (*Pythian* 8.39–55). At the end of Euripides' *Suppliant Women* Athena tells the sons of the Seven that they will take Thebes when they are grown.
