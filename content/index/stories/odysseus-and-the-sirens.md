## The story

**Circe's warning.** When the ship came back from the house of Hades to bury Elpenor, Circe took Odysseus apart for the night and told him the dangers of the voyage, beginning with the first of them (*Odyssey* 12.1–38; see [[odysseus-and-circe]] and [[the-death-of-elpenor]]):

{{quote:8e7ac848-f152-5dea-8136-2402328d3bbc}}

He was to row past and stop his companions' ears with honey-sweet wax that he had kneaded, so that none of them might hear. If he himself wished to listen, they were to bind him hand and foot, upright in the mast-step, with the rope-ends fastened to the mast, and if he begged to be set free, to bind him with still more bonds (*Odyssey* 12.47–54; see [[sirens]] and [[mast]]).

**Past the island.** At dawn Circe went away up the island and sent a following wind behind the ship. Odysseus told his companions what she had said of the Sirens, “so that knowing them we may either die / or else avoid death and doom and escape” (*Odyssey* 12.142–164). As the ship came near the island of the two Sirens the wind fell, and a god lulled the waves to sleep; the companions furled the sail and took to the oars. Odysseus cut a round of wax into pieces with the sharp bronze, kneaded it until it grew warm in his strong hands and the sunlight, and anointed the ears of all his companions in turn; they bound him to the mast and rowed on (*Odyssey* 12.165–180). When the ship was within call, the Sirens saw it and raised their clear song:

{{quote:942650c5-b2f0-5e90-b429-e58644022ae1}}

His heart wished to listen, and he nodded with his brows to his companions to set him free; but they bent forward and rowed, and Perimedes and Eurylochus stood up and bound him with more bonds. When the voice of the Sirens could no longer be heard, the companions took the wax from their ears and freed him (*Odyssey* 12.192–200; see [[perimedes-the-companion]] and [[eurylochus]]). Then the ship came to the smoke and the roar of the strait (see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]).

## In the poem

**The homecoming.** Circe sets the danger against the homecoming. Whoever hears the Sirens “in his ignorance” never comes home: his wife and little children do not stand beside him when he returns, nor are they glad (*Odyssey* 12.41–43), and the bones of rotting men lie heaped about the singers (*Odyssey* 12.45–46). The song promises the opposite, that every man who has heard it “goes home delighted, and knowing more” (*Odyssey* 12.188; see [[nostos]]). The adventure sets forgetting against home, like the lotus that made men forget their homecoming and Circe's drugs, given “so that they might utterly forget their native land” (*Odyssey* 9.97, 10.236; see [[the-lotus-eaters]]). Here the danger is a song.

**The singers of Troy.** The Sirens greet Odysseus with the Iliad's address, “much-praised Odysseus, great glory of the Achaeans”, in which Agamemnon and Nestor speak to him at Troy (*Odyssey* 12.184; *Iliad* 9.673, 10.544; see [[much-praised]] and [[great-glory-of-the-achaeans]]). They offer what the singers of the Odyssey sing: Phemius sang the grim homecoming of the Achaeans (*Odyssey* 1.325–327), and Odysseus praised Demodocus for singing the fate of the Achaeans, “all that they did and suffered, and all that the Achaeans toiled through”, as if he had been there himself (*Odyssey* 8.489–491; see [[phemius]] and [[demodocus]]). The Sirens know “all the things that in wide Troy / the Argives and Trojans toiled through by the will of the gods” (*Odyssey* 12.189–190), with the same verb. D. B. Monro observes that even the song of the Sirens turns on the inexhaustible theme of Troy, like the lays of the Odyssey's singers and the false tales of Odysseus (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix I §3). The singer who tells this tale has himself held the Phaeacians spellbound (*Odyssey* 11.333–334; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]] and [[thelgo]]).

**Instructions carried out.** The adventure is told three times in the same verses: Circe's instructions, Odysseus' words to his companions, and the deed. The binding “upright in the mast-step, and let the rope-ends be fastened to the mast itself” passes from Circe to Odysseus and then to the narrative (*Odyssey* 12.51, 12.162, 12.179), and the “still more bonds” from her command to his and to the companions who carry it out (*Odyssey* 12.54, 12.164, 12.196; see [[retelling]]). Circe gave him a choice, “if you yourself are willing to listen” (*Odyssey* 12.49); he tells his companions that “she bade me alone to hear their voice” (*Odyssey* 12.160). He names the voice and the flowering meadow (*Odyssey* 12.158–159), but not the heap of bones that Circe had described. Escaping the Cyclops, he nodded to the companions to row, “and they bent forward and rowed” (*Odyssey* 9.490); here he nods to them to stop, and they bend forward and row (*Odyssey* 12.194).

**Told to Penelope.** In the summary of his wanderings that he gives Penelope the adventure has one verse: he told “how he heard the voice of the thronging Sirens” (*Odyssey* 23.326, a plain rendering). Monro takes ἁδινάων of their voices, “thick-coming”, “with ever re-sounding song”, but finds no warrant for giving the epithet to the Sirens themselves: the poet, he thinks, had in mind the thronging flies of the Iliad, μυιάων ἁδινάων (Monro on 23.326; *Iliad* 2.469).

## Outside Homer

**The earliest allusion.** Nitzsch, as Merry reports, quotes Alcman, “and then Circe, anointing the ears of the companions of stout-hearted Odysseus”, and remarks that it is the earliest testimony extant to any particular scene of the Odyssey (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.47). In Alcman's line Circe herself anoints the ears; in Homer she tells Odysseus to do it.

**Knowledge.** Cicero translates the song into Latin hexameters, “O decus Argolicum, quin puppim flectis, Ulixes”, and explains it as Homer's picture of the love of knowledge: the Sirens did not call back those who sailed past by the sweetness of their voices or the novelty and variety of their singing, but because they professed to know many things, so that men clung to their rocks from desire of learning. Homer saw, he says, that the story would not be believed if so great a man were held entangled by mere songs; they promise knowledge, which it was not strange for a man eager for wisdom to hold dearer than his native land (*De Finibus* 5.49; see [[cicero]]).

For the Sirens of later poets, their number, names, parents and bird shape, and the places where they were later shown, see [[sirens]] and [[the-sirens-in-later-writers]].
