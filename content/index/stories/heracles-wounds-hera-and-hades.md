## The story

{{quote:3f215c34-dcb1-5b22-a7e3-b8c18354bcb9}}

Dione names the archer twice in four lines, first as “the mighty son of Amphitryon” (5.392), after his mother's husband, then as “the son of aegis-bearing Zeus” (5.396; see [[heracles]] and [[amphitryon]]). He wounded Hera “in the right breast with a three-barbed arrow” (ὀϊστῷ τριγλώχινι), the kind of arrow with which Paris later wounds Machaon (11.507), and “incurable pain” (ἀνήκεστον ἄλγος) seized even her (5.393–394). Nothing is said of her healing.

He shot Hades too, ἐν Πύλῳ ἐν νεκύεσσι, “in Pylos among the dead” (5.397). The words can also be read “at the gate among the dead”, the gate of the underworld, and each reading points to a different exploit: Heracles' war on Pylos, which Nestor remembers (11.689–693), or his descent to fetch the hound of Hades (8.366–369). See [[pylos-or-the-gate]]. The arrow stuck in the god's massive shoulder, and Hades went up to the house of Zeus on Olympus, grieving and “pierced through with pains”. There Paeëon spread pain-killing drugs on the wound and healed him, “for he was not made to be mortal” (5.398–402; see [[hades]] and [[paeeon]]).

Homer does not say why Heracles shot Hera or Hades.

## In the poem

**The mortal who wounds gods.** Dione tells these stories after the binding of Ares, to show her daughter that the gods have suffered at the hands of men before (5.382–384; see [[the-aloadae-bind-ares]] and [[mythological-paradigm]]). Heracles is the great example. Her judgment of him is harsh: “Hard man, doer of violent deeds, who cared nothing for the evil he did, who with his bow brought sorrow on the gods who hold Olympus!” (5.403–404; see [[doer-of-violent-deeds]]). σχέτλιος, “hard, reckless”, and αἴσυλα, “evil, wicked things”, are words of blame.

**Diomedes as Heracles.** The next line turns from the old hero to the new one: “And against you the gray-eyed goddess Athena set this man on” (5.405). Like Heracles, Diomedes is a mortal who has wounded a goddess, and like Heracles he has Athena behind him; she was Heracles' helper in his labors (8.362–369). Dione's warning follows: the son of Tydeus does not know that the man who fights the immortals does not live long, and his children will not climb on his knees when he comes home (5.406–409; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]). Heracles did not escape death either: “not even the might of Heracles escaped death”, Achilles says, “though he was dearest to Zeus; fate overcame him, and the harsh anger of Hera” (18.117–119).

**A paradigm acted out.** Heracles' other deed in Book 5 is the sack of Troy, which his son Tlepolemus boasts of a few hundred lines later (5.638–642; see [[heracles-sacks-troy]]). The wounding of Hades comes back at the end of the book. When Diomedes has wounded Ares, Paeëon heals him in the two lines that told the healing of Hades (5.401–402 = 5.900–901; see [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]). The story that Dione told as an old example is repeated in the present, and the god's wound is healed as quickly as before.

## Elsewhere in Homer

Hera is Heracles' enemy throughout Homer's allusions to him. She tricked Zeus on the day of his birth, so that Eurystheus was born first and became his master (19.95–133). When he sailed home from sacking Troy she sent storms that drove him to Cos, and Zeus hung her from the sky for it (14.249–256, 15.18–30). Achilles counts her anger with fate as what brought him down (18.117–119).

Hades is at the heart of another labor. Eurystheus sent Heracles to the house of Hades “the gatekeeper” to bring back the hound from Erebus, and Athena saved him (8.366–369; see [[heracles-and-cerberus]]). In the Odyssey his ghost tells Odysseus that he brought the dog up from the underworld with the help of Hermes and Athena (Odyssey 11.623–626).

## Outside Homer

Later writers placed the wounding of Hades in Heracles' war on Pylos, when gods fought on the side of the Pylians against him; the sources are set out in [[pylos-or-the-gate]]. Heracles fights another god in the Hesiodic *Shield*: after killing Cycnus, the son of Ares, he wounds Ares himself in the thigh, and Terror and Rout drive the god away.
