## The story

Diomedes has crushed Aeneas' hip with a stone, and black night covers his eyes (5.302–310). His mother sees it.

{{quote:404d182a-8d6a-524c-9d87-0a281dd00a1a}}

Diomedes goes after her, “knowing that she was a feeble god, and not one of those goddesses who rule over the war of men, neither Athena nor Enyo, sacker of cities” (5.331–333). Athena has given him leave to wound this goddess and no other (5.131–132). He catches her in the throng, leaps, and wounds her hand with his spear through the ambrosial robe that the Graces themselves had made for her, above the base of the palm (5.334–339; see [[graces]]).

{{quote:22e0313e-ee00-5cfb-8120-bb9d50179d2e}}

She cries out and lets her son fall, and Apollo takes him up and hides him in a dark cloud (5.343–346). Diomedes shouts after her: “Keep away, daughter of Zeus, from war and combat; is it not enough that you deceive feeble women?” (5.348–349). She goes away in pain, and her skin darkens. Iris leads her out of the throng to Ares, who sits at the left of the battle with his spear and horses resting in a mist, and she begs her brother on her knees for his horses. Iris drives her to Olympus, unyokes the horses and sets ambrosial fodder before them (5.352–369).

Aphrodite falls on the knees of her mother, Dione, who takes her in her arms, strokes her, and asks who has done this. “The son of Tydeus wounded me, high-hearted Diomedes”, she answers; “now the Danaans are fighting even with the immortals” (5.370–380; see [[dione]]). Dione tells her to bear it, for many of the gods have suffered at the hands of men. Ares was bound by the sons of Aloeus and lay for thirteen months in a bronze jar; Hera and Hades were shot by the son of Amphitryon (5.381–404; see [[the-aloadae-bind-ares]] and [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]]). Athena set this man on against her, but the fool does not know that the man who fights with the immortals does not live long: let him beware, or his wife Aegialeia will wake the household with her weeping for the best of the Achaeans (5.405–415; see [[who-fights-the-gods]] and [[aegialeia]]). She wipes the ichor from her daughter's hand, and the hand is healed (5.416–417).

Athena and Hera watch, and Athena teases Zeus: Cypris must have been coaxing some Achaean woman to follow the Trojans she loves so much, and scratched her slender hand on a golden brooch as she caressed her (5.418–425; see [[brooch]]). The father of men and gods smiles and calls golden Aphrodite to him.

## In the poem

**The one goddess.** Diomedes' attack is permitted, and it is chosen. With the mist taken from his eyes he can tell a god from a man (5.127–128), and Athena has named Aphrodite as the one god he may strike (5.131–132). He knows what he is doing: she is ἄναλκις, without the strength to fight, the translation's “feeble” (5.331; see [[alke]]). He uses the same word of the women she leads astray (γυναῖκας ἀνάλκιδας, 5.349). The goddess of love is a feeble god in war, and Zeus agrees: the works of war are for Ares and Athena, hers are the works of marriage (5.428–430; see [[the-works-of-war]]).

**A god's blood.** The wound gives the Iliad its only account of what flows in a god's veins. It is ἰχώρ, “ichor”, “immortal blood”, and the narrator explains it: the gods eat no bread and drink no sparkling wine, and so they are bloodless and are called immortals (5.339–342; see [[ichor]]). Mortals are the ones “who eat the fruit of the field” (6.142); the gods live on ambrosia and nectar (see [[ambrosia]] and [[nectar]]). A god can be wounded and feel pain, and in Dione's stories can even be brought near to perishing (5.388), but the wound heals, “for he was not made to be mortal”, as the story of Paeëon's cure for Hades says (5.402). See [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]].

**A mother and her son.** The scene repeats and reverses the rescue of Paris in Book 3, told in the same turn of phrase: “had not Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus, been quick to see it” (5.312, as at 3.374; see [[aphrodite-rescues-paris]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). There she snatched Paris away with ease. Here she wraps her son in a fold of her robe, is wounded, and drops him, and another god has to save him. Aeneas is her son by Anchises (5.313; see [[aphrodite-and-anchises]]), and his mother's rescue sets the pattern of his part in the Iliad: he is saved again by Apollo in this book and by Poseidon in Book 20 (see [[the-phantom-of-aeneas]] and [[aeneas]]). See [[mothers-and-sons]].

**Olympus as a household.** The scene on Olympus is told as a family scene: a daughter who has been hurt runs to her mother, her mother takes her on her lap, a sister makes fun of her, and the father smiles and tells her to keep to her own work (see [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]] and [[smiling-at-him]]). Athena's joke points at the war itself. The Achaean woman whom Aphrodite has coaxed into following the Trojans, and whose brooch has scratched her, is a picture of Helen (see [[the-abduction-of-helen]]). The lightness has an edge. Dione's comfort ends with a threat against a mortal and his wife, and her stories are of gods bound, shot and nearly destroyed (see [[mythological-paradigm]]).

**The first of two.** The wounding of Aphrodite is the first half of a pair. At the end of the book Diomedes wounds Ares with Athena in his chariot; Ares too goes up to Olympus, shows his wound, complains, and is healed. His mother does not comfort him, and his father calls him the most hateful of the gods (5.855–906; see [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]). Aphrodite and Ares both take the wound as a sign that a mortal now fights the gods (5.380, 5.881–884), and Apollo says of Diomedes, as Aphrodite does, that he “would fight even with father Zeus” (5.362, 5.457).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Aphrodite is struck again in the battle of the gods. When she leads the wounded Ares away, Athena, at Hera's urging, strikes her on the breast with her fist and boasts that if all the Trojans' helpers were like Aphrodite, the war would long be over (21.416–433). In Book 14 Hera borrows from her the embroidered band in which are love, desire and seductive talk, the works that Zeus assigns to her here (14.188–223). The Odyssey's Demodocus sings of her love for Ares and of the net in which Hephaestus caught them (Odyssey 8.266–366).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod gives Aphrodite no mother. She is born from the foam that gathered around the severed genitals of Uranus (*Theogony* 188–206), and his Dione is one of the daughters of Oceanus (*Theogony* 353; see [[dione]]). Plato's Pausanias makes the two stories two goddesses: a heavenly Aphrodite, motherless daughter of Uranus, and a common Aphrodite, daughter of Zeus and Dione (*Symposium* 180d–e). When Alexander the Great was wounded by an arrow, Plutarch says, he told his companions that what flowed was blood, not ichor, quoting the line of this book (*Alexander* 28). In Virgil, Diomedes remembers the day he wounded Venus in the hand as the beginning of his punishment (*Aeneid* 11.275–277).
