## The story

Phoenix has told Achilles of his own youth and of the Prayers. He ends with the men of old:

{{quote:43f3c214-2c44-5c97-a120-347ed50004c3}}

**The war and its cause.** The Curetes and the Aetolians were fighting around Calydon, the Aetolians defending it and the Curetes “eager to sack it in the work of Ares” (9.529–532; see [[curetes]], [[aetolians]] and [[calydon]]). Then Phoenix goes back to the cause. Oeneus had left Artemis out of his harvest offering, “either he forgot, or did not think of it” (9.537), and in her anger she sent a wild boar that tore up his orchard (see [[oeneus]] and [[artemis]]). His son Meleager gathered huntsmen and dogs from many cities and killed it, but over its head and hide the goddess raised “a great din and shouting” between the Curetes and the Aetolians (9.533–549; see [[the-calydonian-boar]]). So the story comes back to the war with which it began (see [[ring-composition]]).

**The anger.** As long as Meleager fought, the Curetes could not hold their ground outside the wall. Then anger entered him, and “angry at heart with his dear mother Althaea” he lay beside his wife Cleopatra, daughter of Marpessa and Idas, “brooding on his heart-grieving anger” (9.550–565; see [[meleager]], [[cleopatra]] and [[idas-and-apollo]]). His mother had cursed him. Grieving over the killing of her brother, she beat the earth with her hands and called on Hades and Persephone “to give death to her son”, and the Erinys heard her out of Erebus (9.566–572; see [[althaea]], [[curses]], [[erinyes]] and [[the-death-of-meleager]]).

**The appeals.** Soon the din rose about the gates and the towers were battered. The elders of the Aetolians begged him and sent the best priests of the gods, promising a domain of fifty acres from the richest part of the plain, half vineyard and half plowland (9.573–580). Then the others came, one after another:

{{quote:9259bcaf-4ef3-5130-af10-15213701d436}}

**The wife.** At last his wife begged him, weeping, and told him what happens to a captured city: “they kill the men, and fire levels the city, / and strangers lead away the children and the deep-girdled women” (9.593–594; see [[women-and-war]] and [[captive-women]]). His heart was stirred, and he armed and went out.

{{quote:39ce92f6-0b0e-5b03-9b99-1626e7151be7}}

## In the poem

**A paradigm.** Phoenix states the rule before he tells the story: the heroes of old, “whenever furious anger came upon one of them”, could be won with gifts and swayed by words (9.525–526). The story is his proof, and the last of the three parts of his speech, after his own life and the Prayers (9.434–523; see [[phoenix]], [[the-flight-of-phoenix]] and [[litai]]). As Nestor told of the Lapiths (1.259–274), an old man tells a younger one in his anger what the men of old did (see [[mythological-paradigm]]). He tells it “among you, who are all my friends” (9.528), to the envoys as well as to Achilles, and draws the lesson:

{{quote:a5f8f90f-db92-530c-b3a9-2d914c47f515}}

**Fitted to Achilles.** The story follows Achilles' case point by point. While Meleager fights, the enemy keeps away from the wall, as Hector came no farther than the Scaean gates while Achilles fought (9.352–355). The hero withdraws in anger, and the enemy comes to the walls with fire, as Hector threatens the ships (9.241–243, 9.347–352). The elders beg him and promise a great gift, as the elders in Agamemnon's hut have sent the gifts (9.89–172). The last to plead before his wife are his companions, “who were the most loyal and dearest to him of all” (9.586), and Achilles greeted the envoys as “the dearest of the Achaeans” (9.198), as Phoenix has just reminded him (9.520–522). Several of the words Phoenix uses for Meleager are used of Achilles too (see [[meleager]]), and in Book 18 Thetis tells the embassy in the verse of Meleager's suppliants: τὸν δὲ λίσσοντο γέροντες, “and the elders begged him” (18.448 = 9.574; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).

**The order of the suppliants.** J. Th. Kakridis called the pattern “the ascending scale of affection”: a man is begged by one person after another, each closer to him than the last, and yields only to the one he loves most (*Homeric Researches*, 1949). Here the scale runs from elders and priests to his father, his sisters and mother, his companions, and his wife. The mother who cursed him is among those who beg, and the companions come after his own family; some see in their place the shape of Achilles' case, whose envoys are his dearest friends. Κλεοπάτρη, “glory of the father”, has the two parts of Patroclus' name in reverse order, and it is she who moves Meleager at the end, as Patroclus will move Achilles. The likeness is often noted; Homer does not point to it (see [[cleopatra]] and [[patroclus]]).

**Phoenix's own story.** The tale of his youth has the same parts in another order (9.447–484). A parent curses a son before the powers below, Hades and Persephone (9.454–457, 9.569). Phoenix's kinsmen kept watch over him for nine nights to hold him at home, and he broke the doors of his chamber and fled (9.464–477); Meleager's father shakes the doors of his son's chamber to bring him out, and he will not come (9.581–583).

**What Phoenix leaves out.** He does not say who killed Althaea's brother; her curse implies that Meleager did, in the fighting over the hide, and later poets said so. He does not tell Meleager's death, though the Erinys heard the curse (see [[the-death-of-meleager]]). There is no Atalanta and no brand. And he calls Meleager's anger χόλος (9.553, 9.565), keeping μῆνις, “wrath”, for Achilles' (9.517; see [[cholos]] and [[menis]]).

**What follows.** Achilles refuses the lesson: he has no need of such honor, for he is “honored by the portion of Zeus” (9.607–608). But to Ajax he names the very moment Phoenix warned against: he will not think of war until Hector reaches the ships of the Myrmidons and “burns the ships with fire” (9.650–653). In Book 16 Patroclus comes to him in tears, and when the first ship burns Achilles sends him out in his armor (16.2–3, 16.112–129); he goes out himself only when Patroclus is dead. The gifts are given to him in Book 19, when he no longer cares for them (19.146–148).

**An old story or a new one?** How much Phoenix found in an older story, and how much the poet made for Achilles, is an old question. M. M. Willcock argued that the poet adapts his paradigms to their moment and at times invents them, and that what fits Achilles best here, the withdrawal, the appeals, the wife and the lost gifts, may have been made to fit (“Mythological Paradeigma in the Iliad”, *Classical Quarterly* 14, 1964). Others have traced behind it an older poem about Meleager. The other ancient accounts of his death do not settle the question: Pausanias knew one in which Apollo killed him and one in which his life hung on a brand, and says that the brand was already famous throughout Greece when Phrynichus put it on the stage (10.31.3–4).

## Outside Homer

Bacchylides lets Meleager tell his own story, in an ode for Hieron of Syracuse (476 BC). Heracles, gone down to fetch the hound of Hades, meets Meleager's shade, who tells him of the boar that Artemis sent in spite of Oeneus' sacrifices, of the fight with the Curetes for its hide, in which he killed his mother's brothers, and of the brand that his mother took from a chest and burned. Heracles weeps, for the only time, and asks whether Meleager has an unmarried sister; Meleager names Deianeira (Bacchylides 5.56–175; see [[heracles]]). Pausanias says that Phrynichus was the first to put the brand on the stage, in his *Women of Pleuron* (10.31.4; see [[pleuron]]), and the chorus of Aeschylus' *Libation Bearers* sets “the child-destroying daughter of Thestius” first among women who destroyed their own kin (602–611; see [[althaea]]). Euripides' lost *Meleager* told of Oeneus' first fruits and brought Atalanta among the hunters (frr. 516, 530 Nauck). In the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* and the *Minyas*, Pausanias says, Apollo helped the Curetes and killed Meleager (10.31.3), and a papyrus of the *Catalogue* has him fall “by the hands of Apollo” while fighting the Curetes (fr. 98 Evelyn-White).

[[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] gives both versions (*Library* 1.8.2–3). In the first Meleager kills the boar, gives the hide to Atalanta, and kills the sons of Thestius when they take it from her, and Althaea burns the brand. In the second, which “others” tell, the Curetes and the Calydonians go to war over the hide, Meleager kills some of his uncles and stays at home under his mother's curse until his wife persuades him, and he dies fighting. Afterward Althaea and Cleopatra hanged themselves, and the women who mourned him were turned into birds. Ovid tells the hunt, the brand and the mourning at length (*Metamorphoses* 8.260–546; see [[ovid]]). Polygnotus painted Meleager among the dead in the Lesche of the Cnidians at Delphi (Pausanias 10.31.3), and Swinburne made the story an English verse drama, *Atalanta in Calydon* (1865).
