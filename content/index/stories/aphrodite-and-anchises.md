## The story

{{quote:9ad11811-e0ed-5d96-b078-d49251675067}}

θεὰ βροτῷ εὐνηθεῖσα, "a goddess who lay with a mortal". The Iliad gives only the fact and the place, the slopes of Ida, where Anchises was herding his cattle (5.313). The fifth *Homeric Hymn*, to Aphrodite, tells the story. Zeus pays Aphrodite back for making the other gods fall in love with mortals by making her desire Anchises. She comes to him on Ida disguised as a mortal girl, the daughter of a Phrygian king, and they lie together. Then she shows herself as a goddess. He is afraid, and she promises him a son, Aeneas, who will rule among the Trojans, to be raised by the nymphs of the mountain; he must never say who the mother is (45–290).

## In the poem

The Catalogue seldom stops for a story. This one gives Aeneas the most distinguished birth of any Trojan leader, and it prepares for Book 5, where his mother carries him out of the battle (5.311–318), and Book 20, where the gods save him again because his line must survive (20.302–308). A goddess's love for a mortal is a painful theme in Homer. Thetis was married to Peleus against her will (18.432–434), and Calypso lists goddesses whose mortal lovers the gods destroyed (Odyssey 5.118–129).

## Outside Homer

In the Hymn, Aphrodite names her son from the "terrible grief" (αἰνὸν ἄχος) that she felt at lying with a mortal (198–199), and warns that Zeus will strike Anchises with his thunderbolt if he boasts of her (286–290). Later tradition said that he did boast and was struck. In Virgil he says that Zeus "blasted me with the wind of his thunderbolt and touched me with fire" (*Aeneid* 2.647–649).
