## The story

{{quote:8bbbde50-067b-5fe0-b589-3047a32154d4}}

[[Polymele]], “beautiful in the dance”, was the daughter of [[Phylas]]. [[Hermes]] saw her among the girls singing and dancing in the chorus of [[Artemis]], and fell in love with her. At once he went up to her upper room and lay with her in secret, and she bore him a son, [[Eudorus]], “surpassingly swift at running, and a fighter”. When [[Eileithyia]], who brings hard birth, had brought the child out into the light, [[Echecles]], son of Actor, took Polymele to his house, after giving a countless bride-price, and the old man Phylas reared the boy and cherished him as if he were his own son. Eudorus grew up to lead the second of the five companies of the Myrmidons.

## In the poem

**Among the leaders.** The story is the longest of the five entries in the catalogue of the Myrmidon leaders, fourteen verses beside six for Menesthius and one to three for the others (16.168–197; see [[myrmidons]] and [[the-birth-of-menesthius]]). Like Menesthius, Eudorus is the son of a god and of a girl who was then married by a mortal; but here the god's love is told step by step, and the marriage leaves the boy in his grandfather's house.

**The language of a secret union.** The verses are built from the poem's language for such births. The god “went up to her upper room and lay with her in secret” (εἰς ὑπερῷʼ ἀναβὰς παρελέξατο λάθρῃ, 16.184), as Ares lay with Astyoche, who went up into the upper room of her father's house (2.514–515; see [[the-birth-of-ascalaphus-and-ialmenus]] and [[astyoche]]). The child of such a union is παρθένιος, “a maiden's son” (16.180), as Bucolion, the eldest son of Laomedon, was born σκότιος, “in secret” (6.24; see [[bucolion]]). Eileithyia, “who brings hard birth”, brings him πρὸ φόως δέ, “out into the light” (16.187–188), with the epithet and the phrase of Agamemnon's tale in Book 19: Zeus boasted that Eileithyia would bring into the light that day a man who would rule his neighbors, and Hera brought Eurystheus out into the light before his time instead (19.103–105, 19.118–119; see [[eileithyia]] and [[who-brings-hard-birth]]). The marriage that follows is made in the formula of a wedding, ἐπεὶ πόρε μυρία ἕδνα, “after giving a countless bride-price” (16.190), said also of Hector and Andromache (22.472; see [[bride-price]]).

**The god's love.** Hermes ἠράσατʼ, “fell in love”, at the sight of her (16.182), with the verb of Zeus' loves (14.317; see [[eros]]). He is κρατὺς ἀργεϊφόντης, “the mighty slayer of Argus”, and Ἑρμείας ἀκάκητα, “Hermes the helper” (16.181, 16.185; see [[argeiphontes]]), and he is the subject of the gift: πόρεν δέ οἱ ἀγλαὸν υἱόν, “and he gave her a splendid son” (16.185; see the translation's note on 16.185). The son's name, Εὔδωρος, joins εὖ, “well”, and δῶρον, “gift”. The Iliad tells of no other love of Hermes; in its last book he is Priam's guide, and the gods would have had him steal Hector's body (24.23–24, 24.333–469; see [[hermes]]).

**The house of Phylas.** The story ends with the grandfather: τὸν δʼ ὃ γέρων Φύλας εὖ ἔτρεφεν ἠδʼ ἀτίταλλεν / ἀμφαγαπαζόμενος ὡς εἴ θʼ ἑὸν υἱὸν ἐόντα, “and the old man Phylas reared the boy well and cherished him, / loving him as if he were his own son” (16.191–192). The poem's stories of upbringing value such love for a child not one's own: Peleus loved the exile Phoenix “as a father loves his son” (9.481), and Phoenix reared Achilles as the son the gods denied him (9.485–495; see [[phylas]] and [[phoenix]]).

## Outside Homer

The chorus of Artemis is the place from which Hermes snatches a girl in the Homeric *Hymn to Aphrodite*. Aphrodite, in the likeness of a Phrygian princess, tells Anchises that the slayer of Argus carried her off “from the chorus of Artemis of the golden distaff, the clamorous”, where many nymphs and maidens danced (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 117–120; see [[aphrodite-and-anchises]]); the verse ends with the words of Iliad 16.183. Walter Leaf, who regards the catalogue of the Myrmidons as a later addition, calls the birth of Eudorus “another genealogical fiction”, which combines descent from a god with Echecles, the legendary ancestor of a family, and suggests that the rearing by Phylas may be a distant memory of marriages in which the children belonged to the mother's family (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.168, 16.180 and 16.191).
