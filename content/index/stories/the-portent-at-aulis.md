## The story

It was “yesterday, it seems, or the day before”, says Odysseus: the ships of the Achaeans were gathering at [[aulis|Aulis]], and the army was offering perfect hecatombs at the holy altars around a spring, under a fine [[plane-tree|plane tree]] where bright water flowed (2.303–307).

{{quote:b33ee2be-da52-52ed-baa2-e6651787495e}}

A blood-red [[serpent]], sent into the light by Zeus himself, darted from under the altar and made for the tree. On the topmost branch were the nestlings of a [[sparrow]], eight of them, and the mother made nine. The serpent ate the chicks as they cheeped, caught the mother by the wing as she fluttered around them crying, and ate her too. Then the god who had sent it turned it to stone.

The army stood amazed, and [[Calchas]], who was to guide the fleet to Troy (see [[calchas-guides-the-fleet]]), spoke at once.

{{quote:61cce681-3bc9-50a0-907b-080ef3797900}}

Zeus has shown them a great sign (τέρας; see [[teras]]), “late in coming, late in fulfillment, whose fame shall never perish”. As many as the birds, so many years they will fight at Troy, and in the tenth they will take the city.

## In the poem

Odysseus tells the story at the crisis of the test (see [[the-test-of-the-army]]). The army has just run for the ships, and he has shamed it back to its seats: they wail like children or widows, and after nine years it is shameful to go home empty-handed (see [[shameful-to-stay-long]]). The portent gives them a reason to stay. It came from Zeus, it was read by the army's seer, and “you are all witnesses” (μάρτυροι, 2.302): the proof is the army's own memory. The Iliad is set in the tenth year, and Odysseus draws the conclusion for his hearers: “now it is all being fulfilled” (2.330). The Argives shout, and the ships ring with it (2.333–335). Nestor then adds a second sign from the same departure, Zeus' lightning on the right (see [[the-departure-from-aulis]]).

The telling is a story inside a speech, with a speech inside the story: Odysseus quotes Calchas in his own words (see [[retelling]]). The portent has the parts of every omen in Homer: a sign sent by a god, a seer who reads it, and a fulfillment still to come (see [[divination]] and [[prophecy]]). Its κλέος will never perish (2.325; see [[kleos]]), and the poem that repeats it is part of that fame. It is also the one prophecy of Calchas at Aulis that Homer tells, and it is good news for the sons of Atreus, though Agamemnon complained in Book 1 that the seer had never foretold him anything good (1.106–108; see [[calchas-evil-prophecies]]).

The details say more than Calchas' arithmetic. The chicks are “helpless young” (νήπια τέκνα; see [[nepios]]), the mother flutters round “lamenting her dear young”, and the serpent coils and seizes her as she shrieks. Calchas reads years. Many readers have also seen in the scene the fate of the city: mothers and children devoured when the ninth year has passed. The poem does not say so.

The serpent's end is a sign too. The god “made him a sign for all to see” (ἀρίζηλον, 2.318) and turned him to stone. An ancient variant read ἀΐζηλον, “made him unseen”.

## Outside Homer

In Proclus' summary of the *Cypria*, the portent comes during the first gathering at Aulis: after the sacrifice, “what happened with the serpent and the sparrows” is shown, and Calchas foretells what will come to pass.

Aeschylus put a different sign at Aulis: two eagles tear a pregnant hare, and Calchas reads them as the two sons of Atreus who will take Troy, and as a cause of Artemis' anger (*Agamemnon* 104–159; see [[aeschylus-aulis]]).

Cicero quotes Odysseus' speech in a Latin verse version, and asks why the sparrows should stand for years rather than months or days (*De divinatione* 2.63–65; see [[cicero-aulis-sparrows]]). Ovid retells the portent, with the serpent coiled round the plane tree and turned to stone (*Metamorphoses* 12.11–23).
