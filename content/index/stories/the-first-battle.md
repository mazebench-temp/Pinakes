## The story

The Danaan battalions move to battle rank upon rank like waves breaking on a shore, and in silence; the Trojans come on with a din like ewes bleating in a rich man's fold, for their allies have no common language (4.422–438; see [[waves-on-the-shore]] and [[sheep-in-the-fold]]). Ares rouses the Trojans and gray-eyed Athena the Achaeans, and with them go Terror and Rout, and Strife, the sister of Ares, small when she first rears her head and then with her head in the sky, throwing the common quarrel into their midst (4.439–445; see [[strife]], [[terror]] and [[rout]]).

{{quote:29c22b36-2608-5add-8471-8725d80d3606}}

The noise of the meeting is the roar of two winter torrents where two valleys meet (see [[torrents-meeting]]), and the six lines of the clash will be used again when the armies meet in Book 8 (see [[the-armies-clash]]). Then the killing is told man by man. Antilochus, son of Nestor, is the first to kill a Trojan: Echepolus, son of Thalysias, struck through the forehead, falls like a tower (4.457–462; see [[antilochus]], [[echepolus]] and [[like-a-tower]]). Elephenor, leader of the Abantes, drags the body away to strip it, and Agenor stabs him in the side his shield has left bare; over him Trojans and Achaeans leap on one another like wolves (4.463–472; see [[elephenor]], [[agenor]] and [[like-wolves]]). Telamonian Ajax kills the young Simoeisius, who was born beside the river Simoeis, and he falls like a poplar (4.473–489; see [[simoeisius]] and [[the-poplar]]). Antiphus, son of Priam, throws at Ajax, misses, and hits Leucus, a companion of Odysseus, as he drags the body away (4.489–493; see [[antiphus-son-of-priam]] and [[leucus]]). Odysseus, angry for his companion, strides through the champions and spears Democoon, a bastard son of Priam, through both temples (4.494–504; see [[democoon]]). The Trojan champions and Hector give ground, and the Argives drag away the dead and push far forward (4.505–507).

{{quote:c687b0f3-a0ae-5cfd-8a55-a1bda23e67e2}}

On the other side Athena, “most glorious Tritogeneia”, goes through the throng and rouses the Achaeans wherever she sees them holding back (4.514–516). Then fate fetters Diores, leader of the Epeians. Peirous, leader of the Thracians, crushes his ankle with a jagged stone, and as Diores lies in the dust stretching out his hands to his companions, Peirous runs up and stabs him beside the navel. Thoas the Aetolian spears Peirous in the chest and finishes him with the sword, but cannot strip him, because the Thracians with their topknots and long spears push him back (4.517–535; see [[diores]], [[peirous]] and [[thoas]]). The two leaders lie stretched side by side in the dust, a Thracian and an Epeian, and many others die around them (4.536–538). The book ends with an imagined witness.

## In the poem

**The first death.** The Iliad marks its first killing as first twice over (πρῶτος, 4.457, 4.459). Every man who dies in the book is named, most with a father's name, and each has his wound. The pattern of these scenes, which the battle books will repeat again and again, is set here (see [[androktasia]]). So is its most moving form, the short life told at the moment of death. Simoeisius is its first example: where he was born, why he had his name, what his parents lost (4.473–479; see [[threptra]] and [[death-in-battle]]).

**Chains of killing.** Each death leads to the next. Elephenor dies trying to strip the first dead man, Leucus dies dragging away the second, Democoon dies for Leucus, and Peirous for Diores. Bodies and their armor are what the fighting is about. The struggles over them grow through the battle books until the fight for Patroclus' body fills Book 17.

**Side by side.** The book ends with two leaders lying together, one from each side (4.536–538), and with many Trojans and Achaeans lying “face down in the dust beside one another” (4.543–544). The Achaeans have the better of this first clash. Four of the dead are Trojans or their allies and three are Achaeans, and the Trojans give ground (4.505–507).

**The gods.** Gods frame the battle. Ares and Athena drive the armies at its beginning (4.439), Apollo and Athena in its middle (4.507–516), and at its end the imagined witness is led by Athena's hand and kept from the missiles (4.541–542). Apollo's call names the absent hero: “Achilles, son of Thetis of the lovely hair, is not fighting, but by the ships he broods over his heart-grieving anger” (4.512–513; see [[achilles-withdraws]]). The first battle of the Iliad is fought without Achilles, as the plan of Zeus requires (see [[plan-of-zeus]]).

**The witness.** The poem has twice before in the book imagined someone watching: “you would not have seen brilliant Agamemnon drowsing” (4.223), and “you would not have said that so great an army followed with voices in their breasts” (4.429–430). At the end the witness is put in the middle of the killing. He could not make light of the work even if he walked through it unhurt, led by a goddess (4.539–542). That is the place the poem gives its audience (see [[watching-the-war]]).

The battle does not end with the book. It goes on through Diomedes' great day in Book 5 and into Books 6 and 7.
