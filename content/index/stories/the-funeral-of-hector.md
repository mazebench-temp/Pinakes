## The story

**Eleven days.** When the body has been washed and laid on the wagon, and the two men have eaten, Achilles asks Priam “for how many days are you minded to give brilliant Hector his funeral rites, / so that for so long I may stay here myself and hold back the army?” (24.656–658; see [[achilles]] and [[truce]]). Priam answers that the Trojans are penned in the city, and the wood is far to bring from the mountain:

{{quote:eb9309ab-c7dd-50f2-8091-711604b4bfe6}}

Achilles promises to hold back the war for as long as he asks, and takes the old man's right hand at the wrist, so that he may feel no fear (24.669–672; see [[priam]]).

**The wood.** When Hector has been brought home and mourned (see [[the-laments-for-hector]]), Priam speaks among the people: “Now, Trojans, bring wood into the city, and have no fear / in your hearts of a cunning ambush of the Argives; for Achilles, / as he sent me from the black ships, gave orders / that they would do us no harm until the twelfth dawn comes” (24.777–781; see [[trojans]] and [[ambush]]). They yoke oxen and mules to the wagons and gather before the city, and for nine days they bring in “a vast load of wood” (24.782–784; see [[mules]] and [[cattle]]).

**The pyre.** “but when the tenth dawn appeared, bringing light to mortals, / then they carried out bold Hector, shedding tears, / and on the top of the pyre they laid the body, and set fire to it” (24.785–787; see [[pyres]], [[cremation]] and [[bold]]).

**The bones and the mound.** At the next dawn the people gather around the pyre of famous Hector (24.788–790; see [[dawn-line]]). They put out the fire with wine, and his brothers and companions gather the white bones, weeping; they lay them in a golden chest, covered with soft purple robes, put it in a hollow trench, spread great stones over it, and heap up the mound, with lookouts all around in case the Achaeans attack before the time (24.791–800; see [[tomb]], [[purple]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]).

**The feast.** “And when they had heaped up the mound they went back; and then / they gathered together and feasted well on a glorious feast / in the house of Priam, the king nurtured by Zeus. / So they held the funeral of Hector, tamer of horses” (24.801–804; see [[feast]], [[priams-palace]] and [[tamer-of-horses]]).

## In the poem

**Asked and refused.** The dying Hector begged Achilles to give his body back, “so that when I am dead / the Trojans and the wives of the Trojans may give me my portion of fire” (22.342–343), the verses of his challenge in *Iliad* 7 (7.79–80), and Achilles refused (22.345–354; see [[the-death-of-hector]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). In the last book Apollo reproaches the gods for leaving the corpse to Achilles when his father and the people “would quickly / burn him in the fire and give him funeral rites” (24.37–38; see [[apollo]]). Zeus has the body given back for a ransom, and Achilles himself asks how many days the rites will need (24.656–658; see [[the-ransom-of-hector]] and [[apoina]]).

**The days.** The rites do not keep the order Priam proposed: nine days of lament in the halls, the burial and the feast on the tenth, the tomb on the eleventh (24.664–666). Hector is lamented on the day he comes home (24.723–776); the nine days go to the wood (24.784); he is burned on the tenth (24.785–787); and the bones, the mound and the feast fill the eleventh (24.788–803). The truce runs “until the twelfth dawn comes” (24.781), the day on which, Priam said, “we will fight, if we must” (24.667). Walter Leaf reports that Peppmüller rejected the reason Priam gives, the wood far off on the mountain, holding that nine days of mourning were fixed by custom, and citing Plutarch's statement that Lycurgus limited the days of mourning and the funeral to eleven. Leaf himself reads the lines as a touch of pride, an apology for accepting the offer at all, and adds Virchow's remark that the wood for Hissarlik and the lower Troad must still be brought by horses from the heights of Ida (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.662; see the translation's note on 24.662–663). For Patroclus the Achaeans cut oaks on Ida and brought them down with mules in a single morning (23.110–126), and in the truce of Book 7 both armies gathered wood and burned their dead in one day (7.417–432; see [[the-truce-for-the-dead]]). In the Odyssey Achilles is wept for seventeen days and nights and burned on the eighteenth (*Odyssey* 24.63–65).

**Two funerals.** The burial is told in the verses of the funeral of Patroclus. “and on the top of the pyre they laid the body” (24.787 ≈ 23.165); “first they put out the pyre with sparkling wine, / all of it, as far as the might of the fire had reached” (24.791–792 ≈ 23.237–238, 23.250–251); the white bones gathered by weeping men into a golden vessel and covered (24.793–796 ≈ 23.252–254); and the half-verse that ends the burial, “And when they had heaped up the mound they went back” (24.801 = 23.257). Leaf calls the scene almost an abstract of the funeral of Patroclus (on 24.791), and takes the golden chest, λάρναξ, for the counterpart of Patroclus' golden bowl, φιάλη (on 24.795). The differences are as plain. Patroclus is burned by his companions far from home, with sheep, cattle, horses, dogs and twelve young Trojans on a pyre a hundred feet each way, which will not burn until the winds come (23.161–225); Hector by his own people, with no victims. Patroclus' mound, Leaf observes, is only a cenotaph: his bones are not laid in it, as Hector's are in his own land, but kept in the huts to go with those of Achilles (on 23.254; 23.243–257). Hector's are buried in a trench beneath the stones of his mound (24.795–799). Patroclus' funeral is followed by games, Hector's by a feast (see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]).

**The last verse.** ἀμφίεπον τάφον, “held the funeral” (24.804): τάφος in Homer is the funeral, with its feast, and never the tomb (Leaf on 23.29). Achilles gave the Myrmidons a funeral feast, τάφον … δαίνυ, before the burning of Patroclus (23.29), and the Trojans feast after the burial, as Priam proposed (24.665, 24.802–803). The first verse of the poem ends with the name of Achilles, son of Peleus (1.1); the last ends with Hector, “tamer of horses”.

## Outside Homer

Walter Leaf reports a variant of the last verse in the T scholia, made to join the Iliad to the *Aethiopis*, which followed it in the epic cycle and told of the deaths of Penthesilea, Thersites, Antilochus, [[Memnon]] and Achilles; so too Quintus' continuation of the Iliad opens with the coming of Penthesilea and her Amazons (on 24.804; see [[the-last-line-of-the-iliad]] and the translation's note on 24.804).
