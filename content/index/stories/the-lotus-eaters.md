## The story

**The storm.** When the ships had left the land of the Cicones, Zeus the cloud-gatherer roused the North Wind against them “with a heaven-sent storm”, covered land and sea with clouds, and “night arose from the sky”; the wind tore the sails in three and four pieces, and the men lowered them and rowed for the land, where they lay for two nights and two days, “eating our hearts out with weariness and sorrows together” (*Odyssey* 9.67–75; see [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]], [[zeus]] and [[boreas]]). On the third day they set up the masts and hoisted the sails, and the wind and the helmsmen steered the ships (*Odyssey* 9.76–78).

**Malea.** Odysseus was all but home: “And now I would have reached my native land unharmed; / but as I was rounding Malea, the wave and the current / and the North Wind pushed me back, and drove me off course past Cythera” (*Odyssey* 9.79–81; see [[malea]] and [[cythera]]). For nine days deadly winds carried him over the sea, and on the tenth the ships came to the land of the Lotus-eaters, “who eat a flowery food” (*Odyssey* 9.82–84; see [[lotus-eaters]]).

**The lotus.** The men drew water and ate by the ships, and Odysseus sent two of them and a herald to find out “what men they might be who eat bread upon the earth” (*Odyssey* 9.85–90; see [[spies-and-scouts]] and [[heralds]]).

**The rescue.** Odysseus brought the scouts back to the ships by force, weeping, dragged them under the thwarts and bound them, and ordered the rest on board at once, “for fear that one of them might eat of the lotus and forget his homecoming” (*Odyssey* 9.98–104; see [[lotus]] and [[nostos]]). “From there we sailed on, grieving at heart” (*Odyssey* 9.105; see [[from-there-we-sailed-on]]), to the land of the Cyclopes (see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]).

## In the poem

**Out of the known world.** Malea and Cythera are the last places of the tale that the Greeks knew. The cape is the place of storms on the way home from Troy: there Zeus scattered Menelaus' ships to Crete and to Egypt, and a storm wind carried Agamemnon away (*Odyssey* 3.286–300, 4.514–518; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]); in the false tale that Odysseus tells Penelope, the force of the wind drove him off course at Malea on his way to Troy and brought him to Crete (*Odyssey* 19.186–187, a plain rendering). After nine days of deadly winds the ships come to a people whose land the poem never places (see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).

**The verses of the storm.** The storm is told in the verses of other storms. Zeus covers “land and sea alike” with clouds and “night arose from the sky” in the verses of Poseidon's storm on Odysseus' raft (*Odyssey* 9.68–69, after 5.293–294), and the same two verses return whole, after a verse that ends, as here, with “Zeus the cloud-gatherer”, when Zeus brings on the storm that holds the ships at Thrinacia (*Odyssey* 12.313–315; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). The two nights and two days on shore end with the verse that ended Odysseus' two nights and two days in the sea after the wreck of the raft (*Odyssey* 9.74–76, after 5.388–390). The landing, the meal and the sending of two men and a herald are told again in the same verses at later landfalls of the tale, on Aeolus' island and in the land of the Laestrygonians (*Odyssey* 9.85–90; 10.56–58, 10.100–102; see [[odysseus-and-aeolus]] and [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]]).

**Forgetting the homecoming.** The Lotus-eaters do the companions no harm: they “devised no destruction for our companions” (*Odyssey* 9.92). The danger is forgetting. The scouts no longer wish to return, and Odysseus fears that “one of them might eat of the lotus and forget his homecoming” (*Odyssey* 9.102; see [[nostos]]). Forgetting comes again in Circe's house, where the drugs she mixes into the food are given “so that they might utterly forget their native land” (*Odyssey* 10.236; see [[circe]] and [[odysseus-and-circe]]), and in Circe's warning about the Sirens: whoever hears their voice has no wife and little children to stand beside him on his return home (*Odyssey* 12.41–43). In the summary that Odysseus gives Penelope the adventure has a single verse: he came “to the rich plowland of the Lotus-eater men” (*Odyssey* 23.311, a plain rendering).

## Outside Homer

Later geographers looked for the land on the coast of Libya. For Herodotus' Lotus-eaters, Polybius' reckoning of the nine days' voyage from Malea, and the island of Meninx, where an altar of Odysseus was shown, see [[lotus-eaters]].
