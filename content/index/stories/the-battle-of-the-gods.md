## The story

**The release.** Achilles has returned to the war, and Zeus has Themis call the gods to assembly in his house (20.4–18; see [[themis-the-goddess]], [[assembly]] and [[zeus]]). Since the second day of battle he has kept them out of the fighting (8.5–27); now he lets them go. He himself will stay on Olympus and watch, and the rest may help whichever side they please, for if Achilles fights alone the Trojans will not hold him, and he may sack the wall against fate (20.20–30; see [[watching-the-war]]).

**The two parties.** “So spoke the son of Cronus, and roused relentless war”:

{{quote:56640ffd-b9c1-518c-8714-9b67722470cb}}

Five go to the ships, the gods whom Poseidon had named as his allies against Zeus (15.213–214; see [[poseidon]], [[hera]], [[athena]], [[hermes]] and [[hephaestus]]); six go to the Trojans (see [[ares]], [[apollo]], [[artemis]], [[leto]], [[scamander]] and [[aphrodite]]). While the gods are still apart from men, the Achaeans exult and the Trojans tremble at the sight of Achilles (20.41–46). When the Olympians come into the throng, Strife rises; Athena shouts by the ditch and on the shore, and Ares answers like a dark storm from the height of the city and along the Simoeis on Callicolone (20.47–53; see [[strife]] and [[callicolone]]).

**The earthquake.** The gods set the armies on, and the world shakes:

{{quote:97106a6c-0f13-520e-8940-87a3efb12a97}}

Zeus thunders from the sky and Poseidon shakes the earth; the third brother, the lord of the dead, leaps from his throne in fear that his house will be laid bare (see [[hades]] and [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]).

**The pairs.** Then each god is set against another:

{{quote:8727b3b9-5607-5a61-9b40-9da7b118eafe}}

“So gods went to meet gods” (20.75).

Ares is named here as Enyalius, and the river by both its names (see [[enyalius]] and [[language-of-gods-and-men]]). Aphrodite, who went with the Trojans, has no opponent; in Book 21 Athena strikes her down when she leads the fallen Ares away (21.416–426).

**Sitting apart.** The battle is put off. Apollo sends Aeneas against Achilles, and Hera asks Poseidon and Athena whether to turn him back or to stand by Achilles (20.79–131; see [[aeneas]]). Poseidon will not have the gods drive one another into strife, since the Achaeans' gods are far the stronger: let them sit apart and leave the war to men, unless Ares or Apollo begins it (20.133–143). He leads his party to the wall of Heracles, where they sit wrapped in an unbreakable cloud, and the gods of the Trojans sit on the brows of Callicolone around Apollo and Ares; both sides are loath to begin harsh war, “though Zeus, sitting on high, bade them” (20.144–155; see [[the-wall-of-heracles]]). Poseidon leaves his place only to rescue Aeneas (20.291–340), and Apollo his to warn and then to rescue Hector (20.375–380, 20.443–444); Athena turns Hector's spear back from Achilles with a breath (20.438–440).

**In Book 21.** The gods fight when Achilles has filled the river with the dead. Hera sets Hephaestus against the river, and his fire burns the plain and the stream until the river yields and swears never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans (21.328–382). Then strife falls on the other gods, the earth roars and the great sky trumpets, and Zeus, sitting on Olympus, laughs for joy to see the gods come together in strife (21.385–390). Ares strikes Athena's aegis, and she fells him with a boundary stone; when Aphrodite leads him away, Athena strikes her down too (21.391–433). Poseidon bids Apollo begin, and reminds him of their service to Laomedon; Apollo will not fight his father's brother for the sake of mortals, who are like leaves (21.435–469). Artemis taunts her brother, and Hera seizes her by the wrists and beats her with her own bow (21.470–496). Hermes will not fight Leto, since it is hard to come to blows with the wives of Zeus, and Leto gathers up her daughter's bow and arrows (21.497–504). Artemis runs weeping to her father; Apollo goes into Ilios, concerned for its wall; the other gods go back to Olympus, some angry, some exulting, and sit down beside Zeus (21.505–520).

## In the poem

**Why now.** The gods come back into the war with Achilles. Since Book 8 Zeus had kept them out, and Poseidon had helped the Achaeans in secret and withdrawn under orders (13.10–38, 15.158–219). Zeus' reason for releasing them is the balance of the war: alone, Achilles might take Troy before its time (20.26–30), and Apollo, at the end of the gods' battle, goes into the city for the same fear (21.515–517). Walter Leaf objects that Zeus seems to forget that Achilles stands to gain from the gods' intervention, since the gods of his side are the stronger (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.26).

**A battle postponed.** The book announces a battle among the gods with the shout, the thunder, the earthquake and the pairs, and then gives none: the gods sit and watch. Leaf, who thought the opening of Book 20 really belonged to the battle at the end of Book 21, explains by it Poseidon's words, that the men's “battle and war have blazed up at very close quarters”, strange at the one pause in the fighting since Book 11 (20.18; on 20.18), and he reports a proposal to remove 66–74, so that the fight of the gods should be kept for Book 21; but line 75, “So gods went to meet gods”, prevents it (on 20.55). He takes Hera's words “We have all come down from Olympus” (20.125) to mean only the gods of the Achaean side, and thinks this part of the book independent of 1–74 in origin (on 20.114, 20.125).

**The pairs.** Leaf sees no principle in the pairing except Hephaestus and the river, fire against water (on 20.67). The pairs are partly those of the earlier books: Athena, who drove Diomedes' spear into Ares (5.855–859), is set against him, and the two of them roused the two armies when they first met (4.439–440; see [[strife]]). Poseidon and Apollo, who built Troy's wall together for Laomedon (7.452–453), are set against each other, and in Book 21 they do not fight.

**The gods' laughter.** Zeus watches “where I shall look on and delight my heart” (20.23), and in Book 21 he laughs (21.389–390); Athena laughs over the fallen Ares (21.408), and Hera smiles as she beats Artemis (21.491). The gods' strife, which shakes the earth and frightens the lord of the dead, ends in bruises and tears, while the men they set on die on the plain (see [[gods-and-mortals]] and [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]]).

## Outside Homer

**Hesiod.** The battles of Hesiod's gods shake the world in the same terms. When the Olympians fight the Titans, the boundless sea resounds, the earth crashes, the wide sky groans as it is shaken, high Olympus is shaken from its base, and the quaking reaches misty Tartarus (*Theogony* 678–683); when Zeus joins the fight, it is as if earth and sky were coming together: τόσσος δοῦπος ἔγεντο θεῶν ἔριδι ξυνιόντων, “so great a din arose as the gods came together in strife”, a verse that ends as 20.66 ends (*Theogony* 702–705; see [[titans]]). When Zeus fights Typhoeus, the earth, the sky and the sea boil, an unquenchable quaking arises, “and Hades trembled, lord of the dead below”, and the Titans under Tartarus with Cronus (*Theogony* 847–852). The half-verse of Hades' dank house, “which even the gods hate” (20.65), is Hesiod's for the sources and limits of earth, Tartarus, sea and sky (*Theogony* 739 = 810; see [[hesiod]]).

**Allegory and censure.** Leaf reports from the scholia an allegorical reading of the pairs, in which, for instance, Athena is wisdom and Aphrodite desire, said by Porphyry to be very old and to go back to Theagenes of Rhegium, the first to write on Homer (on 20.67). Plato's Socrates would not admit into his city “the battles of the gods that Homer composed”, whether they were composed with hidden meanings or without (θεομαχίας ὅσας Ὅμηρος πεποίηκεν, *Republic* 2.378d; see [[plato-hephaestus-hurled]] and [[plato]]), and he strikes out Hades' fear for his dwellings among the verses that make death fearful (*Republic* 3.386d; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]). The critic of *On the Sublime* quotes Hades' terror (20.61–65) as an example of grandeur, and then calls the battle impious unless it is read as allegory (*On the Sublime* 9.6–7; see [[longinus-wounded-gods]] and [[longinus-poseidons-advance]]). Proclus, answering Plato, reads the assembly through Themis and the pairs of gods as figures of the order of the world (see [[proclus-on-the-battle-of-the-gods]]).

**Virgil.** When Hercules tears the rock from the cave of Cacus, the cave lies open as if the earth, gaping deep under some force, were to unlock the seats below and lay bare the pale realms, hateful to the gods, and the shades were to tremble at the light let in (*Aeneid* 8.241–246; see [[virgil]]). Leaf compares it with the fear of Aidoneus (on 20.64).

The title Θεομαχία, “The Battle of the Gods”, is given to the twentieth book (see [[title-of-book-20]]).
