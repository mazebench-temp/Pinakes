## The story

As Xerxes marched on Greece, the Athenian envoys at Delphi received two oracles. The first told them to flee to the ends of the earth. The second, which they begged for as suppliants, said that Athena could not move Olympian Zeus, but that “Zeus grants to Athena a wooden wall” which alone would not be taken, and ended: “divine Salamis, you will destroy the children of women.” The professional interpreters of oracles took the wall to be the thorn hedge that had once fenced the Acropolis, or advised leaving Attica for good, and read Salamis as the place of a defeat. Themistocles argued that the wall was the fleet, and that if the oracle had meant the Athenians to perish there it would have said “cruel Salamis”, not “divine” (Herodotus 7.140–143; see [[themistocles]]). The Athenians evacuated the city, and the fleet won at Salamis (see [[salamis-and-himera]]).

## In Aristotle

The *Rhetoric* counts interpreters of oracles as witnesses about the future, and Themistocles' reading as an example (1.15, 1376a1–2).
