## The story

**From Troy to Sunium.** After the quarrel of the sons of Atreus, Menelaus came “late, after us two”, Nestor and Diomedes, and overtook them at Lesbos (*Odyssey* 3.168–169; see [[the-departure-of-the-greeks]]). He and Nestor sailed on together, “with friendly hearts toward one another”, until at holy Sunium, the cape of Athens, Phoebus Apollo killed his helmsman, Phrontis, son of Onetor, with his gentle arrows, and Menelaus stayed behind to bury him (*Odyssey* 3.276–285; see [[phrontis-son-of-onetor]], [[apollo]] and [[sunium]]).

**Malea.** When he in turn reached the steep mountain of Malea, Zeus poured the breath of shrill winds on him and split the fleet. Some of the ships he drove to Crete, where the Cydonians lived; the waves smashed them on the reefs at the far edge of Gortyn, toward Phaestus, and the men barely escaped. The five dark-prowed ships the wind and the water carried to Egypt (*Odyssey* 3.286–300; see [[malea]], [[crete]], [[gortyn]], [[phaestus]] and [[egypt]]).

**Among men of another tongue.** There he wandered with his ships, gathering much substance and gold, while Aegisthus killed his brother and ruled Mycenae for seven years; he came home on the day Orestes gave the funeral feast for Aegisthus, bringing as many possessions as his ships could carry (*Odyssey* 3.301–312; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). Menelaus tells Telemachus the same in the next book: after much suffering and much wandering he brought his wealth home in his ships and came in the eighth year, having wandered to Cyprus and Phoenicia and the Egyptians, and reached the Ethiopians, the Sidonians, the Erembi and Libya; and while he wandered there, gathering much substance, another man killed his brother by stealth (*Odyssey* 4.81–92; see [[cyprus]], [[sidon]], [[ethiopians]] and [[libya]]). Helen was with him in Egypt: Polybus of Egyptian Thebes gave Menelaus silver baths, tripods and gold, his wife Alcandre gave Helen gifts of her own, and Helen had her drugs from Polydamna, wife of Thon, an Egyptian (*Odyssey* 4.125–132, 4.227–232; see [[helen]]).

**The gods hold him back.** The gods kept him in Egypt, eager though he was to come home, because he had not offered them perfect hecatombs (*Odyssey* 4.351–352). For twenty days no wind blew on the island of Pharos, until Proteus, the old man of the sea, told him that he would not see his home until he had gone back to the water of the river Aegyptus and offered sacred hecatombs to the gods (*Odyssey* 4.354–362, 4.472–480). He did so, heaped a mound for Agamemnon, and the gods gave him a fair wind home (*Odyssey* 4.581–586; see [[hecatomb]]). The renderings of the fourth book are plain renderings, not the translation.

## In the poem

The wanderings explain the murder. Telemachus asks “Where was Menelaus?”, and whether he was away from Argos so that Aegisthus took heart (*Odyssey* 3.249–252), and Nestor answers that he was far away, and that “meanwhile” Aegisthus did his work (*Odyssey* 3.303; see [[aegisthus]]). The story of a king who wanders abroad gathering wealth while his house is ruined at home is also the story of Odysseus, and Nestor draws the lesson at once: Telemachus is not to wander long far from home while the suitors devour his possessions (*Odyssey* 3.313–316; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). Then he sends him to Menelaus, who “has lately come home from abroad”, from a sea so great that not even the birds come back from it within the year (*Odyssey* 3.317–328; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).

## Outside Homer

Strabo calls the episode “the wanderings of Menelaus” (ἡ Μενελάου πλάνη) and sets out the questions that Menelaus' list of peoples raised: which Ethiopians he could have reached by sailing from Egypt, who the Sidonians were, and who the Erembi, a new name. Aristonicus, a grammarian of Strabo's own time, had collected the views of many men on each point in a work on the wanderings of Menelaus. Some made him sail round by Gades as far as India, as Crates did, to account for the eight years; others took him through the isthmus by the Arabian Gulf, or through a canal. Strabo finds the long voyage needless: Menelaus was held back against his will by bad sailing, since only five of his sixty ships were left to him, and of his own will for the sake of gain, for Nestor says that he wandered “gathering much substance and gold” (*Geography* 1.2.31; see [[strabo]]).

Herodotus heard from the Egyptian priests that Menelaus came to Memphis after the fall of Troy, was received with great hospitality, and got back Helen unharmed with all his possessions. Then he wronged the Egyptians: when contrary weather kept him from sailing, he took two children of the country and made them victims of sacrifice, and when the deed was known he fled with his ships toward Libya; where he went from there, the Egyptians could not say (Herodotus 2.119; see [[helen-in-egypt]] and [[herodotus]]). Among the verses by which Herodotus shows that Homer knew the Egyptian story of Paris and Helen are Menelaus' words to Telemachus about the gods who held him in Egypt (Herodotus 2.116, quoting *Odyssey* 4.351–352).

In Euripides' *Helen* Menelaus, shipwrecked in Egypt, will not tell his wife all his sufferings, the disasters in the Aegean, the beacon fires of Nauplius off Euboea, Crete and the cities of Libya that he visited, and the watchtowers of Perseus; asked only how long he wandered on the sea, he answers that besides the ten years at Troy he passed seven rounds of years in his ships (*Helen* 765–776; see [[euripides]]).
