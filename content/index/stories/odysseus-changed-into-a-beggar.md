## The story

**The plan.** When the gifts are hidden in the cave, Odysseus and Athena sit down beside the trunk of the sacred olive and plan destruction for the overbearing suitors (*Odyssey* 13.372–373; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]], [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]] and [[olive]]). For three years, she tells him, they have been lording it in his hall, wooing his wife and offering bride-price, while she gives hope to all and her mind has other things in view (*Odyssey* 13.374–381; see [[the-suitors]] and [[penelope]]). Odysseus sees that he would have died in his halls like Agamemnon, had she not told him each thing; he asks her to weave a plan and stand beside him with the daring she gave him when they loosed the shining veil of Troy, and with her he would fight three hundred men (*Odyssey* 13.382–391; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]] and [[the-fall-of-troy]]). She will be beside him, she answers, and one or another of the suitors will spatter the boundless floor with blood and brains (*Odyssey* 13.392–396).

**The change foretold.** “But come, I will make you unknown to all mortals”: she will wither the fair skin on his limbs, destroy his fair hair, put a [[rags|rag]] about him that anyone would hate to see on a man, and dim his eyes, so that he may seem unsightly to all the suitors and to his own wife and son (*Odyssey* 13.397–403). He is to go first to the swineherd, who is loyal to him, and stay with him by the Rock of Corax and the spring Arethusa, while she goes to Sparta to call Telemachus home (*Odyssey* 13.404–415; see [[eumaeus]], [[the-rock-of-corax]], [[arethusa]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). Odysseus asks why she, who knows all things, did not tell his son; she escorted him there herself, she says, so that he might win noble fame, and though the suitors lie in wait for him, the earth will hold some of them first (*Odyssey* 13.416–428).

## In the poem

**Unknown to all.** The change is the end toward which the goddess has worked since Odysseus landed. She poured the mist around him while he slept “so that she might make him / unknown, and tell him each thing” (*Odyssey* 13.190–191), and D. B. Monro takes ἄγνωστον τεύξειεν there to mean the same process as ἄγνωστον τεύξω, “I will make you unknown”, here: the talk comes first and the change after it, and without both Odysseus would have gone straight to his palace “and all would have been lost” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 13.191). She has already told him to reveal himself to no one, “man or woman”, and to endure in silence (*Odyssey* 13.307–310), and Agamemnon in the land of the dead advised him to bring his ship to shore secretly, not openly (*Odyssey* 11.454–456; see [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]). Halitherses foretold that he would come home ἄγνωστον πάντεσσιν, “unknown to all” (*Odyssey* 2.175; see [[the-prophecy-of-halitherses]]).

**The beggar at Troy.** Odysseus has been a beggar once before. Helen tells how at Troy he marred himself with shameful blows, threw wretched [[rags|rags]] about his shoulders and slipped into the city “like a servant”, made like another man, “a beggar”, and only she knew him (*Odyssey* 4.244–250; see [[odysseus-enters-troy-as-a-beggar]]). At Troy he disguised himself; on Ithaca a goddess changes him, and his own wife and son are not to know him (*Odyssey* 13.402–403).

**Changed and changed back.** Athena has changed his looks before, making him taller and stouter and pouring grace on him for Nausicaa and the Phaeacians (*Odyssey* 6.229–235, 8.18–20; see [[shape-changing]]). The change of the thirteenth book is undone and done again: with her golden wand she gives him back his youth so that his son may know him, and with her wand makes him old again before the swineherd returns (*Odyssey* 16.172–176, 16.454–459, a plain rendering), and before Penelope knows him she pours beauty over him in the verses of the sixth book (*Odyssey* 23.156–162, after 6.230–235, a plain rendering). The pouch and its twisted cord come back in the same verses when the beggar sets out for the city and when he has beaten Irus (*Odyssey* 17.197–198, 18.108–109, a plain rendering; see [[pouch]], [[scepter]] and [[clothing]]). The hair she withers is ξανθάς, “fair” (*Odyssey* 13.399, 13.431), though elsewhere his beard is dark and his hair falls like the hyacinth (*Odyssey* 16.176, 6.231; Monro on *Odyssey* 13.431; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 6.231).

## Outside Homer

**Magic or disguise.** Monro counts the changes of form that Odysseus undergoes at Athena's hands among the incidents of the fairy-tale order in the Odyssey, and of this one he says, “as Kirchhoff has rightly insisted, the change wrought is a magical one,—not a mere illusion”. Kirchhoff, as Monro reports him, held that the two halves of the poem show two different men, the Odysseus of the wanderings still in the prime of life and the Odysseus of Ithaca marked by age and hardship, and that the transformation was inserted to join two poems, since in the later recognitions he is disguised only by his [[rags|rags]] (*Odyssey* 19.467, 21.188, 23.95, 23.115). Monro answers that the hero was not so changed by his wanderings that he ran no risk of being known, so that the transformation, or some equivalent means of concealment, was a poetical necessity; the inconsistency is “an example of the practical difficulty of dealing with supernatural machinery in a logical and consistent way” (Monro, Appendix I §6 and note). He adds that in the *Philoctetes* of Euripides, Odysseus' opening speech told how Athena had promised to change him so that Philoctetes would not know him (Monro, Appendix I §6, note; see [[euripides]]).

**Without the goddess.** Hyginus' summary of the Odyssey has Odysseus come home unknown to men, see the suitors besieging his palace, and pretend to be a guest, with no word of a change of form (Hyginus, *Fabulae* 125). Aristotle asked why Odysseus kept himself hidden from Penelope, though he made himself known to his young son and to the herdsmen: he told those who were to share the danger, one at a time and privately; or he feared that Penelope, used only to weeping, would be so glad that her joy would betray him (*Homeric Problems* fr. 399 Gigon; see [[aristotle]] and [[penelope]]).
