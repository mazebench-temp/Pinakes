## The story

**The two ways.** When the companions had rowed past the Sirens, Circe told Odysseus, he must choose his way for himself (*Odyssey* 12.55–58; see [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]] and [[circe]]). On one side are the overhanging rocks that the blessed gods call the Wanderers, which not even the doves that carry ambrosia to Zeus pass unharmed, and which no ship has passed but the Argo, sent through by Hera for Jason's sake (*Odyssey* 12.59–72; see [[the-wanderers]] and [[the-argo]]). On the other are two crags. In a misty cave high in the first, beyond an arrow's reach, lives Scylla, yelping, with twelve dangling feet and six long necks, a head on each with three rows of teeth, who takes a man with each head from every ship that passes (*Odyssey* 12.73–100; see [[scylla]]). Under the lower crag, beneath a great wild fig tree, Charybdis sucks down the black water three times a day and sends it up three times, and not even the earth-shaker could save a man from her (*Odyssey* 12.101–107; see [[charybdis]]). He is to keep close to Scylla's crag and row past quickly, “since it is far better / to miss six companions in your ship than all of them together” (*Odyssey* 12.108–110). When he asks whether he might escape Charybdis and fight off Scylla, Circe calls him a hard man, always bent on war: Scylla is an immortal evil, not to be fought, and if he lingers to arm himself she will take as many men again; he must row with all his force and call on her mother Crataeis (*Odyssey* 12.111–126; see [[crataeis]]).

**The strait.** Leaving the Sirens' island, the men see smoke and a great wave and hear a roar, and the oars fly from their hands. Odysseus goes through the ship with soothing words, reminding them of the Cyclops' cave, from which his counsel brought them out; he bids them row, and the helmsman keep the ship away from the smoke and the wave and make for the crag. Of Scylla he says nothing, for fear that they will stop rowing and huddle together inside the ship (*Odyssey* 12.201–225; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). Forgetting Circe's command, he puts on his armor, takes two spears and goes onto the deck at the prow to watch for Scylla, but cannot see her anywhere (*Odyssey* 12.226–233). They sail up the narrow strait lamenting, with Charybdis sucking down and vomiting up the sea on the other side, and while they look at her in fear, Scylla seizes six of the strongest from the ship (*Odyssey* 12.234–246):

After the wreck the South Wind carried Odysseus back to the strait alone, and he passed Charybdis a second time, clinging to the fig tree (*Odyssey* 12.426–446; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]).

## In the poem

**The price.** Circe names the price in advance, six companions rather than all (*Odyssey* 12.109–110), and Scylla takes exactly six, “who were the strongest with their hands and in strength” (*Odyssey* 12.246), one for each head (*Odyssey* 12.99–100). At supper on Thrinacia the others weep for them (*Odyssey* 12.309–310). The tale has counted its losses in sixes before: six men from every ship at Ismarus, and six eaten in the Cyclops' cave, two at a time (*Odyssey* 9.60–61; 9.289, 9.311, 9.344; see [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]]).

**A hard man.** When Odysseus asks to fight, Circe calls him σχέτλιε: “Hard man, once again your mind is on the works of war / and toil” (*Odyssey* 12.116–117). He arms all the same, “since she had told me not to arm myself” (*Odyssey* 12.226–227), like a warrior of the Iliad with his two spears, and Scylla never shows herself to him. Merry explains the “grievous” command as one hard for a fighting man, who chafes at passive endurance (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.226). What the hero can do is speak: he keeps the men at their oars with the memory of the Cyclops, and with silence about Scylla, knowing from Circe that he must lose at least six (*Odyssey* 12.208–225; Merry on 12.223).

**The most pitiable.** “That was the most pitiable thing I have seen with my own eyes / of all that I have toiled through, searching out the paths of the sea” (*Odyssey* 12.258–259). Eurylochus will use the same word, οἴκτιστον, for death by hunger, when he persuades the companions to kill the Sun's cattle (*Odyssey* 12.342). In the summary that Odysseus gives Penelope he tells how he came “to the Wandering Rocks and dread Charybdis / and Scylla, whom men never yet escaped unharmed” (*Odyssey* 23.327–328, a plain rendering), with Circe's word ἀκήριοι, “unharmed” (*Odyssey* 12.98; Merry on 12.98).

## Outside Homer

**Timotheus.** The dithyramb *Scylla*, generally ascribed to Timotheus, gave Odysseus a lament for his companions, which Aristotle names as an example of character that is unseemly and inappropriate (*Poetics* 1454a30–31); bad pipers, he says, drag the chorus leader about when they play the *Scylla* (*Poetics* 1461b31–32; see [[scylla-of-timotheus]]).

**Euripides and Virgil.** In the *Trojan Women* Cassandra, foretelling the wanderings of Odysseus, names first “dread Charybdis”, who has made her dwelling in the narrow channel of rock (*Trojan Women* 435–436; see [[euripides]]). Virgil gives Aeneas the speech of the strait, after the storm off Libya: “O socii—neque enim ignari sumus ante malorum”, “O companions, for we are not without knowledge of evils before”; the Trojans too have come near Scylla's rage and the rocks of the Cyclops, and “forsan et haec olim meminisse iuvabit”, “perhaps one day it will please you to remember even these things” (*Aeneid* 1.198–203; Merry on 12.208 and 12.212). In Homer, as Merry observes after Ameis, “these things” are the dangers still ahead; in Virgil, the sufferings already endured (on 12.212; see [[virgil]]). For the strait and its two dangers in later writers, see [[scylla-and-charybdis-in-later-writers]].

For the strait of Messina, where the ancients placed Scylla and Charybdis, and the later shapes of the monsters, see [[scylla]] and [[charybdis]].
