## The story

The armies meet on the plain, and Alexander steps out as champion for the Trojans, with a leopard's skin, a bow, a sword and two spears, and challenges all the best of the Argives (3.15–20). Menelaus sees him and rejoices like a hungry lion that has found a carcass, and leaps from his chariot; Alexander sees Menelaus and slips back into the ranks like a man who has met a serpent (3.21–37; see [[lion-on-a-carcass]] and [[man-who-meets-a-serpent]]). Hector shames him, and Alexander, granting every word, makes an offer.

{{quote:549e51bd-aeaa-581d-be8d-f215bb35e7f1}}

Hector stops the Trojan ranks, and Agamemnon stops the Achaean archers, and Hector announces the offer between the armies (3.76–94). Menelaus accepts. The two peoples have suffered enough “for the sake of my quarrel, and because Alexander began it”, and whichever of the two is fated to die, let him die (3.97–102). He asks for lambs for the oath and for Priam himself to swear it (3.103–110). Both armies rejoice, hoping for an end to the war, and take off their armor (3.111–115). While the heralds go for the victims, Helen is called to the wall, and the old men of Troy watch her come (see [[the-view-from-the-wall]]). Priam drives out, the oaths are sworn over the lambs, and he goes back, unable to watch his son fight (3.245–313; see [[the-oaths-sworn]]).

Hector and Odysseus measure out the ground and shake the lots in a helmet; the lot of Paris leaps out, and he is to throw first (3.314–325; see [[lots]]). He arms, and Menelaus arms in the same way (3.328–339; see [[arming-scene]]). They walk into the middle, glaring terribly, and amazement holds the watchers (3.340–345).

{{quote:9a404170-a225-56b1-af8f-765d65b0e2f3}}

Alexander's spear is turned by Menelaus' shield. Menelaus prays to Zeus for vengeance on the man who wronged his host (3.351–354), and his spear goes through Alexander's shield and corselet and cuts his tunic, but Alexander swerves and avoids black doom. Menelaus strikes his helmet with his sword, and the sword shatters. He cries out that no god is more baneful than Zeus, seizes Alexander by the crest, and drags him toward the Achaeans, choking him with the strap of his own helmet (3.361–372). He would have won untold glory, but Aphrodite breaks the strap, and when Menelaus comes on again with his spear, she snatches Alexander away in a mist and sets him down in his chamber (3.373–382; see [[aphrodite-rescues-paris]]). Menelaus ranges the throng like a wild beast, looking for him, and no Trojan will hide him (3.448–454; see [[like-a-wild-beast]]). Agamemnon declares the victory his brother's and demands Helen, the possessions and the satisfaction the oath promised (3.455–461).

## In the poem

**The war in small.** The duel is fought in the tenth year of the war, and it asks the question of the first: should the whole of two peoples suffer for the quarrel of two men? The stakes are those of the war itself, “Helen and all her possessions” (Ἑλένῃ καὶ κτήμασι πᾶσι, 3.70, 3.91), and Menelaus names the cause, “my quarrel, and because Alexander began it” (3.100). The duel is framed as a contract, with oaths, sacrifices and terms (see [[oath]] and [[single-combat]]). Together with the view from the wall, where Helen names the Achaean leaders, and the breaking of the truce in Book 4, it retells the beginning of the war at the start of the poem. See [[the-war-in-small]] and [[war-for-menelaus]].

**The gods.** The duel is fought in the open, before both armies and under the eyes of gods called as witnesses, and it is decided by a god who hides the loser in mist. Menelaus blames Zeus for the broken sword (3.365). Paris will say that Menelaus won “with Athena's help” (3.439), although no god has been seen helping him. In the next book Zeus, among the gods, judges that the victory belongs to Menelaus, and asks whether the war should go on or friendship be made between the two sides (4.13–19). Hera and Athena will not have peace, and Athena persuades the Trojan Pandarus to shoot Menelaus during the truce (4.20–126). The duel settles nothing.

**Winning without killing.** The terms of the oath were that one man should kill the other (3.281–285). Neither dies. Agamemnon claims the victory all the same (νίκη … φαίνετʼ ἀρηϊφίλου Μενελάου, 3.457), and the poem gives no Trojan answer before the truce is broken.

**Duels to come.** The Iliad has one more duel arranged between the armies. In Book 7 Hector challenges the best of the Achaeans, and Ajax fights him until the heralds part them at nightfall; lines of the spear-casting are repeated from Book 3 (7.249–254 ~ 3.355–360). The last and greatest single combat, of Achilles and Hector, has no truce, no oath (Achilles refuses one: there are no “trusty oaths between lions and men”, 22.261–264), and no rescue. Apollo leaves Hector when the scales of Zeus sink (22.208–213).

## Outside Homer

In Proclus' summary of the *Little Iliad*, Paris dies in another single combat, killed by Philoctetes, and Menelaus outrages his corpse. Virgil rebuilt the duel in the last book of the *Aeneid*: Turnus offers to fight Aeneas alone for Lavinia, a treaty is sworn with the Sun and the Earth as witnesses, and the truce is broken before the champions meet (see [[virgil-duel-for-lavinia]]). Marlowe's Faustus imagines himself as Paris, ready to fight “weak Menelaus” for Helen (see [[marlowe-helen]]).
