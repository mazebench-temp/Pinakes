## The story

Teiresias foretold that death would come to Odysseus ἐξ ἁλός, “a death so very gentle”, when he was worn out by “an old age of ease”, with his people prosperous around him (*Odyssey* 11.134–137). The translation, with most interpreters, takes ἐξ ἁλός as “away from the sea”; W. W. Merry argues for “out of the sea”, and thinks the verses written to fit the story of Telegonus (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.134; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]). The Telegony told how it came. Telegonus, the son born to Circe after Odysseus left her, sailed in search of his father, landed on Ithaca and began to plunder it; Odysseus came out to defend his land and was killed by his son, who did not know him. Recognizing his error, Telegonus carried his father's body, with Penelope and Telemachus, to his mother; Circe made them immortal, and Telegonus married Penelope and Telemachus Circe (Proclus, summary of the *Telegony*). His spear was tipped with the spine of a stingray, so that death did come out of the sea (Apollodorus, *Epitome* 7.36; see [[telegonus]] and [[circe]]). Aeschylus gave the prophecy another form: in his *Soul-Summoners* someone is told that a heron flying overhead will strike him with its dung, and that from it the spine of a creature of the sea will rot his aged skin (fr. 275 Nauck; Merry on 11.134; see [[aeschylus]]). Sophocles told the story in a tragedy known as *Odysseus Struck by the Spine*, probably the play Aristotle calls *Odysseus Wounded* (see [[odysseus-wounded]]).
