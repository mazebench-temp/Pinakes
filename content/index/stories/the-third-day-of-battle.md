## The story

**Dawn.** The day begins with the two verses that begin the fifth book of the Odyssey: Dawn rises from beside Tithonus to bring light to immortals and mortals (11.1–2 = *Odyssey* 5.1–2; see [[tithonus]] and [[eos]]). Zeus sends Strife to the ships. She shouts from Odysseus' ship in the middle of the line, and war becomes sweeter to the Achaeans than going home (11.3–14; 11.13–14 = 2.453–454; see [[strife]]).

**Agamemnon's morning.** Agamemnon arms and the army goes out on foot ahead of the chariots. Through the morning the two lines cut each other down like reapers; at the hour when a woodcutter makes ready his meal, a time of day and not a simile, the Danaans break through, and Agamemnon drives the Trojans past the tomb of Ilus to the Scaean gates (11.15–180; see [[the-aristeia-of-agamemnon]] and [[the-reapers]]).

**Zeus' promise.** Then Zeus sits down on Ida with the lightning in his hands and sends Iris to Hector:

Iris gives the message word for word (11.200–209; see [[message-repeated]]).

**The wounded.** Coon's spear drives Agamemnon from the field, and Hector charges (11.248–309). Diomedes and Odysseus make a stand until Paris shoots Diomedes through the foot (11.310–400); Odysseus is wounded by Socus and rescued by Ajax and Menelaus (11.401–488); Paris hits Machaon and Eurypylus, and Zeus puts Ajax to flight (11.505–595). All who were the best, Eurypylus says, lie among the ships, shot or stabbed (11.825–826 = 16.23–24; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). Achilles, watching from his ship, sends Patroclus to Nestor, “and this was the beginning of evil for him” (11.599–604), and Nestor gives Patroclus the plan that will send him out in Achilles' armor (11.794–803).

**The wall and the ships.** On Polydamas' advice the Trojans leave their chariots at the ditch (12.60–80; see [[polydamas]]). Sarpedon tears down a battlement, Hector breaks the gate with a stone, and the Trojans pour over the wall (12.397–471; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). While Zeus looks away, Poseidon rallies the Achaeans (13.1–45). The wounded kings meet Nestor (14.27–134); Hera lulls Zeus to sleep on Gargarus, and Ajax fells Hector with a stone (14.153–439). Zeus wakes, sets out the course of the war to Hera (15.4–77), and sends Apollo to restore Hector, who lays hold of the stern of Protesilaus' ship and calls for fire (15.236–262, 15.704–725).

**Patroclus.** Patroclus has stayed to tend Eurypylus (12.1–2, 15.390–405). He puts Nestor's plan to Achilles (16.36–45), goes out in his armor when the first ship burns, kills Sarpedon, and is killed by Apollo, Euphorbus and Hector (16.122–867; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). The fight for his body fills Book 17.

**Sunset.** Achilles, unarmed, shouts three times from the ditch, the Trojans fall back, and the Achaeans draw the body out of range (18.203–238). Then Hera sends the unwilling sun down to the streams of Ocean, and the Achaeans rest from the fighting (18.239–242). In the Trojan assembly that follows, Hector rejects Polydamas' advice to go back into the city (18.243–313).

## In the poem

**The term.** Zeus' promise gives the day its shape, power to kill “until he comes to the well-benched ships, and the sun goes down and sacred darkness comes on” (11.193–194). Both parts come true, and both come late. Hector reaches the ships only in Book 15, Zeus repeats the promise, of the Trojans, in the fight over Patroclus' body (17.453–455), and the sun goes down “unwilling” (ἀέκοντα, 18.240), sent by Hera. This is the day that Achilles asked for through Thetis, the Achaeans penned in among the sterns of their ships and slaughtered (1.408–412), and the day Zeus foretold at the end of the second day of battle: Hector would not stop until Achilles was roused beside the ships, over Patroclus dead (8.470–477; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[the-second-day-of-battle]]).

**Hector's day.** On the evening of the second day Hector boasted that at sunrise Diomedes would lie wounded among the foremost (8.535–538). Diomedes does leave the field wounded, but by Paris' arrow, after his own spear has knocked Hector senseless (11.349–400). The day's success is Zeus' gift, and it has Zeus' limit. At nightfall Hector refuses Polydamas' advice to withdraw, and in Book 22, waiting for Achilles, he remembers it (18.284–309, 22.99–103; see [[hector]]).

**The hours.** The poem marks the hours of this day only a few times: morning (11.84–85 = 8.66–67), the woodcutter's meal (11.86–90), the sun astride mid-heaven and then turning toward the hour of unyoking oxen (16.777–780), and sunset (18.239–241). They do not keep strict time, for the woodcutter's meal already marks the middle of the day, and the sun still stands at mid-heaven in Book 16. The day is measured by Zeus' promise more than by the clock.

**The count of days.** In this index's count the day is Day 26, after the second day of battle and the night of Books 9 and 10 (see [[the-doloneia]]). The next day, on which Achilles returns, dawns at 19.1–2, and its second verse is this day's (19.2 = 11.2).
