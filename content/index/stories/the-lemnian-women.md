## The story

**What Homer tells.** When the Achaean wall is finished, ships come in from Lemnos with wine:

{{quote:9e295d6b-7ab0-5acc-81d5-db760bb71c25}}

Elsewhere the Iliad names Jason only in his son's titles, “son of Jason” (21.41, 23.747), and Hypsipyle not at all (see [[jason]], [[hypsipyle]] and [[euneus]]). It knows a king of Lemnos, “godlike Thoas” (14.230), and a silver mixing bowl, Sidonian work, that Phoenician traders gave to Thoas and that Euneus later gave Patroclus as the price of the captive Lycaon (23.740–747; 21.40–41). The Odyssey knows the ship: “the Argo, known to all, sailing from Aeetes”, which Hera brought safely past the Wandering Rocks, “since Jason was dear to her” (*Odyssey* 12.69–72). Homer does not say why Jason came to Lemnos, or where the island's men were when he came. He tells no crime.

**The later story.** The women of Lemnos had neglected the rites of Aphrodite, and she punished them; their husbands turned from them to captive women from Thrace. The women killed all the men of the island, and only Hypsipyle, the king's daughter, spared her father Thoas. When the Argo put in on its way to Colchis, the women, ruled now by Hypsipyle, received the Argonauts, and the heroes stayed with them for a time and fathered children. Hypsipyle bore sons to Jason, Euneus among them. Apollonius of Rhodes tells the whole episode, with Hypsipyle setting her father adrift in a chest (*Argonautica* 1.609–909; see [[apollonius-lemnos]]), and [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] summarizes it, naming the sons Euneus and Nebrophonus (*Library* 1.9.17). In Pindar the Argonauts come to Lemnos on their way home, contend in games for a garment and lie with the “man-slaying” women (*Pythian* 4), and Erginus, one of the crew, wins a race in armor there (*Olympian* 4; see [[pindar]] and [[pindar-lemnian-women]]).

**Hypsipyle's fate.** Later legend made her a slave at Nemea, nurse to the infant son of Lycurgus there. As she showed the Seven on their march to Thebes the way to a spring, a serpent killed the child, and the Nemean games were founded in his honor. Her sons by Jason came to Nemea and found her. Euripides told this part in his *Hypsipyle*, known from papyrus fragments, where the sons are Euneus and Thoas (see [[euripides]], [[euripides-hypsipyle]] and [[the-seven-against-thebes]]).

## In the poem

**The Argonauts' sons.** The men at Troy belong to the generation after the Argo. Euneus is Jason's son; Eumelus, who leads the men of Pherae, is the son of Alcestis, the daughter of Pelias, who in later legend sent Jason for the fleece (2.714–715; see [[eumelus]] and [[pelias]]). In later lists of the crew the fathers of Achilles and Ajax, Peleus and Telamon, sail with Jason (Apollonius, *Argonautica* 1). The half-line ὑπʼ Ἰήσονι ποιμένι λαῶν, “to Jason, shepherd of the people”, is found also in the *Theogony*, where Medea bears Jason a son (*Theogony* 1000; see [[hesiod]]).

**The island and its trade.** Lemnos lies across the sea from the Troad. The Achaeans had feasted there, probably on their way to Troy, and Agamemnon reminds them of their boasts over beef and bowls of wine (8.230–232; see [[lemnos]]). Their wine comes by ship, from Lemnos here and daily from Thrace in Book 9 (9.71–72; see [[wine]]). Euneus sends a thousand measures to the sons of Atreus as a gift (7.470–471), and the rest of the army buys its wine “some with bronze, and some with gleaming iron, some with hides, and some with the cattle themselves, and some with slaves” (7.473–475). ἀνδράποδα, “slaves”, occurs only here in Homer (see the translation's note on 7.475). Euneus deals in people as well as wine: he buys Lycaon from Achilles (21.40–41; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]).

**Wine and thunder.** The Lemnian wine is drunk through a night of fear. Zeus thunders until dawn, devising evil for both sides, and no one dares to drink before he has poured some of it on the ground for the son of Cronus (7.476–482; see [[libation]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).

## Outside Homer

The crime became proverbial. Herodotus says that after the Pelasgians of Lemnos killed their Athenian concubines and the children they had borne, all cruel deeds in Greece came to be called “Lemnian”, from this crime and from the earlier one of the women who killed their husbands “together with Thoas” (Herodotus 6.138.4); in his version Thoas dies with the men (see [[herodotus]]). Aeschylus' chorus sets the Lemnian crime first among the evils of story (*Libation Bearers* 631–634; see [[aeschylus]]). Herodotus also tells of the grandsons of the Argo's crew, driven from Lemnos by those same Pelasgians, who came to Lacedaemon (Herodotus 4.145). Aeschylus wrote a *Hypsipyle* and Sophocles a *Lemnian Women*, both lost. Ovid gives Hypsipyle a letter to Jason, reproaching him for leaving her and for Medea (*Heroides* 6; see [[ovid-heroides]]), and Statius has her tell the Argive army her story at Nemea (*Thebaid* 5; see [[statius]]).
