## The story

Homer tells it once, in Proteus' answer to Menelaus' question about the Achaeans who came home and those who perished:

{{quote:40f5edfa-01b9-5b96-af0c-ecd19cd1ed73}}

The god who saves him is the god who kills him. Poseidon brings him to the rocks, and he would have escaped his doom, though Athena hated him, “had he not let fall an overbearing word and been greatly blinded” (*Odyssey* 4.502–503). The poem does not say why Athena hated him: Nestor speaks of her deadly wrath against the Achaeans after the sack, and says only that they were not all discerning or just (*Odyssey* 3.132–135; see [[athena]] and [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]). The boast is the cause of his death. Poseidon “heard him speaking big words” (*Odyssey* 4.505), took his trident and split the rock on which Ajax sat (see [[poseidon]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).

## Outside Homer

Later writers gave the goddess her reason and her own part in his death. In Euripides' *Trojan Women* Athena reminds Poseidon of the outrage to her temple “when Ajax dragged Cassandra by force”, for which the Achaeans neither punished nor even rebuked him, and asks him to give the army a bitter homecoming; Zeus has promised her his thunderbolt, to strike the Achaeans and burn their ships, and Poseidon promises that the shores of Myconos, the reefs of Delos, Scyros, Lemnos and the headlands of Caphereus will hold the bodies of many dead (*Trojan Women* 65–97; see [[cassandra]] and [[euripides]]). Virgil's Juno remembers that Pallas burned the Argive fleet and drowned the men “for the guilt and madness of one man, Ajax son of Oileus”: she hurled Jupiter's fire from the clouds, and caught up Ajax, breathing flames from his pierced breast, in a whirlwind and impaled him on a sharp rock (*Aeneid* 1.39–45; see [[virgil]]). Seneca has both the thunderbolt and Homer's trident. Pallas strikes Ajax with the thunderbolt, and he stands out of the sea, scorched, until he seizes a rock and thunders that he has conquered the sea and fire, heaven, Pallas and the thunderbolt; then Neptune, lifting his head from the depths, undermines the rock with his trident, and Ajax falls with it, conquered by earth and fire and sea (Seneca, *Agamemnon* 533–556). Quintus of Smyrna, as Merry reports him, sets the Gyraean rock off Caphereus in Euboea, where Eustathius and Hesychius put it near Myconos, and makes Ajax boast, while he is still struggling in the waves, that he would escape even if all the Olympians in their anger stirred up the whole sea (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.499 and 4.504, citing Quintus 14.560).

Strabo held to Homer's version against the later story. Answering the people of Ilium, who pointed to the Locrian maidens sent to their city every year, he says that Homer knows nothing of the violation of Cassandra, and does not say that Ajax was shipwrecked through the wrath of Athena or for any such cause: Athena hated him in common with the rest, since all had been impious toward her temple, and he perished at the hands of Poseidon for his boastful talk (*Geography* 13.1.40; see [[strabo-on-cassandra]] and [[strabo]]).
