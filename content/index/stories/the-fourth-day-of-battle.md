## The story

**Dawn.** Thetis promised to return “at dawn … with the rising sun” (18.136), and the day begins with her:

{{quote:33138573-dfc6-5f72-88bc-e15913d3829a}}

The second verse also brought the dawn of the third day (19.2 = 11.2; see [[eos]] and [[the-third-day-of-battle]]). Thetis finds Achilles lying over Patroclus, lays the armor before him, and tells him to call the Achaeans to an assembly and renounce his wrath (19.4–39; see [[the-making-of-the-arms]] and [[thetis]]).

**The reconciliation.** Achilles goes along the shore shouting, and the whole army gathers, even the helmsmen and the stewards (19.40–46). He puts an end to his anger (19.56–73; see [[the-renunciation-of-wrath]]); Agamemnon answers from his seat that Zeus and Fate and the Erinys blinded him, tells how even Zeus was blinded on the day of Heracles' birth, and offers the gifts (19.76–144; see [[agamemnon]], [[ate]] and [[the-birth-of-heracles]]). Achilles wants to fight at once; Odysseus insists that the army eat first, since “no man all day long, until the sun goes down, / will be able to fight face to face, going without food” (19.162–163; see [[odysseus]]). The gifts are brought into the assembly, Agamemnon swears over a boar that he never touched Briseis, and Achilles sends the army to its meal (19.238–281; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]], [[oath]] and [[briseis]]).

**The fast.** Briseis laments over Patroclus (19.282–302). Achilles refuses food: “until the sun goes down I will wait and endure all the same” (19.308), and laments his companion before the kings who stay with him (19.309–339). Zeus pities him and sends Athena to drip nectar and ambrosia into his breast, so that hunger may not come upon his knees (19.340–356; see [[zeus]] and [[athena]]).

**The arming.** Helmets, shields, corselets and spears pour out of the ships as thick as snowflakes, and Achilles arms in their midst in the god's armor and takes his father's spear (19.357–391; see [[arming-scene]]). Automedon and Alcimus yoke the horses, and Xanthus, given a voice by Hera, tells him that the day of his destruction is near (19.392–418; see [[xanthus-the-horse]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). Achilles answers that he knows it, but “I will not stop until I have given the Trojans their fill of war” (19.423), and drives out with a cry among the foremost (19.424).

**The gods go to war.** In Book 20 the Achaeans arm by the ships around Achilles, and the Trojans on the rise of the plain (20.1–3). Zeus has Themis call all the gods to assembly, rivers and nymphs as well, and lets them go down to help either side as they wish: if Achilles fights the Trojans alone, they will not hold him back even for a little, and he fears that Achilles may sack the wall against fate (ὑπέρμορον, 20.4–30; see [[themis-the-goddess]]). Hera, Athena, Poseidon, Hermes and Hephaestus go to the ships, Ares, Apollo, Artemis, Leto, the river Xanthus and Aphrodite to the Trojans (20.32–40). Zeus thunders from above and Poseidon shakes the earth, so that Hades leaps from his throne in fear (20.56–66).

**Aeneas and Hector.** Apollo sends Aeneas against Achilles; Poseidon sheds a mist over Achilles' eyes and lifts Aeneas away over the ranks, telling him that no other Achaean will kill him once Achilles is dead (20.79–80, 20.318–339; see [[aeneas]] and [[poseidon]]). Apollo warns Hector to keep back (20.375–380), but when Achilles kills Polydorus, Priam's youngest son, Hector goes against him, and Apollo snatches Hector away in a thick mist, which Achilles strikes three times with his spear (20.407–454; see [[polydorus-son-of-priam]], [[hector]] and [[apollo]]). Then Achilles rages through the Trojans like a fire through the deep valleys of a dry mountain, and his horses trample the dead as bulls tread out barley on a threshing floor (20.490–503).

**The river.** At the ford of the Xanthus he cuts the Trojans in two, drives half toward the city and half into the river, kills them in the water with the sword, and takes twelve young men alive as blood-price for Patroclus (21.1–32). He kills Lycaon, son of Priam, who clasps his knees, and Asteropaeus, grandson of the river Axius (21.34–135, 21.139–204; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[asteropaeus]]). The river, choked with the dead, rises against him and pursues him over the plain, until Hera sends Hephaestus, whose fire dries the plain and boils the river into submission (21.211–384; see [[scamander]], [[hephaestus]] and [[the-river-battle-foretold]]).

**The battle of the gods.** Strife falls among the other gods, and Zeus, sitting on Olympus, laughs to see them come together (21.385–390). Athena fells Ares, Hera beats Artemis about the ears with her own bow, and the gods go back to Olympus, while Apollo goes into Ilios, fearing that the Danaans may sack its wall beyond fate that day (21.391–520).

**At the gates.** Priam has the gates opened for the fleeing army (21.526–536). The Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy had not Apollo roused Agenor to stand against Achilles (21.544–598; see [[agenor]] and [[would-have-had-not]]). Then Apollo, in Agenor's likeness, draws Achilles away along the river while the Trojans crowd into the city (21.599–611), and mocks him for chasing a god (22.7–20).

**Hector.** Deadly fate holds Hector outside, before the Scaean gates (22.5–6). Priam and Hecuba beg him from the wall to come in, but he waits for Achilles (22.25–130); when Achilles comes, he runs, and the two go three times around the city (22.131–207; see [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]). At the fourth time Zeus weighs their fates of death, and Hector's sinks (22.208–213; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). Athena, in the likeness of Deiphobus, persuades him to stand (22.226–247), and Achilles drives his spear through his neck (22.326–330). Dying, Hector foretells that Paris and Apollo will kill Achilles at the Scaean gates (22.359–360). Achilles drags the body behind his chariot in the dust, and the Trojans lament in the city (22.395–515).

**The night.** The Achaeans go back to the ships and the Hellespont. The Myrmidons drive three times around the body of Patroclus in lament, and Achilles lays Hector face down in the dust beside the bier (23.1–26). He gives his men the funeral feast; then, among the kings, he swears that no water will touch his head until Patroclus is burned, but bids them yield to “the hateful feast” (στυγερῇ … δαιτί, 23.48), and they eat (23.29–57). He lies down on the shore among the Myrmidons and sleeps, and the ghost of Patroclus comes to him and asks to be buried quickly; all night long, he says, it stood over him (23.58–107). Dawn finds them weeping around the body (23.108–110), and the next day is given to the pyre.

## In the poem

**The day foretold.** Zeus set out its end in Book 15: “And in anger for him brilliant Achilles will kill Hector” (15.68; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). Thetis gave its cost: “straight after Hector your own fate is ready” (18.96). The poem keeps both in view through the day. Xanthus foretells Achilles' death before he drives out (19.409–417), and the dying Hector names its place (22.359–360).

**The meal.** The day turns on eating. Odysseus argues that no one can fight “all day long, until the sun goes down” without food (19.162–163); Achilles would send the army out “fasting and unfed”, and “as the sun goes down / make ready a great supper, when we have avenged the insult” (19.207–208). He himself eats nothing and is fed by Athena with the food of the gods (19.346–354; see [[fasting]]). The supper comes at the end of the day: the insult avenged, Achilles gives the funeral feast, and with the kings yields to “the hateful feast” (23.29–57). The poem never says that the sun went down. The river reproaches Apollo for not standing by the Trojans, as Zeus bade him, until the evening comes, δείελος ὀψὲ δύων, “setting late” (21.229–232), and the next marks of time are the supper, the night and the dawn (23.55, 23.105, 23.109).

**Against fate.** Twice on this day the gods fear that Troy may fall before its time. Zeus sends them into the battle because Achilles may sack the wall “against fate” (20.30), and Apollo goes into the city for the same fear “on that day” (21.516–517); the Achaeans would have taken it then, had Apollo not roused Agenor (21.544–546). The poem holds the city back for a later day (see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).

**The count of days.** In this index's count the day is Day 27, the day after [[the-third-day-of-battle|the third day of battle]]. The narrator calls that day “the day before, when glorious Hector raged” (ἤματι τῷ προτέρῳ, ὅτε μαίνετο φαίδιμος Ἕκτωρ, 21.5). Agamemnon's “yesterday” is not so exact: he says that Odysseus came to Achilles' huts with the promise of the gifts “yesterday” (χθιζός, 19.141; also 19.195), though the embassy was the night before the third day of battle (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]). Walter Leaf calls the word a piece of carelessness, too slight to bear the theories built on it, among them Bergk's, that before the making of the arms was added Achilles killed Hector on the very day of Patroclus' death (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.141). The next day, the twenty-eighth, dawns at 23.109 and is given to the burning of Patroclus.
