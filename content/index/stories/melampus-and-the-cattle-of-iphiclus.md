## The story

**The bride-price.** Chloris, the queen of Pylos, bore Neleus three sons, and a daughter:

{{quote:c5dabb8e-2e51-5eb7-a473-221edc29d9eb}}

Odysseus, telling what he saw among the dead, gives no more than this, and does not name the seer (*Odyssey* 11.281–297; see [[chloris]], [[pero]], [[neleus]], [[iphiclus]], [[phylace]] and [[catalogue-of-heroines]]).

**Melampus.** The narrator of the fifteenth book tells the story again, from the side of the seer, Melampus (see [[melampus]]), when a descendant of his comes to Telemachus' ship at Pylos. Melampus once lived in Pylos, mother of flocks, a rich man with a house far above the others; but he fled his country and great-hearted Neleus, who held his many possessions by force for a full year. All that time he lay in grievous bonds in the halls of Phylacus, suffering hard pains for the daughter of Neleus and for the heavy blindness that the dread Erinys had put in his mind. But he escaped his doom, drove the lowing cattle from Phylace to Pylos, took vengeance on godlike Neleus for his shameful deed, and brought the woman home for his brother; and he went to horse-pasturing Argos, where it was his fate to live and rule over many Argives, married there, and fathered Antiphates and Mantius (*Odyssey* 15.223–242, a plain rendering). From Antiphates came Oicles and the seer Amphiaraus, “who perished at Thebes for the sake of gifts to a woman”, and from Mantius Polypheides, whose son Theoclymenus is the seer who sails with Telemachus to Ithaca (*Odyssey* 15.243–256, a plain rendering; see [[amphiaraus]] and [[alcmaeon-kills-eriphyle]]).

## In the poem

**Two sides of one story.** The two tellings complement each other. In the eleventh book the story belongs to Neleus' family: Pero is a marvel, the cattle are the price her father sets, and the seer, unnamed, wins his freedom by prophecy, while “the plan of Zeus was being fulfilled” (*Odyssey* 11.297; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). In the fifteenth it belongs to the seer's: Neleus is the wrongdoer, who seized his property, the bonds are the price of a blindness sent by an Erinys, the woman is won for a brother, and the seer founds a house of seers in Argos. Neither telling names the brother, or says what prophecies set the seer free. Merry, telling the story from both books, names the brother Bias, and draws the family tree from Salmoneus and Iasus down to Bias and Pero, and from Melampus down to Amphiaraus and Theoclymenus (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.281).

**The seer and the women.** The story ends Odysseus' account of Chloris, and the women who follow it close the catalogue with Eriphyle, the wife of Melampus' great-grandson Amphiaraus, “who took precious gold as the price of her own husband” (*Odyssey* 11.326–327; see [[eriphyle]]).

## Outside Homer

**Hesiod.** The *Great Eoiae* ascribed to Hesiod told how Melampus, dear to Apollo, buried a snake that the king's servants had killed and reared its young, which licked his ears and breathed prophecy into him; caught stealing the cattle of Iphiclus and bound, he warned an old serving woman of Iphiclus that the house was about to fall, and was set free for it (fr. 12 Evelyn-White). A poem called the *Melampodia* after him had a scene in which Mantes takes an ox's tether in his hands while Iphiclus strokes its back, and Phylacus follows with a cup in one hand and a scepter raised in the other, speaking among the servants (fr. 6 Evelyn-White). The *Catalogue of Women* knew Melampus, son of Amythaon, as the healer of the daughters of Proetus (fr. 18 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]).

**Later accounts.** Eustathius, as Merry reports, took the oracles of the eleventh book for a prophecy that cured Iphiclus' childlessness, and a scholium for the seer's foreknowledge that he would be caught and held a year; the later logographers made Melampus understand the speech of all creatures, and told of his listening to the worms as they bored through the beam of his cell (Merry on 11.297). Merry adds that Phylacus seems to have taken the cattle from Tyro, the mother of Neleus, and that the imprisonment may have grown out of the names Phylacus and Phylace, “guard” (on 11.290). Propertius sums the story up: the seer Melampus suffered shameful bonds, caught stealing the cattle of Iphiclus, driven not by gain but by beautiful Pero, soon to be a bride in the house of Amythaon (2.3.51–54; Merry on 11.281; see [[propertius]]).
