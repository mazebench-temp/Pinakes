## The story

**Sleepless.** The book begins in the night after the embassy to Achilles, with the kings asleep in their huts (9.712–713):

{{quote:dcd22b52-4b02-5038-98b5-9b7c0d8635f4}}

His groans come as thick as the flashes of Zeus' lightning before a storm (10.5–10; see [[lightning-before-the-storm]]). When he looks out over the plain he marvels at the many fires burning before Ilios and at the sound of flutes and pipes; when he looks at the ships, he tears out his hair “to Zeus on high” (10.11–16; see the translation's note on 10.15–16). He decides to go to Nestor for a plan, and dresses for the night: tunic, sandals, “the blood-red skin of a lion” and a spear (10.17–24; see [[lion]] and [[agamemnon]]).

**The brothers.** Menelaus cannot sleep either, afraid for the Argives who came to Troy for his sake (10.25–28; see [[war-for-menelaus]]). In a leopard's skin and a bronze helmet he finds his brother arming by his ship and asks whether he will send someone to spy on the Trojans; no one, he fears, will dare, “he would have to be very bold of heart” (10.29–41; see [[leopard-skin]] and [[menelaus]]). Agamemnon answers that they need shrewd counsel, “since the mind of Zeus has turned”: never has one man done so much harm in a day as Hector (10.43–52; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[the-second-day-of-battle]]). He sends Menelaus to call Ajax and Idomeneus, naming each man by his father, and not to stand on his rank, for “at our birth Zeus laid heavy misery on us” (10.53–71).

**Nestor.** Agamemnon finds the old man in a soft bed beside his hut, his armor next to him and the gleaming belt he still wears to war (10.73–79; see [[belt]]). Nestor raises himself on his elbow and challenges him: “Are you looking for one of your mules, or one of your companions?” (10.80–85; see [[mules]]). Agamemnon tells him that he cannot sleep, that his heart leaps out of his breast and his limbs tremble, and asks him to come down to the sentries, in case they have fallen asleep with the enemy so close (10.87–101; see [[night-watch]]). Nestor answers that Zeus will not fulfill all of Hector's hopes, and that Hector will have sorrows enough if Achilles turns from his anger (10.103–107; see [[longing-for-achilles]]). They must wake Diomedes, Odysseus, swift Ajax and Meges; and he blames Menelaus for sleeping, until Agamemnon tells him that his brother woke first and is already out calling the chiefs (10.108–130; see [[nestor]]).

**Nestor rouses the chiefs.** In a double crimson cloak (10.131–136; see [[cloak]]) Nestor wakes Odysseus with a call (10.137–149). He finds Diomedes asleep outside his hut on an oxhide among his sleeping companions, their spears driven upright on their butt-spikes, the bronze shining far off like Zeus' lightning (10.150–156; see [[like-lightning]]). He stirs him with his heel, and Diomedes, up at once, calls him a hard old man who never rests (10.157–167). Nestor agrees, but the need is very great: “For now it stands for all of us on the edge of a razor, / whether grim destruction comes on the Achaeans, or life” (10.173–174; see [[on-the-razors-edge]]). Diomedes, in a lion's skin, goes to rouse swift Ajax and Meges (10.175–179).

**The sentries.** They find the leaders of the watch awake and armed, like dogs keeping a troubled watch over the sheep in a fold when they hear a beast coming through the woods (10.180–189; see [[the-watchdogs]]). Nestor praises them: “Keep watch like this now, dear children” (10.190–193). Then the kings, with Meriones and Nestor's son, cross the ditch and sit down in a clear space free of corpses, where Hector turned back at nightfall (10.194–202; see [[the-achaean-wall]]).

**The volunteers.** Nestor asks whether any man will go among the Trojans, to catch a straggler or hear what they plan, whether to stay by the ships or go back to the city. He would win fame “under heaven”, and every chief who commands a ship would give him a black ewe with a lamb at her udder (10.204–217; see [[kleos]] and [[sheep]]). All are silent (10.218; see [[silence]]). Then Diomedes offers to go, if another man will go with him:

{{quote:bbb8fa8d-b569-5d7b-8d4f-fd125756025f}}

The two Ajaxes are willing, and Meriones, Nestor's son, Menelaus and Odysseus (10.227–232; see [[when-two-go-together]]). Agamemnon, afraid for Menelaus, tells Diomedes to take the best man, not the most kingly (10.233–240). Diomedes chooses Odysseus, whom Pallas Athena loves: with him, “even out of blazing fire / we would both come home” (10.241–247; see [[odysseus-and-diomedes]]). Odysseus cuts the praise short and points to the stars. Two of the night's three portions are gone (10.248–253; see [[do-not-praise-me-too-much]]).

**The arming and the heron.** Thrasymedes gives Diomedes a sword, a shield and a skullcap of bull's hide, and Meriones gives Odysseus a bow, a quiver, a sword and a leather helmet set with rows of a boar's white tusks. The narrator gives that helmet's history: Autolycus stole it from the house of Amyntor at Eleon and gave it to Amphidamas of Cythera at Scandeia; Amphidamas gave it to Molus as a guest-gift, and Molus to his son Meriones (10.254–271; see [[helmet]], [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]], [[autolycus]], [[amphidamas]] and [[molus]]). As they set out, Athena sends a heron on their right. In the dark they cannot see it, but they hear its cry, and Odysseus rejoices at the sign and prays to her for a safe return “with glory” (10.272–282; see [[heron]]). Diomedes prays second, reminding her how she stood by his father Tydeus at Thebes, and vows her a yearling heifer with gold poured around her horns (10.283–294; see [[tydeus-at-thebes]] and [[sacrifice]]). Athena hears them, and they go “like two lions through the black night, / through the slaughter, through the corpses, through the armor and the black blood” (10.295–298; see [[two-lions]]).

**Hector's spy.** Hector, too, keeps the Trojan leaders awake. He offers a chariot and the two best horses at the Achaean ships to any man who will go close to the ships and find out whether they are still guarded or whether the Achaeans, beaten, are planning flight (10.299–312). The Trojans are silent as well (10.313). Dolon, son of the divine herald Eumedes, rich in gold and bronze, ill-favored but swift-footed, the only son among five sisters, offers to go if Hector will swear to give him the horses and the chariot of Achilles (10.314–327; see [[dolon]], [[eumedes]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Hector raises his scepter and swears by Zeus, and the narrator adds that he “swore an oath that would prove false” (10.328–332; see [[oath]]). Dolon puts on the skin of a gray wolf and a cap of ferret skin, takes his bow and a javelin, and sets out; “but he was not to / come back from the ships and bring word to Hector” (10.333–337; see [[wolves]] and [[ferret]]).

**The capture.** Odysseus sees him coming. The two lie down off the road among the dead and let him run past “in his thoughtlessness”, then cut him off from the Trojans and drive him toward the ships, like two sharp-toothed hounds pressing a fawn or a hare through wooded country (10.338–364; see [[two-hounds]] and [[ambush]]). When he is about to fall in with the Achaean sentries, Athena puts might into Diomedes, so that no other Achaean may boast that he struck first (10.365–368). Diomedes calls on him to stand, throws, and “missed the man on purpose”: the spear sticks in the ground over his right shoulder (10.369–374). Dolon stops, stammering, teeth chattering, pale with fear. They seize his hands, and he begs in tears to be taken alive; his father will pay a boundless ransom (10.374–381; see [[supplication]] and [[apoina]]).

**The questioning.** “Take heart”, Odysseus tells him, “and do not let death be on your mind at all” (10.383). He asks whether Dolon came to strip the dead, or was sent by Hector, or came of his own will (10.384–389). Dolon blames Hector's promise of Achilles' horses; Odysseus, smiling, answers that those horses are hard for any mortal to drive but Achilles, “whom an immortal mother bore” (10.390–404; see [[smiling-at-him]]). Then Dolon tells all he is asked, and more. Hector is holding council “by the mound of divine Ilus”, away from the din. The Trojans keep watch at their own fires, but the allies sleep, since their wives and children are far away. He names the allies camped toward the sea and toward Thymbre. And he names, unasked, the farthest out of all, the newly come Thracians and their king Rhesus, son of Eioneus, with horses “whiter than snow, and like the winds in running”, a chariot worked with gold and silver, and golden armor fit for gods (10.405–441; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]], [[trojan-allies]], [[thracians]], [[rhesus]], [[the-horses-of-rhesus]] and [[whiter-than-snow]]). He asks them to take him to the ships, or leave him bound until they have tested his word (10.442–445).

**The death of Dolon.** Diomedes, looking at him darkly, refuses. If they let him go, he will come again, to spy or to fight; if he dies, he will never trouble the Argives again (10.446–453; see [[looking-darkly]]).

{{quote:6875253e-0325-5834-9dd7-dc780f9ab8cf}}

They take the cap, the wolfskin, the bow and the spear, and Odysseus holds them up to Athena, “goddess of the spoil”, praying her to guide them to the Thracians' horses and beds. He sets the spoils on a tamarisk and marks the place with reeds and branches, so that they will not miss it on the way back (10.458–468; see [[stripping-the-dead]] and [[dedication-of-spoils]]).

**Rhesus.** They come to the Thracians, asleep in three rows with their armor beside them and a yoke of horses by each man, Rhesus in the middle with his horses tied to the rail of his chariot (10.469–475). Odysseus points him out, “Here is the man, Diomedes, and here are the horses”, and offers him the choice: let him untie the horses, or kill the men while Odysseus sees to the horses (10.476–481). Athena breathes might into Diomedes, and he kills, turning this way and that, as a lion falls on flocks that have no herdsman (10.482–488; see [[lion-among-the-flocks]]). Behind him Odysseus drags each dead man aside by the foot, so that the horses, “not yet used to them”, will not shy at treading on the dead (10.488–493). The thirteenth is the king:

{{quote:bf31520c-0daa-5b09-9018-98f86bbca9a0}}

Odysseus unties the horses and drives them out of the throng with his bow, since he has not thought to take the whip, and whistles to Diomedes (10.498–502). Diomedes lingers, pondering “what was the most shameless thing he could do”: to take the chariot with the armor in it, or to kill more Thracians (10.503–506). Athena warns him to think of his return, “if some other god should wake the Trojans too”; he knows her voice, mounts the horses, and they fly toward the ships (10.507–514).

**Apollo.** Apollo does not “keep a blind watch”. He sees Athena with Diomedes and, in resentment, wakes Hippocoon, Rhesus' cousin, who sees the empty place where the horses stood and the men gasping in the slaughter, and cries out. The Trojans rush together in uproar and gaze at what the two have done (10.515–525; see [[apollo]] and [[hippocoon]]).

**The return.** At the place where they killed Dolon, Diomedes leaps down and puts the bloody spoils into Odysseus' hands, and they ride on (10.526–531). Nestor is the first to hear the hooves, and hopes aloud that it is the two (10.532–539). They ride in, are welcomed with handclasps, and Nestor asks whether some god gave them these horses: “They are terribly like the rays of the sun” (10.540–553; see [[like-the-sun]]). Odysseus answers that a god could easily give better, and tells the count: the Thracian king and twelve of his companions, “all of the best”, and a thirteenth, Hector's spy, taken close to the ships (10.554–563). He drives the horses across the ditch, laughing aloud, “and the other Achaeans went with him, rejoicing” (10.564–565). The horses are tied at Diomedes' manger, and Odysseus sets Dolon's spoils in the stern of a ship (the poem does not say whose), until they can make ready an offering to Athena (10.566–571). The two wash off their sweat in the sea, bathe in polished tubs, anoint themselves with oil, and sit down to their meal, pouring out honey-sweet wine to Athena (10.572–579; see [[bathing]] and [[libation]]).

## In the poem

**The same night.** Book 10 is the second half of the night that fell at the end of the second day of battle (8.485–488), Day 25 in this index's count. The embassy filled its first part, and the kings went to sleep (9.712–713). The book opens with the shape of verse that begins two other night scenes, everyone asleep but one who lies awake and ponders: Zeus at the start of Book 2, Hermes in Book 24 (2.1–4, 24.677–681; 10.2 = 24.678; see [[war-and-night]] and [[hypnos]]). It ends before dawn. Odysseus says that two of the night's three portions are gone when the two set out (10.251–253; see the translation's note on 10.251–253), and the third day of battle, Day 26, begins at 11.1. Much in the book continues the night as Books 8 and 9 left it. The Trojan fires that Agamemnon marvels at are the thousand fires of their camp on the plain (10.11–12; 8.553–563). The watch he fears may be asleep is the one Nestor posted in Book 9, with Thrasymedes and Meriones among its seven captains (9.80–88; 10.56–59, 10.97–99). The ground beyond the ditch where the chiefs sit, “where the ground showed free of corpses”, is described in the verse that placed Hector's assembly at nightfall (10.199 = 8.491).

**After the embassy.** The night began in despair. Agamemnon proposed flight in earnest (9.17–28), the embassy failed, and Diomedes ended the council by telling Agamemnon to fight among the foremost at dawn (9.707–709; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]). Book 10 opens on the same despair: Agamemnon sleepless, tearing his hair, and Nestor's razor's edge, a sharper form of his own “This night will either shatter the army or save it” (9.78; 10.173–174; see [[this-night-will-shatter-or-save]]). Achilles is absent. The book speaks of him only in Nestor's hope that he may yet relent and in the talk of his horses, which the Trojans covet (10.106–107, 10.322–323, 10.401–404). It ends with the opposite mood: a victory won by two men and a goddess, laughter, a bath, a meal and a libation. The next day both men are wounded: Diomedes by an arrow of Paris, who shoots leaning against the pillar on the tomb of Ilus, where Hector held his council in the night (11.369–377), and Odysseus by the spear of Socus (11.434–438).

**Two councils, two spies.** The book is built in pairs, and the Trojan half answers the Achaean half in the same words. Each side holds a council in the night; each leader asks who will go to spy on the enemy and promises a reward; each council meets the question with the same silence (10.218 = 10.313); and Diomedes and Dolon volunteer in the same verse, “my heart and my proud spirit urge me” (10.220 = 10.319). What Nestor wants to learn, whether the Trojans will stay by the ships or go back to the city, Odysseus asks Dolon (10.208–210 = 10.409–411), and what Hector wants to learn Dolon repeats to his captors (10.309–312 = 10.396–399). The question Nestor put to Agamemnon in the dark, who comes alone along the ships “through the dark night, when other mortals are asleep?”, Odysseus puts to Dolon (10.82–83, 10.385–386). The differences tell. Nestor's prize is a black ewe from every chief, a gift that is all honor, “no possession is like it” (10.215–217); Hector offers horses, and Dolon asks for the horses of Achilles, the best of all (2.769–770; 10.305–306, 10.322–323; see [[doron]]). The Achaeans send two men who pray and are heard; the Trojans send one man alone, who does not pray. The two who go out do come back; Dolon, the narrator says as he sets out, will not (10.336–337).

**Skins and beasts.** The book dresses its men for the night in skins and leather instead of bronze (see [[arming-scene]]). Agamemnon and Diomedes wear lion skins, Menelaus a leopard skin, and Dolon the skin of a gray wolf (10.23–24, 10.29, 10.177–178, 10.334). Diomedes is given a skullcap of bull's hide, Odysseus the helmet of boar's tusks, and Dolon wears a cap of ferret skin (10.257–265, 10.335). The similes take up the animals. The sentries are dogs watching the fold, the two Achaeans go out like two lions, they chase Dolon like two hounds after a fawn or a hare, and Diomedes falls on the sleeping Thracians like a lion on flocks with no herdsman (10.183–189, 10.297, 10.360–364, 10.485–488; see [[lion]] and [[dogs]]). So the man in the wolf's skin is run down by hounds, and Diomedes, in his lion's skin, kills like a lion.

**Athena.** Athena runs through the raid. She sends the heron and hears both prayers (10.274–295), puts might into Diomedes twice (10.366–368, 10.482), and is named as the cause of Rhesus' death, “by the design of Athena” (10.497). She warns Diomedes to leave before another god wakes the Trojans (10.507–511), and the book ends with the offering promised to her and a libation poured to her (10.570–571, 10.578–579). Odysseus asked her to love him “now again … above all” (10.280), and Nestor says it for both: “for Zeus the cloud-gatherer loves you both, / and the daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus, gray-eyed Athena” (10.552–553; see [[athena]]). Against her the poem sets Apollo, who sees too late and can only wake the Thracians to their dead (10.515–525). No god helps Dolon.

**Dolon's death.** Dolon offers ransom as Adrastus did, in nearly the same words (10.378–381 ≈ 6.46–50; compare 11.131–135), and like Adrastus he is killed. Agamemnon killed Adrastus as Menelaus was about to spare him (6.51–65), and Achilles will kill Lycaon after hearing him out (21.64–119). Dolon is not allowed to finish: he was about to take Diomedes by the chin when the sword fell, and his head was in the dust while he was still speaking (10.454–457). Odysseus' “take heart” promised him nothing, and Diomedes gives his reason, that a spy let go comes back (10.449–453). The verse of his death recurs in Homer only in the Odyssey, where Odysseus kills the suppliant Leodes in mid-plea (10.457 = *Odyssey* 22.329). How the scene should be judged, as a hard necessity of war or as a cruelty the poem means us to notice, readers have long disputed (see [[supplication]]).

**The thirteen, the dream and the horses.** Rhesus is the thirteenth man killed in the Thracian camp, after twelve (10.488, 10.495); in Odysseus' report the king is set apart, and Dolon is “a thirteenth” after the twelve companions (10.559–561; see the translation's note on 10.559–561, [[the-evil-dream]] and [[numbers-and-counting]]). The “evil dream” that stood over the sleeping king's head is Diomedes himself, standing over him as dreams stand over sleepers' heads, though the verses can also be read of a dream in which Rhesus saw him (10.496–497; see the translation's note on 10.494–497, [[the-evil-dream]] and [[onar]]). The two men ride the horses back (10.513, 10.529), the only riding in the Iliad outside a simile (15.679–684; see [[horses]]). Odysseus drives the team with his bow because he forgot the whip (10.500–501), yet at 10.530 Diomedes “whipped” the horses, in a verse that returns of Nestor's horses (11.519).

**Its place in the poem.** Whether Book 10 belonged to the Iliad from the first is an old question (see [[the-doloneia-question]] and [[title-of-book-10]]). An ancient note on its first verse, in the T scholia and in Eustathius, reports what “they say”: that Homer composed the book separately, that it was not part of the Iliad, and that Peisistratus placed it in the poem. Aelian names a “Doloneia” among the parts of the Iliad once sung on their own (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14). Modern critics added that the book can be taken out without leaving a gap; that nothing later in the poem mentions the raid, Dolon or the Thracian horses, so that in the chariot race of Book 23 Diomedes drives the horses of Tros that he took from Aeneas (23.290–292); and that its language and manner are its own, with many words found nowhere else in Homer, whole verses shared with the Odyssey, the Iliad's only riding outside a simile and its only bath in tubs, and the killing of a suppliant and of sleeping men. Others answer that the book presupposes the night that Books 8 and 9 have told, as its fires, its watch and Nestor's hope for Achilles show, and that much of what is strange in it belongs to its subject, a night raid with its spies, ambush and disguise. M. L. West, among others, took it to be the work of a different poet (*The Making of the Iliad*, 2011); Casey Dué and Mary Ebbott argued that its peculiarities belong to the traditional poetry of ambush, λόχος, and do not show a later hand (*Iliad 10 and the Poetics of Ambush*, 2010). The question remains open.

## Outside Homer

The tragedy *Rhesus*, handed down among the plays of Euripides though its authorship has been doubted, stages the same night in the Trojan camp (see [[euripides-rhesus]] and [[euripides]]). Its chorus of sentries keeps the watch of a quarter of the night (τετράμοιρον νυκτὸς φυλακήν, *Rhesus* 5), where Odysseus reckons in thirds. Dolon asks Hector for the horses of Achilles and goes out disguised in a wolf's skin, with the beast's gaping jaws over his head, walking on all fours like a wolf: “this is the trick (δόλος) I have contrived” (*Rhesus* 182, 208–215). Rhesus is the son of the river Strymon and a Muse (279, 651–652), his horses are “brighter than snow” (304), and Athena directs Odysseus and Diomedes to him after they have killed Dolon and failed to find Hector (575–607). His charioteer, wounded, tells how he dreamed of wolves riding on the horses' backs (780–788), and his mother the Muse laments him at the end (890–982).

Aristotle's *Poetics* answers four objections drawn from Book 10 (*Poetics* 25, 1461a). The poet says that all slept, yet Agamemnon looks out at the plain and hears flutes and pipes (10.11–13): Aristotle calls this a metaphor, “all” put for “many” (see [[all-the-gods-slept]]). The spears stood upright on their butt-spikes because that was the custom then, as it still is among the Illyrians (10.152–153). Dolon was ill-favored in his face, not misshapen in body (10.316). And πλέω in Odysseus' reckoning of the night is ambiguous (10.252). In the same chapter he suggests that οὐρῆας, the word for the mules at 1.50, may mean guards; οὐρήων at 10.84 is the same word (see [[aristotle-upright-spears]], [[aristotle-dolons-face]], [[aristotle-portions-of-the-night]], [[aristotle-mules]] and [[aristotle]]). Elsewhere he answers those who took the verse of Dolon's death to mean that his severed head spoke (see [[aristotle-the-speaking-head]]). For Virgil's use of Rhesus and Dolon, see [[virgil-rhesus-and-dolon]], and for his own night raid, Nisus and Euryalus in the ninth book of the *Aeneid*, see [[nisus-and-euryalus]].
