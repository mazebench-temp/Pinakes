The two notices share a line: "he was brought down at the hands of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus in the river" (2.860 = 2.874). ποδώκης, "swift-footed" ([[swift-footed]]); Αἰακίδης, "grandson of Aeacus", is Achilles. The river is the Scamander, whom the gods call Xanthus ([[scamander]]). Which of the Carian brothers wore the gold is not said ([[nastes]]).

## In the poem

In Book 21 Achilles drives half the Trojan army into the Scamander and kills them in the water, until the river itself rises against him (21.1–382). He kills Lycaon, a son of Priam, and the Paeonian Asteropaeus, and then many Paeonians (21.34–210). Neither Ennomus nor either of the Carians is named there. The Catalogue's notices are the only account of their deaths. Some scholars have taken this as a sign that the Trojan catalogue was composed apart from the rest of the poem; others think that the poet simply knew more deaths in the river than he chose to tell.

In Book 2 the effect is plain. In the middle of a list of allies marching out to fight, the narrator names two who will die, and names their killer, Achilles, who is at that moment sitting idle by his ships (2.769–779). The man in gold is νήπιος, "the fool" ([[nepios]]). The gold did not keep off destruction, and Achilles carried it off.
