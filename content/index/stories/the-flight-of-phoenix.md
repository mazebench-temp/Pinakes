## The story

**The quarrel.** Phoenix would not leave Achilles even if a god made him young again, “as I was when I first left Hellas of the lovely women” (9.447). Then he tells why he left:

{{quote:991cec53-e0c0-5094-b22a-cf769925fbd0}}

The quarrel belongs to a household. An old man prefers a fair-haired concubine to his wife, and “dishonored his wife” (ἀτιμάζεσκε δʼ ἄκοιτιν, 9.450); the wife begs her son by his knees, a suppliant's plea, to lie with the girl first, so that she will turn from the old man (see [[concubines]], [[supplication]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]). Phoenix obeys.

**The curse.** The father suspects it at once (αὐτίκʼ ὀϊσθείς, 9.453), and he curses:

{{quote:21febacc-4a4d-5963-ad7e-e7e355e1b4c4}}

The curse fits the wrong. The son who came between his father and a woman is to have no son, and Amyntor will never hold a grandson on his knees. ἐφέσσεσθαι is taken as causative, “that he would never set upon his knees a dear son” (9.455); it can also be read “that no son should sit”. The father calls on the Erinyes, and the gods below fulfill the curse: Ζεὺς καταχθόνιος, “Zeus of the underworld”, who is Hades, and Persephone (see [[curses]], [[erinyes]], [[hades]], [[zeus-of-the-underworld]] and [[persephone]]).

**The four lines.** Four verses that are in no manuscript follow here. Phoenix planned “to kill him with the sharp bronze”, but one of the immortals checked his anger, putting in his heart “the talk of the people and the many reproaches of men, so that I might not be called a father-killer among the Achaeans” (9.458–461). Plutarch, who quotes them, says that Aristarchus removed them out of fear (*How the Young Man Should Study Poetry* 26F–27A; see [[phoenix-patricide-lines]]). The text prints them in brackets. Without them, the curse leads straight to Phoenix's flight (9.462).

**The nine nights.** “Then the heart in my breast could no longer at all be held to staying in the halls while my father was angry” (9.462–463). His kinsmen and cousins begged him to stay. They slaughtered sheep, cattle and hogs and drank the old man's wine, and for nine nights they slept around him, keeping watch by turns, with fires burning under the colonnade of the court and in the porch before his chamber (9.464–473). On the tenth night he broke open the doors of his chamber and leapt the fence of the court, “unseen by the watchmen and the slave women” (9.474–477; see [[murky]]).

**Peleus.**

{{quote:0a17f63e-0109-59c3-9bae-48536b65dd1d}}

Phoenix comes to Phthia as an exile and is received as an heir (see [[as-a-father-loves-his-son]], [[peleus]] and [[phthia]]). Peleus makes him rich and gives him a people, and he lives “at the far edge of Phthia, ruling over the Dolopians” (ἐσχατιὴν Φθίης, 9.483–484; see [[dolopians]]). There Phoenix reared the infant Achilles, and since the gods gave him no child of his own, he made Achilles his son (9.485–495; see [[phoenix]]).

## In the poem

**Why he tells it.** Phoenix tells the story to explain why he cannot be parted from Achilles, and it leads into his care of the child (9.444–495). It is his title to speak. The man who could have no son by the curse set the son of the house that took him in on his knees, and fed him there (9.488–489): the knees of the curse (9.455) become the knees of the nursing. Peleus sent him to Troy to teach Achilles (9.438–443), and now he asks the son he reared to ward off ruin from him (9.495; see [[fathers-and-sons]] and [[threptra]]).

**A story for Achilles.** Phoenix draws no lesson from it, but readers have seen in it a first example set before the Prayers and the story of Meleager (see [[litai]] and [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). An older man and a younger quarrel over a woman, and the young man, entreated by his kin, will not stay. The four bracketed lines make the likeness closer, for there a god checks a young man's anger against an older one, as Athena checked Achilles' hand on his sword (1.193–222; see [[athena-restrains-achilles]]). Whether the story warns Achilles of what anger costs, or simply explains how Phoenix came to be his foster father, the text leaves open.

**Two curses.** Phoenix's speech tells of two curses laid by a parent on a son. Amyntor's is fulfilled by Hades and Persephone (9.453–457). Althaea calls on Hades and Persephone to give death to her son Meleager, and the Erinys hears her out of Erebus (9.566–572; see [[the-death-of-meleager]]). In both, the powers below take the parent's side.

## Outside Homer

In later versions his father blinds him. Euripides' lost *Phoenix*, older than Aristophanes' *Acharnians* (425 BC), showed him blind: the *Acharnians* names “blind Phoenix” among the ragged heroes of Euripides (*Acharnians* 421), and a fragment is spoken by a man who once mocked the blind and has now fallen into their state (fr. 816 Nauck). In Apollodorus the concubine, Phthia, falsely accused Phoenix of seducing her; Amyntor blinded him, and Peleus brought him to Chiron, who restored his sight, and made him king of the Dolopians (*Library* 3.13.8; see [[chiron]] and [[apollodorus]]). The Homeric son who obeys his mother has become a young man falsely accused by his father's woman, like Bellerophon (see [[bellerophon]]) or Euripides' Hippolytus.

Where the story took place was disputed. Book 9 makes Phoenix leave “Hellas” (9.447, 9.478), but in Book 10 Amyntor's house is at Eleon (10.266), and ancient scholars placed it in Thessaly, Phocis or Boeotia (Strabo 9.5.18; see [[hellas-or-ormenium]] and [[amyntor]]).
