## The story

**Where the stranger should go.** The ship has come in at dawn, and the companions have eaten on the shore; Telemachus sends them on to the city and means to go to his fields himself (*Odyssey* 15.495–507; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). The seer Theoclymenus, whom he took on board at Pylos, asks to whose house he should go, of the men who rule over craggy Ithaca, or whether he should go straight to his mother (*Odyssey* 15.508–511; see [[theoclymenus]]). At another time, Telemachus answers, he would bid him come to their house, but he himself will be away, and his mother seldom shows herself to the suitors; let him go to “Eurymachus, the splendid son of wise-hearted Polybus, / whom the men of Ithaca now look upon as a god”, the best man there and the most eager to marry his mother and to hold the prize of honor of Odysseus. “But Zeus the Olympian, who dwells in the upper air, knows this, / whether before the marriage he will bring about an evil day for them” (*Odyssey* 15.512–524; see [[eurymachus]] and [[the-suitors]]).

**Telemachus' answer.** “Would that this word, stranger, might be fulfilled; / then you would quickly know friendship and many gifts / from me, so that anyone who met you would call you blessed” (*Odyssey* 15.535–538; see [[it-will-be-fulfilled]]). Then he calls his trusted companion Peiraeus, son of [[clytius-father-of-peiraeus|Clytius]], and asks him to take the stranger to his house and welcome him with care until he comes; Peiraeus answers that the stranger shall not lack good cheer, and the ship sails on to the city while Telemachus walks to the swineherd's fold (*Odyssey* 15.539–557; see [[peiraeus-son-of-clytius]] and [[xenia]]).

**The plain reading.** The next day, in the palace, [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] gives Penelope the reading he did not give her son. He calls Zeus to witness, and the table of hospitality and the hearth of Odysseus, that Odysseus is already in his native land, “and he is planting evil for all the suitors. / Such a bird of omen I made out, as I sat on the well-benched ship, / and I declared it aloud to Telemachus” (*Odyssey* 17.151–161, a plain rendering); Penelope answers him in the three verses with which Telemachus answered him at the landing (*Odyssey* 17.163–165 = 15.536–538; see [[penelope]]).

## In the poem

**The bird and the name.** The sign comes on the name of Eurymachus. To D. B. Monro the name seems at first to have no sufficient motive and to lead to nothing in the sequel; it is “merely intended to give occasion for the prophecy of Theoclymenus, one of the steps which prepare us for the final dénouement”: Telemachus, who cannot receive a stranger in his own house, sends him to the man who seems likely to be the future lord of Ithaca, the seer sees the omen and proclaims that the line of Odysseus will remain the most kingly, and “In consequence of this prophecy the resort to Eurymachus is tacitly given up” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 15.519; see [[theoclymenus]]). Eurymachus is the suitor who scorned the eagles of the assembly, “and not all of them are birds of fate” (*Odyssey* 2.181–182; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagles]]). In the hall, when the seer sees the suitors' doom, it is Eurymachus who calls him mad, and the seer goes out to [[peiraeus-son-of-clytius|Peiraeus]], “who received him gladly” (*Odyssey* 20.350–372, a plain rendering; see [[eurymachus]]).

**The kingship.** The reading answers the question the suitors raised in the first book of the Odyssey, who will be king in Ithaca. Antinous prayed that the son of Cronus might never make Telemachus king in sea-girt Ithaca, “which is yours by birth from your father”, and Eurymachus answered that these things “lie on the knees of the gods” (*Odyssey* 1.386–387, 1.400–401; see [[basileus]] and [[telemachus-and-the-suitors]]). Telemachus has just named Eurymachus as the man most eager to “hold the prize of honor of Odysseus” (*Odyssey* 15.522), and the seer answers that no race in the land is more kingly than his, βασιλεύτερον (*Odyssey* 15.533).

**Apollo's messenger.** The eagle of the omen at Sparta is the bird of Zeus, the “swift messenger” for which Priam asks him (*Iliad* 24.292, 24.310; see [[eagle]] and [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]); the falcon is “the swift messenger of Apollo” (*Odyssey* 15.526), and Apollo himself goes down from Ida “like a hawk, / a swift dove-killer, which is the swiftest of winged birds” (*Iliad* 15.237–238; see [[falcon]], [[like-a-hawk]] and [[apollo]]). The reader of the sign is of a house of seers that Apollo favored: Apollo loved Amphiaraus with every kind of love, and after Amphiaraus died he made Polypheides a seer, “by far the best of mortals”, and [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] is the son of Polypheides (*Odyssey* 15.244–256; see [[polypheides]] and [[melampus]]).

**The two signs of the way home.** Both signs of the journey home come in the same verse, “As he spoke, a bird flew by on the right” (*Odyssey* 15.160 = 15.525), and each on a word of Telemachus about his house: at Sparta his wish to find his father in it, at the landing the name of the man who may marry his mother. Helen reads the first as the vengeance of Odysseus; the seer reads the second as the kingship of his line, and keeps the vengeance for Penelope (see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]). The pair is matched in reverse in the twentieth book of the Odyssey, when the suitors are plotting Telemachus' death and a bird comes to them on the left, an eagle flying high and holding a timid dove, and Amphinomus turns them from the plot (*Odyssey* 20.240–247; see [[doves]]).

**The seer's knowledge.** Helen prophesied as the immortals put it in her heart (see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]]); [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] claims to know: “for I knew when I saw it face to face that it was a bird of omen” (*Odyssey* 15.532). W. W. Merry sets the words beside Eurymachus' scorn of the birds: “The first business of the seer is to discriminate the ἐναίσιμοι”, the birds that tell of fate (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 2.182). He compares the arts of divination that Prometheus in Aeschylus claims to have given men: he first judged which dreams must come true, made known chance utterances hard to read, and marked out exactly the flight of crooked-taloned birds, which are by nature on the right and which on the left (*Prometheus Bound* 484–490, of which Merry quotes 484–487; see [[divination]]).

## Outside Homer

**Plato.** Socrates in the *Ion* names [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] “the seer of the line of Melampus” and gives the seer's art his vision of the suitors' doom, beside the eagle and the serpent of *Iliad* 12, as passages that belong to the seer “both to examine and to judge” (*Ion* 538e–539d; see [[plato-ion-the-seers-passages]]).

**Statius.** In Statius' augury before the war of the Seven, hawks exulting in their plunder are among the birds of ill omen that Melampus sees, with a vulture and the owls of the night (*Thebaid* 3.508–512; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagle-and-the-goose]] and [[statius]]).
