## The story

**The night.** The Trojans camp on the plain, and panic holds the Achaeans; their hearts are torn like the sea when the North Wind and the West Wind come down on it from Thrace (9.1–8; see [[two-winds-from-thrace]] and [[the-second-day-of-battle]]). Agamemnon has the heralds call each man to the assembly by name, without shouting, and stands up weeping like a spring of dark water (9.9–16; see [[the-spring-of-dark-water]]). Zeus has deceived him, he says, and he proposes in earnest the flight he proposed as a test in Book 2 (9.17–28; see [[the-test-of-the-army]]). After a long silence Diomedes answers that Agamemnon may go if he likes; the rest will stay (9.29–49; see [[diomedes]]). Nestor sets a watch outside the wall and tells Agamemnon to feast the elders and hear their counsel: “This night will either shatter the army or save it” (9.52–78; see [[night-watch]], [[nestor]] and [[this-night-will-shatter-or-save]]).

**The offer.** After the feast Nestor tells Agamemnon that when he took Briseis, against all advice, he dishonored “the best of men, whom the immortals themselves honored”, and that they must win him back “with kindly gifts and with soothing words” (9.96–113). Agamemnon admits it, “I was blinded” (ἀασάμην, 9.116; see [[ate]]), and names the gifts (9.115–157; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). At once there will be tripods, gold, cauldrons and horses, seven women of Lesbos, and Briseis, with an oath that he never lay with her. When Troy falls there will be a shipload of spoil and twenty Trojan women. At home Achilles may have one of Agamemnon's three daughters, without bride-price and with a dowry, and seven cities on the borders of Pylos (see [[the-seven-cities]]). Then the condition: “Let him be tamed … And let him submit to me, inasmuch as I am more kingly, and inasmuch as I claim to be the elder by birth” (9.158–161).

**The envoys.** Nestor chooses the men. Phoenix, dear to Zeus, is to lead the way, then great Ajax and brilliant Odysseus, with the heralds Odius and Eurybates (9.162–170; see [[phoenix]], [[ajax]], [[odysseus]], [[odius-the-herald]] and [[eurybates]]). They pray and pour libations, and Nestor gives them many instructions, “glancing at each of them, and at Odysseus most of all” (9.171–181).

**The welcome.**

{{quote:2422a6ae-8a96-5126-b386-278cfc3e6a3f}}

The lyre is a spoil of Eetion's city (see [[sack-of-thebe]], [[lyre]] and [[song-and-fame]]). Achilles springs up in amazement and greets them: “Welcome, both of you! Surely you come as friends; surely the need is great, you who are to me, angry as I am, the dearest of the Achaeans” (9.192–198). He seats them, has Patroclus mix the wine stronger, and with Patroclus and Automedon prepares a meal of mutton, goat and pork; Patroclus throws the gods' portion into the fire (9.199–221; see [[feast-type-scene]], [[automedon]] and [[xenia]]).

**Odysseus.** Ajax nods to Phoenix, but Odysseus sees it and speaks first (9.222–224). The ships are in danger, Zeus' lightning shows signs for the Trojans, and Hector waits for the dawn to burn the ships; Peleus told Achilles that “kindliness is better” than strife (9.225–261; see [[peleus]]). Odysseus repeats the gifts, “I will give him” (9.131) turned into “he will give you” (9.273), and stops before the condition. In its place he asks Achilles to pity the army, which will honor him like a god, and to win glory by killing Hector (9.262–306; see [[message-repeated]]).

**Achilles' reply.** Hateful to him as the gates of Hades is the man who hides one thing in his mind and says another (9.308–314; see [[achilles]]). There is no thanks for fighting: the coward and the brave are held in a single honor, and he has toiled for others like a bird bringing every morsel to her unfledged chicks (9.315–327; see [[the-bird-and-her-chicks]]). He sacked twenty-three cities and gave the spoil to Agamemnon, and from him alone Agamemnon took his prize, “the wife dear to my heart” (9.328–345; see [[achilles-raids]] and [[taking-of-briseis]]). Let Agamemnon save the ships with his wall; while Achilles fought, Hector would not come out beyond the Scaean gates and the oak (9.346–355; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[hectors-escape-at-the-oak]]). Tomorrow he will sail for Phthia (9.356–363). He hates the gifts. Not all the wealth of Orchomenus or of Egyptian Thebes, nor as much as the sand and the dust, would persuade him “until he has paid me back for all his heart-grieving insult” (9.378–387; see [[the-sand-and-the-dust]]). He will not marry Agamemnon's daughter (9.388–400). Nothing is worth his life, for a man's soul cannot be raided back like cattle (9.401–409; see [[psyche]] and [[a-mans-soul-cannot-come-back]]), and his mother has told him of two fates (see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]):

{{quote:47e1e08a-eeb7-5938-9759-5a184966aa85}}

The others should sail home too, for Zeus has held his hand over Troy. The envoys are to take his message to the chiefs, and Phoenix is to stay the night (9.417–429).

**Phoenix.** The old man speaks in tears. Peleus sent him with the boy to make him “a speaker of words and a doer of deeds” (9.430–443; see [[speaker-of-words-doer-of-deeds]]). He tells how his father cursed him with childlessness and he fled to Peleus, who loved him like an only son, and how he raised Achilles as his own (9.444–495; see [[the-flight-of-phoenix]] and [[as-a-father-loves-his-son]]). Even the gods can be turned. The Prayers, lame daughters of Zeus, follow behind Blind Folly to heal the harm she does, and they send her after the man who refuses them (9.496–512; see [[litai]]). Agamemnon has sent the men dearest to Achilles; the heroes of old could be won with gifts, and Meleager, who refused every suppliant, saved his city at last without the gifts (9.513–599; see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). Let him come while the gifts are offered (9.600–605).

**Achilles answers Phoenix.** He needs no such honor; he is honored by the portion of Zeus. Phoenix must not serve Agamemnon, but share Achilles' kingship, and at dawn they will consider whether to go home or stay (9.606–619). He nods to Patroclus to spread a bed for Phoenix (9.620–622).

**Ajax.** Ajax tells Odysseus that it is time to go. Achilles is savage and pitiless. Men accept a blood-price even for a brother or a son, but he holds out “for the sake of a girl, one girl”, when they offer seven; let him respect his own roof (9.622–642; see [[poine]] and [[aidos]]). Achilles grants that Ajax has spoken after his own heart, but his anger swells when he remembers how Agamemnon treated him, “as if I were some dishonored vagrant” (9.643–648; see [[like-a-dishonored-vagrant]]):

{{quote:c183e4a7-f4b4-5dfe-9c2a-a3c083aef940}}

**The night in the hut.** The envoys pour libations and go. Phoenix sleeps in the bed spread for him, Achilles beside Diomede, a woman he brought from Lesbos, and Patroclus beside Iphis, a captive from Scyros (9.656–668; see [[diomede]] and [[iphis]]).

**The report.** In Agamemnon's huts Odysseus reports the first answer. Achilles rejects Agamemnon and his gifts and will drag his ships to the sea at dawn; Ajax and the two heralds can say so too; Phoenix has stayed behind (9.669–692). After a long silence Diomedes speaks. Agamemnon should never have entreated him; Achilles will fight “when the heart in his breast bids him and a god rouses him”, and at dawn Agamemnon must lead the army himself. The kings approve and go to sleep (9.693–713).

## In the poem

**The frame.** The embassy fills the space between two meetings, each opened by a long silence and answered by Diomedes (9.29–31, 9.693–696). Nestor called it the night that would “shatter the army or save it” (9.78). It saves nothing, and it ends in sleep: Phoenix waits for the dawn in Achilles' hut (9.662), and the kings take “the gift of sleep” (9.713). Agamemnon cannot sleep (10.1–4), and the same night goes on in Book 10 (see [[war-and-night]]).

**“The two.”** The verbs of the walk and of the welcome are dual. “The two went” along the shore (βάτην, 9.182, 9.192) and “came” to the huts (ἱκέσθην, 9.185), and Achilles greets “the two” (τώ, 9.196) with dual verbs, χαίρετον, ἱκάνετον and ἐστον (9.197–198). Yet five men set out (9.168–170), and plurals stand beside the duals (εὗρον, 9.186; στάν, 9.193). Phoenix was told to lead (ἡγησάσθω, 9.168), but Odysseus leads the way in (9.192), and Phoenix is not named again until Ajax nods to him after the meal (9.223). Why Phoenix, Achilles' own man and later a leader of the Myrmidons (16.196), is in Agamemnon's hut at all is not said. Who “the two” are has long been debated, and the main answers are these:

- Ajax and Odysseus, the envoys who speak for the army. Phoenix either went ahead, as his order to lead may suggest, or is not counted because he belongs to Achilles' own household, and the heralds are attendants.
- The two heralds, the formal bearers of a message.
- No two in particular: the dual is used loosely for a plural, as when Hector names four horses and urges them on in the dual (8.185–191; compare 5.487).
- Traces of an older form of the story, in which Ajax and Odysseus went alone, and Phoenix was added later, by another poet, as Denys Page argued (*History and the Homeric Iliad*, 1959), or by the poet himself, who left the verbs as they were, as M. L. West argued (*The Making of the Iliad*, 2011).

Gregory Nagy has argued that the dual greeting takes in Phoenix and Ajax and leaves out Odysseus (*The Best of the Achaeans*, 1979). Any answer must reckon with Book 1. The walk is told in the verses that took the heralds Talthybius and Eurybates, a true pair, to fetch Briseis: “Unwilling, the two went along the shore of the barren sea, and came to the huts and ships of the Myrmidons” (1.327–328; compare 9.182, 9.185). Then Achilles was not glad to see them (1.330); now he springs up and calls the envoys his dearest friends. The path that took Briseis away is walked again to bring her back. Odysseus' report names its witnesses with a true dual: “Ajax and the two heralds, both wise men” (9.689).

**What Odysseus leaves out.** Odysseus repeats Agamemnon's list nearly word for word (9.264–299 ≈ 9.122–157) and stops before its last four lines, Hades, implacable and untamable, and the demand that Achilles submit (9.158–161; see the translation's note on 9.158–161). The silence is usually admired as tact. Achilles' first words have been read as an answer to it: “hateful to me as the gates of Hades is that man who hides one thing in his mind and says another” (9.312–313). Whom he means is not said: Odysseus, whose craft he knows; Agamemnon, whose deceit he names three times (9.344, 9.371, 9.375–376); or no one, as a declaration of his own plain speech (see [[hateful-as-the-gates-of-hades]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). Either way he answers the demand he was not told. Let Agamemnon find another son-in-law, “one who befits him and who is more kingly” (βασιλεύτερος, 9.392), the word of the demand (9.160).

**Three appeals, three answers.** Each envoy argues in his own way. Odysseus speaks for the army: danger, gifts, pity and glory. Phoenix speaks as a foster father, from his own life, from the Prayers, and from the story of Meleager, which he fits point by point to Achilles' case: the anger, the withdrawal, the suppliants coming one after another with the dearest last, and the gifts lost by waiting. Ajax speaks last and shortest, as a comrade, of friendship, of the blood-price that settles even a killing, and of the respect a host owes the guests under his roof. Achilles' answers move. To Odysseus he says that he will sail at dawn (9.356–363); to Phoenix, that at dawn they will consider whether to go or stay (9.618–619); to Ajax, that he will not think of war until Hector reaches the huts and ships of the Myrmidons (9.650–655), the place where the envoys found him (9.652; compare 9.185). The last answer is the one he keeps. In Book 16 he recalls it, “I said I would not end my wrath until the war cry and the battle reached my own ships” (16.61–63), and sends Patroclus out when the first ship is burning (16.122–129). It is also the term Zeus set at nightfall: Hector will not cease from war until Achilles is roused beside the ships (8.473–476; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). Diomedes' last word points the same way, and in Book 18 a god does rouse him: Iris comes from Hera with ὄρσεο Πηλεΐδη, “rouse yourself, son of Peleus” (18.166–180).

**The report.** Odysseus reports only the first answer, with Achilles' advice to the others and his word about Phoenix, in Achilles' own verses (9.682–692; 9.417–420, 9.427–429). He was told to report it “openly” (9.369–370); the later answers were spoken to Phoenix and to Ajax. Whether he keeps back the softer answers so as not to raise false hopes, or the poem lets the first answer stand for all, is debated (see the translation's note on 9.356–363). Diomedes takes it up as “whether he goes or stays” (9.701–702). The verse after the report, “for he had spoken very forcefully” (9.694), is the verse that followed Achilles' refusal with ἀγόρευσε in place of ἀπέειπεν, “refused” (9.431), and it suits the refusal better than the report (see the translation's note on 9.694).

**The refusal.** The offer is the richest in the poem, far more than the three times as many gifts that Athena promised in Book 1 (1.213; see [[triple-gifts-foretold]]). Achilles does not deny their worth. He denies that they can pay: “His gifts are hateful to me” (9.378), and nothing will move him “until he has paid me back for all his heart-grieving insult” (9.387; see [[honor-and-dishonor]]). Agamemnon offers them as ἀπερείσια ἄποινα, “a boundless ransom” (9.120), the word for the price that frees a captive (see [[apoina]]), and with them a demand for submission. Nestor had asked for amends “with kindly gifts and with soothing words” (9.113); Agamemnon sends the gifts without the words.

Achilles also goes beyond the quarrel. If the coward and the brave are held in one honor and die alike (9.318–320), and a man's life cannot be raided back (9.406–409), then nothing is worth his life (9.401). The complaint is not new: in Book 1 he said that he did most of the fighting for the smaller prize (1.163–168), and that the Trojans had done him no wrong (1.152–160; see [[war-for-menelaus]]). What is new is the conclusion, and the choice between two fates. Sarpedon reasons from the same premises to the opposite end: since death cannot be escaped, kings honored like gods must fight in the front (12.310–328). Adam Parry argued that Achilles has no language but that of the code he questions, and must misuse its words to say what it cannot (“The Language of Achilles”, *Transactions of the American Philological Association* 87, 1956); others answer that his words are the ordinary ones and only his conclusions are new. The next day, when Patroclus is dead, he makes the other choice: “now let me win noble fame” (18.114–121; see [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]).

**Suppliants and an old visit.** The envoys come as suppliants: Agamemnon “has sent the best men to entreat you” (9.520), and the Prayers are λιταί (see [[supplication]]). In Book 1 Achilles swore that the Achaeans would long for him and asked that Agamemnon might learn his blind folly (1.240–244, 1.411–412); both have come to pass, and he still refuses (see [[longing-for-achilles]]). The welcome also replays the visit that brought him to Troy. When Nestor and Odysseus came to Peleus' house to gather the army, Achilles “sprang up in amazement” (ταφὼν δʼ ἀνόρουσεν, 11.777 = 9.193), led them in and fed them, and was persuaded to come (11.769–782). Odysseus, who heard Peleus' parting charge that day, now quotes it back to him (9.252–259; see [[peleus-sends-achilles-to-troy]]).

**The embassy remembered.** Later Achilles sometimes speaks as though the embassy had not come. Watching the rout from his ship, he tells Patroclus: “Now I think the Achaeans will stand around my knees beseeching me; for a need has come that is no longer bearable” (11.608–610). In Book 16 the Trojans would fill the gullies with their dead “if powerful Agamemnon were kindly disposed to me” (16.72–73), and Patroclus is to win him honor, so that the Danaans “send back the very beautiful girl, and give splendid gifts besides” (16.84–86). Analytic critics took these lines as proof that Book 9 was added to a poem that did not know it; George Grote made it a later addition to an older poem of Achilles (*History of Greece*, vol. 2, 1846), and Page renewed the argument (1959). Others answer that the lines fit the story Book 9 tells: Achilles wants the army at his knees in its need, not Agamemnon's offer on Agamemnon's terms. And the poem does remember the embassy. Achilles recalls his answer to Ajax (16.61–63); Thetis tells Hephaestus that “the elders of the Argives begged him and named many glorious gifts” (18.448–450), in the words of the Meleager story (τὸν δὲ λίσσοντο γέροντες, 18.448 = 9.574); and Agamemnon offers “all the gifts that brilliant Odysseus came yesterday and promised you in your huts” (19.140–141) and gives them, with Briseis (19.243–248). Most modern readers take Book 9 as part of the poem's design and explain the later lines from Achilles' state of mind, but the question remains open.

## Outside Homer

Book 9 has two traditional titles, Πρεσβεία πρὸς Ἀχιλλέα, “the embassy to Achilles”, and Λιταί, “the Prayers”; the oldest witnesses, Plato and Strabo, use the second (see [[title-of-book-9]]). Plato's Hippias cites Achilles' first words to Odysseus as spoken “in the Prayers” (ἐν Λιταῖς), to show that Homer made Achilles the truest and simplest of men and Odysseus a liar; Socrates answers that Achilles told Odysseus he would sail at dawn, told Ajax something else, and never sailed (*Hippias Minor* 364e–365b, 369e–371e; see [[plato-hippias-minor]]). In the *Republic* Socrates will not praise Phoenix for advising Achilles to take the gifts and help the Achaeans, and not to give up his wrath without them (3.390e; see [[plato-phoenix-and-the-gifts]]). Aeschylus put the withdrawal and the refusal on the stage in his lost *Myrmidons*; how the play brought on the envoys and the gifts, the fragments do not say (see [[aeschylus-myrmidons]]).
