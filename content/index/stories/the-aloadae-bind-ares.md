## The story

{{quote:b885b05d-20ba-552b-a3ba-d7982bb9a108}}

Otus and Ephialtes are called only “the sons of Aloeus” (παῖδες Ἀλωῆος), from which later writers named them the Aloadae (see [[otus]], [[ephialtes]] and [[aloeus]]). They bind Ares “in strong bonds”, and he lies bound in a κέραμος, a jar, made of bronze. The word is used elsewhere for the great jars in which wine was stored (9.469). The time is precise, “thirteen months”, a year and more, and the prisoner is near death: “and now Ares, insatiable of war, would have perished there” (5.388). The rescuer is an unexpected one, the brothers' stepmother (μητρυιή) Eeriboea, who tells Hermes (see [[eeriboea]]), and the rescue is a theft: Hermes “stole Ares away” (ἐξέκλεψεν, 5.390), when “the cruel bonds were overpowering him” (5.391; see [[hermes]]). Hermes is the thief among the gods; in the last book of the Iliad the gods urge him to steal Hector's body from Achilles (24.23–24).

Homer says nothing of why the brothers bound Ares, where they kept him, or what became of them after.

## In the poem

**A paradigm of suffering.** The story is the first of three that Dione tells her daughter, wounded by Diomedes: “for many of us who have our homes on Olympus have endured cruel pains at the hands of men, bringing them on one another” (5.383–384). Ares, Hera and Hades suffered at the hands of mortals, and each is introduced by the same verb, τλῆ, “endured” (5.385, 5.392, 5.395; see [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]] and [[mythological-paradigm]]). In Dione's telling the sons of Aloeus are among the “men” at whose hands the gods have suffered (ἐξ ἀνδρῶν, 5.384), though in the Odyssey they are the huge sons of Poseidon.

**Ares, of all gods.** The first example is the god Aphrodite has just gone to for help, whose chariot brought her to Olympus (5.355–369). It is also the god whom the same mortal will wound before the book is out, and who will then tell Zeus that but for his swift feet he would have suffered long among the heaps of the dead, “or lived on strengthless from the blows of the bronze” (5.885–887; see [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]). The war god who is always eager for battle is twice the god whom mortals nearly destroy.

**Would have perished.** “And now Ares … would have perished there, had not …” (5.388–390) is the turn the narrator used seventy lines earlier for Aeneas: “And now Aeneas, lord of men, would have perished there, had not Aphrodite … been quick to see it” (5.311–312; see [[would-have-had-not]]). The formula of a mortal's rescue is used of a god, and it is the only place in Homer where a god is said to have come near perishing (ἀπόλοιτο). The old stories of the gods' sufferings went further than the Iliad's own narrative does, where a wounded god is healed at once.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Odyssey tells more about the brothers. Among the women Odysseus sees in the underworld is Iphimedeia, the wife of Aloeus, who said that she lay with Poseidon, and bore two sons, short-lived, “godlike Otus and far-famed Ephialtes”, the tallest and most beautiful men the earth ever reared after Orion. At nine years old they were nine cubits broad and nine fathoms tall. They threatened to bring war to the gods on Olympus, and they would have piled Ossa on Olympus and Pelion on Ossa, to climb to heaven, if they had reached their prime; but Apollo killed them both before the down grew on their cheeks (Odyssey 11.305–320).

## Outside Homer

Apollodorus joins the two Homeric stories. The sons of Poseidon and Iphimedeia grew a cubit in breadth and a fathom in height every year; at nine they set out to fight the gods, piled the mountains, and wooed Hera and Artemis; they bound Ares, and Hermes stole him away. Artemis destroyed them on Naxos by a trick: she changed herself into a deer and ran between them, and in trying to hit her they killed each other with their spears (Apollodorus 1.7.4).
