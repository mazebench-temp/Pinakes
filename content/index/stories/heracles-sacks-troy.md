## The story

{{quote:7400d433-c36f-5e27-9b06-3e4f3c1393ad}}

Tlepolemus tells the story in three lines: Heracles came “for the sake of the horses of Laomedon”, with only six ships and fewer men, “and sacked the city of Ilios, and widowed its streets” (5.640–642). χήρωσε, “widowed”, is from χήρη, “widow”: the men of the city were killed, and their wives left without husbands.

Sarpedon supplies the cause.

{{quote:d6044c66-71e8-5a17-93fe-509749bb8127}}

Heracles had done Laomedon a good turn (εὖ ἕρξαντα), and the king repaid him with harsh words and did not hand over (ἀπέδωκε, “give what is due”) the horses he had come for (see [[laomedon]]). What the service was, the Iliad does not say. It does mention the sea monster from which Heracles was sheltered. When the gods gather to watch the fighting in Book 20, Poseidon leads his party to “the high wall of godlike Heracles”, which the Trojans and Pallas Athena built for him so that he could escape the monster (κῆτος) when it rushed from the shore onto the plain (20.144–148).

The Iliad does not say which horses they were, but Laomedon owned the famous breed that Zeus gave his grandfather Tros as the price of Ganymede, “the best of all horses under the dawn and the sun”, and Anchises bred a strain from it without his knowledge (5.265–273; see [[ganymede-and-the-horses-of-tros]] and [[anchises-steals-the-horses]]). Apollodorus makes the horses Heracles was promised the ones Zeus gave for Ganymede (2.5.9).

Heracles did not come home easily. When he sailed from Ilios after sacking the city, Hera sent storms against him and drove him to Cos, and Zeus punished her for it (14.249–256, 15.24–30).

## In the poem

**Two uses of one story.** Tlepolemus tells the sack to shame Sarpedon: his own father, a true son of Zeus, took Troy with six ships, and Sarpedon, who claims to be a son of Zeus, cannot even defend it (5.633–646). Sarpedon grants the fact and changes its meaning. The city fell because its king broke faith with a benefactor, not because a few men were stronger than it (5.648–651). Each speaker takes the version he needs (see [[tlepolemus-and-sarpedon]] and [[mythological-paradigm]]).

**Laomedon's bad faith.** The Iliad remembers Laomedon as a king who cheats those who serve him. He refused Poseidon and Apollo the wage they had earned by a year's work, building the wall and herding his cattle, and sent them off with threats (21.441–457). Heracles is another of his creditors, and the horses were his wage.

**The first sack and the last.** The poem never reaches the sack that the Achaeans have come for, though its people speak of it again and again (see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). The fullest account it gives of a fall of Troy is of the first one, told here; Book 14 alludes to it again (14.250–251). In both wars the Trojans are in the wrong in the same way: Laomedon broke faith with the man who had served him, and Alexander with the man who had received him as a guest (3.351–354; see [[the-abduction-of-helen]]).

## Outside Homer

Apollodorus tells the whole story. Apollo and Poseidon, cheated of their wages, sent a plague and a sea monster; an oracle said the city would be saved if Laomedon exposed his daughter Hesione to the monster. Heracles, passing on his way back from the Amazons, killed it for the promise of the mares that Zeus had given for Ganymede, and when Laomedon refused them he threatened war (2.5.9). He came back with eighteen ships of fifty oars, not six. Telamon was the first to break through the wall, Heracles killed Laomedon and all his sons but Podarces, and Hesione ransomed her brother, who was then called Priam (2.6.4). The first and second wars against Troy were carved on the two pediments of the temple of Aphaia on Aegina, early in the fifth century BCE; on the east pediment Heracles, the archer, fights beside Telamon.
