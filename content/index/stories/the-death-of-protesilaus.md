## The story

{{quote:18c19717-694e-5eba-ac7d-260fa39432fa}}

Homer tells it in four lines. Protesilaus leapt from his ship "by far the first of the Achaeans", and "a Dardanian man" killed him. The killer is left unnamed.

## In the poem

**The dead leader.** The Catalogue counts the army ship by ship, but here it looks past the landing to what came after. Protesilaus led his men "while he lived". The contingent is listed under a dead man, and then under the brother who replaced him (2.703–709).

**The house.** ἀμφιδρυφής, "torn on both sides", from δρύπτω ("tear, scratch"): the wife has torn both her cheeks in mourning. A related adjective returns when Diomedes boasts that the wife of any man he hits has cheeks "torn on both sides" (ἀμφίδρυφοι), and his children are orphans (11.393). ἡμιτελής, "half-finished", has been read in two ways: a house left half-built, or a household left incomplete. Either way the image is of a life cut off before its time.

**Three absent leaders.** Protesilaus is one of three leaders whom the Catalogue marks as missing from the field. [[Achilles]] lies idle by his ships (2.686–694), [[Philoctetes]] lies wounded on Lemnos (2.721–725), and Protesilaus lies under the earth. The contingents of Protesilaus and Philoctetes are introduced with the same line: "nor were they without a leader, though they longed for their leader" (2.703, 2.726). Achilles' Myrmidons have no one to lead them at all (2.687).

## Outside Homer

In Proclus' summary of the *Cypria*, the Trojans oppose the landing "and Protesilaus is killed by Hector". Later writers add that an oracle had said the first to land would be the first to die (Hyginus, *Fabulae* 103). For his wife, Laodameia, and his shrine at Elaeus, see [[protesilaus]].
