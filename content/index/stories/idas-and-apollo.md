## The story

{{quote:3cb79c24-ee08-56f4-b333-21499a986205}}

The story is told in a parenthesis. Phoenix names Meleager's wife, her parents lead him to their story, and after eight lines he returns to Meleager “beside her” (9.565). What happened is clear in outline and not in its details. Apollo “snatched away” Marpessa (ἀνήρπασε, 9.564), the verb used of storm winds that carry people off (*Odyssey* 4.515, 5.419, 20.63). Idas, her husband, took up his bow against the god “for the sake of the bride of the lovely ankles” (9.560). Phoenix does not say whether he shot, or how she came back to him. She did come back, since Cleopatra is the child of both (9.557–558). In her grief she had “the lot of the halcyon bird of many sorrows” (9.563), and so her parents called their daughter Alcyone (9.562). τότε, “in those days” (9.561), and καλέεσκον, “used to call”, put the naming in the time of her grief (see [[halcyon]], [[marpessa]], [[idas]] and [[cleopatra]]).

## In the poem

The aside gives Meleager's wife a family of strong feeling: a father who stood against a god for his wife, a mother carried off who mourned like the halcyon. The daughter of that lament is the one whose tears at last move Meleager (9.590–596). It is also the story of a man who took up arms when a stronger one took his woman, told to a man who reached for his sword when Agamemnon threatened to take his (1.188–222), and who has just said that “any man who is good and sensible / loves his own and cares for her” (9.341–342). Homer does not draw the comparison. Idas belongs, like Nestor's Lapiths, to an earlier and stronger generation (1.266), and like Lycurgus and Eurytus he is a man who faced a god (see [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]], [[who-fights-the-gods]] and [[apollo]]). Unlike them he is not said to have been punished.

## Outside Homer

[[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] tells the whole story (*Library* 1.7.8–9). Apollo was courting Marpessa when Idas, son of Aphareus, carried her off in a winged chariot that Poseidon had given him; her father Evenus pursued them and, unable to overtake them, drowned himself in the river that took his name (see [[evenus-father-of-marpessa]]). In Messene Apollo met Idas and took the girl from him. They fought over her until Zeus parted them and let her choose, and she chose Idas, fearing that Apollo would leave her when she grew old. On the chest of Cypselus at Olympia, Idas was shown leading her home, with a verse that calls her, as Homer does, “Marpessa of the lovely ankles”, whom Apollo “snatched away”, and says that she came “not unwilling” (Pausanias 5.18.2).
