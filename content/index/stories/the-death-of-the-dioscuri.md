## The story

Helen has named Agamemnon, Odysseus, Ajax and Idomeneus for Priam, and she could name all the others. Two men she looks for and cannot see.

{{quote:70e03702-ee22-558f-a91f-bbf93bc38989}}

Helen gives two explanations: either her brothers did not come from Lacedaemon, or they came but will not enter the battle “for fear of the shame and the many reproaches that are mine” (αἴσχεα … καὶ ὀνείδεα, 3.242). The narrator gives the true one. They are dead, and buried in their own country. The Iliad says no more: not how they died, nor when, nor whether they died before or after Helen left.

## In the poem

**What the narrator knows.** The two lines at the end are spoken over the heads of everyone on the wall. Helen does not know, Priam does not know, and the audience is told. The narrator uses the gap to show Helen's mind. Her first thought about her brothers' absence is that they are ashamed of her (see [[aischros]] and [[oneidos]]), the self-reproach that runs through all her speeches in the book (3.173–180, 3.404, 3.410–412).

**One mother.** Helen calls them “my own brothers, whom one mother bore with me” (αὐτοκασιγνήτω, τώ μοι μία γείνατο μήτηρ, 3.238). Homer calls Helen the daughter of Zeus (3.199, 3.418), but gives no father for the brothers, and the Odyssey makes them sons of Tyndareus (Odyssey 11.298–300). “One mother” leaves room for two fathers. See [[castor]], [[polydeuces]] and [[helen]].

**The life-giving earth.** The earth that holds them fast is φυσίζοος, “life-giving” (see [[life-giving-earth]]). The same words, ἤδη … κάτεχεν (“already held fast”), were used of Protesilaus, the first Achaean killed at Troy: he led his men “while he lived; but by now the black earth held him fast” (ἤδη ἔχεν κάτα γαῖα μέλαινα, 2.699). The epithet was praised by Ruskin as the mark of a poet who grieves without letting grief change what he sees (see [[ruskin-life-giving-earth]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Odyssey knows more. In the underworld Odysseus sees Leda, the wife of Tyndareus, who bore Castor, tamer of horses, and Polydeuces, good with his fists, in the same line as Iliad 3.237. “The life-giving earth holds them both, alive”: they have honor from Zeus even under the earth, and live and die on alternate days, with honor equal to the gods' (Odyssey 11.298–304). The Iliad's line says only that the earth held them.

## Outside Homer

The *Cypria*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, told the death. While Paris was taking Helen to Troy, Castor and Polydeuces were caught stealing the cattle of Idas and Lynceus. Idas killed Castor, and Polydeuces killed Lynceus and Idas; Zeus then granted the brothers immortality on alternate days. On this account they died after Helen had left Sparta, which suits her not knowing.

Pindar tells the fight at length. Lynceus, whose sight was the sharpest of men, saw the brothers from Mount Taygetus hiding in a hollow oak, and Idas struck Castor with his spear; Polydeuces killed Lynceus, and Zeus struck Idas with his thunderbolt. Polydeuces, the son of Zeus, would not live forever while his brother, the son of Tyndareus, was dead, and Zeus let them share one life: a day with their father on Olympus, a day under the earth at Therapne (*Nemean* 10.55–90). In Theocritus' hymn to the Dioscuri the quarrel is over the daughters of Leucippus, the brides of Idas and Lynceus, whom the brothers had carried off (*Idyll* 22). The *Homeric Hymns* to the Dioscuri call both brothers sons of Zeus and Leda, born under Taygetus, and saviors of sailors in storms (*Hymns* 17 and 33).
