## The story

{{quote:3d3eb9d9-432c-5e1a-8b5b-b5977726e84c}}

The description is a horse-breeder's. ὄτριχας, "alike in coat" (ὁ- "same" + θρίξ "hair"); οἰέτεας, "alike in age" (ὁ- + ἔτος "year"); σταφύλῃ ἐπὶ νῶτον ἐΐσας, "their backs level by the builder's line", where σταφύλη is the mason's level. ποδώκεας ὄρνιθας ὥς, "swift-footed like birds" (see [[swift-as-birds]]). They are both mares (θηλείας), and they carry φόβον Ἄρηος, "the panic of Ares", the rout that sends an enemy running.

Apollo "reared them in [[Pereia]]". Homer does not say why. Later tradition had an answer: Apollo once served Admetus, Eumelus' father, as a herdsman for a year (Euripides, *Alcestis* 1–9; Apollodorus 3.10.4). Homer knows of the god serving a mortal in this way, for Apollo herded the cattle of Laomedon of Troy (21.448–449).

## In the poem

The narrator asks the Muse "who was the best of them, of the men and of the horses who followed the sons of Atreus" (2.761–762), and the horses come first. The answer to both halves is at once corrected: the best horses were those of Achilles, and the best man Achilles himself, while he kept up his wrath (2.769–770; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). Achilles' horses, Xanthus and Balius, are immortal, the children of the West Wind (16.148–151).

The mares have their race in Book 23. At the funeral games of Patroclus, Eumelus leads the chariot race until Apollo knocks the whip from Diomedes' hand; Athena gives it back and breaks the yoke of Eumelus' mares, and he comes in last (23.375–397). "The best man is driving his horses in last," says Achilles (23.536). The Catalogue's praise and Apollo's care both come to nothing against Athena.
