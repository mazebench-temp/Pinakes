## The story

{{quote:73cfa874-a10e-543d-9039-31f9bfc95fdf}}

Athena tells the story in eight lines, and as a grievance. The verbs of the first part are iterative: she “used to save” Zeus' son (σώεσκον) when he was worn out by the labors that Eurystheus set him (ὑπʼ Εὐρυσθῆος ἀέθλων); he “would weep” to the sky (κλαίεσκε), and Zeus “would send” her down to help him (προΐαλλεν, 8.362–365). It happened many times (μάλα πολλάκις), and she names one time. Eurystheus sent Heracles “to the house of Hades the gatekeeper”, εἰς Ἀΐδαο πυλάρταο, “to bring from Erebus the hound of hateful Hades”; and if she had known then what she knows now, “he would never have escaped the sheer streams of the water of Styx” (8.366–369; see [[eurystheus]], [[hades]], [[erebus]] and [[styx]]). πυλάρτης, “the gatekeeper”, “the one who shuts the gates”, is Hades' epithet also at 13.415 and *Odyssey* 11.277 (see [[gatekeeper]]). Ἔρεβος is the darkness of the underworld, and the Styx, by whose water the gods swear their great oath, is a river of the underworld (2.755, 14.271, 15.37; *Odyssey* 10.513–514). Homer does not name the dog or describe it.

The Odyssey gives the other side of the story. Among the dead Odysseus meets the image of Heracles, bow in hand, and the shade greets him as a fellow sufferer: he was the son of Zeus, he says, but he had endless misery, for he was made subject to a far worse man, who set him hard labors; “and once he sent me here to fetch the dog, for he could think of no harder labor for me; I brought it up and led it out of Hades, and Hermes guided me, and gray-eyed Athena” (*Odyssey* 11.617–626; see [[hermes]]).

## In the poem

**A complaint, not a boast.** Hera has proposed that the two goddesses go down to help the Achaeans, and Athena answers with anger against her father, who “rages with a mind that is not good” (8.358–361). The labors are her proof. She helped his son whenever Zeus sent her, and he does not remember (8.362); now he hates her and has fulfilled the plans of Thetis (8.370–372; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). The past service that gave Thetis her claim on Zeus (1.393–406) has given Athena none. Her regret is harsh: had she known, she would have left Heracles in the underworld. In Book 5 Dione told a darker story of the same hero, who shot Hera and Hades (5.392–404), and one reading of her words puts the wounding of Hades “at the gate”, on this same descent (see [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]] and [[pylos-or-the-gate]]).

**Told to Hera.** Athena tells it to the goddess who made Heracles subject to Eurystheus. In Book 19 Agamemnon tells how Hera tricked Zeus on the day Heracles was to be born, so that Eurystheus was born first and became his master, and how Zeus groaned whenever he saw his son at the shameful labors Eurystheus set him (19.95–133; see [[heracles]] and [[hera]]). The two goddesses who drive out together against Zeus in Book 8 had been on opposite sides in his son's life.

## Outside Homer

[[hesiod|Hesiod]] names the dog. Cerberus is a child of Typhaon and Echidna, “the eater of raw flesh, the bronze-voiced dog of Hades, fifty-headed, shameless and strong” (*Theogony* 310–312). He guards the house of Hades and Persephone, fawning with his tail and both his ears on those who go in, but he lets no one out again, and devours anyone he catches going out through the gates (*Theogony* 767–773). In later tradition the descent for Cerberus is the last of the twelve labors. [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] tells how Heracles was initiated at Eleusis, went down at Taenarum, freed Theseus, mastered the dog with his bare hands, showed it to Eurystheus and took it back to Hades (*Library* 2.5.12). In [[euripides|Euripides]]' *Heracles* the hero comes home from this descent to find his wife and children about to be put to death.
