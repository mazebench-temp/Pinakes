## The story

{{quote:2ee77242-ccd1-5199-a52d-f4140299d2e0}}

Achilles tells it to Odysseus in his answer to the embassy, as the measure of what his absence has cost. Agamemnon has toiled at much without him: he has built a wall and driven a ditch along it and planted stakes in it, “but not even so can he hold the strength of man-slaying Hector in check” (9.348–352; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). While Achilles fought, Hector would not rouse the battle far from the wall of Troy but came only as far as the Scaean gates and the oak, “there once he waited for me alone, and barely escaped my charge” (9.353–355; see [[scaean-gates]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). οἶον, “alone”, means that Hector waited for him in single combat (see the translation's note on 9.352–355). Nothing more is said: not when it was, nor how Hector escaped. The Iliad never narrates the meeting.

## In the poem

**The years before.** Other speakers describe those years in the same way. Hera, in the likeness of Stentor, shames the Argives: as long as Achilles went to war, the Trojans never came out even before the Dardanian gates, for they dreaded his heavy spear, and now they fight far from the city, by the ships (5.788–791; see [[dardanian-gates]]). Agamemnon, holding Menelaus back from Hector's challenge, claims that even Achilles “shudders to meet this man” (7.113–114). That is usually taken as an exaggeration meant to keep his brother out of the fight (see the translation's note on 7.114). Achilles' account is the one the poem bears out: since he withdrew, Hector has driven the Achaeans back to their ships and camped on the plain (8.335–349, 8.489–565).

**The place.** “The Scaean gates and the oak” mark the edge of the city. Hector came there on his way into Troy in Book 6, in the same half-verse (6.237), and the Trojans in flight halt there in Book 11 (11.170). It is where the war passes from the plain to the walls.

**The meeting to come.** The story looks forward. When Achilles returns to the fighting, he meets Hector in Book 20, and Apollo snatches Hector away in a mist; Achilles shouts after him, “Again you have escaped death, dog” (20.419–454), in a verse Diomedes has used of Hector before (11.362). In Book 22 Hector waits for Achilles once more before the Scaean gates, alone, when the other Trojans have fled into the city (22.5–6, 22.35–36, 22.92–97). This time he does not escape (see [[hector]] and [[achilles]]).
