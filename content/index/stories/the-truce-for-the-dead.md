## The story

**Nestor's proposal.** At the feast after the duel, Nestor reminds the kings that many long-haired Achaeans are dead, their blood scattered along the Scamander and their souls gone down to Hades (7.327–330). He proposes a pause in the fighting:

{{quote:8180886d-2a4f-5a83-986a-a6217206cd25}}

A single mound is to be heaped over the pyre, and a wall built against it (7.336–343; see [[the-building-of-the-wall]]). The kings approve (7.344).

**The Trojan request.** On the same evening the Trojans meet in a stormy assembly at Priam's doors. Antenor urges them to give back Helen and her possessions, and Paris refuses to give up Helen, though he will return the possessions and add more of his own (7.345–364; see [[war-for-menelaus]]). Priam sends his herald Idaeus to the ships at dawn with Paris' offer, and with a second word: will the Achaeans cease from war “until we have burned the dead; afterward we will fight again, until a god decides between us and gives victory to one side or the other” (7.368–378; see [[priam]] and [[idaeus]]).

**Agamemnon's answer.** Idaeus finds the Achaeans in assembly by Agamemnon's ship and repeats the message, adding a wish of his own that Paris had perished first (7.381–397; see [[message-repeated]]). The Achaeans are silent, until Diomedes says that no one should take Paris' possessions, or even Helen, for the Trojans' destruction is plain to a fool (7.398–402; see [[even-a-fool-can-see]]). The army shouts its approval. Agamemnon refuses the offer but grants the truce:

{{quote:0da0fd8b-f4c8-5a39-a114-50f7a5a3d910}}

He lifts his scepter to all the gods, and Idaeus goes back to Ilios (7.412–413; see [[scepter]]).

**The burning.** The Trojans, waiting in assembly, make ready at once, some to bring in the dead and some to fetch wood, and the Argives do the same from their ships (7.414–420). At sunrise the two parties meet on the plain (7.421–423).

## In the poem

**Two peoples alike.** The burning is told twice, first for the Trojans and then for the Achaeans, in the same words: “some to bring the dead, and others to go for wood” (7.418, 7.420), and “heaped the dead on the pyre, grieving at heart, and when they had burned them in the fire they went back” (7.428–429, 7.431–432). The repetition makes the two armies one in their work and their grief. It is hard to tell one dead man from another until the blood is washed away (7.424–425). The Trojans weep “hot tears” (7.426), but Priam forbids them to wail aloud, and they heap their dead on the pyre in silence; the Achaeans do the same “in the same way” (7.427–431). The poem does not say why Priam forbids the wailing. The two armies work in silence on the same plain (see [[honoring-the-dead]], [[cremation]], [[pyres]] and [[lament]]).

**The oaths kept.** “As for the oaths, let Zeus be witness” (7.411). The truce is the first agreement sworn since Pandarus broke the truce of Book 3, which the Trojans themselves now admit they betrayed (7.351–352; see [[pandarus-breaks-the-truce]], [[oath]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]). This one is kept. Agamemnon grants it with a maxim: one should not grudge the dead “the quick comfort of fire” (7.409–410; see [[the-comfort-of-fire]]). The Trojans ask for a truce and are granted it, but the Achaeans had planned the same pause (7.331–332), and it serves them. It gives them the day to burn their dead and the next night to build their wall.

**The bones.** Nestor wants each man to carry the bones of the dead home to their children (7.334–335). Some ancient critics rejected the two lines: the Achaean dead are then buried together under one mound at Troy (7.336, 7.435), and elsewhere in the Iliad the bones of the dead stay at Troy (4.174–175; 23.83–92, 23.243–248). The custom they describe resembles the Athenian one of bringing home the bones of the war dead (see the translation's note on 7.334–335, and [[bones-for-the-children]]).

**The last book.** The Iliad has one other truce to bury the dead. Priam asks Achilles for eleven days in which to mourn Hector, bury him and heap his mound, and Achilles holds back the war for that time (24.656–670). Then the Trojans yoke oxen and mules and bring in wood for nine days, and Hector is burned and his bones gathered and buried (24.777–804). The truce of Book 7 is made between armies, and its dead are burned in silence; the truce of Book 24 is made between two men, and its dead man is mourned in the laments with which the poem ends (24.719–776).

## Outside Homer

In classical Greece a truce to take up the dead was a fixed custom of war. The side that asked for its dead by herald admitted defeat, and to refuse the dead their burial was an outrage. After the battle of Delium the Boeotians would not give up the Athenian dead while the Athenians held the sanctuary there (Thucydides 4.97–101), and Euripides' *Suppliants* turns on Thebes' refusal to let the Argive dead of the Seven be buried. The Athenians brought home the bones of their war dead and buried them in a public tomb (Thucydides 2.34; see [[thucydides]]). An ancient title of Book 7 names the truce: νεκρῶν ἀναίρεσις, “the taking up of the dead” (see [[title-of-book-7]]). Virgil gives the Latins a truce of twelve days to bury their dead in the eleventh book of the *Aeneid* (see [[virgil-truce-for-the-dead]]).
