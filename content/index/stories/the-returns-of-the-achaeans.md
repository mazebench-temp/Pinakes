## The story

Homer never tells it whole. The fullest accounts are Nestor's and Menelaus' to Telemachus in the third and fourth books of the Odyssey; the renderings of the fourth and later books here are plain renderings, not the translation.

**The anger.** Nestor begins at the sack: a god scattered the Achaeans, and Zeus devised for the Argives a grim homecoming (λυγρὸν … νόστον), since not all of them were discerning or just; many met an evil fate through the deadly wrath of the gray-eyed daughter of the mighty father, who set the two sons of Atreus at strife (*Odyssey* 3.130–136; see [[athena]] and [[zeus]]). Menelaus wanted to sail at once, Agamemnon to stay and appease Athena's anger with hecatombs, and the army split in two (*Odyssey* 3.137–157; see [[the-departure-of-the-greeks]]).

**Those who came home.** At Tenedos strife broke out again, and Odysseus turned back with his ships to Agamemnon (*Odyssey* 3.159–164). Nestor and Diomedes crossed the open sea toward Euboea at a sign from the god, sacrificed to Poseidon at Geraestus, and reached home, Diomedes' men at Argos on the fourth day and Nestor at Pylos, with a fair wind all the way (*Odyssey* 3.165–183; see [[nestor]] and [[diomedes]]). Of the rest Nestor knows only what he has heard: Achilles' son brought the Myrmidons home safely, so did Philoctetes, and Idomeneus brought all his companions back to Crete (*Odyssey* 3.184–192; see [[neoptolemus]], [[philoctetes]] and [[idomeneus]]); and Agamemnon came home to be murdered by Aegisthus (*Odyssey* 3.193–198; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]).

**Menelaus.** He sailed with Nestor until Apollo killed his helmsman, Phrontis, son of Onetor, at Sunium (*Odyssey* 3.276–285; see [[phrontis-son-of-onetor]] and [[sunium]]). Off Malea Zeus sent a storm; some of his ships were broken on the rocks of Crete, and five were carried to Egypt (*Odyssey* 3.286–300; see [[malea]]). He wandered among men of another tongue, gathering wealth, and came home in the eighth year, on the day Orestes gave the funeral feast for Aegisthus (*Odyssey* 3.301–312; 4.81–85; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]], [[menelaus]] and [[egypt]]). The gods held him in Egypt because he had not offered them perfect hecatombs, and he came home only when he had made them and heaped a mound for Agamemnon (*Odyssey* 4.351–352, 4.581–586).

**The lost.** In Egypt Proteus, the old man of the sea, told Menelaus that of the Achaean leaders only two had perished on the homecoming, and one was alive but kept on the wide sea (*Odyssey* 4.495–498). Locrian Ajax was wrecked; Poseidon brought him to the Gyrean rocks and saved him from the sea, though Athena hated him, but when he boasted that he had escaped the gulf of the sea against the gods' will, Poseidon split the rock with his trident and he drowned (*Odyssey* 4.499–511; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). The other was Agamemnon (*Odyssey* 4.512–537), and the one alive was Odysseus, held by the nymph Calypso with no ships and no companions to take him home (*Odyssey* 4.555–560; see [[calypso]]).

## In the poem

The returns frame the Odyssey. It begins when they are over, “all the others” who escaped are at home, and Odysseus alone is held (*Odyssey* 1.11–13; see [[nostos]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). Telemachus' journey takes him to two of the men who came home, Nestor first and then Menelaus, the last of them (*Odyssey* 1.284–286; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]), and what he hears of his father he hears inside their stories of their own homecomings. The murder of Agamemnon is the return the poem tells most often, set against that of Odysseus (see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]).

## Outside Homer

The returns were the subject of the *Returns* (Νόστοι) of the Epic Cycle, known from Proclus' summary (see [[proclus]]). J. G. Frazer cites the poem, which he ascribes to Hagias, “summarized by Proclus”, for the quarrel of the sons of Atreus, the death of Locrian Ajax and the murder of Agamemnon; he thinks that it probably told of the false lights of Nauplius too, though the summary mentions merely “the storm at the Capherian Rocks” (*Apollodorus: The Library*, 1921, on *Epitome* 6.1, 6.6, 6.7, 6.23).

In the *Library* of Apollodorus, after the quarrel Diomedes and Nestor have a good voyage, and Menelaus loses all but five ships in a storm and reaches Egypt (*Epitome* 6.1). The rest of the fleet is caught by a storm off Tenos, for Athena has begged Zeus to send a tempest against the Greeks; she strikes Ajax's ship with a thunderbolt, and when he reaches a rock and declares that he is saved in spite of her, Poseidon splits it with his trident, and Thetis buries his body on Myconos (*Epitome* 6.5–6.6). The others are driven onto Euboea by night and wrecked on the Capherian rocks, lured by the beacon that Nauplius lights on Mount Caphareus (*Epitome* 6.7). At the sack Ajax had violated Cassandra as she clung to the wooden image of Athena (*Epitome* 5.22; see [[cassandra]] and [[apollodorus]]), and Merry takes that outrage for the cause of the anger of which Phemius sings (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.326). In Virgil, Juno remembers that Pallas burned the Argive fleet and drowned the men “for the guilt and madness of one man, Ajax son of Oileus”: she hurled the fire of Jupiter from the clouds and scattered the ships, and caught Ajax up in a whirlwind, breathing flames from his pierced breast, and impaled him on a sharp rock (*Aeneid* 1.39–45; see [[virgil]]).
