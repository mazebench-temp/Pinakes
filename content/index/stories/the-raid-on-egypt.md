## The story

**To Egypt.** “But for me, wretched man, Zeus the counselor devised evils” (*Odyssey* 14.243). The Cretan of the tale to Eumaeus has been home from Troy only a month, with his children, his wedded wife and his possessions, when “my heart bade me sail to Egypt” (*Odyssey* 14.244–246; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]] and [[egypt]]). He fits out nine ships, feasts his companions for six days with many victims for the gods, and on the seventh sails from wide Crete with a fresh and fair North Wind, “easily, as if downstream”, with no ship harmed and no man sick; on the fifth day they come to the fair-flowing Aegyptus and halt the ships in the river (*Odyssey* 14.247–258; see [[crete]], [[boreas]], [[as-if-downstream]] and [[aegyptus-river]]).

**The raid.** He bids his companions stay and guard the ships and sends scouts to the lookouts; “but they, giving way to arrogance, following their own might”, lay waste the fields of the Egyptians, carry off the women and the little children and kill the men. The cry comes to the city, and at dawn the Egyptians come out: “the whole plain was filled with foot soldiers and horses / and the flash of bronze”. Zeus casts panic among the raiders; many are killed, and the rest are led inland alive, to work for the Egyptians under compulsion (*Odyssey* 14.259–272; see [[hybris]], [[piracy]] and [[horses]]).

He stays seven years and gathers many goods among the Egyptians, “for they all gave to me” (*Odyssey* 14.285–286; see [[supplication]] and [[xenia]]). In the eighth year a Phoenician carries him off, and the tale goes on (*Odyssey* 14.287; see [[phoenicians]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]).

**To Antinous.** In his own hall, begging from the suitors, the beggar tells Antinous the raid again, as the reason why a man once rich now begs. He too, he says, once lived “prosperous, a rich one, and often I gave to a vagrant”, until Zeus sent him “with far-wandering pirates / to go to Egypt, a long way, so that I might be destroyed” (*Odyssey* 17.419–426, a plain rendering). The raid follows in the verses of the fourteenth book (*Odyssey* 17.427–441 = 14.258–272), but there is no king to clasp: “But me they gave to a stranger whom they met, to take to Cyprus, / Dmetor son of Iasus, who ruled Cyprus with might” (*Odyssey* 17.442–443, a plain rendering). Antinous answers with a threat drawn from the tale, “lest you soon come to a bitter Egypt and Cyprus” (*Odyssey* 17.448, a plain rendering; see [[antinous]] and [[cyprus]]).

## In the poem

**Two hearers, two endings.** Odysseus tells the raid to two hosts, and each ending suits its hearer. Eumaeus has just received the stranger because “they are all from Zeus, / strangers and beggars” (*Odyssey* 14.57–58; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]]), and he hears of a king of Egypt who spared a raider at his knees for fear of “the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests” (*Odyssey* 14.283–284; see [[zeus]] and [[god-of-guests]]); in answer he says that he will respect the stranger himself “in fear of Zeus, the god of guests” (*Odyssey* 14.389; see [[eumaeus]]). Antinous has refused the beggar bread, and he hears of a rich man who gave to vagrants and became one (*Odyssey* 17.415–444; see [[the-suitors]]). On the Iliad's battlefields the man who clasps a victor's knees is killed, as Adrastus, Tros and Lycaon are (*Iliad* 6.45–65, 20.463–472, 21.64–119); the king of the tale is the rare victor who spares.

**The raid on the Cicones.** The story has the shape of Odysseus' own first adventure after Troy. There, too, the men would not obey him and stayed by the shore; the people of the land gathered against them, fighting from chariots and on foot, and came “early in the morning”; “an evil fate from Zeus” stood beside them, and many died (*Odyssey* 9.39–61; see [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]] and [[cicones]]). In the tale the Cretan is blameless: he bade his men stay by the ships, and their own arrogance sent them to plunder (*Odyssey* 14.259–265).

**Egypt in the Odyssey.** The Egypt of the tale is the land where a wanderer grows rich: Menelaus gathered “much substance and gold” there (*Odyssey* 3.301–302; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]), and the Cretan leaves it with many goods (*Odyssey* 14.285–286). D. B. Monro remarks that in the Odyssey “the voyage to Egypt is described more than once, and with a fair approach to correctness”, and cites this voyage among the instances (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix II §6 and note, citing *Odyssey* 3.300, 4.351, 14.257, 17.426). The seven years in Egypt stand for the seven that Odysseus spent with Calypso (Monro on *Odyssey* 14.285; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]).

## Outside Homer

**Greek raiders in Egypt.** Herodotus tells how the oracle at Buto promised the exiled Psammetichus vengeance “from the sea, when bronze men appeared”. Soon after, Ionians and Carians who had sailed out for plunder were driven to Egypt, and when they landed in bronze armor an Egyptian brought word that bronze men had come from the sea and were plundering the plain; Psammetichus made friends of them, won his kingdom with their help, and gave them land to live in, the places called the Camps (*Histories* 2.152.3–5, 2.154.1; see [[herodotus]]). Strabo says that the earlier kings of Egypt, content with what they had, distrusted all who sailed there, “and especially the Greeks (for they were sackers and covetous of other men's land, through want of land of their own)”, and set a guard on the coast, with herdsmen to keep off anyone who came from outside (*Geography* 17.1.6; see [[strabo]]). Strabo's word for the Greeks, πορθηταί, “sackers”, comes from the verb that the tale uses of the Cretan's men, πόρθεον, “they laid waste” (*Odyssey* 14.264).

**The king who spares a stranger.** In Herodotus' account of Helen in Egypt, King Proteus tells Paris that he would have punished him for the wrong he did his host, “if I did not count it of great importance to kill none of the strangers who have been driven to my land by winds”, and instead orders him out of the country within three days, since he will not kill a stranger, μὴ ξεινοκτονέειν (*Histories* 2.115.4–6; see [[helen-in-egypt]]). Greek writers disagreed about how Egypt treated strangers. Eratosthenes, as Strabo reports him, held that the expulsion of strangers was a custom common to all barbarians, and that the Egyptians had been reproached for it through the myths of Busiris, told by later writers who wished to decry the inhospitality of the place, though there never was a king or tyrant called Busiris (*Geography* 17.1.19). Plato's Athenian bids his citizens receive strangers “honoring Zeus Xenios”, and not drive them out “as the nurslings of the Nile do now” (*Laws* 12.953e; see [[plato]] and [[god-of-guests]]).
