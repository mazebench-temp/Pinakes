## The story

The Odyssey tells the story in pieces: Zeus in the council of the gods, Nestor and Menelaus, who heard it from Proteus, to Telemachus, and the shade of Agamemnon twice. The renderings of books other than the first four are plain renderings, not the translation.

**The seduction.** While the Achaeans toiled at Troy, Aegisthus sat at his ease “in a corner of horse-pasturing Argos” and kept beguiling Agamemnon's wife with words. At first she refused the shameful deed, for her mind was good, and a singer was beside her, whom Agamemnon, leaving for Troy, had charged to guard his wife. But when the fate of the gods fettered her, Aegisthus took the singer away to a desert island and left him to be the prey of birds, and led her, willing, to his house; and he burned many thigh-pieces on the altars of the gods and hung up many offerings, having accomplished a great deed he had never expected (*Odyssey* 3.262–275; see [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]]). The gods had warned him: Zeus says that they sent Hermes to tell him neither to kill the man nor to woo his wife, for vengeance would come from Orestes (*Odyssey* 1.35–43; see [[aegisthus]], [[clytemnestra]] and [[hermes]]).

**The homecoming.** Off Malea a storm carried Agamemnon toward the edge of the land where Thyestes had once lived and Aegisthus lived now, but the gods turned the wind, and he set foot on his own land and kissed it, weeping (*Odyssey* 4.514–523; see [[malea]]). A lookout, posted by Aegisthus for wages of two talents of gold, had watched for a year; he brought the news, and Aegisthus chose twenty men to lie in ambush, had a feast prepared, and went with horses and chariot to invite the king. He led him up, unaware of his destruction, and killed him at the feast, as one kills an ox at the manger, and none of Agamemnon's companions was left, nor any of Aegisthus' (*Odyssey* 4.524–537; see [[agamemnon]]). The shade of Agamemnon tells Odysseus the same in the underworld: Aegisthus killed him with his accursed wife, having invited him home and feasted him; his companions were slaughtered around him like white-tusked swine, among the mixing bowl and the laden tables, and the floor ran with blood; he heard Priam's daughter Cassandra cry out as Clytemnestra killed her beside him, and as he died his wife turned away and would not close his eyes or his mouth (*Odyssey* 11.405–426; see [[cassandra]]).

**The vengeance.** While Menelaus wandered far away, Aegisthus ruled Mycenae, rich in gold, for seven years; in the eighth Orestes came back from Athens and killed him, and gave the Argives a funeral feast for his hateful mother and the coward Aegisthus (*Odyssey* 3.301–310; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]], [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]] and [[mycenae]]).

**Who struck.** In Zeus' words, in Nestor's and in Proteus', the killer is Aegisthus (*Odyssey* 1.35–36; 3.193–194, 3.303–304; 4.529–535). The shade of Agamemnon says that Aegisthus killed him “with my accursed wife” (σὺν οὐλομένῃ ἀλόχῳ, *Odyssey* 11.409–410) and that she contrived the murder of her wedded husband (*Odyssey* 11.429–430), and in the last book that she killed him (κουρίδιον κτείνασα πόσιν, *Odyssey* 24.199–200; compare 24.96–97).

## In the poem

The Odyssey keeps coming back to the story, and always with Odysseus' own homecoming in view. Zeus opens the council of the gods with it (*Odyssey* 1.32–43; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]); Athena and then Nestor hold up Orestes' vengeance to Telemachus in almost the same verses (*Odyssey* 1.298–302; 3.195–200); Menelaus learns it from Proteus (*Odyssey* 4.512–537); the shade of Agamemnon warns Odysseus to bring his ship to his own land secretly, since women are no longer to be trusted (*Odyssey* 11.454–456); and in the underworld of the last book the shade sets Penelope's fame against the hateful song that will be sung of Clytemnestra (*Odyssey* 24.192–202; see [[penelope]]).

## Outside Homer

Aeschylus' *Agamemnon* makes Clytemnestra the killer. Cassandra sees the king struck down in the bath, caught in a robe (*Agamemnon* 1125–1129); Clytemnestra, standing over the body, says that she cast around him an endless net, as for fish, struck him twice, and gave him a third blow as he lay fallen (1379–1387); and Aegisthus claims the deed as just revenge for his father Thyestes, whom Atreus had fed on his own children, calling himself the man who stitched the murder together and planned it from outside the house (1577–1611; see [[aeschylus]] and [[the-feast-of-thyestes]]). In the *Library* of Apollodorus, Agamemnon comes home to Mycenae with Cassandra and is murdered by Aegisthus and Clytemnestra: she gives him a shirt without sleeves and without a neck, and he is cut down as he puts it on; Aegisthus reigns over Mycenae, and they kill Cassandra too (*Epitome* 6.23; see [[apollodorus]]). J. G. Frazer sums up the tradition: according to Homer and the author of the *Returns*, with whom Pausanias agrees, Aegisthus killed Agamemnon; according to Aeschylus, Clytemnestra; and Sophocles and Euripides speak of the two together (*Apollodorus: The Library*, 1921, on *Epitome* 6.23). How the tragedians changed Nestor's version is set out under [[the-murder-of-agamemnon-in-tragedy]].

Strabo cites the singer whom Agamemnon left to guard his wife as proof that Homer made singers teachers of self-control: Aegisthus could not prevail over Clytemnestra until he had taken the singer to a desert island (*Geography* 1.2.3; see [[strabo]] and [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]]).
