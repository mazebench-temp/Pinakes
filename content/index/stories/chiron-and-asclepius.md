## The story

{{quote:0e1ce1ad-1f84-5cd3-90c5-caf768b12e7a}}

The story is told in one line: the drugs are those “which Chiron once gave to his father in friendship” (τά οἵ ποτε πατρὶ φίλα φρονέων πόρε Χείρων, 4.219). ποτε, “once”, puts the gift in the past, in the generation before the war. φίλα φρονέων is “with friendly thoughts”, the language of a friend's gift. The father is [[Asclepius]], “the noble healer” (4.194), and the son [[Machaon]], who uses the drugs on Menelaus' wound (see [[pandarus-breaks-the-truce]] and [[healing]]). Homer does not say when or why the Centaur gave them, or that he taught Asclepius; in Book 4 he does not even call him a Centaur (see [[chiron]]).

The Iliad names Chiron three times more. The wounded Eurypylus asks Patroclus for the soothing drugs that he learned from Achilles, “whom Chiron taught, the most just of the Centaurs” (11.830–832). And the great ash spear that only Achilles could wield is “the Pelian ash, which Chiron gave to his dear father from the peak of Pelion, to be the death of heroes” (16.140–144, repeated at 19.387–391).

## In the poem

**Gifts to fathers.** Both of Chiron's gifts are given to a father and used by a son, and the half-lines that tell them are nearly the same: πατρὶ φίλα φρονέων πόρε Χείρων of the drugs (4.219), πατρὶ φίλῳ πόρε Χείρων of the spear (16.143). One gift heals, and the other kills. The Centaur of Pelion stands behind both arts at Troy, the healer's and the killer's.

**Knowledge handed down.** Healing in the Iliad is a craft that passes from one man to another, like a weapon or a scepter. The drugs went from Chiron to Asclepius and so to Machaon and Podalirius, “good healers” (2.731–732); the knowledge went from Chiron to Achilles and from Achilles to Patroclus (11.830–832). Machaon is valued for it: when he is wounded, Idomeneus says that “a healer is a man worth many others” (11.514).

**The just Centaur.** Homer's other Centaurs are “the beasts who lived in the mountains” (1.268), whom the Lapiths destroyed in Nestor's youth (2.742–744; see [[centaurs]] and [[nestor-and-the-centaurs]]). Chiron is their opposite: a friend and teacher of heroes, “the most just of the Centaurs”.

## Outside Homer

Later poets told the story that Homer leaves out. In Pindar Apollo snatches the child Asclepius from the pyre of his mother Coronis and gives him to the Magnesian Centaur to be taught to heal the painful diseases of men (*Pythian* 3.1–7, 3.45–46), and Chiron teaches him “the gentle-handed art of drugs” (*Nemean* 3.53–55). Apollodorus tells the same story (3.10.3). On Chiron as the teacher of heroes, see [[chiron]].
