## The story

Glaucus tells it to Diomedes as part of his lineage (6.152–211; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]). In Ephyra, “in a corner of horse-pasturing Argos”, lived Sisyphus, the craftiest of men; his son was Glaucus, and Glaucus' son was Bellerophon, to whom the gods gave beauty and lovely manhood (6.152–157; see [[ephyra-in-argos]], [[sisyphus]] and [[glaucus-son-of-sisyphus]]).

**Anteia.** Proetus devised evil against him and drove him from the land, for Proetus was far the mightier among the Argives, whom Zeus had made subject to his scepter (6.157–159; on the reading of these lines see the translation's note on 6.158–159, and [[proetus]]). His wife, “brilliant Anteia”, was mad with longing to lie with Bellerophon in secret love, and could not persuade him, “for his thoughts were good” (6.160–162). So she lied to her husband:

**The tablet.** Proetus was angry, but he would not kill Bellerophon himself (see [[anteia]]):

The “father-in-law” is the king of Lycia, Anteia's father, whom Homer does not name. Bellerophon went “under the blameless escort of the gods”, and when he came to Lycia and the flowing Xanthus, the king honored him, entertained him for nine days and sacrificed nine oxen (6.171–174; see [[lycia]] and [[xanthus-of-lycia]]). On the tenth day he asked to see the sign, and when he had received it, he set his guest his tasks.

**The tasks.**

{{quote:56e5fba0-42b3-5371-81ba-b2f149fffcb1}}

The Chimaera was “of divine race, not of men”, and he killed her “obeying the portents of the gods” (θεῶν τεράεσσι πιθήσας, 6.180–183; see [[chimaera]] and [[teras]]). Then the Solymi, the fiercest battle of men he ever entered, and the Amazons, “the equals of men” (6.184–186; see [[solymi]] and [[amazons]]). On his way back the king set an ambush of the best men of Lycia, and Bellerophon killed them all (6.187–190).

**The reward.** When the king knew that he was “the noble offspring of a god” (θεοῦ γόνον ἠΰν, 6.191), he kept him in Lycia, gave him his daughter and half of all his royal honor, and the Lycians marked out for him a domain (τέμενος) “of orchard and plowland” (6.191–195). His wife bore him Isander, Hippolochus and Laodameia, and Zeus lay with Laodameia, who bore Sarpedon (6.196–199; see [[isander]], [[hippolochus]], [[laodameia]] and [[sarpedon]]).

## In the poem

**A lineage.** Glaucus tells the story to say who he is, and it explains how a family of Ephyra in Argos came to rule in Lycia (see [[lycians]]). The domain that the Lycians gave Bellerophon is still held by his grandsons: Sarpedon reminds Glaucus of the great domain the two of them hold “by the banks of the Xanthus, a fair one, of orchard and wheat-bearing plowland” (12.313–314), in the words of 6.195. And the story holds the proof that ends the fight, for Diomedes knows the name. His grandfather Oeneus entertained Bellerophon for twenty days, and the cup Bellerophon gave him is in Diomedes' house (6.215–221; see [[xenia]] and [[guest-gifts]]).

**The gods.** The story is framed by the gods' favor and their hatred. They give him beauty and manhood (6.156–157), escort him to Lycia (6.171), send him the portents by which he kills the Chimaera (6.183), and the king takes him for a god's offspring (6.191). Then, with no reason given, “he too was hated by all the gods” (6.200). The poem does not say why, nor why Artemis killed his daughter “in her anger” (see the translation's note on 6.200–205). καὶ κεῖνος, “he too”, links him to the man in Diomedes' story. Lycurgus “was hated by all the immortal gods” (6.140; see [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]]), and the two stories told at the meeting end with the same half-line. Diomedes told of a man who fought a god and paid for it. Glaucus answers with a man who did nothing against the gods that the poem records, and paid anyway. The name of the Aleian plain, τὸ Ἀλήϊον, plays on what he did there: he “wandered” (ἀλᾶτο), “avoiding” (ἀλεείνων) the paths of men (6.201–202). His son died fighting the Solymi, the people he had once defeated, and Ares killed him (6.203–204).

**The tablet and the guest.** Proetus will not kill Bellerophon himself; “his heart held back in awe from that” (σεβάσσατο γὰρ τό γε θυμῷ, 6.167). He sends him to be killed by another man, who receives him as a guest. The Lycian king entertains his guest for nine days before he asks what he has brought (6.174–177), as a host should, and then tries to have him killed. The same half-line tells how Achilles would not strip the body of Eetion (6.417); in Homer it occurs only in these two places. The σήματα λυγρά, “grim signs”, are the only thing like writing in Homer (see [[writing]] and [[bellerophons-letter]]). The woman who accuses the man she could not seduce, and the letter that orders the death of its bearer, have long been compared with the stories of Joseph and Potiphar's wife (Genesis 39.7–20) and of David's letter about Uriah (2 Samuel 11.14–15); whether the Greek tale borrowed from such stories or shared a common stock with them cannot be shown.

**The Chimaera.** The monster is described once more in the Iliad. Two of Sarpedon's companions, killed by the sons of Nestor, were sons of Amisodarus, “who reared the irresistible Chimaera, a bane to many men” (16.317–329). Homer never names Pegasus.

## Outside Homer

The later story gives Bellerophon the winged horse. Hesiod names the Chimaera's killers as “Pegasus and noble Bellerophon” (*Theogony* 325). In Pindar, Athena gives him a golden bridle at Corinth, and from the horse's back he kills the Amazons, the Chimaera and the Solymi (*Olympian* 13.63–92); when he tries to ride to the houses of heaven, Pegasus throws him (*Isthmian* 7.44–47). Later readers found in that ride the reason for the gods' hatred that Homer does not give. [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] tells the whole story: Bellerophon came to Proetus to be purified of a killing; the queen, whom he calls Stheneboea after the tragedians, accused him; Proetus sent him with letters to Iobates, king of Lycia; and when he had survived every task, Iobates showed him the letters and gave him his daughter Philonoe and, at his death, the kingdom (*Library* 2.2.1, 2.3.1–2). [[euripides|Euripides]] wrote two lost tragedies on the story, *Stheneboea* and *Bellerophon*. See [[bellerophon]] for the hero's later legend.
