## The story

Homer tells no story, only its aftermath. Typhoeus lies "among the Arimi", and Zeus in his anger still "lashes the earth around" him. The fight itself is told by Hesiod. After Zeus had driven the Titans from heaven, Gaia bore her youngest child, Typhoeus, to Tartarus. The battle between the god and the monster set earth, sky and sea on fire and made Hades himself tremble. Zeus leapt down from Olympus, burned all the monster's heads with his thunderbolt, and flung him into Tartarus (*Theogony* 820–868).

## In the poem

The allusion closes the Catalogue of Ships and gives the size of the army a cosmic scale: the march of the Achaeans shakes the earth the way Zeus' blows shake it. It may also carry a hint of menace. The army marches out because of a dream Zeus has sent to deceive Agamemnon, and the god whose anger the simile recalls is planning its defeat (see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[the-earth-groans-as-for-typhoeus]]).

## Outside Homer

The Homeric *Hymn to Apollo* makes Typhaon a son of Hera alone (305–355). Pindar, Aeschylus and Apollodorus put him under Etna (*Pythian* 1.15–28; *Prometheus Bound* 351–372; Apollodorus 1.6.3). In Apollodorus the war ranges from Egypt, where the gods flee from Typhon in the shapes of animals, to Mount Casius in Syria. Modern scholars compare Near Eastern myths of a storm god who defeats a monstrous challenger, such as the Hittite story of the serpent Illuyanka. Nonnus opens his *Dionysiaca* with a long version of the war (books 1–2). See [[typhoeus]].
