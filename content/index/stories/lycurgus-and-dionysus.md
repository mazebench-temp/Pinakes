## The story

Diomedes tells it to the stranger who has come out to meet him, whom he suspects of being a god (see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]):

{{quote:5969c907-d704-59a1-af96-11db2ca6c422}}

Lycurgus is “the son of Dryas” and “mighty” (κρατερός), and when he attacks, “man-slaying” (ἀνδροφόνος; see [[lycurgus]], [[dryas-father-of-lycurgus]] and [[man-slaying]]). Homer does not say where he lived or what he ruled. He “strove with the gods of heaven” (θεοῖσιν ἐπουρανίοισιν ἔριζεν, 6.131), and the story shows how: he fell on the god's nurses (τιθῆναι), not on the god. They were on the “sacred Nysean mount” (ἠγάθεον Νυσήϊον), a place named only here (see [[nysa]] and the translation's note on 6.133). Struck with his βουπλήξ, an ox-goad or an axe for felling oxen, they let their θύσθλα fall to the ground, the sacred things of the rite, usually taken as thyrsi (see the translation's note on 6.134–135). Dionysus, called μαινόμενος, “raving”, the god of frenzy named by the frenzy he brings (see [[dionysus]]), “plunged beneath the wave of the sea”, and Thetis took him to her bosom, for he was trembling at the man's shout (6.135–137; see [[thetis]]).

The punishment came from all the gods: they “were angry with him, and the son of Cronus made him blind”, and he did not live long, “since he was hated by all the immortal gods” (6.138–140; see [[zeus]]). Diomedes ends where he began: “Nor would I wish to fight with the blessed gods” (6.141).

The god of the story is a child. He has nurses, and he trembles. Homer names his mother elsewhere: “Semele bore Dionysus, a joy to mortals” (14.325).

## In the poem

**Why Diomedes tells it.** The story is a paradigm, a tale from the past offered as a reason for present action (see [[mythological-paradigm]]). Diomedes will not fight a god, and Lycurgus shows why. The story is framed by the refusal, stated at its start and at its end (6.129, 6.141; see [[ring-composition]]). He says it on the same day on which he has wounded Aphrodite and Ares, with Athena at his side and at her bidding (5.330–351, 5.855–863; see [[diomedes-wounds-aphrodite]] and [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]). Dione, comforting her daughter, warned that “the man who fights with the immortals does not live long” (5.407; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]). Diomedes did not hear her, and now he says the same thing himself: Lycurgus “did not live long” (δὴν ἦν, 6.131, 6.139–140). Alone, without the goddess and facing a man he does not know, he keeps to the limit Apollo set him: “do not wish to think yourself equal to the gods” (5.440–442; see [[gods-and-men-not-alike]]).

**A god in flight.** The Iliad's gods are seldom afraid of men, and never so afraid as here. Dione told of gods who suffered at the hands of mortals: Ares shut in a bronze jar by the sons of Aloeus, Hera and Hades wounded by Heracles (5.385–404; see [[the-aloadae-bind-ares]] and [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]]). In her stories the gods endure and survive; in Diomedes' story the god flees, and the gods together punish the man. Thetis is the rescuer. She freed Zeus when the other gods would have bound him (1.396–406; see [[binding-of-zeus]]), and she and Eurynome took Hephaestus “to their bosom” when Hera threw him from heaven, in the words used here (ὑπεδέξατο κόλπῳ, 18.398 ~ 6.136; see [[fall-of-hephaestus]]). The sea, where she lives, is where gods in trouble go.

**Two stories.** Diomedes' story is answered by Glaucus'. Lycurgus was hated by all the immortal gods (6.140), and Bellerophon, “when he too was hated by all the gods”, wandered alone on the Aleian plain (6.200; see [[bellerophon-in-lycia]]). Lycurgus fought a god and lost his sight and his life; Bellerophon did nothing that the poem records against the gods, and lost his children.

**Madness.** μαίνομαι, “rage, be mad”, runs through the book. Helenus says that Diomedes “rages beyond measure” (6.100–101), and Diomedes calls the god whom he will not fight “raving” (6.132). Later Andromache hurries to the wall “like a woman out of her mind” (6.389; see [[like-a-woman-out-of-her-mind]]).

**When.** Nothing in Homer dates the story. The god in it is still a child with nurses, and his mother Semele (14.325) is in Hesiod a daughter of Cadmus, the founder of Thebes (*Theogony* 940–942, 975–976). The story belongs to the oldest generations of the heroic age.

## Outside Homer

Later writers made Lycurgus king of the Edonians in Thrace. Aeschylus told his story in a lost tetralogy. In Sophocles, “the quick-tempered son of Dryas, king of the Edonians”, is shut up by Dionysus in a prison of rock for his mockery, until his madness drains away (*Antigone* 955–965). In [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] Lycurgus insults and drives out the god, who takes refuge with Thetis in the sea; he is driven mad and kills his own son Dryas with an axe, thinking he is pruning a vine, and when the land becomes barren the Edonians bind him on Mount Pangaeum, where he is destroyed by horses (*Library* 3.5.1). In these versions Dionysus takes his own revenge. In Homer the god only flees, and Zeus acts for him. A *Homeric Hymn* tells how the nymphs received the infant Dionysus “into their bosoms” and nursed him in the glens of Nysa (*Hymn* 26.3–5; see [[nymphs]]).
