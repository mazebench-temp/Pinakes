## The story

{{quote:5e7fcbc1-de56-57b6-8339-0df9381c2bed}}

Homer tells it in outline. The wound is "evil", the snake ὀλοόφρων, "deadly-minded", and the Achaeans simply "left him" (λίπον). Where he was bitten, and why he had to be left, are not said.

## In the poem

**Why it is told.** Like the notice on [[Protesilaus]] just before it, the story explains why a contingent has another leader, here [[Medon]]. The line that brings on the substitute is the same in both places: "nor were they without a leader, though they longed for their leader" (2.703, 2.726).

**Two men lying apart.** The language repeats the notice on Achilles a little earlier. Achilles "lay" (κεῖτο) among his ships (2.688), and "for her sake he lay grieving; but soon he was to rise up" (τῆς ὅ γε κεῖτʼ ἀχέων, τάχα δʼ ἀνστήσεσθαι ἔμελλεν, 2.694). Philoctetes "lay" on the island (2.721), and "there he lay grieving; but soon they were to remember" him (ἔνθʼ ὅ γε κεῖτʼ ἀχέων· τάχα δὲ μνήσεσθαι ἔμελλον, 2.724). Both are great warriors kept from the war, one by his anger and the other by his wound, and both will be needed. The first prophecy is fulfilled within the Iliad, when Achilles returns in Book 19. The second points beyond its end.

## Outside Homer

In Proclus' summary of the *Cypria*, Philoctetes is bitten by a water snake on Tenedos while the army feasts, and is left on Lemnos because of the stench of his wound. In the *Little Iliad* Diomedes brings him back, Machaon heals him, and he kills Paris. Sophocles' *Philoctetes* (409 BC) sets the whole play on Lemnos, ten years after the abandonment: Odysseus and Neoptolemus come for the bow of Heracles, without which Troy cannot be taken.
