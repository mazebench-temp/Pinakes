## The story

Athena comes down among the armies like a shooting star, and both sides wonder whether it means war or friendship (4.73–85; see [[shooting-star]]). In the likeness of Laodocus, a son of Antenor, she finds Pandarus, son of Lycaon, among the men who followed him from the Aesepus, and tempts him (see [[laodocus]] and [[pandarus]]).

{{quote:505dae43-d440-50b7-a29d-c9332c98490b}}

She tells him to pray to Apollo and vow a hecatomb of firstborn lambs when he comes home to Zeleia, “and she persuaded the mind of the fool” (4.100–104). He uncovers his bow, made from the horns of a wild goat he had shot himself (4.105–111; see [[the-bow-of-pandarus]] and [[goats]]), strings it with his companions holding their shields in front of him, takes an arrow never shot before, makes the vow, and draws.

{{quote:4df5ff6c-adce-5c77-b61c-9b5901fca372}}

But the gods have not forgotten Menelaus. Athena stands in front of him and turns the arrow “as a mother brushes a fly from her child”, and guides it to where the golden clasps of the belt meet the double corselet (4.127–133; see [[mother-and-fly]]). It goes through the belt, the corselet and the band beneath, and grazes the skin, and the dark blood runs down his thighs like purple on ivory (4.134–147; see [[belt]], [[corselet]], [[band]] and [[ivory-stained-with-purple]]).

Agamemnon shudders at the sight, and so does Menelaus, until he sees that the binding and the barbs are outside the wound (4.148–152). Holding his brother by the hand, Agamemnon mourns him as if he were dying. The oaths he swore were his brother's death, since the Trojans have shot him and trampled on the trusty oaths.

{{quote:c0711eda-b774-5db9-9728-99640c92c26d}}

The Olympian, he goes on, fulfills an oath in the end, even late, and the breakers pay with their own heads and their wives and children: “a day will come when sacred Ilios will perish” (4.160–168; see [[a-day-will-come]]). But if Menelaus dies, the Achaeans will go home and leave Argive Helen to the Trojans, and a Trojan will leap on his tomb and mock Agamemnon's wasted army (4.169–182; see [[war-for-menelaus]]). Menelaus reassures him: the shaft did not stick in a deadly place, for the belt stopped it, and underneath the kilt and the band (4.183–187; see [[kilt]]). Agamemnon sends the herald Talthybius for Machaon, son of Asclepius, since “someone well skilled with the bow” has hit Menelaus, “one of the Trojans or Lycians: fame for him, and grief for us” (4.188–197; see [[talthybius]]).

Talthybius finds Machaon among his men from Tricca and brings him to the circle of chiefs around the wounded man (4.198–212). Machaon draws the arrow out of the belt, and its sharp barbs break back as it comes. He loosens the belt, the kilt and the band, sucks out the blood, and spreads on the wound the soothing drugs “which Chiron once gave to his father in friendship” (4.213–219; see [[machaon]], [[healing]] and [[chiron-and-asclepius]]). While they tend him, the Trojan ranks come on, and the Achaeans put on their armor again (4.220–222; see [[the-review-of-the-army]]).

## In the poem

**Whose fault?** The gods decided that the Trojans should break the oaths, and Athena chose the archer and gave him his reasons: thanks and glory from all the Trojans, and gifts from Alexander (4.64–72, 4.93–99). The narrator calls him a fool (ἄφρων, 4.104). The act has a divine cause and a human one, and the poem holds the human side to account. One man shoots, and the Achaeans charge the whole city with it. “The Trojans have shot you and trampled on the trusty oaths” (4.157), and the curse sworn over the lambs, on whichever side first did harm, now has its object (3.298–301; see [[oath]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]). In Book 7 Antenor admits it: the Trojans are fighting having broken the trusty oaths (7.351–352).

**A wound, not a death.** Athena turns the arrow so that it strikes where the armor is thickest. The truce has to be broken, and Menelaus has to live. If he died, Agamemnon says, the Achaeans would remember their native land and sail home, leaving Helen to the Trojans (4.169–175): the war would end with the husband whose wrong began it. With a wound the war goes on, and its cause is still standing. Menelaus is fighting again in the next book (5.49–58).

**The unknown archer.** The Achaeans never learn who shot. Agamemnon knows only that it was a Trojan or a Lycian, and that the shot will bring the archer fame (4.196–197, repeated by Talthybius at 4.206–207; see [[kleos]] and [[penthos]]). Pandarus claims it himself in Book 5: he has hit two of the best Achaeans, Menelaus and Diomedes, and only roused them (5.206–208). Diomedes kills him soon after (5.290–296). The hecatomb he vowed to Apollo when he came home to Zeleia is never paid (see [[lambs]]).

**The truce undone.** The oaths of Book 3 were sworn in the sight of both armies, with Priam and Agamemnon as parties, the gods as witnesses and the victims killed on the plain (see [[the-oaths-sworn]]). They are broken by one man, hidden behind his companions' shields, on a goddess's word, in a few lines. Agamemnon answers with the most famous prophecy of the book, and the poem gives it to him just after the audience has watched Zeus give Troy up to Hera (4.31–49; see [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]]). He is right for reasons he cannot know.

**The first wound and the first healing.** Menelaus' is the first wound the Iliad describes, and Machaon's treatment is its first cure, told step by step: the arrow drawn, the barbs broken back, the belt loosened, the blood sucked out, the drugs spread (4.213–219). A healer is worth many other men, Idomeneus will say when Machaon himself is wounded (11.514). See [[healing]].

## Outside Homer

The traditional title of Book 4 names this episode Ὁρκίων σύγχυσις, “the confounding of the oaths”, after Idomeneus' words σύν γʼ ὅρκιʼ ἔχευαν, “since the oaths have been confounded by the Trojans” (4.269–270; see [[titles-of-book-4]]). Plato's Socrates would not have the young hear that Athena and Zeus caused Pandarus to break the oaths (*Republic* 2.379e; see [[plato-broken-oaths]]).

Virgil's truce in the last book of the *Aeneid* is broken in the same way. At Juno's urging the nymph Juturna stirs up the Rutulians in disguise, the augur Tolumnius throws the first spear, and an arrow from an unknown hand wounds Aeneas, who is healed only when Venus secretly brings the herb dittany (*Aeneid* 12.134–160, 12.222–323, 12.391–429; see [[virgil-duel-for-lavinia]]). Lessing took the shot of Pandarus, told moment by moment from the opening of the quiver to the flight of the arrow, as his example of what only a poet can paint (see [[lessing-pandarus]]).
