## The story

**The fall.** On the morning of the departure from Aeaea, as Odysseus went through Circe's house rousing his companions, the last loss of the tenth book of the Odyssey came:

{{quote:dce031f3-8784-5c57-a9ab-8445ae18fb8d}}

Odysseus did not stay to bury him. He told the others that their way lay to the house of Hades, and they went down to the ship (*Odyssey* 10.561–574; see [[odysseus-and-circe]] and [[elpenor]]).

**The soul.** At the pit beyond Oceanus, the first soul to come to Odysseus was Elpenor's, “for he had not yet been buried beneath the wide-wayed earth; / for we had left his body behind in the hall of Circe, / unwept and unburied, since another toil was pressing us” (*Odyssey* 11.51–54, a plain rendering). Odysseus wept and asked him how he had come beneath the misty darkness, faster on foot than Odysseus in his black ship. Elpenor told his death in the verses in which Odysseus has told it, and begged him by his wife, his father and Telemachus, since he would put in at Aeaea on his way back: “Do not go away and leave me behind unwept, unburied, / turning away, lest I become a cause of the gods' wrath against you; / but burn me with my armor, whatever I have, / and heap up a mound for me on the shore of the gray sea, / the mound of an unhappy man, even for men to come to hear of. / Do this for me, and fix on the tomb the oar / with which I rowed when I was alive, among my companions” (*Odyssey* 11.55–78, a plain rendering). Odysseus promised, and the two sat exchanging hateful words, Odysseus on one side holding his sword over the blood, and the phantom on the other (*Odyssey* 11.79–83, a plain rendering; see [[psyche]] and [[eidolon]]).

**The burial.** When the ship came back from the house of Hades, Odysseus sent companions at dawn to Circe's house to bring the dead man. They cut logs where the headland ran out farthest, and burned him with his armor, weeping and shedding big tears; then they heaped up a mound, dragged a pillar onto it, and fixed the well-fitted oar on the top of the mound (*Odyssey* 12.8–15, a plain rendering; see [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[cremation]]).

## In the poem

**Told twice.** The fall is told twice, first by Odysseus and then by the soul, in the first person and in nearly the same verses: “Lying down in the hall of Circe I did not think / to come back down by going to the long ladder, / but fell straight down from the roof; and my neck / was broken from the vertebrae, and my soul went down to Hades” (*Odyssey* 11.62–65, a plain rendering, after 10.557–560). καταντικρύ, “straight down”, is found only in these two places (see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 10.552–560). The soul gives the cause in the words of blind folly, ἆσέ με δαίμονος αἶσα κακὴ καὶ ἀθέσφατος οἶνος, “an evil fate from a god led me into blind folly, and endless wine” (*Odyssey* 11.61, a plain rendering), as Odysseus, before Aeolus, blamed his evil companions and sleep for leading him into blind folly (*Odyssey* 10.68–69; see [[ate]] and [[odysseus-and-aeolus]]). The narrator's “sodden with wine”, οἰνοβαρείων (*Odyssey* 10.555), is the word he used of the drunken Cyclops (*Odyssey* 9.374; see [[wine-sodden]] and [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]).

**The first of the dead.** Merry explains why Elpenor's soul comes first: being unburied, he could not pass within the gates of Hades and take his place among the rest, as the soul of Patroclus, unburied, could not cross the river (*Iliad* 23.71–74; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.51; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]]). The Iliad's translation takes the passage the other way: in the Odyssey the unburied Elpenor is not kept from the shades, and Leaf put the difference between the poems down to their different ages and places (see the translation's note on *Iliad* 23.71–74). Merry also reports the scholia's question why none of those killed by the Laestrygonians or eaten by the Cyclops come to meet their captain, and their answer, that these had been buried after a fashion, even if lawlessly; the answer is not absurd, he adds, since to have been eaten by the Cyclops at least hid the body from sight (on 11.58; see [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]]). For the doubts that Hermann, Lauer and Merry raised against the meeting, and for Plutarch's reading of it, see [[elpenor]].

**The oar.** The soul asks for the oar he rowed with to be fixed on his tomb, πῆξαί τʼ ἐπὶ τύμβῳ ἐρετμόν (*Odyssey* 11.77). When the companions fix it on the mound, πήξαμεν ἀκροτάτῳ τύμβῳ ἐυῆρες ἐρετμόν (*Odyssey* 12.15, Greek only), the words are those that Teiresias, soon after Elpenor in the eleventh book, uses for the oar that Odysseus himself must one day fix in the ground, far inland among men who do not know the sea: γαίῃ πήξας ἐυῆρες ἐρετμόν, “fix your well-fitted oar in the ground” (*Odyssey* 11.121–129, a plain rendering; again 23.276, Greek only; see [[oars]] and [[tomb]]). Nitzsch, as Merry reports, compared an epitaph of Sappho on Pelagon the fisherman, with an oar for a memorial (*Palatine Anthology* 7.505; Merry on 11.74).

## Outside Homer

Virgil gives Aeneas two companions of Elpenor's kind. Aeneas raises over the trumpeter Misenus a great mound and sets on it the dead man's arms, his oar and his trumpet, *suaque arma viro remumque tubamque*, and the mountain bears his name (*Aeneid* 6.232–235), a burial that Merry sets beside Elpenor's (on 12.15). In the underworld the helmsman Palinurus, who fell from the stern and lies unburied on the shore, begs Aeneas by his father and by the hopes of Iulus to throw earth on his body or take him across; the Sibyl answers that the unburied may not cross the Stygian waters, but that the neighboring peoples will raise him a mound and the place will keep his name forever (*Aeneid* 6.337–383; see [[virgil]]).
