## The story

When Menelaus accepts Paris' challenge, he asks for two lambs from the Trojans, a white male and a black female, for Earth and the Sun, while the Achaeans bring a third for Zeus. And Priam must come to swear the oaths himself, “for his sons are overbearing and not to be trusted” (3.103–107). Hector sends two heralds to the city, and Agamemnon sends Talthybius to the ships (3.116–120).

While Helen watches from the wall, the heralds carry the offerings through the city: two lambs, wine in a goatskin, a mixing bowl and golden cups. The herald Idaeus rouses the old king.

{{quote:7f51e50f-d90f-5bdb-9198-ae7999d8ff0e}}

Priam shudders, but has the horses yoked and drives out through the Scaean gates with Antenor beside him (3.259–263). On the plain the kings rise, and the heralds mix the wine and pour water over their hands. Agamemnon cuts hair from the lambs' heads, and the heralds give it out to the best of both sides. Then he prays aloud with lifted hands:

{{quote:21e4284e-c1b5-5925-be78-6308bf3433a8}}

He states the terms. If Alexander kills Menelaus, he keeps Helen and the possessions, and the Achaeans sail home; if Menelaus kills Alexander, the Trojans give back Helen and the possessions and pay a satisfaction that men to come will remember, and if they refuse, Agamemnon will fight on until the end of the war (3.281–291). He cuts the lambs' throats and lays them gasping on the ground. The men of both armies pour out wine from their cups and pray that whoever first breaks the oaths may have their brains poured on the ground like the wine, and their wives taken by others (3.292–301; see [[like-this-wine]]).

“So they spoke, but the son of Cronus was not yet going to fulfill their prayer” (3.302). Priam says he cannot bear to watch his son fight Menelaus, puts the lambs in his chariot, and drives back to Ilios with Antenor (3.303–313).

## In the poem

This is the fullest oath ritual in Homer, and the rite itself, the witnesses, the terms and what the victims are for, is described under [[oath]]. As a story, the oaths are the hinge of the book. They turn a challenge between two men into a treaty between two peoples, and they make the war's end depend on a single fight. The truce they seal holds only for the length of the book. The narrator's “not yet” (3.302) looks forward to Book 4, where the gods decide that the Trojans shall be the first to harm the oaths, and Pandarus shoots Menelaus during the truce (4.66–72, 4.86–126). In Book 7 Antenor tells the Trojans that they are now fighting “having broken the trusty oaths”, and that nothing good will come of it until Helen is given back (7.350–353). See [[oaths-and-their-breaking]].

The old men carry the weight of the rite. Menelaus asks for Priam because the young are flighty and an old man looks “both before and after” (3.108–110; see [[before-and-after]]). Priam comes, stands by in silence while Agamemnon speaks for both sides, and leaves before the fighting, “since I could never bear to see with my own eyes my dear son fighting with Menelaus, dear to Ares” (3.306–307). Antenor, the Trojan who once entertained Menelaus as a guest (3.207), drives with him both ways.

## Elsewhere in Homer

Agamemnon swears again, over a boar, when he makes peace with Achilles in Book 19, calling Zeus, Earth, the Sun and the Erinyes to witness; afterward the herald throws the victim into the sea (19.250–268). For later treaty rites and the curse by analogy, see [[oath]].
