summary: The seventh adventure of Odysseus' homecoming, which fills the eleventh book of the Odyssey. Sent by Circe to consult the soul of Teiresias, Odysseus sailed for a day to the limits of Oceanus, by the land of the Cimmerians, dug a pit, poured drink offerings and cut the throats of sheep over it, and kept the souls of the dead away from the blood with his sword. Elpenor's soul came first and begged for burial; Teiresias drank and foretold his homecoming and his death; his mother told him of his house and of her own death, and slipped from his arms three times. Then he saw the wives and daughters of chieftains, and, after a pause in the tale, the souls of Agamemnon, Achilles and Ajax, Minos and the punished, and the phantom of Heracles, until the din of the dead drove him back to his ship (*Odyssey* 11.1–640).

## The story

**The voyage.** The ship left Aeaea with the sheep that Circe had tethered beside it, and the men went aboard weeping; Circe sent a following wind, and all day the sails were stretched until the sun set and the ship came to “the limits of deep-streaming Oceanus”. There lie “the land and the city of the Cimmerian men, / wrapped in mist and cloud”, on whom the sun never looks, and “deadly night is spread over those wretched mortals” (*Odyssey* 11.1–19; see [[odysseus-and-circe]], [[oceanus]] and [[cimmerians]]). They beached the ship, took out the sheep, and went along the stream of Oceanus “until we came to the place that Circe had pointed out” (*Odyssey* 11.20–22).

**The rite.** Perimedes and Eurylochus held the victims. Odysseus dug a pit “about a cubit's length this way and that”, poured around it a drink offering to all the dead, “first with honey and milk, then with sweet wine, / and the third time with water”, sprinkled white barley meal, and vowed a barren heifer at home in Ithaca and a black sheep for Teiresias alone; then he cut the sheep's throats over the pit (*Odyssey* 11.23–36; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]], [[perimedes-the-companion]] and [[eurylochus]]). The souls gathered “out of Erebus”, brides and young men and old men, girls and men killed in battle, “with a wondrous cry; and pale fear took hold of me”. The companions flayed and burned the sheep and prayed to Hades and Persephone, and Odysseus sat with his sword drawn and did not let the dead come near the blood “until I had questioned Teiresias” (*Odyssey* 11.36–50; see [[erebus]], [[psyche]], [[hades]] and [[persephone]]).

**Elpenor.** The first to come was the soul of Elpenor, who had fallen from Circe's roof and lay unburied in her hall. He told how he died, and begged Odysseus, by his wife, his father and Telemachus, not to leave him “unwept, unburied”, but to burn him with his armor and heap him a mound on the shore with his oar fixed on it; Odysseus promised (*Odyssey* 11.51–83; see [[the-death-of-elpenor]] and [[elpenor]]).

**Teiresias.** Then came the soul of Odysseus' mother, whom he had left alive when he sailed for Troy; he wept, but did not let her near the blood. Theban Teiresias came next, “holding a golden scepter”, and knew him: “why now, unhappy man, have you left the light of the sun / and come, to see the dead and this joyless place?” He drank, and foretold the anger of Poseidon, the cattle of the Sun on Thrinacia, a late homecoming, the suitors and their death, a journey inland with an oar, and a gentle death in old age; and he told Odysseus that whichever of the dead he let come to the blood would speak truly to him (*Odyssey* 11.84–151; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]] and [[teiresias]]).

**His mother.** His mother drank and knew him at once. She asked how he came beneath the misty darkness while he was alive; he answered that need brought him “to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias”, and asked how she had died, and how it was with his father, his son and his wife (*Odyssey* 11.152–179; see [[anticleia]]). She told him that Penelope waited in tears, that Telemachus held his domains, and that Laertes lived on the farm in rags, grieving; she herself had died of longing for him: “but longing for you, and for your counsels, shining Odysseus, / and for your gentleness, robbed me of my honey-sweet life” (*Odyssey* 11.180–203; see [[penelope]], [[telemachus]] and [[laertes]]). Three times he tried to clasp her, “and three times she flew from my hands like a shadow or a dream”; this, she told him, is the way of mortals when they die, and she bade him long for the light (*Odyssey* 11.204–224).

**The women.** Persephone sent up the wives and daughters of chieftains, and Odysseus, keeping them from the blood with his sword, let them drink one at a time and questioned them all: Tyro, Antiope, Alcmene and Megara, the mother of Oedipus, Chloris, Leda, Iphimedeia, Phaedra, Procris and Ariadne, Maera, Clymene and Eriphyle (*Odyssey* 11.225–327; see [[catalogue-of-heroines]]). There he broke off: he could not name them all before the night was gone, and it was time to sleep (*Odyssey* 11.328–332).

**The pause.** The Phaeacians sat silent, held by a spell. Arete praised the guest and asked for more gifts, Echeneus agreed, and Alcinous promised the gifts and the escort for the next day; he said that Odysseus had told his tale “skillfully, as a singer does”, and asked whether he had seen any of the companions who died at Troy: the night was long, and he could listen until dawn (*Odyssey* 11.333–376; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]], [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[alcinous]]). Odysseus went on (*Odyssey* 11.377–384).

**Agamemnon.** When Persephone had scattered the women, the soul of Agamemnon came, with the men who died with him in the house of Aegisthus. He drank and wept, and told how Aegisthus killed him at the feast “with my ruinous wife”, “as one kills an ox at the manger”, how Clytemnestra killed Cassandra beside him and would not close his eyes, and what he had learned of women; he warned Odysseus to come home in secret, and asked after his son Orestes, of whom Odysseus knew nothing (*Odyssey* 11.385–466; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]], [[agamemnon]] and [[clytemnestra]]).

**Achilles.** Then came the souls of Achilles, Patroclus, Antilochus and Ajax. Achilles asked how Odysseus dared to come down to Hades, “where the dead / dwell without sense, the phantoms of dead mortals”. Odysseus called him the most blessed of men, honored like a god in life and mighty among the dead, and Achilles answered, “Do not speak comfortingly to me of death, shining Odysseus”:

He asked after Peleus and his son; Odysseus knew nothing of Peleus, but told him how Neoptolemus spoke in council and fought at Troy, killed Eurypylus son of Telephus, never trembled in the wooden horse, and sailed home unharmed with his prize; and the soul of Achilles went off “with long strides across the meadow of asphodel, / glad because I said that his son was preeminent” (*Odyssey* 11.467–540; see [[achilles]], [[rather-a-hired-laborer]], [[the-meadow-of-asphodel]], [[neoptolemus-comes-to-troy]], [[neoptolemus-kills-eurypylus]] and [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]]).

**Ajax.** The other souls stood grieving, each asking about its own sorrows. “Alone the soul of Ajax, son of Telamon, / stood apart”, still angry over the arms of Achilles, which Odysseus had won from him; Odysseus wished that he had never won such a prize, and begged him to put away his anger, but “he answered me nothing”, and went off into Erebus among the other souls (*Odyssey* 11.541–567; see [[the-judgment-of-arms]] and [[ajax]]).

**The judge and the punished.** Then Odysseus saw Minos, “holding a golden scepter, giving the law to the dead”; the giant Orion, still herding across the meadow of asphodel the beasts he had killed; Tityus, stretched over nine plethra while two vultures tore his liver, for the outrage he had done to Leto; Tantalus, standing in a lake that vanished whenever he stooped to drink, under fruit that the wind flung up to the clouds whenever he reached for it; and Sisyphus, heaving his stone up a hill, until “back then to the plain rolled the shameless stone” (*Odyssey* 11.568–600; see [[minos]], [[orion]], [[tityus]], [[tantalus]] and [[sisyphus]]).

**Heracles.** Last he saw the phantom of Heracles, “but he himself among the immortal gods / takes his delight in their feasts”; the dead scattered around him like birds, and he stood like dark night with his bow drawn, wearing a terrible golden baldric. He knew Odysseus and greeted him as a fellow sufferer, “ah, wretch, truly you too drag out some evil fate”, and told how he had been sent down to bring back the hound of Hades (*Odyssey* 11.601–627; see [[heracles]], [[the-baldric-of-heracles]] and [[heracles-and-cerberus]]).

**The flight to the ship.** Odysseus waited for more of the heroes of old, Theseus and Peirithous, but the countless tribes of the dead gathered with a wondrous din, and pale fear took him that Persephone would send the Gorgon's head against him out of Hades. He went back to the ship, and the current carried it down the river Oceanus, first with rowing and then with a fair wind (*Odyssey* 11.628–640; see [[gorgon]]). At Aeaea they buried Elpenor, and Circe greeted them: “Hard men, who have gone down alive into the house of Hades, / twice dying, when other men die once” (*Odyssey* 12.1–22, a plain rendering).

## In the poem

**Circe's errand.** The journey is the price of the homecoming: when Odysseus asked Circe to send him home, she answered that he must first go “to the house of Hades and of dread Persephone, / to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias”, who would tell him the way and the measures of his path (*Odyssey* 10.490–495, 10.538–540; see [[odysseus-and-circe]]). He tells his mother that need brought him (*Odyssey* 11.164–165), and Achilles that he came to consult Teiresias (*Odyssey* 11.479). Teiresias tells him less than Circe promised. He foretells the dangers and the end, but not the way, and Circe herself gives the course past the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis when the ship comes back to Aeaea, repeating the seer's warning about the cattle of the Sun in his own verses (*Odyssey* 12.25–141, Greek only; 12.137–141 = 11.110–114; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]] and [[the-cattle-of-helios]]).

**At the pit or below.** Circe told Odysseus to beach his ship on the far side of Oceanus “and go yourself into the dank house of Hades” (*Odyssey* 10.508–512), but in his tale he never says that he went down. He sits by the pit, holding his sword over the blood, and the dead come to him one at a time (*Odyssey* 11.48–50, 11.82, 11.152, 11.628). Yet the poem speaks of the journey as a descent: “need brought me down to the house of Hades” (*Odyssey* 11.164), Achilles asks how he dared “to come down to Hades” (*Odyssey* 11.475), Circe greets the men as those who “have gone down alive into the house of Hades” (*Odyssey* 12.21, a plain rendering), and Odysseus tells Penelope of “the day when I went down into the house of Hades” (*Odyssey* 23.252, a plain rendering). In the last sights, Minos on his seat, the hunter and the punished, he tells what goes on in the depths as an eyewitness of Erebus; for Merry this, with other signs, marks the passage as a later addition (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.565). For what the dead are in the book, the blood they drink and the minds they lack, see [[the-land-of-the-dead]].

**The two halves.** The tale is cut in two by the pause in the hall. The first half is Odysseus' own: his companion, the seer, his mother, and the women of the old houses of Thessaly and Boeotia. The second answers Alcinous' question about the men who died at Troy (*Odyssey* 11.370–372), and holds the heroes of the war, Agamemnon, Achilles and Ajax, before the sights of the oldest dead, Minos, Orion, Tityus, Tantalus, Sisyphus and Heracles. Persephone frames the women, sending them up and scattering them (*Odyssey* 11.226, 11.385–386), and at the end it is the fear of what she may send that drives Odysseus away (*Odyssey* 11.633–635).

**The second journey to the dead.** The last book of the poem has a second Nekyia. Hermes of Cyllene calls out the souls of the suitors with his golden wand, and they follow him gibbering, like bats in a cave, past the streams of Oceanus and the White Rock, past the gates of the Sun and the land of dreams, to the meadow of asphodel, “where the souls dwell, the phantoms of the dead” (*Odyssey* 24.1–14, a plain rendering; see [[hermes]]). There, in the verses of the eleventh book, are the souls of Achilles, Patroclus, Antilochus and Ajax, and the soul of Agamemnon with those who died with him in the house of Aegisthus (*Odyssey* 24.15–22, after 11.467–470 and 11.387–389); Agamemnon, hearing the suitors' story, praises Penelope and sets her against the daughter of Tyndareus, who killed her wedded husband, in a verse that he spoke to Odysseus here (*Odyssey* 24.191–202; 24.202 = 11.434). Merry calls it “the second νέκυια” (on 10.515).

**Told again.** In the summary that he gives Penelope, Odysseus tells how he came “to the dank house of Hades, to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias, in his ship of many benches, and saw all his companions and his mother, who bore him and reared him when he was small” (*Odyssey* 23.322–325, a plain rendering). Of the seer's words he has already told her the journey with the oar and the gentle death (*Odyssey* 23.248–284, Greek only).

## Outside Homer

**Virgil.** Merry sets beside the gathering of the dead at the pit the souls that throng to Orpheus' song in the *Georgics*, mothers and men, the bodies of great-hearted heroes, boys and unwed girls and young men laid on the pyre before their parents' eyes (*Georgics* 4.471–477), verses that come again in the crowd on the bank of the river of the underworld in *Aeneid* 6 (*Aeneid* 6.305–308; Merry on 11.38–43). Aeneas, like Odysseus, tries three times to embrace a ghost, his wife Creusa at Troy and his father Anchises in the underworld, and three times it slips from his hands, light as the winds and very like a fleeting dream (*Aeneid* 2.792–794 = 6.700–702; Merry on 11.206; see [[virgil]] and [[virgil-the-unburied]]).

**Plato and Proclus.** Socrates begins the myth of Er, his own account of the dead, by saying that it will not be a tale to Alcinous but the tale of a valiant man (*Republic* 10.614b; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]); Proclus takes the jest as Plato all but saying that he set Homer's Nekyia before him as his model (*On the Republic* I 169 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). For the verses of the book that Plato would strike from the poems his guardians hear, see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]].

**Tragedy.** Aeschylus wrote a *Soul-Summoners* (Ψυχαγωγοί). In one fragment the men of a race that lives “about the lake” say that they honor Hermes as their ancestor (fr. 273 Nauck); in another someone is told that a heron flying overhead will strike him with its dung, and that from it the spine of a creature of the sea will rot his aged skin (fr. 275 Nauck), a prophecy that Merry takes as another form of Odysseus' death “out of the sea” (on 11.134; see [[telegonus-kills-odysseus]] and [[aeschylus]]).

**A Boeotian book.** Lauer, as Merry reports, claimed a Boeotian author for the whole book, since the visit to the dead belongs most fitly to a country devoted to the worship of Hades and Persephone and full of oracles of the dead: of Amphiaraus at Thebes, of Teiresias at the springs of Tilphosa, of Trophonius at Lebadea, and an opening into the lower world at Coronea. Merry does not go so far for the whole book, but accepts the argument for the catalogue of women (on 11.225; see [[catalogue-of-heroines]]). For the ancient names of the book, see [[the-title-of-odyssey-11]].
