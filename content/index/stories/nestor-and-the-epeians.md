## The story

**The raid.** The quarrel “between the Eleans and us” arose “over a cattle raid”: Nestor was driving off cattle “in reprisal” (ῥύσια) when he killed Itymoneus, who was defending his cows (11.671–676; see [[itymoneus]] and [[raiding]]).

{{quote:c461112a-ff1f-57f1-a8a0-93b46c8393cf}}

The plunder was fifty herds each of cattle, sheep, pigs and goats, and a hundred and fifty mares, many with foals, and it was driven “into Neleian Pylos, by night”, to the joy of Neleus (11.677–684; see [[cattle]], [[sheep]], [[boar|pigs]], [[goats]], [[horses]] and [[neleus]]).

**The debts.** At dawn the leaders of the Pylians divided the spoil among all to whom the Epeians owed a debt (11.685–688; see [[division-of-spoils]]). Pylos was weak: Heracles had killed its best men, eleven of the twelve sons of Neleus among them, and the Epeians, “haughty because of this”, had treated the Pylians “with outrage” (11.689–695; see [[heracles-sacks-pylos]]). Neleus chose a large share for himself, for Augeas had kept the four prize-winning horses he had sent to race for a tripod in Elis, and gave the rest to the people, “so that no one might go away cheated of his fair share” (11.696–705; see [[augeas]] and [[contests]]).

**The siege.** On the third day the Epeians came in force, the young Moliones among them, and besieged Thryoessa on the Alpheus, “on the border of sandy Pylos” (11.706–713; see [[the-moliones]] and [[thryum]]). Athena came running from Olympus by night to bid the Pylians arm (11.714–716; see [[athena]]).

{{quote:7169e58c-3cf3-5c14-b48c-96e206062b32}}

**The battle.** The horsemen waited for dawn by the Minyeios near Arene, reached the Alpheus at midday, sacrificed, and slept in their armor by the stream (11.722–732; see [[minyeios]], [[arene]], [[alpheus]] and [[sacrifice]]). At sunrise Nestor killed the first man, Mulius, Augeas' son-in-law, and leapt into his chariot. The Epeians fled; he took fifty chariots, and would have killed the Moliones had Poseidon not hidden them in mist (11.735–752; see [[mulius]]).

{{quote:86f19102-62fb-51d4-9776-1b4d482743ff}}

Buprasium, the Olenian Rock and Alesium belong to the land of the Epeians in the Catalogue (2.615–617; see [[buprasium]], [[olenian-rock]] and [[alesium]]): the Pylians drove them back into their own country.

## In the poem

**Why he tells it.** Nestor begins with the wish that opens his other tales, “If only I were young like that, and my strength were steadfast” (11.670 = 7.157; compare 23.629; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]), and ends in the present: “Such was I among men, if that was ever so. But Achilles / will have the profit of his prowess alone” (11.762–763; see [[such-was-i-if-ever]]). The young Nestor fought though his father held him back, and his people thanked him beside Zeus; Achilles keeps his prowess to himself. Like Phoenix's Meleager, the story is an example told to persuade (see [[mythological-paradigm]] and [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). It is aimed at Achilles, but it moves the one who hears it: Nestor ends with the plan that Patroclus go out in Achilles' armor, and “stirred the heart in his breast” (11.794–804; see [[nestor]] and [[patroclus]]).

**The horseman on foot.** The “Gerenian horseman” fights his first battle on foot and takes fifty chariots (see [[gerenian-horseman]]). “But even so I stood out among our horsemen” (ἀλλὰ καὶ ὧς ἱππεῦσι μετέπρεπον, 11.720) answers what the old man told Agamemnon in Book 4: “But even so I will be among the horsemen” (ἀλλὰ καὶ ὧς ἱππεῦσι μετέσσομαι, 4.322).

**Debts and shares.** The story is exact about property: debts, reprisals, herds counted out, the king's share, a fair division. The Odyssey tells of a quarrel of the same kind, in which Messenians carried off three hundred sheep from Ithaca “and their herdsmen” (τριηκόσιʼ ἠδὲ νομῆας, as 11.697), and the young Odysseus was sent to Messene to recover the debt (*Odyssey* 21.15–21).

**A tale of Pylos.** The story names men and places found nowhere else in Homer, and many scholars have thought that it comes from older poetry about Pylos (F. Bölte, “Ein pylisches Epos”, *Rheinisches Museum* 83, 1934). Families of historical times claimed descent from Neleus: Herodotus calls the Peisistratids of Athens “Pylians and Neleids” by descent (5.65.3; see [[herodotus]]). The speech that holds the story runs to 148 verses (11.656–803), the longest in the Iliad after Phoenix's (9.434–605), though Patroclus had said there was no time to sit (11.648). Some readers have found it too long for its moment; Patroclus, delayed by it and then by Eurypylus, does not reach Achilles again until Book 16 (16.2).

## Outside Homer

Strabo retells the story step by step and argues from it that Nestor's Pylos was neither the Elean Pylos, which Heracles could not have sacked without ruining the Epeians too, nor the Messenian Pylos by Coryphasium, more than a thousand stades' drive for the raiders' pigs and sheep, but the Pylos in Triphylia (*Geography* 8.3.28–29; see [[strabo-on-nestors-pylos]] and [[pylos]]). Later accounts do not keep Nestor's sequence: Apollodorus has Heracles kill Augeas and the Moliones before he takes Pylos and kills Neleus (*Library* 2.7.2–3; see [[heracles-sacks-pylos]]).
