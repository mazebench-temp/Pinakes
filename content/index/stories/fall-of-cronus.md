## The story

[[Cronus]], youngest of the Titans, ruled the gods until his son Zeus overthrew him. Zeus and his allies defeated the Titans and shut them away below the earth. Zeus then shared out the world with his brothers.

## In the poem

Book 1 calls Zeus "son of Cronus" seven times (1.397, 1.405, 1.498, 1.502, 1.528, 1.539, 1.552). Two of these come at his greatest moments. Thetis finds him "far-seeing son of Cronus, sitting apart from the others" on the highest peak of Olympus (1.498–499). And he confirms his promise to her with a nod:

The one myth of the gods' early days in Book 1 comes from a time when Zeus' rule could still be challenged. When Hera, Poseidon and Athena tried to bind him, Thetis saved Zeus by calling up the hundred-handed Briareus (1.396–406; see [[binding-of-zeus]]). In Hesiod the Hundred-Handers are the allies who helped Zeus win the war against the Titans. The binding story gives Briareus the same role, as the power that keeps Zeus on his throne.

Other books of the Iliad assume the whole story. Cronus and Iapetus sit in the depths of Tartarus, far from the sun and the winds (8.478–481). Zeus "set Cronus beneath the earth and the barren sea" (14.203–204). Hera swears by "the gods beneath Tartarus, who are called Titans" (14.278–279). Poseidon tells how the three sons of Cronus and Rhea drew lots for the sky, the sea and the misty dark, and left the earth and Olympus common to all (15.187–193).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells the full story in the *Theogony*. Cronus swallowed his children as they were born, so that none of them would take his throne. Rhea gave him a stone wrapped in swaddling clothes in place of the infant Zeus (453–506). When the war against the Titans had lasted ten years, Zeus freed the Hundred-Handers, Briareus, Cottus and Gyges, and with their help he won it. The Titans were shut in Tartarus (617–735). Apollodorus tells the same story more briefly (*Library* 1.1.5–1.2.1).
