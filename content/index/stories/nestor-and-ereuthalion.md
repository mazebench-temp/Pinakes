## The story

Book 4 gives the story a single line. Agamemnon, reviewing the army, finds Nestor marshaling the Pylians and wishes that the old man's knees could follow his spirit, “and your strength be steadfast”, and that old age had come to someone else (4.313–316).

{{quote:e11a5ff3-2647-523f-8a92-c6c75fdc877b}}

The whole story comes in Book 7, when Hector has challenged the best of the Achaeans and no one but Menelaus will stand up (7.124–160). Nestor shames them with his youth. The Pylians and the Arcadians, “fighters with the spear”, were fighting beside the swift-flowing Celadon, under the walls of Pheia, about the streams of the Iardanus (see [[arcadians]], [[pylians]], [[fighters-with-the-spear]], [[celadon]], [[pheia]] and [[iardanus]]). The Arcadians' champion was Ereuthalion. He wore the armor of [[areithous|Areithous]], called the mace-man because he fought not with bow or spear but broke the battalions with an iron mace, until [[lycurgus-of-arcadia|Lycurgus]] killed him by guile in a narrow road where the mace was no use, and stripped him of the armor that bronze Ares had given him. When Lycurgus grew old, he gave it to Ereuthalion, his dear attendant (see [[mace]]). In it Ereuthalion challenged all the best men. They trembled and were afraid, and none dared; but Nestor's spirit drove him to fight, though he was the youngest of them all, and Athena gave him the triumph. He killed the tallest and mightiest man he ever killed, who lay sprawled this way and that (7.132–156; see [[ereuthalion]]). If he were young as he was then, Hector would soon have his fight (7.157–160). Nine men stand up (7.161–169).

## In the poem

**The wish for youth.** Nestor's stories of his youth begin with the same wish. “If only I were young like that, and my strength were steadfast” (εἴθʼ ὣς ἡβώοιμι, βίη δέ μοι ἔμπεδος εἴη) opens the Ereuthalion story in Book 7 (7.157), his tale of the cattle raid on Elis in Book 11 (11.670), and his memory of the funeral games of Amarynceus in Book 23 (23.629). In Book 4 Agamemnon speaks the second half of the line for him, wishing that his “strength be steadfast” (βίη δέ τοι ἔμπεδος εἴη, 4.314), and Nestor answers with the wish in his own form, to be as he was when he killed Ereuthalion (4.318–319). See [[old-age-and-memory]] and [[nestor]].

**Two uses of one memory.** In Book 7 the story is a paradigm, told to shame younger men into action: I, the youngest, faced a greater champion than Hector (see [[mythological-paradigm]]). It works. In Book 4 the story is not told at all. Nestor names the day and draws a different lesson from it: “the gods never give men all things at once” (4.320). He had strength then; he has counsel now. He will be among the horsemen and command them with advice and words, “for that is the prize of the old”, and the younger men will wield the spears (4.322–325; see [[geras]]). The memory that shames others in Book 7 reconciles Nestor to his age in Book 4.

**A duel before Troy.** The story is a single combat with a champion who challenges all comers, and Nestor tells it in full when Hector has just issued such a challenge (7.67–91; see [[single-combat]]). The armor that passes from Ares to Areithous, to Lycurgus and to Ereuthalion has a history, like the scepter of Agamemnon (2.100–108; see [[the-scepter-of-agamemnon]]). Nestor's other stories of his youth belong to the same world of Pylos and its neighbors: the Lapiths and the Centaurs (1.260–272; see [[nestor-and-the-centaurs]]), the war with the Epeians (11.670–761; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]) and the games at Buprasium (23.629–642).
