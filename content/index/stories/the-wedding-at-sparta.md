## The story

**Two marriages.** The travelers drive up to the house of Menelaus and find him feasting his kinsmen for a double wedding (*Odyssey* 4.1–4; see [[menelaus]] and [[lacedaemon]]):

{{quote:4c06b464-e559-59a9-bda3-f50563fde502}}

The daughter goes out of the house and a bride comes into it. Hermione, Helen's only child, is sent with horses and chariots to the son of Achilles in the city of the Myrmidons, to keep a promise Menelaus made at Troy (see [[hermione-daughter-of-helen]], [[neoptolemus]], [[myrmidons]] and [[chariot]]). For Megapenthes, his son by a slave woman, Menelaus is bringing home the daughter of Alector of Sparta (see [[megapenthes]] and [[alector]]). The son's name, “great grief”, recalls the sorrow Helen's flight had caused, W. W. Merry suggests (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.11); his mother, though the text calls her δούλη, was no slave by birth but a captive (Merry on 4.12), and some ancient readers took Δούλη for her name (scholia, in Merry's critical note on 4.12; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 4.12). The gods gave Helen no more children after Hermione (*Odyssey* 4.12–14; see [[helen]]).

**The feast.** The neighbors and kinsmen feast throughout the high-roofed house, and a singer and two tumblers entertain them (see [[feast]], [[house]], [[tumblers]] and [[lyre]]):

## In the poem

**A house in festival.** The wedding is the first thing the strangers find at Sparta, and it explains what happens at the door. Merry takes δαινύντα γάμον as “giving a wedding feast”, comparing δαίνυ τάφον, the funeral feast that Orestes gave the Argives (*Odyssey* 3.309; Merry on 4.3 and on 3.309). It was not the wedding day itself: Merry follows Athenaeus, as he reports him, in placing the scene after the chief days of the festival, when the feasting still went on, but he doubts Nitzsch's inference that Hermione had already gone, since the imperfects πέμπε, ἤγετο and ἐξετέλειον (“were bringing their wedding to pass”) suggest that her departure was being prepared and that Alector's daughter was on her way to Megapenthes (Merry on 4.3 and 4.7). Telemachus comes in at the end of the feasting, Merry thinks, and the bustle of the house would explain why Eteoneus asks whether to unyoke the strangers' horses or send them on to some other man (*Odyssey* 4.22–29; see [[eteoneus]] and [[xenia]]): Merry finds in his doubt a house still “in a state of bustle with the festivities”, and reports a scholium that thought the treachery of Paris, once a guest in that house, had made the household wary of strangers (Merry on 4.29).

**Silence after the opening.** After *Odyssey* 4.19 the wedding is not mentioned again. The strangers are bathed, seated beside Menelaus and served a meal of their own (*Odyssey* 4.47–68; see [[feast-type-scene]] and [[water-for-the-hands]]), and no singer, dancer or bride appears in the talk that follows, that evening or the next day. The banqueters who come to the king's house at the close of the Sparta scenes, driving sheep and bringing wine while their wives send bread (*Odyssey* 4.620–624), are not the wedding guests of the opening. They bring their own food, as to a shared feast, an ἔρανος, which Merry calls “altogether a different thing” from the wedding feast Menelaus gives his neighbors and kinsmen; for that confusion most editors since Wolf reject the verses (Merry on 4.621–624; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 4.620–624).

**Two weddings and a third.** The bride goes to the son of the man Menelaus had fought beside at Troy. Briseis remembered that Patroclus had promised to make her Achilles' wedded wife, to take her in the ships to Phthia, “and give the marriage feast among the Myrmidons” (δαίσειν δὲ γάμον, *Iliad* 19.297–299; see [[briseis]] and [[marriage]]); the son of Achilles now has his bride sent to the same city. In Ithaca, in the same book, the suitors think that Penelope is preparing a marriage for them (γάμον, *Odyssey* 4.770; see [[the-suitors]]).

**The text.** Athenaeus, as Merry reports him (on 4.3–19 and 4.15–19, from *Deipnosophistae* 5.180), says that Diodorus, a follower of Aristophanes, struck out the whole wedding, and that the singer and the tumblers were brought into Menelaus' house from the Shield of Achilles by Aristarchus' school, who did not see that the wedding feast was over (*Iliad* 18.604–606; see [[the-singer-on-the-shield]], [[athenaeus]] and [[aristarchus]]). Here the singer leads the song and dance (ἐξάρχοντος, singular), where the Shield has the plural, of the tumblers (see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 4.19). A scholium calls the five verses Aristarchus', not Homer's (Merry's critical note on 4.15–19). Merry answers the first charge that without *Odyssey* 4.3–19 the passage from 4.2 to 4.20 is very awkward (on 4.3–19). See the translation's notes on *Odyssey* 4.3–19 and 4.19.

## Outside Homer

The later tradition gave Hermione another suitor. In Euripides' *Andromache* she is the wife of Neoptolemus, and Orestes tells her that Menelaus had given her to him as his wife before the war, and afterwards promised her to the man who now has her, if he sacked the city of Troy (*Andromache* 966–970); when Neoptolemus came home from Troy, Orestes begged him to give up the marriage and was insulted for the murder of his mother (*Andromache* 971–978; see [[euripides-andromache]] and [[orestes]]).
