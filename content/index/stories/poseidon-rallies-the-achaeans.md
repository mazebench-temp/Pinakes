## The story

**The crossing.** Zeus has brought Hector to the ships and turns his eyes away, sure that no god will help either side (13.1–9; see [[zeus]]). Poseidon is watching.

{{quote:acadcc9f-8855-5637-b022-836c6c09ca56}}

In three strides and a fourth he reaches his golden house at Aegae, drives over the sea, leaves his horses in a cave between Tenedos and Imbros, and goes to the army (13.17–38; see [[samothrace]], [[aegae]] and [[chariot-journey]]).

**Calchas and Thoas.** In Calchas' likeness he fills the two Ajaxes with strength and darts away like a hawk, and the son of Oileus knows the god from behind (13.43–82; see [[calchas]], [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). He shames the weary men in the rear, and the battalions close around the two Ajaxes, and Hector's charge breaks on them (13.83–148; see [[battle-order]]). When Hector kills Amphimachus, Poseidon's grandson, the god speaks to Idomeneus in the voice of Thoas and sends him back to the fight (13.206–245; see [[amphimachus]], [[thoas]] and [[idomeneus]]).

**The brothers.** Zeus wills victory for Hector, to give glory to Achilles, but not the destruction of the Achaean army; Poseidon helps the Argives, but in secret, and the two stretch over both armies the rope of strife and war (13.345–360; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[the-rope-of-war]]).

{{quote:2018589c-4fed-5861-a440-daab6c48ff18}}

He beats down Alcathous for Idomeneus' spear, guards Antilochus, breaks Adamas' spear-point, and drives the Argives on until “the glory would have been the Achaeans'” (13.434–444, 13.554–565, 13.676–678; see [[alcathous]], [[antilochus]] and [[adamas]]).

**Openly.** In Book 14 he takes Agamemnon by the hand in the likeness of an old man and shouts like nine or ten thousand men (14.135–152; see [[agamemnon]]). Hera puts Zeus to sleep on Ida, and Sleep tells Poseidon to help the Danaans while Zeus sleeps (14.153–360; see [[hera]] and [[hypnos]]). He leads them openly, holding a terrible long sword “like lightning”; Ajax fells Hector with a stone, and the Trojans are routed (14.361–510).

**Ordered out.** Zeus wakes and sees “Poseidon the lord” among the Achaeans (15.4–8). Through Iris he orders him out of the war, as far the stronger and “elder by birth” (15.158–167). Poseidon protests that he is Zeus' equal, one of three brothers who divided the world by lot, but when Iris reminds him that the Erinyes follow the elder, he yields in indignation, threatening lasting anger if Zeus spares Troy (15.184–217; see [[iris]]). He plunges into the sea, “and the Achaean heroes missed him” (15.218–219), and Apollo takes his place beside Hector (15.220–262; see [[apollo]]).

## In the poem

**Against the ban.** Zeus forbade the gods to help either side (8.5–17). Poseidon refused Hera's plea to resist him then, “since he is far the stronger” (8.211); Hera and Athena, setting out against the ban, were turned back by Iris (8.397–437), and Ares will be stopped by Athena (15.113–142). Poseidon alone defies the ban, and for a book and a half his defiance holds back the Trojans (see [[divine-intervention]]).

**In disguise.** In Book 13 he is Calchas, then Thoas, then simply “a man” (13.45, 13.216, 13.357), and only the son of Oileus sees through him, from behind (13.71–72; see [[seeing-the-gods]] and [[divine-epiphany]]). The secrecy is his concession to Zeus' seniority, “Zeus was born first and knew more” (13.355). Once Zeus sleeps he fights openly, with a sword like Zeus' lightning (14.385–386).

**The elder brother.** Zeus claims to be elder and stronger (15.165–167), Poseidon claims equal honor by the lot (15.185–199), and Iris settles it by the Erinyes (15.204). In the battle of the gods Poseidon takes the elder's part toward Apollo, in the narrator's words of Zeus: “since I was born first and know more” (21.440; see [[poseidon]] and [[fall-of-cronus]]). The help slows the plan of Zeus without changing it, and on waking Zeus states the plan in full (15.49–77; see [[the-battle-at-the-ships]] and [[the-third-day-of-battle]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes Zeus the youngest of Rhea's children, not the first-born (*Theogony* 453–458, 478; see [[hesiod]]). The Neoplatonist Proclus quotes “but Zeus was born first and knew more” to explain Zeus' rank above Poseidon (*Commentary on the Cratylus* 151), and quotes the verse again in his commentary on the *Timaeus* (see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). The author of *On the Sublime* counts Poseidon's advance, when the mountains tremble under his feet, among the passages that show the divine in its true greatness (9.8; see [[longinus-poseidons-advance]] and [[longinus-wounded-gods]]).
