## The story

The gods sit in assembly beside Zeus on the golden floor. Hebe pours the nectar, and they pledge one another in golden cups, “looking out upon the city of the Trojans” (4.1–4; see [[hebe]] and [[nectar]]). Zeus sets out to provoke Hera “with cutting words, speaking with sly intent” (4.5–6; see [[kertomia]]). Menelaus has two goddesses to help him, he says, Argive Hera and Athena of Alalcomenae, but they sit apart and look on, while Aphrodite is always at Paris' side and has just saved him from death (4.7–12; see [[aphrodite-rescues-paris]]). The victory belongs to Menelaus (4.13). Then he makes an offer.

{{quote:474927d2-1f6f-5867-b037-55b0e578471a}}

Athena and Hera mutter against it. Athena says nothing, sulking at her father, but Hera cannot hold in her anger. Is her labor to be for nothing, the sweat she sweated and the horses she wore out gathering the army against Priam and his sons? “Do it; but not all of us other gods approve” (4.20–29). Zeus answers, deeply troubled. What have Priam and his sons done to her, that she rages to sack Ilios? If she could go through the gates and eat Priam raw, and his sons and the other Trojans, then she might heal her anger (4.30–36). He will not quarrel over it. But he sets a condition.

{{quote:d9eb6c4b-2749-5d81-9161-86d4cfc81488}}

Hera answers at once with three cities of her own.

{{quote:b9f6ebb9-3565-53c5-bd48-647d61afbfa9}}

In return she asks that her own labor not go unfulfilled, since she too is a god, of the same birth as Zeus, and his wife. “Let us give way to each other in this, I to you and you to me”, and the other gods will follow (4.57–64). Zeus should send Athena at once, to contrive that the Trojans be the first to do harm in defiance of the oaths. The father of men and gods does not disobey. He gives Athena the order in Hera's words, and she goes, eager already, darting down from Olympus like a shooting star (4.64–79; see [[shooting-star]] and [[pandarus-breaks-the-truce]]).

## In the poem

**Does Zeus mean it?** Zeus proposes peace, though he has promised Thetis to honor Achilles by giving the Trojans the upper hand until the Achaeans honor her son (1.505–510, 1.524–527; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). A peace now would leave that promise unkept. The narrator says how the offer is made: Zeus “set about provoking Hera” (ἐρεθιζέμεν, 4.5), “speaking with sly intent” (παραβλήδην, 4.6). Readers have taken the offer as a move made to be refused, and Hera refuses it: Zeus gets the war his promise needs, and it is Hera who asks for it. Yet his love for Troy is real (4.44–49), and he gives the city up “with an unwilling heart” (4.43). The poem leaves both in view.

**A treaty in heaven.** The scene is a bargain. On earth two armies have sworn oaths, with the gods as witnesses, to settle the war by a duel (3.264–301; see [[the-oaths-sworn]]). In heaven two gods make their own agreement, “I to you and you to me” (4.63), and one of its terms is that the oaths on earth be broken. The treaty of men cost lambs and wine. The treaty of the gods is paid in cities: Hera gives three that she loves for one that she hates. See [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]].

**The cities.** Hera's three cities are the cities of the kings she fights for: in the Catalogue Argos is Diomedes' city, Mycenae Agamemnon's, and Sparta one of the towns of Menelaus (2.559, 2.569, 2.582). She does not say why she hates Troy, and Zeus' question, what Priam and his sons have done to her, gets no answer. The Iliad names a cause only in its last book, in lines that Aristarchus rejected (24.25–30). See [[hera]], [[argos]], [[sparta]] and [[mycenae]].

**The city Zeus loved.** Zeus gives up Ilios “willingly, though with an unwilling heart” (4.43). Of all the cities of men it was the most honored in his heart, because its people fed his altar with “libation and the savor of the burnt fat” (4.44–49; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). The same two lines return in Book 24, when Zeus tells the gods why Hector's body should be ransomed: Hector too never failed in his gifts (24.66–70). Here the lines give up the city; there they save the body of its defender. See [[zeus]] and [[troy]].

**Hatred.** The picture of Hera eating Priam raw is the first of three such wishes in the poem. Achilles wishes his fury would let him carve Hector's flesh and eat it raw (22.346–347), and Hecuba wishes she could fasten her teeth in Achilles' liver (24.212–213).

**Quarrel and agreement.** The scene answers the quarrel on Olympus at the end of Book 1, when Hera challenged Zeus over his promise to Thetis and he silenced her with a threat (1.536–567; see [[quarrel-on-olympus]]). This time Zeus avoids a quarrel (νεῖκος, 4.37), and the two agree. Their agreement costs mortals more than their quarrel did.

## Outside Homer

Readers have asked whether Hera's offer remembers a real destruction of her cities, the fall of the Mycenaean palaces around 1200 BCE or the sack of Mycenae by Argos in the 460s (Diodorus 11.65; Pausanias 2.16.5). The text points to neither, and Zeus never takes up the offer; most critics read it within the scene (see [[hera]]).

Virgil ends his war with a scene of the same kind. In the last book of the *Aeneid* Jupiter and Juno come to terms: Juno gives up her anger on condition that the Latins keep their name, their language and their dress, and Jupiter grants it (*Aeneid* 12.791–842). Virgil's bargain ends a war; Homer's begins one again.
