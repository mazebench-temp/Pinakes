## The story

Odysseus, washed up on Scheria, has been received by Alcinous, king of the Phaeacians, without giving his name (see [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]). At the morning feast the blind singer Demodocus sings of the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles, and Odysseus draws his purple mantle over his head and weeps; only Alcinous notices, and he turns the company to the games (*Odyssey* 8.62–103; see [[demodocus]], [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]] and [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]]). In the evening Odysseus himself asks for the song of the wooden horse, and weeps again, as a woman weeps who falls upon the body of her husband; Alcinous stops the singer and asks the stranger his name, his country and why he grieves at the fate of the Argives (*Odyssey* 8.485–586; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and [[alcinous]]). “I am Odysseus, son of Laertes,” he answers, and tells the story of his wanderings (*Odyssey* 9.19–20, a plain rendering; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).

## In the poem

**A song of himself.** Both songs at which Odysseus weeps are about him, and the singer does not know that he is present. The first is of his quarrel with Achilles; the third, which he asks for himself, is of the horse that he brought into the citadel of Troy and of his fight at the house of Deiphobus. Between them comes the song of Ares and Aphrodite, at which he is delighted (*Odyssey* 8.367–369; see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]]).

**Tears that are noticed.** The poem tells his tears twice in the same verses, and twice the king alone sees them (*Odyssey* 8.93–95 = 8.532–534). In Sparta his son had wept in the same way at the mention of his father, holding up his purple cloak before his eyes, and Menelaus noticed him (*Odyssey* 4.113–119; see [[telemachus]] and [[menelaus]]); in Ithaca his wife wept at Phemius' song of the returns (*Odyssey* 1.336–344; see [[the-song-of-the-returns]]). The first time Alcinous covers the tears with the games; the second time he names them, and from the stranger's grief he comes to the question of his name (*Odyssey* 8.540–556; see [[tears]]).

**Recognition.** The tears answer what the stranger has not said. Arete had asked him “Who are you among men, and from where?”, and he answered only how he came to Scheria (*Odyssey* 7.238–297; see [[who-are-you-among-men]]); now Alcinous asks his name, his land and his city, and why he weeps (*Odyssey* 8.550–586). The answer, at the beginning of his great tale, gives his name at last (*Odyssey* 9.19–20).
