## The story

**Zeus looks away.** Having brought Hector and the Trojans to the ships, Zeus turns his shining eyes from Troy to the far peoples of the north, “for he did not expect in his heart that any of the immortals / would come to help either the Trojans or the Danaans” (13.8–9; see [[zeus]] and [[abii]]). But Poseidon is watching from the peak of Samothrace. Pitying the Achaeans and “fiercely indignant with Zeus” (13.16), he strides to his house at Aegae, drives his chariot over the sea and leaves his horses in a cave between Tenedos and Imbros (13.10–38; see [[samothrace]], [[aegae]], [[imbros]] and [[chariot-journey]]).

**The close ranks.** In the likeness of Calchas he strikes the two Ajaxes with his staff and fills them with might; the son of Oileus knows the god from behind as he goes (13.43–80; see [[the-two-ajaxes]], [[calchas]] and [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). He shames the weary men at the rear, who weep to see the Trojans over the wall, and the battalions form around the two Ajaxes, “shield pressed on shield, helmet on helmet, man on man” (13.131; see [[battle-order]]). Hector comes down on them like a boulder loosed by a winter torrent and is stopped, reeling (13.136–148; see [[hector]] and [[the-rolling-boulder]]). Teucer kills Imbrius, Priam's son-in-law; Hector kills Amphimachus, Poseidon's grandson; and the son of Oileus sends Imbrius' head rolling to Hector's feet (13.169–205; see [[teucer]], [[imbrius]] and [[amphimachus]]).

**Idomeneus and Meriones.** Angry for his grandson, Poseidon rouses Idomeneus in the likeness of Thoas (13.206–245). Idomeneus meets Meriones, who has come for a spear; they speak of the ambush, where the brave man shows himself, and go out like Ares and his son Rout to the left, since the two Ajaxes and Teucer hold the middle (13.246–329; see [[idomeneus]], [[meriones]] and [[ares-and-rout]]). The battle closes like a dust storm, and the narrator gives its cause: Zeus wants victory for Hector to honor Achilles, but not the ruin of the Achaean army, and Poseidon, the younger brother, helps the Achaeans in secret; between them they stretch the rope of strife and war over both armies (13.330–360; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[the-rope-of-war]]).

**On the left.** Half-gray as he is, Idomeneus kills Othryoneus, who had been promised Cassandra, and Asius, on foot in front of his horses, as the narrator foretold at the wall (13.361–401; 12.113–117; see [[othryoneus]] and [[asius]]). Deiphobus, aiming at him, kills Hypsenor; Idomeneus kills Alcathous, whom Poseidon holds fast, and boasts of his descent from Zeus through Minos (13.402–454; see [[deiphobus]], [[alcathous]] and [[minos]]). Deiphobus fetches Aeneas, and the fight over Alcathous costs Ascalaphus, son of Ares, his life (13.455–539; see [[aeneas]] and [[ascalaphus]]). Poseidon keeps every weapon from Antilochus (13.540–575; see [[antilochus]]). Menelaus wounds Helenus, kills Peisander and denounces the Trojans, who can never have their fill of war (13.576–642; see [[menelaus]] and [[satiety-of-all-things]]). Meriones kills Harpalion, whose father follows the body in tears, and Paris, in anger for his guest-friend, kills Euchenor of Corinth (13.643–672; see [[harpalion]], [[paris]] and [[euchenor]]).

**At the breach.** Hector, unaware of these losses, holds on where he first broke in, by the ships of Ajax and Protesilaus, where the wall was built lowest; the Boeotians, Ionians, Locrians, Phthians and Epeians hold him off, the two Ajaxes stand side by side like oxen at the plow, and the Locrians' arrows break the Trojan ranks (13.673–722; see [[the-achaean-wall]], [[locrians]] and [[oxen-at-the-plow]]). The Trojans would have fallen back to Ilios had not Polydamas told Hector to call the best men together, for “a man insatiable of war” waits by the ships (13.723–747; see [[polydamas]]). Hector agrees, finds his brothers and allies dead or wounded, rebukes Paris and leads the Trojans on like a storm-driven sea (13.748–808). Ajax challenges him, an eagle flies on the right, and Hector answers with the wish to be the son of Zeus and Hera (13.809–832; see [[ajax]]):

## In the poem

**Against Zeus' design.** Zeus has promised Hector power to kill “until he comes to the well-benched ships” (11.193; see [[the-third-day-of-battle]]), and Achilles will not fight until Hector “comes to the huts and ships of the Myrmidons, / killing the Argives, and burns the ships with fire” (9.652–653). Zeus brings Hector to the ships and looks away, and Poseidon, who would not fight Zeus in Book 8 (8.208–211), acts in secret, “in the likeness of a man” (13.357). So the first book of the battle at the ships is a book of Achaean success inside a day of Trojan victory: “soon the glory would have been the Achaeans'”, so hard did Poseidon urge them on (13.676–678; see [[would-have-had-not]]).

**Two fronts.** Most of the killing happens on the left, while Hector holds the breach and comes to the left only to look for his brothers (13.765). No great hero falls; the dead are lesser men, many of them given a family: Priam's sons-in-law, Cassandra's suitor, Poseidon's grandson, Ares' son.

**How it goes on.** Nestor hears the din and sees the wall thrown down (14.1–15). While Hera lulls Zeus to sleep, Poseidon leads the Argives openly, and Ajax fells Hector with a stone (14.354–439). Zeus wakes, sees Poseidon among the Argives, and sends him back to the sea (15.4–8, 15.158–219). Apollo throws down the wall, and Hector lays hold of the stern of Protesilaus' ship and calls for fire while Ajax keeps it off with his spear (15.355–366, 15.704–746; see [[protesilaus]]). The first ship burns in Book 16, and Patroclus puts out the fire and drives the Trojans away (16.112–124, 16.284–305; see [[patroclus]]).

## Outside Homer

Strabo already uses the name. Explaining who the Phthians of 13.685–700 are, he says that “in the battle at the ships” (ἐν τῇ ἐπὶ ναυσὶ μάχῃ) the poet keeps Achilles' Phthians at rest beside the ships and has Philoctetes' men under Medon and Protesilaus' men under Podarces fight (*Geography* 9.5.7; see [[phthians]] and [[strabo]]). Aelian's list of the parts of the Iliad once sung on their own begins with “the battle at the ships” (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14; see [[aelian]]). It became the traditional title of Book 13 (see [[title-of-book-13]]).
