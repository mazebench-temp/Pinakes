## The story

The Dream has told Agamemnon to arm the Achaeans “with all speed” (πανσυδίῃ), for Troy can be taken now (see [[agamemnons-dream]]). He does not do it at once. First he seats a council of the elders beside Nestor's ship, tells them the dream, and adds a plan of his own.

{{quote:5c269b4c-6904-58ac-9ae6-b48cdbc739b4}}

He will test the men (πειρήσομαι; see [[peira]]) “as is the custom” (ἣ θέμις ἐστί) by ordering them to flee, and the elders are to hold them back. [[Nestor]] says only that the dream must be taken seriously, since the man who saw it claims to be the best of the Achaeans; of the test he says nothing (2.79–83).

At the assembly Agamemnon rises with the scepter of his fathers and gives the army every reason to go home. Zeus has bound him in blind folly ([[ate|ἄτη]]) and devised an evil deceit ([[apate|ἀπάτη]]); the Achaeans outnumber the Trojans many times over, but the allies thwart him; nine years have passed, the ships' timbers have rotted, and wives and children wait at home. “Let us flee with our ships to our dear native land” (2.110–141).

The army takes him at his word. The assembly heaves like the sea and bows like a field of grain under the wind, and the men run for the ships with a shout, clearing the launching trenches and pulling out the props.

{{quote:2ee51c7c-6e2d-51bf-81a6-2731f4973478}}

Hera sends Athena, and Athena finds [[Odysseus]] standing grieved by his ship. He takes Agamemnon's scepter and goes through the camp, holding back the kings with gentle words and striking the commoners (2.182–206). The men stream back to the assembly. Only [[Thersites]] goes on shouting, until Odysseus beats him (see [[odysseus-and-thersites]]). Then Odysseus reminds the army of the sign at Aulis (see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]), Nestor calls for battle and advises marshaling the men by tribes and clans, and Agamemnon sends them to their meal before the fighting (2.278–393).

## In the poem

The poem gives the test no motive beyond Agamemnon's “as is the custom”. What custom he means is not said, and the plan is strange. A king who has just been promised victory proposes flight, and trusts the elders to stop an army that has been at war for nine years. The episode is usually called the Diapeira, “the Testing”, and it has been read in several ways. Some take it as a general's ruse: propose retreat, so that the men will insist on fighting. Others see a king who has misjudged his army, or a poet exploring how near the army is to breaking. Nineteenth-century analysts took the awkward join of Dream and Test as a sign that two versions had been combined. Most modern readers take the scene as designed.

Whatever the plan, the test exposes the army and its king. The men believe the order to flee at once; the Dream's promise of victory was never told to them. Agamemnon's pretended complaint is truer than he knows, since Zeus has indeed deceived him (2.114). The man who saves the day is Odysseus, not Agamemnon, and he does it with the king's own scepter (see [[scepter]]). The nearly accomplished homecoming shows what Achilles' withdrawal and nine years of war have cost. In Book 9, after a day of defeat, Agamemnon repeats the same lines in tears, and this time he means them (9.17–28).

The test also turns the book. An army that almost sailed home is argued back into the war, marshaled by Nestor's tribes and clans, and then counted in the Catalogue. When Athena passes through the ranks with the aegis, “war became sweeter to them than to go home” (2.453–454; see [[nostos]] and [[the-army-and-its-leaders]]).

## Outside Homer

Socrates' accuser, Xenophon reports, said that he often quoted Odysseus' words to the kings and to the men of the people (2.188–191, 2.198–202) as if the poet approved of beating the poor. Xenophon answers that Socrates meant only that the useless, whether rich or poor, must be kept in check (*Memorabilia* 1.2.58–59; see [[xenophon-odysseus-scepter]]).
