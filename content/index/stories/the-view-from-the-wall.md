## The story

Iris, in the likeness of Priam's daughter [[Laodice]], finds Helen in the hall, weaving a great purple cloth, “and working into it the many trials of the horse-taming Trojans and the bronze-armored Achaeans that they were suffering for her sake at the hands of Ares” (3.125–128). The war has stopped, Iris tells her; the armies sit leaning on their shields, and Alexander and Menelaus will fight for her. The goddess puts in her heart “sweet longing … for her former husband, and her city, and her parents” (3.139–140). Helen veils herself and goes out in tears, with her handmaids [[Aethra]] and [[Clymene]], to the Scaean gates (3.141–145; see [[scaean-gates]] and [[helen-and-theseus]]).

There the elders of Troy sit with Priam, too old to fight but fine speakers, like cicadas on a tree (3.146–153; see [[trojan-elders]] and [[like-cicadas]]). When they see her coming, they speak softly among themselves.

{{quote:476eb6c1-ae21-5073-8975-ce9354c33f2e}}

“But even so,” they go on, “let her go home in the ships” (3.159–160; see [[no-blame]]). Priam calls her to sit in front of him and see her former husband and her kinsmen.

{{quote:0bf4d405-d02f-599f-ab87-63f250320f9f}}

He asks her to name a huge man, so handsome and so stately that he looks like a king. Helen answers that she respects and fears her father-in-law, wishes that evil death had pleased her when she followed his son to Troy, and names Agamemnon, “both a good king and a strong spearman”, once her brother-in-law, “dog-faced as I am, if that was ever so” (3.171–180). Priam calls Agamemnon blessed, and remembers the huge army of the Phrygians he joined as an ally long ago, on the day the Amazons came; even they were not as many as the Achaeans (3.181–190; see [[priam-and-the-amazons]]).

Next he asks about a man shorter by a head but broader in the shoulders, who goes along the ranks like a ram among the sheep (3.191–198; see [[ram-among-the-sheep]]). Helen names Odysseus, and Antenor adds what he saw of him on the embassy to Troy, when he looked like a man who knew nothing until he let loose his great voice and words like the snowflakes of winter (3.203–224; see [[the-embassy-to-troy]] and [[snowflakes-of-winter]]). Third, Priam asks about a man who towers over the Argives, and Helen names “huge Ajax, the bulwark of the Achaeans”, and, unasked, Idomeneus among the Cretans, who often stayed as Menelaus' guest in their house (3.225–233). Then she looks for her brothers.

See [[the-death-of-the-dioscuri]]. The heralds come through the city with the offerings for the oaths, and Priam is called down to the plain (3.245–258; see [[the-oaths-sworn]]).

## In the poem

**Why now?** The scene raises an old question. In the tenth year of the war, why does Priam need to be told who Agamemnon and Odysseus are? The poem gives no answer. Most readers take the scene to belong in spirit to the beginning of the war, like the Catalogue of Ships before it and the duel for Helen around it: the first books of the Iliad retell the war's opening while its last year is under way (see [[the-war-in-small]]). The questions also present the Achaean leaders to the audience through Trojan eyes. Others have suggested that Priam could not tell men apart at a distance, or that his questions are a kindness, a way of drawing Helen into talk. The explanations are not exclusive (see [[priams-question]]).

**Helen.** The scene is her first appearance in the poem, and it shows her from every side. She weaves the war, as if she were its chronicler (see [[weaving]] and [[helens-web]]). The old men who suffer for her admire her and want her gone. Priam lays the blame on the gods, while Helen lays it on herself: she wishes she had died, she wastes away in tears (3.176), and she calls herself “dog-faced” (see [[aitios]], [[dog-face]] and [[helen]]). She knows every Achaean by sight, and is not asked to name Menelaus, whom Priam has just called her “former husband” (3.163).

**The cost.** Priam's memory of Phrygia measures the Achaean army by the greatest army he ever saw, and finds it greater (3.190). Antenor's memory looks back to a time before the war, when envoys still came to Troy and were entertained as guests. Helen's brothers are dead without her knowing it, and the narrator tells the audience what no one on the wall can see.

## Outside Homer

The traditional Greek title of Book 3 names three episodes: the Oaths, the View from the Wall (Τειχοσκοπία), and the Duel of Alexander and Menelaus. Euripides retold the scene in the *Phoenician Women*, where an old servant names the Argive leaders for Antigone from the palace roof at Thebes (88–201; see [[euripides-teichoskopia]]).
