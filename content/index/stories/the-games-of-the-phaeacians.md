## The story

**The games proposed.** At the morning feast in the house of Alcinous, Demodocus sings of the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles, and the stranger hides his face in his mantle and weeps whenever the song begins again; only Alcinous, sitting near him, notices (*Odyssey* 8.72–95; see [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]] and [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]). He says nothing of the tears, but turns the company from the feast to the games:

{{quote:ca780242-03b5-574c-8e43-769b080174f3}}

The herald hangs up the lyre and leads the blind singer out, and a countless throng follows them to the place of assembly “to marvel at the games” (*Odyssey* 8.104–110; see [[heralds]], [[demodocus]], [[assembly]] and [[contests]]).

**The contests.** “Young men stood up, many and noble” (*Odyssey* 8.110): twelve whose names, like almost all Phaeacian names, are drawn from seafaring (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.111), Euryalus, “the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals”, the handsomest of the Phaeacians after Laodamas, and the three sons of Alcinous, Laodamas, Halius and Clytoneus (*Odyssey* 8.111–119; see [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]], [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]], [[halius-son-of-alcinous]] and [[clytoneus]]). Each contest is told in a verse or two. In the footrace they fly together over the plain, raising the dust, and Clytoneus finishes ahead of the rest by “the furrow-length of a pair of mules in the fallow”; Euryalus wins the wrestling, Amphialus the jump, Elatreus the discus, and Laodamas, “the good son of Alcinous”, the boxing (*Odyssey* 8.120–130; see [[mules]], [[amphialus]], [[elatreus]] and [[discus]]).

**The invitation and the taunt.** When they have delighted their hearts with the contests, Laodamas proposes to ask the stranger whether he knows some contest: in build he is no mean man, but the sea has broken him (*Odyssey* 8.131–139; see [[nothing-worse-than-the-sea]]). He invites him, “for there is no greater fame for a man, as long as he lives, / than what he achieves with his feet and with his hands” (*Odyssey* 8.147–148; see [[no-greater-fame]] and [[kleos]]). Odysseus answers that sorrows are on his mind far more than contests; he sits in their assembly begging for his homecoming (*Odyssey* 8.153–157). Then Euryalus rebukes him to his face:

{{quote:31663da5-457a-51e2-acbc-f95dda7c5ee7}}

“Looking at him darkly”, Odysseus answers that the gods do not give their gracious gifts to all men alike: one man is less in looks, but a god crowns his words with beauty, another is like the immortals in looks, and no grace crowns his words; “so with you too, your looks are outstanding, and not even a god / would fashion them otherwise, but in mind you are worthless” (*Odyssey* 8.165–177; see [[different-gifts]] and [[looking-darkly]]). He was among the foremost once, when he trusted in his youth and his hands; now he is held fast by misery; “But even so, though I have suffered many evils, I will make trial of the contests; / for your word bites the heart” (*Odyssey* 8.178–185).

**The discus.** He springs up with his mantle still on and seizes a discus bigger and heavier than any the Phaeacians had thrown:

{{quote:9e8f2fef-5937-5179-9d4a-3f8b529ce155}}

**The challenge.** Odysseus is glad, “rejoicing that he saw a gentle companion in the gathering”, and he speaks more lightly: let the young men reach that, and he will send another as far or farther; and let any of them who will make trial of him in boxing or wrestling or even in running, except Laodamas himself, “For he is my host; who would fight with one who makes him welcome?” (*Odyssey* 8.199–213; see [[xenia]]). He knows the polished bow: Philoctetes alone surpassed him at Troy, and of the men now on earth he is far the best, though he will not vie with the men of former days, Heracles and Eurytus of Oechalia, whom Apollo killed in anger because he challenged him to shoot with the bow; he throws the spear as far as another man shoots an arrow; only in running he fears that a Phaeacian may outstrip him, for the sea has loosened his limbs (*Odyssey* 8.214–233; see [[philoctetes]], [[heracles]], [[eurytus]] and [[who-fights-the-gods]]).

**Alcinous' answer.** They all fall silent, and Alcinous alone answers. The stranger's words are not ungracious; he wished to show his excellence, angry at the man who rebuked him; now let him hear what to tell the heroes at home of the Phaeacians' excellence: “For we are not flawless boxers, nor wrestlers, / but we run swiftly with our feet, and are the best with ships; / and always dear to us are the feast and the lyre and the dances / and changes of clothing and warm baths and beds” (*Odyssey* 8.234–249; see [[alcinous]] and [[phaeacians]]). He calls on the dancers, so that the stranger may tell his friends how far the Phaeacians surpass others “in seafaring and in running and in dancing and in song”, and sends for the lyre (*Odyssey* 8.250–255).

**The dance.** Nine chosen stewards level a dancing floor and widen the gathering; Demodocus goes into the middle with the lyre, the young men strike the floor with their feet, and Odysseus gazes at the flashing of their feet and marvels in his heart (*Odyssey* 8.256–265; see [[dance]] and [[lyre]]). Then Demodocus sings of the love of Ares and Aphrodite (*Odyssey* 8.266–366; see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]]). After the song Halius and Laodamas dance alone with a purple ball, one throwing it toward the clouds and the other leaping to catch it before his feet touch the ground, and then dancing on the earth while the other young men beat time (*Odyssey* 8.370–380; see [[ball]]). Odysseus gives Alcinous the praise he had asked for: “you boasted that your dancers were the best, / and indeed it has proved true; awe holds me as I look” (*Odyssey* 8.383–384; see [[awe-holds-me]]).

**Amends.** Alcinous then bids the twelve kings give the stranger guest-gifts, and Euryalus make amends with words and with a gift, “since the word he spoke was by no means fair and right”; Euryalus gives him a bronze sword with a silver hilt and an ivory sheath, and they part with blessings on each other (*Odyssey* 8.385–415; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[amends-and-reconciliation]]).

## In the poem

**Contests and trials.** Athena prepares the day. Before the assembly she pours a wondrous grace on Odysseus and makes him taller and stouter, “so that he might be dear to all the Phaeacians, / and feared and respected, and might accomplish the many contests / in which the Phaeacians made trial of Odysseus” (*Odyssey* 8.18–23; see [[athena]] and [[gold-poured-around-silver]]). He enters only one contest, and Zenodotus rejected the last of those verses for that reason (see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 8.22–23). ἄεθλοι, the word for the contests, is also the Odyssey's word for the trials of its hero (*Odyssey* 1.18; see the translation's key words), and Odysseus answers Laodamas with the two senses side by side: “Sorrows are on my mind far more than contests, / who before have suffered very much and toiled much” (*Odyssey* 8.154–155).

**Games for a guest.** The Iliad's games are funeral games: the games for Patroclus, and those Nestor remembers for Amarynceus (*Iliad* 23.257–897, 23.629–642; see [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-amarynceus]]). The Phaeacians hold theirs for the living, so that a stranger may carry their fame home (*Odyssey* 8.101–103), and Alcinous' two lists, boxing, wrestling, jumping and running before the stranger has thrown, seafaring, running, dancing and song after it, are set in the same verses (*Odyssey* 8.101–103, 8.251–253). Of the contests he first named, running alone is kept, the one that Odysseus has said he fears.

**Odysseus at the games.** In the Iliad's games Odysseus wrestles Telamonian Ajax to a draw and wins the footrace when Athena, at his prayer, makes his limbs light, so that his rival complains that the goddess “always before / stands by Odysseus like a mother and helps him” (*Iliad* 23.700–739, 23.768–783; see [[odysseus]]). In Scheria she stands by him again, in the likeness of a man, to mark his throw (*Odyssey* 8.193–198). The Iliad's weight throw ended in laughter at Epeius' throw (*Iliad* 23.838–840; see [[epeius]]); here the Phaeacians “of the long oars, men famed for ships” cower to the ground at the rush of the stone, and Merry hears intentional irony in the grand epithets set beside their terror (on 8.191; see [[long-oared]] and [[famed-for-ships]]).

**The bow.** The bow is not among the Phaeacians' contests, and Odysseus claims it as his own (*Odyssey* 8.215–228). The bow that he will string in his own hall was a gift of Iphitus, the son of the Eurytus he names here, and Odysseus never took it to war but kept it at home as a memorial of his friend (*Odyssey* 21.11–41, Greek only; see [[eurytus]]).

**The guest who will not compete with his host.** “Witless indeed and a nobody is that man / who brings on strife in contests with his host / in a foreign land; he cuts short all that is his own” (*Odyssey* 8.209–211; see [[strife-with-ones-host]]). The rule of guest-friendship shapes the challenge: Odysseus spares Laodamas, the king's son, who invited him, and quarrels only with Euryalus (see [[xenia]]). The quarrel ends with the gifts, before sunset, when Euryalus makes amends at the king's bidding and Odysseus wishes him prosperity (*Odyssey* 8.396–415).

**One day.** The games fill the middle of the structure file's Day 33, between the morning feast and the gifts at sunset (*Odyssey* 8.417), and the second song of Demodocus is sung in their pause.

## Outside Homer

**The throne at Amyclae.** Merry notes that the dance of the Phaeacians was figured on the throne of Apollo at Amyclae by Bathycles of Magnesia, and quotes Pausanias: “and there is a dance of Phaeacians on the throne, and Demodocus singing” (Pausanias 3.18.7, as Merry cites him on 8.251; see [[pausanias]]). Thiersch placed Bathycles in the twenty-ninth Olympiad and Sillig, more probably in Merry's view, in the fortieth; even the later date, Merry argues, would show that the Homeric poems were popularly known before the recension of the Peisistratids, and that the Odyssey then contained the episode of the Phaeacians (on 8.251).
