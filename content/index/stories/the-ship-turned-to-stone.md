The god complains to Zeus that mortals no longer honor him, since his own descendants have brought his enemy home asleep with more gifts than he would have won at Troy; Zeus lets him do as he wishes, and advises him to turn their ship to stone as it comes home, in sight of the city. ## The story

**The complaint.** When the Phaeacians have set Odysseus ashore on Ithaca and turned for home, Poseidon does not forget the threats he once made against him, and asks Zeus his plan (*Odyssey* 13.125–127; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]). No longer, he says, will he be honored among the gods, since mortals do not honor him, the Phaeacians, “who after all are sprung from my own stock”. He had meant Odysseus to come home only after many sufferings, without taking away his homecoming altogether, since Zeus had promised it; but they have carried him home asleep and given him more gifts than he would have won from Troy (*Odyssey* 13.128–138; see [[poseidon]] and [[phaeacians]]). Zeus answers that the gods do not dishonor the eldest and the best of them; if any man, yielding to his own force, does not honor him, vengeance is always his: “Do as you wish, and as has pleased your heart” (*Odyssey* 13.139–145; see [[zeus]]). Poseidon wishes to shatter the Phaeacians' ship as it comes back from the escort, so that they may cease from escorting men, and to cover their city round about with a great mountain (*Odyssey* 13.146–152). **The stone.** Poseidon goes to Scheria and waits. The ship comes coursing swiftly home, and he comes near her, “and turned her to stone and rooted her fast beneath / with a blow of the flat of his hand; and then he was gone” (*Odyssey* 13.159–164; see [[scheria]]). The Phaeacians on the shore ask one another who has fettered the swift ship on the sea as she was driven home, when she was already in plain sight, “but they did not know how things had been brought about” (*Odyssey* 13.165–170).

**The prophecy fulfilled.** Alcinous knows. “Ah, truly the oracles spoken long ago have come home to me”, he begins, “the oracles of my father”, who used to say that Poseidon begrudged the Phaeacians because they are escorts of all men and come to no harm, and that one day he would shatter a Phaeacian ship as it came back from an escort, and cover their city round about with a great mountain: “So the old man used to say; and now it is all being fulfilled” (*Odyssey* 13.171–178; see [[alcinous]], [[nausithous]] and [[thesphaton]]). He bids them stop escorting mortals and sacrifice twelve picked bulls to Poseidon, in the hope that he may have pity and not cover their city with a towering mountain. They are afraid, and make the bulls ready; and the leaders and rulers of the people of the Phaeacians stand praying to lord Poseidon around the altar (*Odyssey* 13.179–187; see [[sacrifice]], [[prayer]] and [[pompe]]). There the poem leaves them, and turns back to Odysseus waking on Ithaca.

## In the poem

**The prophecy.** The story was foretold before Odysseus told his name. Alcinous, promising him a ship that needs no helmsman and fears no harm, remembered what his father Nausithous used to say, in the verses that he now repeats, and added that the god might fulfill it or leave it unfulfilled, as he pleased (*Odyssey* 8.564–571; see [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]). The god's choice is told here, in the same words: the half-verse of the end, “and now it is all being fulfilled”, is the one with which Halitherses and Odysseus close remembered prophecies (*Odyssey* 13.178; 2.176, 5.302; *Iliad* 2.330; see [[prophecy]] and [[the-prophecy-of-halitherses]]). Alcinous' first verse is the Cyclops' when he learned that he had been blinded by Odysseus, as the seer Telemus had foretold: “Ah, truly the oracles spoken long ago have come home to me” (*Odyssey* 13.172 = 9.507; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). Both prophecies end in Poseidon's anger, one at the son's blinding and one at the escort of the man who blinded him.

**Poseidon's last act.** The anger of Poseidon began with the blinding of his son (*Odyssey* 1.68–75, 9.526–536) and wrecked the raft within sight of Scheria (*Odyssey* 5.282–387); the proem limited it, for he “raged relentlessly / against godlike Odysseus, until he reached his own land” (*Odyssey* 1.20–21). On the morning that Odysseus wakes in Ithaca the god takes his vengeance on those who brought him there, and Athena tells Odysseus that she would not fight with her father's brother (*Odyssey* 13.341–343). After it the god has no further part in the story: he is named again only in a simile, in the instructions of Teiresias as Odysseus repeats them to Penelope, and in a question among the dead (*Odyssey* 23.234, 23.277, 24.109).

**The honor of the gods.** Poseidon's complaint is a complaint of dishonor (*Odyssey* 13.128–130; see [[time]], [[honor-and-dishonor]] and [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]]), and it has a model in the Iliad. When the Achaeans built their wall without hecatombs to the gods, he complained that its fame would spread as far as the dawn and men would forget the wall that he and Apollo built for Laomedon; Zeus answered him in the verse that answers him here, “Ah, shaker of the earth, wide in strength, what a thing you have said!”, and bade him sweep the wall into the sea when the Achaeans had gone home (*Iliad* 7.446–463; *Odyssey* 13.140; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). In both stories Zeus gives Poseidon leave to destroy a work of men that offended him.

**Stone.** At Aulis Zeus turned the serpent that had eaten the sparrows to stone, λᾶαν γάρ μιν ἔθηκε, “for the son of crooked-counseling Cronus turned him to stone, / and we stood there and marveled at what had come to pass” (*Iliad* 2.319–320; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]). Here Zeus advises a stone ship “so that all men may marvel”, and Poseidon ὅς μιν λᾶαν ἔθηκε, “turned her to stone” (*Odyssey* 13.157, 13.163). Niobe, in Achilles' story, is stone on Sipylus, and broods there over the sorrows the gods gave her (*Iliad* 24.614–617; see [[niobe]]).

**The mountain.** The text makes Zeus approve the mountain too, “and to cover their city round about with a great mountain” (*Odyssey* 13.158); Aristophanes read μηδέ, “and not to cover”, doubtless because of the Phaeacians' prayers, but Monro answers that these need not affect what Zeus says now (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 13.158; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 13.158). Merry took the threat to be not that the god would crush the city by hurling a mountain on it, but that he would pen the Phaeacians in with a mountain wall, cutting off their access to the sea and putting an end to their seafaring life (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 8.569). The poem leaves the city's fate untold. The Phaeacians are named again only in Odysseus' accounts of his homecoming, in which they carry him home and give him gifts (*Odyssey* 16.227–232, 19.279–282, 23.338–341).

## Outside Homer

**The rock off Corcyra.** Those who placed Scheria on Corcyra pointed out the ship. Pliny records that off Phalacrum, a promontory of Corcyra, there is a rock “into which, from its likeness, the story goes that Ulysses' ship was changed”, *in quem mutatam Ulixis navem a simili specie fabula est* (*Natural History* 4.53; see [[scheria]]). Monro reports a small island near the entrance of the old harbor of Corfu, which is pointed out as the Phaeacian ship and is perhaps sufficiently “like a swift ship” to have given rise to the story; it is not, he adds, the only claimant, for a rock outside the harbor of Trapani in Sicily was said to have been a Turkish war vessel turned into stone by the Madonna, as Andrew Lang wrote in *Longman's Magazine* (January 1898), quoting Samuel Butler's *The Authoress of the Odyssey* (Monro on *Odyssey* 13.154–158). Monro's volume prints a plate of the old harbor of Corfu after its notes on the thirteenth book.

**Ovid.** In the *Metamorphoses* the ships of Aeneas, turned into sea nymphs, still hate the Greeks: they watched with glad faces the wreck of the ship of Ulysses, and with glad faces saw “the stern of Alcinous stiffen, and stone grow over the wood”, *rigescere puppim … Alcinoi saxumque increscere ligno* (*Metamorphoses* 14.562–565; see [[ovid]]). Ovid makes the Phaeacian ship a ship of Alcinous, and the change a growth of stone over timber.

**Proclus.** Defending the truth of Plato's story of Atlantis, sunk by the sea, Proclus says that one must not claim that the author made the island vanish so as to take away the means of refuting it, “as Homer did with the Phaeacians and with the wall that the Greeks made” (*On the Timaeus* I 190 Diehl; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). The phrase was older, and was used of the Achaean wall alone (see [[aristotle-and-the-wall]]); Proclus' contrast shows that some readers took the Phaeacians, cut off from men by the god, for another of the poet's fictions removed out of reach.
