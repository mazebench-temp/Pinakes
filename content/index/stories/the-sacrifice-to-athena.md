## The story

**The vow.** At sunset, when Athena has said that she will go back to the ship, she goes away “in the likeness of a sea eagle; and amazement seized all who saw it” (*Odyssey* 3.371–372; see [[phene]] and [[thambos]]). Nestor takes Telemachus by the hand: he will be no coward, if the gods go with him as his escorts so young, for this was no other of the gods of Olympus “but the daughter of Zeus, most glorious Tritogeneia, / who also honored your brave father among the Argives” (*Odyssey* 3.374–379; see [[tritogeneia]] and [[athena]]). He asks the goddess for noble fame for himself, his children and his revered wife, and makes his vow:

{{quote:dbdb9966-e203-53a3-957d-7effb131a589}}

“So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him” (*Odyssey* 3.385). In his house he mixes a bowl of the wine that the housekeeper has opened in its eleventh year, and prays to Athena as he pours the libation (*Odyssey* 3.390–394; see [[wine]] and [[libation]]).

**Nestor's orders.** At dawn he sits, holding the scepter, on the polished stones before his high doors, where Neleus used to sit, and his six sons come from their chambers and gather round him, Echephron, Stratius, Perseus, Aretus, Thrasymedes and Peisistratus; they seat Telemachus beside them (*Odyssey* 3.404–416; see [[neleus]], [[scepter]] and [[house]]). Nestor wants the rite done quickly, “so that first of all the gods I may propitiate Athena, / who came to me in plain sight at the rich feast of the god” (*Odyssey* 3.419–420; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]). One son is to go to the plain for a heifer, and the herdsman of the cattle is to drive her in; one to bring Telemachus' companions from the ship, leaving only two; one to call the goldsmith Laerces, “so that he may pour gold around the heifer's horns”; and the rest are to stay and tell the slave women to prepare a feast throughout the house and bring seats, wood and bright water (*Odyssey* 3.421–429; see [[cattle]], [[laerces-the-goldsmith]] and [[handmaids]]).

**The gilding.** Everyone bustles about, and all that he has ordered arrives:

{{quote:7f4f3a65-b7b3-5f55-9d55-5fa2d13366b1}}

**The rite.** Stratius and Echephron lead the heifer by the horns. Aretus comes from the chamber with water for the hands in a flowered basin, and in his other hand the barley grains in a basket; Thrasymedes stands by with a sharp axe; Perseus holds the bowl for the blood. Nestor begins the rite with the water and the barley grains, and prays earnestly to Athena as he makes the first offering, throwing hairs from the victim's head into the fire (*Odyssey* 3.439–446; see [[stratius]], [[echephron]], [[aretus-son-of-nestor]], [[perseus-son-of-nestor]], [[hand-washing]], [[barley-grains]] and [[bowl-for-the-blood]]). Then the heifer is killed:

{{quote:56fe20e9-924f-526a-ba57-0948769167d0}}

**The god's portion and the feast.** They joint her and cut out the thigh-pieces, wrap them in fat in a double fold and lay raw flesh on them; the old man burns them on split wood and pours sparkling wine over them, and young men beside him hold five-pronged forks; when the thigh-pieces are burned up and they have tasted the entrails, they cut up the rest, thread it on spits and roast it (*Odyssey* 3.456–463; see [[thigh-pieces]], [[five-pronged-forks]] and [[spits]]). Meanwhile Polycaste, the youngest daughter of Nestor, bathes Telemachus, and he steps from the bath “like the immortals in form” and sits down beside Nestor (*Odyssey* 3.464–469; see [[polycaste]] and [[bathing]]). When the outer flesh is roasted and drawn off they sit and feast, with good men pouring wine into golden cups, and when they have put away the desire for drink and food Nestor bids his sons yoke the horses for Telemachus (*Odyssey* 3.470–476; see [[feast-type-scene]] and [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).

## In the poem

**The fullest sacrifice in Homer.** The rite is told in the verses of the sacrifice at Chryse, opened out. “And when they had prayed and scattered the barley grains” is *Iliad* 1.458; the thigh-pieces in their double fold, the burning on split wood, the five-pronged forks, the tasting of the entrails and the spits are *Iliad* 1.461–465 (*Odyssey* 3.458–462, with κατὰ μῆρʼ ἐκάη at 3.461 for the Iliad's κατὰ μῆρε κάη); and the cutting of the thigh-pieces is the Iliad's verse made over, with κατὰ μοῖραν, “in good order” (*Odyssey* 3.456–457; *Iliad* 1.460; see [[sacrifice-type-scene]]). What the Iliad says in one verse, “first they drew back the victims' heads, and cut their throats, and flayed them” (*Iliad* 1.459), is here told in its parts, the blow of the axe, the women's cry, the lifting of the head and the knife. And every task has its man: two of Nestor's sons lead the heifer, one brings the water and the barley, one strikes with the axe, one holds the bowl and the sixth cuts the throat, while the old king begins the rite, prays and burns the god's share. The single blow did not kill the heifer, as W. W. Merry observes: it severed the muscles of the neck and “loosed the strength” of the beast, which dies only when the blood has flowed (*Odyssey* 3.450, 3.455). Lifting the head, the act the Iliad calls αὐέρυσαν, bared the throat to the knife and turned the victim's face up toward the gods; a victim for the powers below is killed with its head turned toward Erebus (*Odyssey* 10.527–528; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.450 and 3.453).

**A vow kept.** Nestor's vow is the vow of Diomedes in the Doloneia, verse for verse (*Iliad* 10.292–294; see [[gold-poured-around-her-horns]]), and the verse that follows it, “So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him”, is the verse that closes Diomedes' prayer in *Iliad* 5 (5.121). The Iliad never tells how that vow was paid (see [[yearling]]). The Odyssey tells the paying of Nestor's in full, from the heifer in the plain to the meal, and makes the gilding a scene of its own. The gold that the vow promised is “poured around” the horns by a smith with anvil, hammer and tongs: Merry takes περιχεύας and περίχευεν of gold leaf spread round the horns, not of molten metal, since the tools show that nothing more was done; Nitzsch quoted Millin, that no adhesive was laid on first and the leaf only wrapped the horns, for the beast was soon to be killed and needed nothing firmer (Merry on 3.384 and 3.437; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 3.384 and 3.437, [[forge]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). The gilded heifer is “the offering” (ἄγαλμα) that the goddess is to rejoice to see (*Odyssey* 3.438), the word used for the many offerings that Aegisthus hung up for the gods after his great deed (*Odyssey* 3.274; see [[aegisthus]]).

**The goddess at the rite.** Athena has left the feast as a bird, and she comes back to share in the sacrifice, ἱρῶν ἀντιόωσα (*Odyssey* 3.435–436): not now in the person of Mentor, Merry notes, but in her godhead, and unseen (on 3.435; see [[athena]]). Nestor's reason for haste is that she came to him ἐναργής, “in plain sight”, at the feast on the shore (*Odyssey* 3.420); Merry compares the Iliad's “for gods are hard to face when they appear in plain sight” (*Iliad* 20.131; Merry on 3.420; see [[divine-epiphany]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). The second sacrifice of the book answers the first: the people of Pylos sacrificed black bulls to Poseidon on the shore, and Nestor's household now sacrifices a heifer to Athena (see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]).

**A household rite.** Nestor's sacrifice is a household rite, and he performs it as the head of his house. Merry, writing against the notion that kingship and priesthood were at first one office, holds that a Homeric king was a priest only in the sense in which every head of a family was the priest of his own house; the gods had their own priests, and the sacrifice here “was purely a domestic sacrifice” (Merry on 3.444; see [[nestor]] and [[priests]]).

**The women's cry.** When the axe falls, Nestor's daughters, his sons' wives and his wife Eurydice raise the ὀλολυγή (*Odyssey* 3.450–452; see [[eurydice]] and [[ololyge]]). It is no shriek of horror, in Merry's words, but a religious shout of joy at the moment the sacrifice is consummated; he compares the cry of the Trojan women as they lift their hands to Athena in her temple (*Iliad* 6.301), Penelope's cry at the end of her prayer (*Odyssey* 4.767), and Odysseus' word to Eurycleia when she would raise it over the dead suitors: rejoice in your heart, old woman, and do not cry out, “it is not holy to boast over slain men” (*Odyssey* 22.411–412; plain renderings; Merry on 3.450).

**The guest.** Telemachus is a guest at the rite, not a sacrificer. He is seated beside Nestor's sons (*Odyssey* 3.416), his companions are brought up from the ship (*Odyssey* 3.423–424, 3.431–432), and while the meat roasts he is bathed and dressed, so that he comes to the meal from the bath, as guests come to their meals in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 3.464–469; see [[xenia]]). Merry gives λοῦσεν the force of a pluperfect, “meanwhile Polycasta had bathed” (on 3.464). When the feast is over, Nestor does what the goddess asked of him and sends the young man on to Sparta with his son, in his own chariot (*Odyssey* 3.368–370, 3.475–485; see [[peisistratus]]).

## Outside Homer

In Euripides' *Electra* a messenger tells how Orestes killed Aegisthus at a sacrifice in which Aegisthus was the host and Orestes and Pylades his guests. Aegisthus greets the strangers, asks who they are and where they come from, and bids them share his hearth at the feast, for he is sacrificing to the Nymphs (*Electra* 779–786). The rite has the parts of Nestor's. Water for washing is to be brought for the strangers, so that they may stand at the altar near the lustral water; the slaves bring the vessel for the blood (σφαγεῖον) and the baskets, kindle the fire and set cauldrons round the hearths; Aegisthus takes the barley and throws it on the altars, prays, takes the knife from the basket, cuts a lock of the calf's hair and puts it on the holy fire with his right hand, and cuts the calf's throat as the slaves lift it on their shoulders (*Electra* 791–814). Then Orestes flays the victim, and as Aegisthus bends over the entrails to read them Orestes strikes him in the spine (*Electra* 815–843; see [[euripides]] and [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]]). W. W. Merry quotes the passage beside Nestor's barley, hair and lifted victim (Merry on 3.445 and 3.453).

Athenaeus, on the order of Homer's feasts, has Nestor sacrifice through his own sons, though he is a king with many subjects, and quotes his order for the heifer, “But come, let one go to the plain for a heifer” (*Odyssey* 3.421), for a sacrifice performed by one's own kin is holier and dearer to the gods; he calls it a sacrifice to Poseidon by the sea (*Deipnosophistae* 1.15 Kaibel; see [[athenaeus]]).
