## The story

**Hurled by Zeus.**

{{quote:b2b207ac-383e-519a-93c3-e8c20c7e4877}}

ῥῖψε ποδὸς τεταγών: “seizing me by the foot, he hurled me”. βηλὸς θεσπέσιος: “the divine threshold” of Olympus. The fall lasts a whole day, measured by the sun, and ends on Lemnos, where the Sintians take him in (see [[lemnos]] and [[sintians]]).

**Cast out by his mother.**

{{quote:7b0f0966-51a9-58c9-860c-247760d2b8d5}}

Here Hephaestus does not say how he fell, only that he fell “far”, “by the will of my dog-faced mother, who wished / to hide me away because I was lame” (18.395–397; see [[hera]] and [[dog-face]]). Thetis and Eurynome, daughter of Oceanus, took him to their bosom, and for nine years he made jewelry for them in a hollow cave, with the stream of Oceanus flowing around it, and no god or mortal knew of it (18.398–405; see [[thetis]], [[eurynome]] and [[oceanus]]).

## In the poem

Both tellings are told to a listener for a purpose. In *Iliad* 1 Hephaestus tells Hera not to provoke Zeus, since he could not protect her; he tried once, and was thrown from heaven (1.586–594; see [[mythological-paradigm]]). In Book 18 he tells his wife Charis why Thetis must be welcomed: she saved him, and he owes her “the full price for my life” (18.394–407; see [[charis-wife-of-hephaestus]] and [[the-making-of-the-arms]]).

The two falls cannot be one fall. In the first his father throws him, he falls for a day and lands on Lemnos at sunset; in the second his mother wants him hidden for his lameness, and the goddesses of the sea hide him by Oceanus for nine years. Walter Leaf notes that the story is applied to Hephaestus in both places, “though the details are different”, and that Hephaestus and his mother are generally represented as close allies (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.395). In *Iliad* 1 he takes her side and suffers for it; in Book 18 it is she who casts him out. Book 1 does not connect the fall with his lameness; Book 18 makes it the cause.

## Outside Homer

The *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* gives Hera's side of the second story. Angry with Zeus, who had brought forth Athena without her, she complains that her own son Hephaestus, whom she bore herself, was born weak among all the gods and shriveled in his feet, and that she took him up in her hands and threw him into the wide sea; but Thetis, the silver-footed daughter of Nereus, received him and cared for him among her sisters (*Hymn to Apollo* 316–320). In Hesiod, Hera bears Hephaestus without union with Zeus, in anger and in strife with her husband (*Theogony* 927–929; see [[hesiod]]).

Pausanias tells how the story went on. Paintings in the old sanctuary of Dionysus by the theater at Athens showed Dionysus bringing Hephaestus up to heaven, and the Greeks told that Hera had thrown Hephaestus down when he was born; he took his revenge by sending her a golden throne with invisible bonds, which held her fast when she sat in it, and he would listen to none of the gods but Dionysus, who made him drunk and brought him to heaven (Pausanias 1.20.3; see [[dionysus]]).

Plato's Socrates would not admit into his city the binding of Hera by her son, or the hurlings of Hephaestus by his father when he was about to defend his mother from a beating, or any of the battles of the gods that Homer composed, whether they were meant as allegories or not (*Republic* 2.378d; see [[plato-hephaestus-hurled]]).
