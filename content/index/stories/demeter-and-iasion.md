## The story

Homer tells it once, in Calypso's answer to Hermes, after the death of Orion (see [[dawn-and-orion]]):

{{quote:b19ca566-1c22-5d81-90c2-49a7e4d8712b}}

**The goddess's choice.** ᾧ θυμῷ εἴξασα, “yielding to her own heart”, is “yielding to her passion”, not compelled, as Meleager in the Iliad yields to his own heart when he goes out to save the Aetolians (*Iliad* 9.598; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.126). μίγη φιλότητι καὶ εὐνῇ, “lay with him in love and bed”, is the phrase of the Naiad Abarbarea and Bucolion, the son of Laomedon, who lay with her while he tended his sheep (*Iliad* 6.21–26; see [[abarbarea]] and [[bucolion]]).

**The field.** νειῷ ἔνι τριπόλῳ, “in a thrice-plowed fallow field”: τρίπολος refers to three plowings, at the end of autumn, in spring to freshen the soil after the winter's frost, and in summer, and the land freshened by the third was the νειός (Merry on 5.127). The only other thrice-plowed field in Homer is on the Shield of Achilles, “a soft fallow field, rich plowland, / broad, thrice plowed” (*Iliad* 18.541–542; see [[thrice-plowed]] and [[the-plowing]]). The scholia quote a reading τριπύλῳ, and disapprove of it (Merry's critical note on 5.127).

**The thunderbolt.** “And not long without hearing of it / was Zeus” (οὐδὲ δὴν ἦεν ἄπυστος / Ζεύς), in the words said of Penelope when she learns of the suitors' plot against her son (*Odyssey* 4.675, 5.127–128), and he killed Iasion “striking him with a gleaming thunderbolt” (*Odyssey* 5.128; see [[zeus]], [[thunderbolt]] and [[of-the-gleaming-thunderbolt]]). Iasion is named nowhere else in Homer (see [[iasion]]). Zeus himself names Demeter “of the lovely tresses, the queen” among the goddesses and women he has loved (*Iliad* 14.326; see [[the-loves-of-zeus]] and [[demeter]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes Iasion the father of Wealth. His list of the goddesses who lay with mortal men and bore them children like the gods begins with Demeter, who bore Plutus “joined in delightful love with the hero Iasion / in a thrice-plowed fallow field, in the rich land of Crete”, a kindly god who goes over all the earth and the broad back of the sea and makes rich the man who meets him, into whose hands he comes (*Theogony* 969–974, a plain rendering; see [[hesiod]] and [[crete]]). The first half of the verse, νειῷ ἔνι τριπόλῳ, is the Odyssey's (*Odyssey* 5.127). Merry cites the verse with the form Ἰάσιος (on 5.125); the text of the *Theogony* read here prints Ἰασίωνʼ ἥρωι. The same list ends with Circe and Calypso, who bore sons to Odysseus (*Theogony* 1011–1018).

A papyrus of the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, printed in the appendix of Evelyn-White's edition, tells that Electra bore Dardanus and Eetion; as the broken lines are restored there, she bore them to Zeus, Eetion once loved Demeter, and Zeus in his anger struck him with his thunderbolt because he laid hands on her (see [[dardanus]]).

Merry thinks the story points to an early legend that identified Iasion with the fertile power of the soil. Later mythology, he says, made Iasion the inventor of growing grain, like Triptolemus; Theocritus couples him with Endymion (*Idylls* 3.50); and he appears with different parents and different mystic meanings in the cults and legends of Arcadia, Crete and Samothrace (on 5.125; see [[samothrace]]).
