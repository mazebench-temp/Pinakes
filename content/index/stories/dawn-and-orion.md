## The story

Homer tells it once, in four verses of Calypso's answer to Hermes:

{{quote:a21b7f8a-daae-57e1-a983-79d5014e8054}}

**The hunter.** Orion is the giant whom Odysseus sees among the dead, driving together over the meadow of asphodel the beasts he himself had killed in the lonely mountains, with a club of solid bronze in his hands (*Odyssey* 11.572–575, a plain rendering). Otus and Ephialtes, the tallest men the earth ever reared, were the most beautiful “after famous Orion” (*Odyssey* 11.309–310, a plain rendering). He is also a constellation, the one that the Bear watches (*Iliad* 18.488 = *Odyssey* 5.274; see [[orion]]).

**Dawn's mortal loves.** Homer gives Dawn three. Tithonus is her husband, from whose bed she rises in the morning (*Iliad* 11.1 = *Odyssey* 5.1; see [[tithonus]] and [[eos]]); Cleitus, the grandson of Melampus, she carried off “for his beauty, so that he might be among the immortals” (*Odyssey* 15.250–251, a plain rendering); and Orion, whom she “took … for herself” (ἕλετο, the middle voice, *Odyssey* 5.121). Only Orion is killed.

**The arrows of Artemis.** Artemis killed him with her “gentle arrows”, the arrows of a sudden death (see [[gentle-arrows]] and [[artemis]]). W. W. Merry observes that an arrow shot in anger is still called gentle, so that the painlessness of the death is meant, not mercy; and that Orion's death breaks the usual rule by which Apollo brings death to men and Artemis only to women: here she kills not as a goddess of death but from jealousy (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.124). The scholia report that some struck out the two verses, because nowhere in Homer does Artemis kill a man (Merry's critical note on 5.123–124).

**Ortygia.** Homer does not say where Ortygia lay. Merry takes it for Delos, since Apollodorus, whom he quotes, says that Artemis killed Orion in Delos; the Syracusan Ortygia, he holds, only shows that the colonists brought the cult of the Delian Artemis to their new home (on 5.121, 5.123; see [[ortygia]] and [[apollodorus]]). Strabo ends his note on Rhenaea, the small island four stadia from Delos where the Delians buried their dead, since no one might be buried on Delos itself, with the words “it was formerly also called Ortygia”, which Merry takes of Rhenaea (*Geography* 10.5.5; Merry on 5.123).

**The sky.** Merry reads the myth in the stars: Orion appears as Dawn's lover when, at the end of July, he rises in all his splendor with the dawn in the eastern sky, and then pales before the morning light (on 5.121).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod's list of the goddesses who lay with mortal men gives Dawn two lovers, Tithonus, to whom she bore Memnon and Emathion, and Cephalus, to whom she bore Phaethon, but not Orion (*Theogony* 984–987; see [[hesiod]] and [[memnon]]). The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* tells how Dawn asked Zeus to make Tithonus immortal and forgot to ask for youth, so that he grew old in her halls (*Hymn* 5.218–238; see [[tithonus]]). The *Astronomy* ascribed to Hesiod gave Orion another death: he boasted, while he hunted in Crete with Artemis and Leto, that he would kill every beast on earth, and Earth sent up a huge scorpion that stung him to death (fr. 4 Evelyn-White; see [[orion]]).

Merry reports a later story in which Orion's offense was an attempt on Opis as she was bringing ears of grain to Delos; Opis, some say, was a name of Artemis herself, and that, he thinks, explains the form of the story in Horace (on 5.121). Horace calls Orion to witness, beside the hundred-handed giant, that force without counsel falls by its own weight: *notus et integrae / temptator Orion Dianae, / virginea domitus sagitta*, Orion, known as the assailant of chaste Diana, brought down by the virgin's arrow (*Odes* 3.4.65–72; see [[horace]]).
