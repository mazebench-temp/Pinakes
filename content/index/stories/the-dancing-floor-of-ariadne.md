## The story

Homer tells no more than these verses. Daedalus made it for Ariadne, the daughter of Minos, at Cnossus, the city of Minos (see [[daedalus]], [[ariadne]] and [[cnossus]]). ἤσκησεν, “fashioned”, is the verb of skilled work in horn, cloth and metal: of the craftsman who worked in horn and fitted Pandarus' bow, of Athena, who smoothed and worked Hera's robe and set many δαίδαλα in it, and of the Sidonians, πολυδαίδαλοι, who made the silver mixing bowl of the funeral games (4.110, 14.178–179, 23.741–743). χορός is both the dance and the place where it is danced. Here it is the floor, as in εὐρύχορος, “with its wide dancing floors” (2.498; see [[with-wide-dancing-floors]]); a few verses later it is the dance itself, around which the crowd stands (18.603; see the translation's table of key words). Why Daedalus made it for Ariadne, and what was danced there, the poem does not say.

## In the poem

**The one name on the shield.** The comparison is the only place in the description of the shield where a person, a place or a story is named. Walter Leaf calls 18.591–592 “unique in the Shield for their local and mythological allusion” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.590). The dance that follows is Cretan in character too: Leaf reports that the scholia call the tumblers and the armed dance peculiarly Cretan institutions, and that some have concluded that the whole passage of the dance (18.590–605) is a Cretan poet's addition. He cannot refute it, but finds the evidence insufficient, since Crete was for the Greeks above all the home of the dance, and of the war dance especially, and a poet describing an early dance would naturally give it Cretan traits (on 18.590). In the Iliad the Trojan Aeneas taunts the Cretan Meriones as a dancer (16.617–618; see [[meriones]]).

**A god's work and a mortal's.** οἷον, “like the one that”, sets the god's work beside a mortal craftsman's. Some critics, ancient and modern, were offended that a god should imitate a mortal; some put a full stop after 18.590 and read οἶον, “the only thing ever made like it” (Leaf on 18.591; see the translation's note on 18.590–606). Leaf answers that imitation is not implied by the words. The mortal's name is the god's word for his own work: Hephaestus made δαίδαλα πολλά, “many intricate designs”, on the shield (18.482), and the comparison names Δαίδαλος.

**The dance on the shield.** On Hephaestus' floor young men and maidens “who would bring many oxen” dance with their hands on one another's wrists, now running in a ring as lightly as a potter's wheel, now in lines toward each other, while a great throng watches and two tumblers whirl in the midst (18.593–606; see [[dance]] and [[the-shield-of-achilles]]). Leaf speaks of “this dancing-floor or labyrinth” (on 18.591), but the poem names no labyrinth.

## Outside Homer

Pausanias lists among the works of Daedalus that still existed in his day an Athena at Cnossus, and adds that the Cnossians also had “the dance of Ariadne, which Homer too mentioned in the Iliad, worked in relief on white stone” (Pausanias 9.40.3; see [[daedalus]] and [[pausanias-the-dance-of-ariadne]]). Plutarch tells that Theseus, sailing home from Crete, put in at Delos and danced with the young men a dance that the Delians still performed, an imitation of the circuits and exits of the Labyrinth; the Delians called it the Crane, as Dicaearchus reports (*Theseus* 21; see [[plutarch]] and [[theseus]]). In Apollodorus' account it was Daedalus who showed Ariadne the way out of the labyrinth, and at his suggestion she gave Theseus the thread (*Epitome* 1.8–9; see [[apollodorus]]).
