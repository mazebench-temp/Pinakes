## The story

Agamemnon tells it to Diomedes, and he begins by admitting that he knows it only by report. Tydeus never cowered, but fought far ahead of his companions, “so said those who saw him at his work; for I myself never met him or saw him; but they say he was above all others” (4.372–375).

Once, he says, Tydeus came to Mycenae, not in war, as a guest (ξεῖνος), with godlike Polynices, gathering an army, for they were making war on the sacred walls of Thebes. The Mycenaeans were willing to give them allies, but Zeus turned them back with ill-omened signs (4.376–381; see [[polynices]] and [[mycenae]]). The army went on, and when it reached the Asopus, “deep in rushes, with grassy meadows”, the Achaeans sent Tydeus ahead on an embassy (4.382–384; see [[asopus]] and [[embassy]]).

“Such was Tydeus the Aetolian; but the son he fathered is worse than he in battle, though better in assembly” (4.399–400). See [[cadmeians]], [[eteocles]], [[contests]], [[ambush]], [[maeon]], [[haemon-of-thebes]], [[polyphontes]] and [[autophonus]].

The Iliad tells the embassy twice more. In Book 5 Athena makes Agamemnon's charge herself: Tydeus fathered a son little like himself. He was small, but a fighter; when he went alone as a messenger to Thebes, she told him to feast quietly in the halls, but he challenged the young Cadmeians and won everything easily, so great a helper was she (5.800–813). In Book 10 Diomedes prays to her before the night raid to stand by him as she stood by his father, who left the Achaeans at the Asopus, brought a gentle message to the Cadmeians, and on his way back did dreadful deeds with her help (10.284–294). Tydeus died at Thebes. Diomedes says that “the heaped earth covers him at Thebes”, and that he does not remember his father, who left him as a small child “when at Thebes the host of the Achaeans perished” (14.114, 6.222–223).

## In the poem

**A paradigm.** Agamemnon uses the father to shame the son (see [[fathers-and-sons]]). The details are chosen for the rebuke. Agamemnon asks Diomedes “why do you cower, why do you stare at the bridges of war?” (4.371; see [[obscure-gephyrai]]); Tydeus went alone among many enemies and was not afraid (4.387–388). Like Nestor's stories of his youth, the tale measures the present by a greater past (see [[mythological-paradigm]] and [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]). Its teller never saw his hero. The greatness of the fathers reaches the sons as report, what “they say” (see [[kleos]]).

**Signs.** The story is full of signs from the gods. Zeus turned the Mycenaeans back with ill-omened signs (4.381), Athena helped Tydeus win (4.390), and he spared Maeon “obeying the portents of the gods” (4.398). Sthenelus takes up the theme at once: the sons took Thebes “trusting in the portents of the gods and the help of Zeus”, while the fathers “perished through their own recklessness” (4.408–409; see [[the-epigonoi]] and [[atasthalie]]). The war of the Seven was fought against the gods' warnings, and Mycenae, which heeded them, stayed out.

**The envoy.** Tydeus came to Mycenae as a guest and to Thebes as an envoy, “though he was a stranger” (ξεῖνός περ ἐών, 4.387). The Cadmeians met his victories at their feast with an ambush on the road. The Trojans had their own man who wanted an envoy killed: Antimachus urged the Trojan assembly to kill Menelaus when he came to Troy on an embassy with Odysseus (11.138–142; see [[the-embassy-to-troy]] and [[xenia]]).

**The answer.** Diomedes does not reply. He says nothing, “in awe of the rebuke of the king he revered”, and Sthenelus answers for him (4.401–410). The rebuke is answered in deeds in Book 5, where Diomedes, with Tydeus' goddess beside him, becomes the greatest fighter on the field; even there Athena spurs him on with the same charge (5.800–813). It is answered in words in Book 9. Speaking in the assembly, “as is the right, lord, in assembly”, Diomedes reminds Agamemnon that he reproached his courage first among the Danaans (9.32–36). Agamemnon allowed that he was better than his father in assembly, and it is in assembly that he answers. See [[diomedes]], [[tydeus]] and [[the-review-of-the-army]].

## Outside Homer

The war of the Seven was the subject of a lost epic, the *Thebaid* (see [[the-seven-against-thebes]]). Apollodorus tells the embassy much as Agamemnon does. From Mount Cithaeron the army sent Tydeus to tell Eteocles to give up the kingdom to Polynices; when Eteocles refused, Tydeus challenged the Cadmeians one by one and beat them all, and of the fifty armed men who lay in wait for him on his way back he killed all but Maeon (Apollodorus 3.6.5). Aeschylus' scout describes Tydeus raging at the Proetid gate (see [[aeschylus-seven]]). Statius told the embassy and the ambush at length in the second book of his *Thebaid*, and in the third made Maeon bring the news to Eteocles, denounce him, and kill himself (see [[statius-tydeus]]).
