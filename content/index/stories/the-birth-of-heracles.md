## The story

{{quote:e653286d-e0c9-57b1-b1bf-e6f4f09c42cc}}

**Zeus' boast.** Agamemnon tells it. On the day Heracles was to be born, Zeus spoke among all the gods, “boasting” (εὐχόμενος, 19.100), and began with the two verses with which he opened his ban on the gods in Book 8 (19.101–102 ≈ 8.5–6; see [[hear-me]] and [[zeus]]):

{{quote:74a769a4-d9eb-5989-894b-8d5cf4881605}}

**The oath.** Hera, “with treachery in her heart” (19.106; see [[with-treachery-in-her-heart]] and [[hera]]), tells him that he will prove false, and asks him to swear “a mighty oath” that the man of his stock who “on this day falls between a woman's feet” will be lord over all who dwell around him (19.107–111). “So she spoke; and Zeus did not perceive her treachery at all, / but swore a great oath, and in this was greatly blinded” (19.112–113; see [[oath]] and [[ate]]).

**Two births.** Hera leaves Olympus for Argos, where the wife of Sthenelus, son of Perseus, is carrying a child (see [[sthenelus-son-of-perseus]] and [[argos]]):

{{quote:c2e8bbc4-728a-56f0-920c-e7f5cacc09fc}}

Then she brings Zeus the news herself: “now a brave man is born who will be lord of the Argives, / Eurystheus, child of Sthenelus the son of Perseus, / of your race; it is not unseemly for him to be lord of the Argives” (19.122–124; see [[eurystheus]]). The oath binds Zeus, for Eurystheus too is of his blood, through Perseus, the son whom Danae bore him (14.319–320; see [[perseus]]).

**Blind Folly hurled from heaven.** Zeus cannot take back his oath. He punishes the power that blinded him:

{{quote:634eb8bf-d3e9-5a97-bd4a-b046a355d797}}

The story ends not with the birth of Heracles, which it never tells, but with his servitude: “Over her he always groaned whenever he saw his dear son / doing unseemly work under the labors that Eurystheus set him” (19.132–133; see [[heracles]]). The last words, ὑπʼ Εὐρυσθῆος ἀέθλων, are those of Athena's complaint in Book 8, that Zeus had forgotten how often she saved his son “when he was worn out by the labors that Eurystheus set him” (8.363; see [[heracles-and-cerberus]]).

## In the poem

**Agamemnon's apology.** Achilles has renounced his wrath before the army (19.56–73; see [[the-renunciation-of-wrath]] and [[achilles]]), and Agamemnon answers “from where he sat, and not standing up in their midst” (19.77; see [[agamemnon]]). The Achaeans have often rebuked him, “but I am not to blame, / but Zeus and Fate and the Erinys who walks in darkness, / who in the assembly cast savage blind folly into my mind / on the day when I myself took away the prize of Achilles” (19.86–89; see [[who-is-to-blame]] and [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]]). Blind Folly is the eldest daughter of Zeus and walks over the heads of men (19.91–94), and “For once even Zeus was blinded” (19.95). The story is the proof: if she blinded the best of men and gods, she could blind a king. Agamemnon draws the lesson himself, ὣς καὶ ἐγών, “So I too”: while Hector was killing the Argives at the sterns of the ships he “could not forget Blind Folly, by whom I was first blinded” (19.134–136), and now he will make amends and give a boundless ransom (19.137–138; see [[mythological-paradigm]] and [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). The day before, Achilles closed his example of Heracles' death with the same words, ὣς καὶ ἐγών, “So I too, if indeed a like fate is prepared for me” (18.117–120).

**The likeness.** The king and the god are blinded alike, in public and at once: Zeus by a boast before all the gods and an oath sworn on the spot, Agamemnon “in the assembly” (εἰν ἀγορῇ, 19.88). Walter Leaf finds Zeus' blind folly not in any change of words by Hera but in his rashness in swearing an unconditional oath, limited to a single day (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.111). Neither can undo what was done. Zeus groans over Ate whenever he sees his son's labors (19.132), as Agamemnon could not forget her while the Argives died at the ships (19.136). Leaf, who thought the digression unsuited to its place, adds that “many speakers with a bad case take refuge in telling stories” (on 19.95).

**A mortal tells the gods' story.** Leaf observes that here a man in the story tells the doings and even the words of the gods, which elsewhere the poet tells himself, and takes it as a sign that the episode was worked in from an independent poem on Heracles (on 19.95; see the translation's note on 19.95–133). Achilles, too, told how the gods once tried to bind Zeus, but as he had heard it from his mother (1.396–406; see [[binding-of-zeus]]). An ancient critic wanted Hera to leave “the starry sky”, not “the peak of Olympus” (19.114), and Leaf takes the objection to mean that Agamemnon knows too much for a mortal of the ways of the gods (on 19.114).

**Words of birth.** Zeus' boast is made in the poem's language of childbirth. “Eileithyia, who brings hard birth, will bring into the light” (μογοστόκος Εἰλείθυια, φόως δέ, 19.103) are the words of Eudorus' birth (16.187–188; see [[eileithyia]], [[who-brings-hard-birth]] and [[the-birth-of-eudorus]]). “Falls between a woman's feet” (19.110) is, to Leaf, a naive phrase for being born (on 19.110). Eurystheus is born in the seventh month (19.117) and ἠλιτόμηνος, “short of his months” (19.118), a word found nowhere else in Homer. The Eileithyiai whom Hera holds back from Alcmene (19.119) are “the daughters of Hera” in a simile of Book 11 (11.270–271).

**Hera's enmity.** The story carries back to his birth an enmity that the poem has already shown: Hera drove Heracles to Cos with a storm when he sailed home from Troy (14.249–256, 15.18–30; see [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]]), and Achilles named “the grievous anger of Hera” among the things that brought him down (18.119). Hera deceives Zeus twice in the poem. On Ida she lulls him to sleep (14.153–353; see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]), setting out from Olympus in the same verse (19.114 = 14.225), and when he wakes he tells her that she “deceived” him (καί μʼ ἀπάτησας, 15.33), the verb of 19.97 (ἀπάτησεν).

**The fall of Blind Folly.** Zeus hurls her from heaven as he hurled Hephaestus from its threshold (ῥῖψε, 1.591; ἔρριψεν, 19.130; see [[fall-of-hephaestus]]), but he also binds himself by a second oath that she will never come back (19.127–129). Barred from Olympus, she comes “to the works of men” (19.131), and lives among them: Agamemnon's Ate walks over the heads of men, “doing harm to men” (19.93–94; see [[ate]]).

## Outside Homer

**Hesiod.** The *Theogony* says that Alcmene bore βίην Ἡρακληείην, “mighty Heracles”, the words of Iliad 19.98, after lying with Zeus (*Theogony* 943–944), and uses the phrase ἐυστεφάνῳ ἐνὶ Θήβῃ, “in well-crowned Thebes”, for the children whom Harmonia bore to Cadmus (*Theogony* 975–978; see [[hesiod]]). The Hesiodic *Shield* tells the conception: Zeus came down from Olympus by night to Alcmene, wishing to beget a defender against ruin for gods and men, and Amphitryon came home the same night; in seven-gated Thebes she bore twin sons, Heracles to Zeus and Iphicles to Amphitryon (*Shield* 27–56; see [[shield-of-heracles]] and [[alcmene]]). In the same poem Heracles tells his charioteer Iolaus that Zeus took away the wits of his brother Iphicles (φρένας ἐξέλετο Ζεύς, the words of Iliad 19.137), so that he left his home and parents to honor “the sinful Eurystheus”, and groaned for it afterward, bearing his own blind folly (ἄτη), while a god laid hard labors on Heracles (*Shield* 89–94).

**Eileithyia held back.** In the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo*, Hera in her jealousy keeps “Eileithyia, who brings hard birth” on Olympus, unaware, while Leto is in labor on Delos for nine days and nights, because Leto was about to bear a blameless and mighty son; when the goddesses send Iris to fetch her, the child leaps out into the light (ἐκ δʼ ἔθορε πρὸ φόωσδε) as soon as she sets foot on the island (*Hymn to Apollo* 91–119; see [[leto]] and [[apollo]]). Leaf compares it (on 19.119).

**Apollodorus.** In Apollodorus, Zeus declares among the gods that the descendant of Perseus then about to be born will reign over Mycenae, and Hera out of jealousy persuades the Eileithyiai to delay Alcmene's labor and contrives that Eurystheus, the son of Sthenelus, is born a seven-month child (ἑπταμηνιαῖον, *Library* 2.4.5; see [[apollodorus]] and [[mycenae]]).

**The delay undone.** Later writers tell how the delay was broken. Pausanias describes at Thebes some worn figures of women in relief, whom the Thebans called the Witches (Φαρμακίδες): Hera sent them to hinder Alcmene's labor, and Historis, daughter of Teiresias, tricked them by raising a cry of joy within their hearing that Alcmene had given birth, so that they went away and Alcmene bore her child (Pausanias 9.11.3; see [[pausanias]]). In Ovid, Alcmene tells it to Iole. For seven nights and as many days she was in labor; Lucina, won over by Juno, sat at the altar before the door with her legs crossed, the right over the left, and her fingers locked together, and held back the birth with spells, until the servant Galanthis cried out that Alcmene was delivered; the goddess leapt up and loosed her hands, and the birth came. Galanthis laughed at the deceived goddess and was changed into a creature that bears its young through the mouth, because a lying mouth had helped a mother in labor (*Metamorphoses* 9.281–323; see [[ovid]]).
