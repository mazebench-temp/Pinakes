## The story

**The question.** The stranger has eaten in the hut, heard the swineherd mourn his master and seen his oath declined (see [[euangelion]]), when Eumaeus asks for his story: “But come, old sir, tell me of your own sorrows, / and tell me this truly, so that I may know it well: / Who are you among men, and from where? Where are your city and your parents?”, and on what ship and with what sailors he came to Ithaca (*Odyssey* 14.185–190; see [[eumaeus]], [[who-are-you-among-men]] and [[tell-me-this-truly]]). The stranger answers, “Well then, I will tell you all this very exactly”, and says that even a whole year would not be enough to tell “the sorrows of my heart, / all that I have toiled through by the will of the gods” (*Odyssey* 14.192–198).

**The Cretan.** “I claim that my line is from wide Crete” (*Odyssey* 14.199). He is the son of a rich man, Castor, son of Hylax, by a bought concubine; his father honored him equally with his lawful sons, but when the father died they cast lots for his livelihood and left the bastard a house and little else (*Odyssey* 14.199–210; see [[castor-son-of-hylax]], [[concubines]], [[nothos]] and [[lots]]). He took a wife “from people of many allotments” for his excellence, and he was a fighter, always first out of the ambush, who cared nothing for work or the care of a household, but loved ships and wars, javelins and arrows (*Odyssey* 14.211–228; see [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]] and [[different-gifts]]). Nine times before the war he led ships against foreign men, grew rich and was “feared and respected among the Cretans”; then the people made him lead the Cretan ships to Ilios with Idomeneus, and when the city of Priam was sacked in the tenth year “a god scattered the Achaeans” (*Odyssey* 14.229–242; see [[piracy]], [[cretans]] and [[idomeneus]]).

**Egypt and the Phoenician.** A month after his homecoming he sailed to Egypt with nine ships; his men plundered the fields of the Egyptians against his orders and were cut down, and he saved his life at the knees of the king and stayed seven years, growing rich (*Odyssey* 14.243–286; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]). In the eighth year “a Phoenician man, skilled in deceits” took him to Phoenicia, and after a year put him on a ship bound for Libya, meaning to sell him there (*Odyssey* 14.287–298; see [[phoenicians]] and [[libya]]). Past Crete Zeus struck the ship with his thunderbolt and all on board were lost, but Zeus put the mast into the Cretan's hands, and he drifted on it for nine days until a wave cast him up in the land of the Thesprotians (*Odyssey* 14.299–315; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and [[thesprotians]]).

**News of Odysseus.** There Pheidon, king of the Thesprotians, took care of him without price, for the king's son had found him and brought him home and clothed him; and the king told him of Odysseus: he had been Pheidon's guest on his way home, had gathered a great treasure, and had gone to Dodona to hear the will of Zeus from the oak, how he should return to Ithaca, “openly or in secret”; and the king swore that a ship and a crew were ready to take him home (*Odyssey* 14.316–333; see [[pheidon]], [[thesprotians]], [[dodona]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). The Cretan was sent first, on a Thesprotian ship bound for Dulichium and its king, Acastus (*Odyssey* 14.334–336; see [[dulichium]] and [[acastus-king-of-dulichium]]).

**The escape.** Out at sea the crew “contrived the day of slavery” for him: they stripped him of his cloak and tunic and put a vile rag on him, and when they put in at Ithaca in the evening they bound him in the ship and went ashore for supper. The gods undid his bonds; he wrapped his head in the rag, slid down into the sea and swam ashore (*Odyssey* 14.337–352; see [[doulios]], [[cloak]] and [[rags]]). So the tale ends:

## In the poem

**An answer in the goddess' words.** The tale answers the host's questions, and its last episode answers the last of them: he had asked on what ship the stranger came, what sailors brought him and who they claimed to be, “For I do not think that you came here on foot” (*Odyssey* 14.188–190), and the Cretan came on a [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] ship and left it by swimming. The questions are those that Telemachus put to the disguised Athena in the first book, and the stranger begins his answer with the verse in which the goddess began her false account of herself (*Odyssey* 14.186–192; 1.170–174, 1.179; see [[athena-visits-telemachus]]); as she said “I claim to be Mentes”, he claims his line from Crete and his descent from Castor (*Odyssey* 1.180; 14.199, 14.204; see [[euchomai]] and [[castor-son-of-hylax]]). A scholion on the first book says that the verses about the ship and the sailors would be spoken more fittingly by Eumaeus, which is why some copies lacked them there, and D. B. Monro takes them to be interpolated in that book (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix I §10, note).

**The truth inside the lie.** Monro observes that the false stories of Odysseus all turn upon events and characters of the Trojan war, and he cites this tale among them (Appendix I §3 and note, citing *Odyssey* 13.256, 14.235, 14.468 and 19.172; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]). It is made, too, of Odysseus' own story. “This chronology is not quite arbitrary”, Monro says of the seven years in Egypt, which take the place of the seven years that Odysseus in truth spent on Calypso's island (Monro on *Odyssey* 14.285; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]), and the verses bear him out: “There I stayed for seven years” and “But when the eighth year came round in its course” are the verses in which Odysseus told Arete of his years on Ogygia (*Odyssey* 14.285, 14.287; 7.259, 7.261; see [[calypso]]). The wreck is told in the verses of his last shipwreck and the drift in those of his account to Arete (*Odyssey* 14.301–309, 14.314; 12.403–406, 12.415–419; 7.253; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). The king's son who finds the castaway worn out and brings him to his father's house, and the king who has a ship made ready to take his guest home, are the Phaeacians' welcome over again (*Odyssey* 14.317–333; 6.127–250, 8.31–36; see [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]]), and the treasure that Odysseus is said to have gathered stands for the gifts that he hid in the cave of the Naiads that morning (*Odyssey* 14.323–326; 13.361–371; see [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]). The [[rags|rag]] that the [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] sailors put on the Cretan is the rag that Athena put on Odysseus, in the same verse (*Odyssey* 14.342–343; 13.434–435; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]). Even [[pheidon|Pheidon]]'s news is true in substance: Odysseus is on his way home, and comes in secret (*Odyssey* 14.329–330; see [[dodona]]).

**A tale full of gods.** The Cretan gives every turn of his life to the gods. Ares and Athena gave him his daring, a god put the love of raids in his mind, Zeus devised the hateful journey to Troy, and a god scattered the Achaeans after it (*Odyssey* 14.216, 14.227, 14.235, 14.242). Zeus devised evils for him in Egypt, cast panic among his men and put the saving thought in his mind; Zeus wrecked the Phoenician's ship and put the mast into his hands; the gods undid his bonds on Ithaca, hid him, and brought him to the swineherd's farmstead (*Odyssey* 14.243, 14.268–269, 14.273–274, 14.300–312, 14.348, 14.357–359; see [[zeus]] and [[theos]]). The last of these is half true: a goddess did bring him there (*Odyssey* 13.404–411; 14.1–4; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]).

**The listener.** Eumaeus is moved, “Ah, wretched stranger, truly you have stirred my heart greatly”, but he rejects the one part of the tale that is true: “But in this, I think, you have not spoken aright, nor will you persuade me / with what you say of Odysseus” (*Odyssey* 14.361–364). An Aetolian once deceived him with such news (*Odyssey* 14.379–385; see [[aetolians]]), and he bids the stranger not to please him with lies or bewitch him: he will respect him “in fear of Zeus, the god of guests, and in pity for you yourself” (*Odyssey* 14.386–389; see [[pseudos]], [[thelgo]], [[xenia]] and [[god-of-guests]]). The beggar answers with a wager on his master's return, which Eumaeus will not take (*Odyssey* 14.391–406; see [[rhetre]]). Eumaeus tells the tale again, to Telemachus, “He claims that his line is from wide Crete”, and that the stranger has escaped from a ship of [[thesprotians|Thesprotian]] men (*Odyssey* 16.61–67, a plain rendering); and to Penelope, of the stranger whom he kept three nights and three days in the hut and who held him like a singer, “so he bewitched me”, and who claims to be a guest-friend of Odysseus from their fathers' time, living in Crete, and to have heard that Odysseus is near, in the land of the Thesprotians (*Odyssey* 17.513–527, a plain rendering), details that the tale of the fourteenth book does not contain.

**The other Cretan tales.** The tale to Eumaeus is the second of Odysseus' Cretan tales, and the longest told at one stretch. In the first, told to Athena that morning, he is a Cretan in exile for killing the son of Idomeneus (*Odyssey* 13.256–286; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]). To Antinous he tells the raid on Egypt again, in the same verses, but ends it in Cyprus (*Odyssey* 17.419–444; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]); and to Penelope he is Aethon, the younger brother of Idomeneus, who entertained Odysseus in Crete, and he gives her [[pheidon|Pheidon]]'s report in the verses he gave Eumaeus (*Odyssey* 19.165–307, with 19.288–299 after 14.323–335; see [[penelope]]).

## Outside Homer

**The Hymn to Demeter.** The *Homeric Hymn to Demeter* has a false Cretan tale of its own. The goddess, in the likeness of an old woman, sits by the well at Eleusis, and the daughters of Celeus ask her, τίς πόθεν ἐσσί, γρῆυ, παλαιγενέων ἀνθρώπων; “Who are you, old woman, and from where, of the people born long ago?”, with the τίς πόθεν of Eumaeus' question (*Homeric Hymn to Demeter* 98–113; *Odyssey* 14.187). It is not unseemly, she answers, to tell them the truth, and she tells a lie: she has come from Crete, carried off against her will by pirates, who put in at Thoricus and went ashore to make ready their supper by the ship's stern-cables; she slipped away across the dark land and fled her overbearing masters, so that they should not sell her, whom they had got for nothing, and profit by her price, ἀπριάτην περάσαντες ἐμῆς ἀποναίατο τιμῆς (*Homeric Hymn to Demeter* 119–134). The disguised teller, the Cretan home, the slavers who go ashore for supper while their captive escapes, and the sale foiled are all in the tale to Eumaeus, and the Hymn's two words are the tale's: περάσειε, “so that he might sell me”, is the Phoenician's purpose, and ἀπριάτην, “without price”, is the way [[pheidon|Pheidon]] cared for the castaway (*Odyssey* 14.297, 14.317, 14.345–352). For a line of Aeschylus' *Penelope* in which a speaker claims Cretan birth, see [[the-first-cretan-tale]].

**False mixed with true.** Horace says of Homer, *atque ita mentitur, sic veris falsa remiscet, / primo ne medium, medio ne discrepet imum*, “and so he lies, so mixes the false with the true, that the middle does not jar with the beginning, nor the end with the middle” (*Ars Poetica* 151–152; see [[horace]]). He speaks of the poet's own fictions, but the words fit the tale, in which the false is built on the true. For Strabo and Plato on Odysseus' lies, see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]; for Aristotle's praise of Homer's art of lying, see [[homer-taught-lies]]. When Strabo cites “the narrative to Eumaeus, contrived by Odysseus”, he means the story of the cloak, told that night (*Geography* 13.1.36; see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]).

**Sinon.** Virgil's Sinon, the Greek who gives himself up to the Trojans with a false tale of his misfortunes, offers a comparison, though no ancient writer sets the two side by side. He begins by promising Priam the truth, *Cuncta equidem tibi, rex, fuerit quodcumque, fatebor / vera*, “Everything, king, whatever comes of it, I will confess to you, and truly”, and says that if Fortune has made Sinon wretched, she will not make him false and a liar as well (*Aeneid* 2.77–80). Priam pities him, *His lacrimis vitam damus et miserescimus ultro*, “To these tears we grant your life, and we pity you besides”, has his bonds loosed and bids him tell the truth, and Sinon goes on with his lies, calling the eternal fires and the altars to witness (*Aeneid* 2.145–156; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and [[virgil]]). Sinon's lies are believed, and his appeal to the gods with them; the beggar's lies are believed, and the oath he offers, the one true thing he says, is declined (*Odyssey* 14.149–171; see [[oath]]).
