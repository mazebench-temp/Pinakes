**The end.** The poem tells the wall's end long before its time, at the beginning of Book 12, as the Trojans are about to storm it. It was built “against the will of the immortal gods”, and so it did not stand firm for long. It stood as long as Hector lived, Achilles kept his wrath and Priam's city was unsacked. When the city had fallen in the tenth year and the Argives had sailed home, Poseidon and Apollo turned the rivers of Ida against it. For nine days Apollo drove their floods at the wall while Zeus rained without ceasing, and Poseidon with his trident swept its foundations of logs and stones into the waves, smoothed the shore of the Hellespont, covered the great beach again with sand, and sent the rivers back into their beds (12.3–33; see [[the-achaean-wall]]).

## In the poem

**A wall for the war to come.** The wall is built in the tenth year, and its lateness has been a problem since antiquity (see [[the-wall-in-the-tenth-year]]). Inside the poem it is built at a turn. The Achaeans have lost many men in a day (7.327–330), and at dawn after the wall is finished Zeus will begin to give the Trojans the victory he promised Thetis (8.1–77; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). The wall becomes the stage of the next eight books. Hector scorns it (8.177–179), Achilles mocks it (9.348–352), the Trojans storm it and Hector breaks its gate (12.445–471), the Achaean leaders find it fallen, the wall they had trusted to be “an unbreakable safeguard for the ships and for ourselves” (14.56), in the words of Nestor's plan (7.338), and Apollo throws it down as a boy scatters a sand castle by the sea (15.355–366).

**The gods' grievance.** Poseidon's complaint has two parts. The Achaeans have built without sacrifice, “and have not given glorious hecatombs to the gods” (7.450), and the narrator says it again when the wall's end is told (12.6). And the wall will take his fame: men will forget the wall that he and Apollo built for Laomedon (7.451–453; see [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]] and [[the-walls-of-troy]]). Zeus answers the second part with Poseidon's own line, changed in its first word: it is Poseidon's fame that “will reach as far as the dawn spreads” (τοῦ δʼ ἤτοι κλέος, “the fame of it”, 7.451; σὸν δʼ ἤτοι κλέος, “your fame”, 7.458; see [[kleos]]). Then he gives Poseidon the wall to destroy. In Book 12 the destroyers are the two gods who, by Poseidon's account, built the walls of Troy, and Zeus adds his rain (12.17–26).

**Monuments.** The wall stands against a grave, the single mound over the Achaean dead, heaped “one for all alike” (7.336–337, 7.435–436; see [[tomb]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). The book began with Hector's imagined mound for the man he would kill, a monument by the Hellespont that sailors would point out long after (7.86–91; see [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]). It ends with a real mound and a wall on the same shore, which the gods agree shall vanish. When Poseidon has done his work, the shore by the Hellespont is smooth again (12.30–31). In the same passage the dead of the war are called “the race of men half-divine” (ἡμιθέων γένος ἀνδρῶν, 12.23), the only place where Homer uses the word. The wall and its builders pass away together, and the poem that tells of them is what remains.

## Outside Homer

Thucydides took the wall as evidence that the Greeks had won a battle when they landed, since otherwise they could not have fortified their camp, and so put it at the start of the war (1.11.1; see [[thucydides-and-the-wall]]). Aristotle, as Strabo reports, said that the wall never existed and that the poet who invented it made it vanish (13.1.36; see [[aristotle-and-the-wall]]). Some ancient and modern critics suspected the gods' scene (7.443–464), and analytic critics took the building of the wall for a later addition; others hold that the battles at the ships in Books 12–15 need it, and that the poem explains it by Achilles' absence (9.348–355). Ruth Scodel read its destruction by rivers and rain as an echo of the floods that end an age of men in Near Eastern and Greek myth (“The Achaean Wall and the Myth of Destruction”, *Harvard Studies in Classical Philology* 86, 1982).
