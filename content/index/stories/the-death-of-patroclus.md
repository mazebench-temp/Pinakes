## The story

When the Trojans reach the ships in Book 16, Patroclus begs Achilles to let him go out in his armor, and the narrator says what he is asking for: “so he spoke in supplication, great fool; for it was his own evil death and doom that he was begging for” (16.46–47; see [[nepios]]). Achilles lets him go, warning him to drive the Trojans from the ships and come back (16.80–96). Achilles prays to Zeus that his friend may win glory and drive the battle from the ships, and come back unhurt; Zeus grants the first and refuses the second (16.233–252). Patroclus drives the Trojans from the ships and kills Sarpedon, Zeus' son, but he forgets the warning and pursues them to the walls of Troy, “and if he had kept the word of the son of Peleus, he would have escaped the evil fate of black death” (16.684–687). Three times he climbs the angle of the wall, and three times Apollo thrusts him back; at the fourth the god warns him off, and he gives way (16.698–711). Later, in the fight over Hector's charioteer Cebriones, he charges three times and kills nine men each time, and at the fourth onset Apollo, unseen in a thick mist, strikes him from behind, knocks off his helmet, shatters his spear and looses his corselet (16.777–804). Euphorbus wounds him in the back, and Hector drives a spear through his belly (16.805–822; see [[cebriones]]). Dying, he tells Hector that Zeus and Apollo gave him the victory and that Euphorbus wounded him first, “and you are the third to strip me”, and that Achilles will soon kill him (16.843–854).

The fight for his body fills Book 17. Hector strips him of the armor of Achilles and puts it on (17.125–131, 17.192–197), and the Achaeans carry the body back toward the ships while the two Ajaxes hold the Trojans off (17.715–761). Antilochus brings Achilles the news (18.18–21), and Achilles, unarmed, shows himself at the ditch and shouts three times, and the Trojans fall back (18.203–231; see [[the-achaean-wall]]).

## In the poem

The death is foretold before it is told. Zeus says it first, to Hera, at the end of Book 8: Hector will not cease from war until Achilles is roused beside the ships, “over Patroclus dead” (8.473–477; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). The narrator foreshadows it when Patroclus first comes out of his hut, “and this was the beginning of evil for him” (11.604), and Zeus sets it out again for Hera in Book 15, with its place, “before Ilios”, and its consequence, Achilles' killing of Hector (15.64–68). Achilles is the one who does not know. His mother often told him the mind of Zeus, but not this (17.401–411). His grief and his vengeance for his friend turn the wrath of Book 1 against Hector, and he returns to the war knowing that his own death will follow Hector's (18.88–96; see [[achilles]], [[patroclus]] and [[hector]]).
