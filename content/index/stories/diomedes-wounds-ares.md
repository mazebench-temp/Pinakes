## The story

**The goddesses arm.** Hera sees Ares and Hector killing the Argives and says to Athena that they promised Menelaus in vain that he would sack Troy and sail home, if they let baneful Ares rage like this (5.711–718). She harnesses her horses, and Hebe fits to the chariot its wheels of bronze with golden rims and silver hubs on an iron axle (5.719–732; see [[chariot]] and [[hebe]]). Athena lets her robe fall on her father's floor, puts on the tunic of Zeus, and arms: the aegis, with Rout around it and Strife, Courage, Onslaught and the Gorgon's head on it, a golden helmet, and the heavy spear with which she overpowers the ranks of heroes (5.733–747; see [[arming-scene]], [[aegis]] and [[gorgon]]). The gates of heaven, kept by the Hours, groan open of themselves (5.748–752; see [[hours]]). Hera asks Zeus, who sits apart on the highest peak of Olympus, whether he will be angry if she drives Ares from the battle, and he tells her to set Athena on him, “who more than anyone is used to bringing evil pains upon him” (5.753–766).

**The descent.** The horses cover at each leap as much as a man can see from a lookout over the sea (5.767–772; see [[the-lookout]]). Hera leaves them where the Simoeis meets the Scamander, and the goddesses go on foot like timid doves to where the best of the Argives stand around Diomedes like lions or wild boars (5.773–783; see [[timid-doves]] and [[lions-and-boars]]). Hera, in the likeness of bronze-voiced Stentor, shames the Argives (5.784–792; see [[stentor]]).

**Athena and Diomedes.** Athena finds Diomedes beside his chariot, cooling the wound Pandarus gave him, and taunts him: Tydeus was small but a fighter, and his son is little like him (5.793–813; see [[tydeus-at-thebes]]). Diomedes answers that he knows her, and that he is only keeping her own order not to fight the gods, since he recognizes Ares ruling the battle (5.814–824). She releases him from it.

{{quote:28fa326c-264e-510f-b1cd-556698e7439e}}

She pushes Sthenelus out of the chariot and mounts beside Diomedes, and the oaken axle groans under “a dread goddess and the best of men” (5.835–839). Ares is stripping Periphas, the best of the Aetolians, whom he has just killed; Athena puts on the helmet of Hades, so that he cannot see her (5.842–845; see [[periphas]] and [[helmet-of-hades]]). Ares lunges at Diomedes over the yoke, and Athena catches his spear and pushes it away.

{{quote:20519e70-1170-5774-b5b2-1b016f8b31a2}}

Ares bellows as loud as nine or ten thousand men, and both armies tremble; then he goes up to the sky like a dark mist rising from the clouds (5.859–867; see [[ten-thousand-men]] and [[dark-mist-from-the-clouds]]).

**Olympus.** He sits down beside Zeus, shows him the immortal blood flowing from the wound, and complains. All the gods suffer by one another's will for the sake of men; Zeus fathered that senseless, ruinous daughter and never checks her; she set on the son of Tydeus, who wounded Cypris and then rushed at Ares himself, and but for his swift feet he would have suffered among the heaps of the dead (5.868–887). Zeus looks at him darkly.

{{quote:9e8e2638-58c8-5466-9e8c-e3b92358623c}}

He has his mother Hera's fury, Zeus goes on, and had he been the son of any other god, he would long ago have been lower down than the sons of Uranus. But he is Zeus' own son, and Zeus tells Paeëon to heal him (5.892–899). Paeëon heals the wound as quickly as fig juice curdles milk, Hebe bathes him and dresses him in lovely clothes, and he sits down beside Zeus “exulting in his glory” (5.900–906; see [[paeeon]] and [[fig-juice]]). Hera and Athena go back to the house of Zeus, “having stopped Ares, ruin of mortals, from his slaughter of men” (5.907–909).

## In the poem

**The end of the day.** The wounding of Ares is the height of Diomedes' aristeia and the end of Book 5 (see [[the-aristeia-of-diomedes]]). The book began with Athena leading Ares out of the battle by the hand with gentle words (5.29–36). It ends with her driving him out with a spear. In between, Diomedes has been allowed to wound Aphrodite and no other god (5.131–132), and has given ground before Ares because of that order (5.596–606, 5.815–824). Athena lifts the order, and she makes the attack her own: she drives the chariot, turns Ares' spear, and pushes Diomedes' spear home. The mortal who can see gods, since she cleared his eyes (5.127–128), strikes the god who cannot see her (5.845).

**Two wounded gods.** The scene answers the wounding of Aphrodite (see [[diomedes-wounds-aphrodite]]). Both gods go up to Olympus and complain that a mortal now fights the gods (5.380, 5.872–884), and both are healed at once. Aphrodite runs to her mother, who comforts her; Ares goes to his father, who calls him the most hateful of the gods. The cure of Ares is told in the lines Dione used for the cure of Hades, shot by Heracles (5.401–402 = 5.900–901; see [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]]): the story told as a paradigm in the middle of the book is acted out at its end. See [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]].

**Father and son.** Zeus rebukes Ares in the words Agamemnon used to Achilles in the quarrel of Book 1: “you are the most hateful to me … strife is always dear to you, and wars and battles” (1.176–177; 5.890–891; see [[strife-is-always-dear]]). On Olympus, as in the Achaean camp, the king rebukes the warrior who loves strife, and here the warrior is his son (see [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]]). Zeus blames the mother: Ares has Hera's fury, which Zeus himself can hardly keep down. Ares, for his part, blames Zeus for Athena, “since you yourself begot this destructive child” (5.880). Hesiod tells how Zeus gave birth to Athena from his own head (*Theogony* 924–926), and Ares' words may point to that story, though Homer never tells it. The threat of the “sons of Uranus” (5.898) is the threat of the Titans' fate, shut away below the earth (see [[heavenly-gods]] and [[fall-of-cronus]]).

**A god restored.** A wounded man in this book is healed slowly, if at all. Diomedes fights on with Pandarus' wound under his shield strap, wiping away the dark blood (5.795–798), and Sarpedon is carried off with a spear dragging from his thigh (5.663–667). The wounded god is healed in a moment, bathed, dressed, and sits by his father in glory. The last line of the healing, κύδεϊ γαίων, “exulting in his glory” (5.906), is the one used of the hundred-handed Briareus sitting beside Zeus in Book 1 (1.405). Nothing the mortal did to him lasts. See [[gods-and-mortals]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

In Book 8 Hera and Athena set out again against Zeus' orders, in the same lines: the harnessing, Athena's robe and tunic, the spear, the gates kept by the Hours (8.381–396). This time Zeus sends Iris to turn them back. In Book 15 Athena disarms Ares when he wants to go down and avenge his son Ascalaphus, and calls him a madman (15.113–142). In the battle of the gods Ares attacks her and reminds her of the day she set Diomedes on him and thrust the spear herself, “and tore my fair skin” (21.394–399). She fells him with a boundary stone, and Aphrodite leads him away (21.403–417). The Odyssey's Demodocus sings of Ares caught in Hephaestus' net with Aphrodite, while the gods laugh (Odyssey 8.266–366).

## Outside Homer

The helmet of Hades, which makes its wearer invisible, returns in the story of Perseus and the Gorgon ([Hesiod], *Shield* 226–227; Apollodorus 2.4.2; see [[helmet-of-hades]]). Plato's Socrates would admit into his city none of the battles of the gods that Homer composed (*Republic* 2.378d).
