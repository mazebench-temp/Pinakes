## The story

Achilles has given Nestor the fifth prize of the chariot race, a two-handled bowl, “just as it is”, without a contest, because old age keeps him from the boxing, the wrestling, the javelin and the footrace (23.615–623; see [[nestor]]). Nestor accepts it, says that his limbs are no longer steady, and wishes himself young (23.626–629):

{{quote:ac8fd971-cc53-5741-8072-af0c3df01b87}}

The Epeians of Elis buried their king at Buprasium, one of their towns (2.615; see [[amarynceus]], [[epeians]] and [[buprasium]]), and his sons set out the prizes. Nestor names three peoples among whom he had no match, the Epeians, his own Pylians and the Aetolians (see [[pylians]] and [[aetolians]]), and the five men he beat in four contests: [[Clytomedes]], son of Enops, at boxing; [[ancaeus-of-pleuron|Ancaeus of Pleuron]], an Aetolian, at wrestling; Iphiclus in the footrace; and Phyleus and Polydorus with the spear (see [[iphiclus]], [[phyleus]] and [[polydorus-at-buprasium]]). One contest he lost:

{{quote:e931794e-8d4d-5109-a8b1-5d45ddc6d7c4}}

The twins are the Moliones, “the two sons of Actor” (Ἀκτορίωνε, 23.638; see [[the-moliones]] and [[actor-of-elis]]). How they got in front “by their numbers” (πλήθει, 23.639) the poem does not say. Walter Leaf thinks the couplet 23.639–640 “defies interpretation”, and reports the explanations of the scholia: that the twins entered more than one chariot, that most of the starters conspired to give them the advantage, or, as Aristarchus held, citing Hesiod, that they were not twins like the Dioscuri but a double being, two bodies grown together, whom the people, against Nestor's protest, allowed to drive as one man (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.639; see the translation's note on 23.639–640 and [[aristarchus]]). Nestor's own account of the race is plainer: one held the reins “steadily”, and the other used the whip (23.641–642).

## In the poem

**An answer to Achilles.** Achilles named four contests that Nestor will no longer enter: “you will not fight with fists, nor wrestle, / nor enter the javelin throw any more, nor run / with your feet” (23.621–623). Nestor answers with the day he won all four, boxing, wrestling, the footrace and the spear, and lost only the fifth, the chariot race, the contest that his son has just driven with his advice (23.304–350; see [[antilochus]]). Leaf takes the two lists together, with the chariot race, for an epic pentathlon, and notes after Monro that Nestor's “only with horses” (οἴοισιν, 23.638) implies that his list of five is complete (Leaf on 23.621 and 23.638; see [[contests]]).

**Funeral games in funeral games.** The memory is of another funeral and its games, told in the middle of the games for Patroclus (see [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]). Nestor's last words turn it into a charge: “But go, and likewise honor your companion with funeral games” (23.646); καί, “likewise”, means, as Leaf explains, that as the men of old honored their dead with games, so Achilles should honor his friend (Leaf on 23.646).

**The wish.** “If only I were young like that, and my strength were steadfast” belongs to all three of Nestor's long tales of his youth: it closes the duel with Ereuthalion (7.157) and opens the war with the Epeians (11.670) and this tale, where it reads βίη τέ μοι for βίη δέ μοι (see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]], [[nestor-and-the-epeians]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]). The earlier tales were told to move younger men to act, and they did: nine men stood up to face Hector (7.161), and Patroclus' heart was stirred (11.804; see [[mythological-paradigm]]). This one asks nothing of its hearer. It is told to the young man who has honored the old one, and it ends with Nestor's acceptance of his age: “let younger men take on / such deeds” (23.643–644). “So I was once” (ὥς ποτʼ ἔον, 23.643) echoes the end of the Epeian tale, “Such was I among men, if that was ever so” (11.762), without its doubt (see [[such-was-i-if-ever]]).

**The fathers of the men at Troy.** The names in Nestor's story are the fathers of Epeian leaders in the Catalogue. Amarynceus is the father of Diores, who leads ten of the Epeian ships; the twin sons of Actor are the fathers of Amphimachus and Thalpius, who lead twenty; and Phyleus, if he is the Phyleus of the Catalogue, as Leaf thinks (on 23.637), is the father of Meges, who leads the men of Dulichium (2.620–629; see [[diores]], [[amphimachus]], [[thalpius]] and [[meges]]). If the Iphiclus of the footrace is the son of Phylacus, his sons Protesilaus and Podarces led the men of Phylace (2.704–708; see [[iphiclus]]). The games at Buprasium belong to Nestor's generation, the fathers of the men who fight at Troy.

**After the war of his youth.** The Moliones were “still boys” in the war with the Epeians, and their father Poseidon had to hide them from Nestor's spear (11.709–710, 11.750–752; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]); at Buprasium they are grown men and beat him. In that war the Pylians drove the Epeians back as far as Buprasium (11.756–760); here the Pylians compete among them at the funeral of an Epeian king.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells of games of the same kind, at which he himself won a prize. He crossed from Aulis to Chalcis “for the games of warlike Amphidamas”, where “his great-hearted sons” set out many prizes, and won a tripod with handles with a song (ἄεθλʼ ἔθεσαν παῖδες μεγαλήτορος, *Works and Days* 654–657; see [[hesiod]]). In both poems the dead man's sons set out the prizes, in almost the same words (παῖδες δʼ ἔθεσαν βασιλῆος ἄεθλα, 23.631), and Leaf compares Hesiod's verse for the genitive, prizes “of” the dead man (on 23.631).
