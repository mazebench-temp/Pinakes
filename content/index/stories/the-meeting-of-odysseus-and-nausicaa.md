## The story

**The dream.** Odysseus has swum ashore at the mouth of the river and sleeps in the wood near the water, under a wild olive and an olive, heaped over with leaves (*Odyssey* 5.441–493; see [[the-river-of-scheria]]). That night Athena goes to the city of the Phaeacians, “devising a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus” (*Odyssey* 6.14; see [[athena]], [[phaeacians]] and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]), and to the intricately worked chamber in the house of Alcinous where his daughter sleeps, “like the immortals in stature and in looks”, with two handmaids “who had their beauty from the Graces” by the doorposts (*Odyssey* 6.15–19; see [[nausicaa]], [[alcinous]], [[handmaids]] and [[graces]]). The goddess rushes “like a breath of wind” to the girl's bed and stands above her head in the likeness of the daughter of Dymas, “famed for ships”, a girl of her own age and dear to her heart (*Odyssey* 6.20–24; see [[like-a-breath-of-wind]], [[dymas-the-phaeacian]], [[famed-for-ships]], [[dream]] and [[dream-scene]]). She scolds her: “Nausicaa, how did your mother bear you so slack?” Her glossy clothes lie uncared for, and her marriage is near, when she must wear fine clothes herself and give others to those who will lead her home; from such things good talk goes up among men; the best of the Phaeacians are already wooing her. At dawn she must ask her father for the mules and the wagon, for the washing troughs are far from the city (*Odyssey* 6.25–40; see [[marriage]], [[wagon]] and [[mules]]). Then Athena goes back to Olympus, where the seat of the gods “is not shaken by winds, nor ever / wet with rain, nor does snow come near it” (*Odyssey* 6.41–47; see [[olympus]]).

**The wagon.** At dawn Nausicaa, marveling at the dream, goes through the house to tell her parents (*Odyssey* 6.48–51). Her mother sits at the hearth with her handmaids, “turning the sea-purple yarn”; her father she meets at the door, on his way to the council of the kings (*Odyssey* 6.52–55; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[hearth]]). She asks him for the high wagon, to take the clothes to the river: a man who sits in council among the foremost should wear clean clothes, and her five brothers, two married and three unmarried, always want freshly washed clothes for the dance (*Odyssey* 6.56–65). “So she spoke; for she was ashamed to name her blooming marriage / to her dear father. But he understood everything” (*Odyssey* 6.66–67; see [[aidos]]). The slaves make ready the mule wagon; her mother puts food and meats in a box and wine in a goatskin, and gives her olive oil in a golden flask; and Nausicaa takes the whip and the reins and drives off, her handmaids going with her (*Odyssey* 6.68–84; see [[oil-flask]]).

**The washing and the game.** At the river, where much fair water wells up and flows through the unfailing washing troughs, the girls turn the mules loose to graze on the honey-sweet grass, trample the clothes in the pits, “vying with one another in strife”, and spread them along the shore of the sea, where the waves wash the pebbles clean; they bathe, anoint themselves and eat their meal by the river while the clothes dry in the sun (*Odyssey* 6.85–98; see [[washing-troughs]], [[grass]] and [[bathing]]). Then they throw off their veils and play with a ball, and Nausicaa leads the song and dance, standing out among her handmaids as Artemis among her nymphs (*Odyssey* 6.99–109; see [[ball]], [[veil]] and [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]):

**The waking.** When she is about to yoke the mules and go home, Athena “thought of another thing, / that Odysseus should wake and see the fair-faced girl, / who would lead him to the city of the Phaeacian men” (*Odyssey* 6.110–114; see [[thought-of-another-thing]] and [[fair-faced]]). The princess throws the ball to one of her handmaids, misses her, and throws it into a deep eddy; the girls shout aloud, and Odysseus wakes (*Odyssey* 6.115–117). He sits up and wonders to what people he has come, whether arrogant and savage or kind to strangers, and whether the cry of girls around him is of nymphs, “who hold the steep peaks of the mountains / and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows”, or of human beings near at hand (*Odyssey* 6.119–126; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]] and [[nymphs]]). He breaks off a leafy branch to cover his nakedness and comes out like a mountain lion driven by hunger (*Odyssey* 6.127–134; see [[the-hungry-lion]]):

**The supplication.** Odysseus weighs whether to clasp the girl's knees or to beg her with soothing words, standing apart, and chooses words, “lest the girl be angry in her heart with him if he clasped her knees” (*Odyssey* 6.141–147; see [[supplication]] and [[decision-scene]]). “I implore you, queen: are you some goddess, or a mortal?” (*Odyssey* 6.149). If she is a goddess, he likens her to Artemis; if a mortal, three times blessed are her parents and her brothers, and most blessed of all the man who prevails with his bride-price and leads her home (*Odyssey* 6.150–159; see [[artemis]], [[three-times-blessed]] and [[bride-price]]). Awe holds him as he looks: he once saw such a thing at Delos, a young palm springing up beside the altar of Apollo, when many people followed him there “on that journey on which I was to have evil sorrows” (*Odyssey* 6.160–169; see [[awe-holds-me]], [[the-palm-on-delos]] and [[delos]]). Merry places the visit on the voyage of the Greeks from Aulis to Troy, through the Cyclades to Delos and on by Icaria, Samos and Lesbos to Lemnos and the Trojan coast (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.162; see [[the-departure-from-aulis]]). Then he tells his plight, twenty days at sea from Ogygia and cast ashore here by some god, asks her to show him the city and give him a rag to throw around him, and prays that the gods may give her a husband, a home and oneness of mind (*Odyssey* 6.170–185; see [[ogygia]], [[nothing-better-than-one-mind-in-a-house]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]).

**Her answer.** Nausicaa answers that Zeus deals out prosperity to good and bad as he wishes, and he must endure what he has been given; but now that he has come to her land he shall not lack clothing, nor anything that is fitting for a much-tried suppliant. She tells him the name of the people, the Phaeacians, and that she is the daughter of Alcinous (*Odyssey* 6.186–197; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]], [[zeus]] and [[much-tried]]). Her own name she does not tell him: he hears it only in the gossip she imagines (*Odyssey* 6.276), and speaks it only when he takes leave of her (*Odyssey* 8.464). Then she calls back her handmaids: no enemy comes to the Phaeacians, who are dear to the immortals and live far apart, the farthest of men; this is an unhappy wanderer, “for they are all from Zeus, / strangers and beggars, and a gift is small but dear”; they are to give him food and drink and bathe him in the river, out of the wind (*Odyssey* 6.198–210; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]] and [[xenia]]).

**The bath.** The handmaids lay a mantle and a tunic beside him, with the golden flask of oil, but he asks them to stand off: “But I will not bathe in front of you; for I am ashamed / to strip naked when I have come among girls of the lovely tresses” (*Odyssey* 6.211–222). He washes the brine from his back and shoulders and the scurf of the sea from his head, anoints himself and dresses, and Athena makes him taller and stouter, with thick hair falling like the hyacinth, and pours grace on his head and shoulders as a skilled man pours gold around silver (*Odyssey* 6.223–235; see [[hyacinth]], [[gold-poured-around-silver]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). He sits apart on the shore, “shining with beauty and grace; and the girl gazed at him” (*Odyssey* 6.236–237). Nausicaa tells her handmaids that the man who seemed unsightly before is now like the gods, and wishes that such a man might be called her husband and stay among them; they set food and drink beside him, and he eats greedily, “for he had long tasted no food” (*Odyssey* 6.238–250; see [[posis]] and [[fasting]]).

**Her instructions.** Nausicaa folds the clothes, yokes the mules and tells Odysseus how he is to come to the city (*Odyssey* 6.251–315). While they go through the fields he is to follow the wagon with the handmaids. But the city has its high rampart, a harbor on either side, the curving ships drawn up along the road and the place of assembly around the precinct of Poseidon, and its men are overbearing: she shuns their talk, for some baser man would ask who this tall stranger is, and say that she has found herself a husband from elsewhere and scorns the Phaeacians who woo her (*Odyssey* 6.262–288; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]], [[the-talk-of-the-people]], [[nemesis]] and [[oneidos]]). So he is to wait in the grove of Athena by her father's domain until she has reached home, then ask the way to the house of Alcinous, which even a child could show him, go quickly through the hall, pass by her father on his chair and throw his arms around her mother's knees, for if she thinks of him in friendship he may hope to reach his home (*Odyssey* 6.289–315; see [[the-grove-of-athena]] and [[throne]]).

**The grove.** She drives at a pace the handmaids and Odysseus can keep on foot, and at sunset they come to the grove of Athena, where Odysseus sits down and prays to the goddess, who, he says, never listened to him while Poseidon was wrecking him, to grant that he come to the Phaeacians “as one welcomed and pitied” (*Odyssey* 6.316–327; see [[prayer]] and [[hear-me]]). Athena hears him, “But she did not yet appear to him face to face; for she respected / her father's brother; and he raged furiously / against godlike Odysseus, until he reached his own land” (*Odyssey* 6.328–331; see [[poseidon]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]).

**The sequel.** The story is finished in the house of Alcinous (the renderings of the later books here are plain renderings, not the translation). Nausicaa drives home, her brothers unyoke the mules and carry the clothes in, and the old woman who nursed her lights a fire in her chamber (*Odyssey* 7.1–13); Odysseus follows and, as she told him, throws his arms around the knees of her mother, Arete (*Odyssey* 7.139–145; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). The queen knows the mantle and the tunic he is wearing, for she made them herself with her handmaids, and asks him who gave him these clothes (*Odyssey* 7.233–239). He tells of the girl among her handmaids at play on the shore, like the goddesses, who did not fail in good sense, as one would not expect of someone so young, and gave him food and wine, a bath in the river and these clothes (*Odyssey* 7.290–297). When Alcinous blames her for not bringing him home with her handmaids, Odysseus takes the blame on himself: she bade him follow with them, he says, but he would not, from fear and shame, lest the king be angry at the sight, “for we tribes of men on earth are quick to anger” (*Odyssey* 7.298–307). Alcinous wishes that such a man might have his daughter and be called his son-in-law, staying there, and promises him escort home if he will not stay (*Odyssey* 7.308–328). On the evening of the feast, as Odysseus comes from his bath to join the men at their wine, Nausicaa stands by the doorpost of the hall and takes leave of him: “Farewell, stranger, so that even in your native land you may remember me, since you owe the price of your life to me first”; and he answers that if Zeus grants him his homecoming, he will pray to her there as to a god all his days, “for you gave me my life, girl” (*Odyssey* 8.457–468).

## In the poem

**Athena's design.** The goddess begins and ends the book. She goes to the city devising a homecoming (*Odyssey* 6.14), sends the dream, contrives the waking so that the girl may lead him to the city (*Odyssey* 6.112–114), puts daring in Nausicaa's mind (*Odyssey* 6.139–140), makes Odysseus taller and pours grace on him (*Odyssey* 6.229–235), and at the end hears his prayer without showing herself (*Odyssey* 6.328–331). The pretext of her dream is the girl's marriage; its purpose is the stranger's homecoming. Her reserve is explained in the last verses: she respects her father's brother, who rages against Odysseus “until he reached his own land” (*Odyssey* 6.330–331; 6.331 = 1.21). Merry notes that she throws off this reserve as soon as Odysseus has landed in Ithaca and is beyond Poseidon's power, since it was the custom of the gods not to oppose one another openly (on 6.330; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]); there she tells him that she would not fight with her father's brother, angry because Odysseus had blinded his son (*Odyssey* 13.341–343).

**Marriage.** Athena's dream speaks of the girl's marriage (*Odyssey* 6.27, 6.33–35), and the theme runs through the book. Nausicaa is ashamed to name it to her father (*Odyssey* 6.66–67); Odysseus calls most blessed the man who will win her (*Odyssey* 6.158–159) and wishes her a husband and a home (*Odyssey* 6.180–185); she wishes, to her handmaids, that such a man might be called her husband (*Odyssey* 6.244–245; see [[nausicaas-wish]]); and the gossip she fears is that she has found a husband for herself (*Odyssey* 6.276–284), when she herself would blame a girl who mingled with men before she came openly to her marriage (*Odyssey* 6.286–288; see [[marriage]] and [[posis]]). Merry hears in her wish the thoughts of an innocent girl to whom marriage was a certainty, spoken to her own handmaids while Odysseus sits apart; before men, even before her father, she will not name her marriage (on 6.244, citing *Odyssey* 6.236 and 6.66). Aristarchus athetized both verses of the wish, hesitating over the first, and the fourteen verses of the gossip and her comment on it were athetized as unfitting to the speaker (*Odyssey* 6.275–288; see the translation's notes on 6.244–245 and 6.275–288). In the next book Alcinous offers Odysseus his daughter, in verses that Aristarchus doubted were Homer's: how could the king offer his daughter to a man he did not know, and press her on him unasked? (*Odyssey* 7.311–316; the scholia, in Merry's critical note there). The marriage is never made. The man she wished for is going home to his wife, and in the account of his wanderings that he gives Penelope the Phaeacians honor him and send him home, but the girl has no place (*Odyssey* 23.338–341).

**Odysseus' tact.** The waking speech shows a man who does not know where he is; the speeches that follow show him choosing every step. He speaks from a distance rather than clasp a girl's knees (*Odyssey* 6.141–147), and his words are “soothing and shrewd” (*Odyssey* 6.148; see [[kerdos]]). He calls her ἄνασσα, “queen”, a title Homer gives elsewhere only to Demeter and to Athena, so that the word pays a woman a special compliment (Merry on 6.149). He will not strip before the girls (*Odyssey* 6.221–222). In the next book he tells her parents only what is to her credit, and when her father blames her he covers her with an invention of his own (*Odyssey* 7.303–307): Eustathius remarks that here Odysseus lies openly, as a wise man would do at the right moment (as Merry reports him on 7.305).

**Goddess or mortal.** Odysseus' first question to the girl is the question Diomedes puts to a stranger on the battlefield of the Iliad, whether he is a mortal man or one of the immortals come down from heaven (*Iliad* 6.123–129; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]). The narrator likens Nausicaa to Artemis among her nymphs (*Odyssey* 6.102–109), and Odysseus, hearing the cry of the girls, first thinks of nymphs (*Odyssey* 6.122–124) and then likens her to Artemis himself (*Odyssey* 6.151–152). After Athena's gift of grace the comparison is turned on him: the man who seemed unsightly “is like the gods who hold the wide sky” (*Odyssey* 6.243), as Telemachus, when Athena has changed his father, takes him for one of the gods who hold the wide sky (*Odyssey* 16.181–183). The verses of Athena's gift return when Odysseus is bathed before Penelope (*Odyssey* 23.156–162, where 23.157–161 = 6.230–234).

**Arrivals.** Odysseus' question on waking, “Ah me, to the land of what mortals have I come this time?”, with its doubt whether the people are arrogant and savage or kind to strangers (*Odyssey* 6.119–121), comes back when he wakes in Ithaca and does not know his own land (*Odyssey* 13.200–202; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]), and its last two verses are the question with which he goes to find out what men the Cyclopes are (*Odyssey* 9.175–176; see [[xenia]] and [[cyclopes]]). Nausicaa gives the Phaeacians' answer before he has asked it of her: strangers and beggars are from Zeus (*Odyssey* 6.207–208).

**One night and one day.** The episode fills the night after Odysseus comes ashore and the following day, from his sleep under the olives (*Odyssey* 5.491–493, 6.1–2) to the sunset at the grove (*Odyssey* 6.321); the structure file counts them as the night of Day 31 and Day 32. He reached the land, he tells the girl, “Yesterday, on the twentieth day” of his voyage from Ogygia (*Odyssey* 6.170–172), the twentieth day of Zeus' decree (*Odyssey* 5.34); in his account to Arete he slept all night and into the middle of the next day, and woke as the sun was declining (*Odyssey* 7.286–289).

## Outside Homer

**Aristotle.** The *Rhetoric* calls the last verse of Odysseus' prayer “well said” and takes from it the two things a speaker's proem must aim at, to be welcomed and to be pitied (*Rhetoric* 1415b26–28, quoting *Odyssey* 6.327; see [[welcomed-and-pitied]], [[prooimion]] and [[aristotle]]).

**Plutarch's table.** At a dinner in Plutarch's *Table Talk* the question is put why Homer made Nausicaa wash the clothes in the river and not in the sea, which was near and might have been warmer and clearer. The answer given is Aristotle's: the sea is mixed with a rough, earthy element that makes it salt, while fresh water is light and fine, passes through the cloth and draws out the stains. One of the company finds the poet's accuracy in Odysseus, who washes the brine from his head and shoulders in the river, since sea water dried by the sun leaves a salty crust on the body that only fresh water removes (Aristotle, *Physical Problems* fr. 724 Gigon, from Plutarch, *Table Talk* 1.9, on *Odyssey* 6.137, 6.218–219 and 6.224–226; see [[plutarch]]).

**The stage and the painters.** Eustathius records that the most skillful ball player among private men was Sophocles, who wrote a satyr play, the *Washerwomen*, and himself took the part of Nausicaa in it (Eustathius, as Merry reports him on 6.115; see [[sophocles]]); among the plays of the Old Comedy poet Philyllius was a *Washerwoman, or Nausicaa* (Suda φ 457). Polygnotus painted the scene in the Propylaea at Athens (Pausanias 1.22, as Merry reports him on 6.138; see [[polygnotus]] and [[pausanias]]). Virgil gave the simile of Artemis among her nymphs to Dido (*Aeneid* 1.498–504), and Valerius Probus, as Gellius reports, judged that Virgil had translated nothing from Homer so unhappily: Nausicaa at play among her friends in a lonely place is rightly likened to the goddess hunting on the mountains among the nymphs, but Dido walking gravely through the middle of her city among the Tyrian lords is not (Merry on 6.102, quoting Gellius, *Attic Nights* 9.9; see [[virgils-dido-as-diana]] and [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]).

**Nausicaa's later story.** Later writers gave her a husband after all. Aristotle, in the *Constitution of the Ithacans*, and Hellanicus said that Telemachus married Nausicaa, daughter of Alcinous, and had by her the son whom the Hesiodic *Catalogue* gives to Polycaste, Nestor's daughter (*Constitution of the Ithacans* fr. 512 Gigon, from Eustathius on *Odyssey* 16.118; see [[telemachus]] and [[polycaste]]); Hellanicus also made the orator Andocides a descendant of Telemachus and Nausicaa (Suda α 2148). Anagallis of Corcyra, a woman grammarian, credited Nausicaa with the invention of the game of ball (Suda σ 1720).
