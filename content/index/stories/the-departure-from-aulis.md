## The story

{{quote:7440031a-789f-5696-b741-28c4914b88a7}}

On the day the Argives went aboard their swift ships, “bringing slaughter and doom to the Trojans”, Zeus nodded his assent (κατανεῦσαι) and flashed his lightning on the right (ἀστράπτων ἐπιδέξιʼ), “showing signs of good omen” (ἐναίσιμα σήματα; see [[teras]]). The right was the lucky side, and lightning is Zeus' own sign.

Nestor names no place. Odysseus, a few lines before, has placed the gathering of the ships at [[aulis|Aulis]] (2.303–304), and the sailing Nestor remembers is the fleet's departure from there. The two old signs belong together: one at the sacrifice before the sailing, one at the embarkation (see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]).

## In the poem

Nestor speaks after Odysseus, in the assembly that has almost dissolved (see [[the-test-of-the-army]]). He begins with a rebuke: the Achaeans talk like children, and their compacts and oaths, the libations of unmixed wine and the right hands they trusted, are going into the fire (2.337–343; see [[oath]] and [[libation]]). Then he gives the sign.

The lightning answers Agamemnon. In his test speech the king said that Zeus “once promised me and nodded his assent” that he would sack Ilios, and now bids him go home (ὑπέσχετο καὶ κατένευσεν, 2.112–113). Nestor takes the same promise at face value: no one should go home “before they know whether the promise of aegis-bearing Zeus is a lie or not” ([[pseudos|ψεῦδος]], 2.348–349). Whoever is desperate to leave may lay hands on his ship and be the first to die (2.357–359).

The listener knows more than Nestor. The nod that governs the Iliad is the one Zeus gave Thetis in Book 1, to honor Achilles by giving the Trojans the upper hand (1.524–530; see [[plan-of-zeus]]), and that very night Zeus has sent Agamemnon a lying dream. Both signs will come true: the Achaeans will suffer first, and Troy will fall. In Book 9, with the Trojans camped close to the Achaean ships, Odysseus tells Achilles that Zeus now flashes his lightning on the right for them (9.236–237).

## Outside Homer

Proclus' summary of the *Cypria* tells a longer story of the sailing. After the portent at Aulis the Achaeans put to sea, landed by mistake in Teuthrania and sacked it, fought Telephus, and were scattered by a storm on the way. They gathered at Aulis a second time, where Agamemnon offended Artemis and Iphigenia was to be sacrificed, and only then sailed for Troy. Homer mentions none of this.
