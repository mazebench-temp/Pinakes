## The story

**The parting wish.** The gifts are in the chariot and the meal is over when Menelaus comes out after the two young men with wine in a golden cup, stands before the horses and pledges them, bidding them greet Nestor, “for truly he was kind to me like a father, / while we sons of the Achaeans were fighting in Troy” (*Odyssey* 15.147–153; see [[menelaus]], [[libation]] and [[kind-as-a-father]]). Telemachus answers that they will tell Nestor all of it: “would that, / having come home to Ithaca and finding Odysseus in the house, / I might tell him so, as surely as I come from you having met with nothing but kindness, / and bring many fine treasures” (*Odyssey* 15.155–159; see [[telemachus]] and [[guest-gifts]]).

**The answer.** “So may Zeus now grant it, the loud-thundering husband of Hera; / then even there I would pray to you as to a god”, Telemachus answers; then he lays the whip on the horses, and they speed through the city toward the plain (*Odyssey* 15.179–183; see [[prayer]]). The next day he sails for Ithaca from Pylos (see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]).

## In the poem

**A sign for the son.** The bird comes on Telemachus' wish to find his father at home, as the two eagles of the assembly came “for him” when he had called on Zeus (*Odyssey* 2.146; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagles]]), and Peisistratus' question, whether the sign is for the two young men or for their host, is answered by the reading: it concerns the house of Odysseus. The verse that brings the bird is the verse of the eagle that flies by on the right as Ajax ends his challenge to Hector (*Odyssey* 15.160 = *Iliad* 13.821), and the joy of those who see it is the joy of the Trojans at the eagle that Zeus sends to Priam, “darting on the right across the city” (*Iliad* 24.320–321, which *Odyssey* 15.164–165 follows; see [[eagle]] and [[wings-as-wide-as-a-door]]).

**A likeness for a reading.** Helen reads the sign as a likeness, ὡς … ὣς, “As this eagle … so Odysseus” (*Odyssey* 15.174–176): the bird that comes “from the mountain, where he was born and bred” is the master coming home from far away. She does not say what the goose is. Penelope's dream says it four books later. In the dream an eagle comes from the mountain, ἐλθὼν δʼ ἐξ ὄρεος, with Helen's participle and phrase (*Odyssey* 19.538; 15.175), breaks the necks of the twenty geese that eat wheat in her house, and then speaks with a human voice: “The geese are the suitors, and I was the eagle, a bird, / before, but now I have come again as your husband” (*Odyssey* 19.548–549, a plain rendering; see [[geese]] and [[dream]]).

**Helen's gift.** Helen prophesies “as the immortals / put it in my heart, and as I think it will be fulfilled” (*Odyssey* 15.172–173), in the verses with which Athena, in the likeness of Mentes, began her prophecy to Telemachus that his father would soon come home; the goddess added, “though I am no seer and have no clear knowledge of birds” (*Odyssey* 1.200–202; see [[athena-visits-telemachus]] and [[prophecy]]). Helen too speaks from what the gods put in her heart, not from an art, and she does not wait for her husband's reading: she “was before him”, ὑποφθαμένη, as she was the first to know Telemachus for the son of Odysseus (*Odyssey* 15.171; 4.138–146; see [[helen]]). Her last half-verse, “and is planting evil for all the suitors”, takes up the reading of the seer Halitherses in the assembly, who said that Odysseus was already near “and plants slaughter and doom” for the suitors (*Odyssey* 15.178; 2.164–165; see [[halitherses]] and [[the-prophecy-of-halitherses]]), and the seer [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] repeats it to Penelope (*Odyssey* 17.159). It is true when she speaks it: Odysseus is already on Ithaca, in the swineherd's hut (*Odyssey* 13–14; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]).

**Two signs of the way home.** The journey home begins and ends with a bird on the right, told in the same verse. As Telemachus lands on Ithaca a falcon, “the swift messenger of Apollo”, flies by with a dove in its feet, and the seer [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] reads it (*Odyssey* 15.525–534; see [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]). The first sign is read by a woman who claims no skill, the second by a professional seer; the first foretells the vengeance, the second the kingship of the house.

**Telemachus' answer.** His two verses are those with which Odysseus took leave of Nausicaa, who had saved his life: “So may Zeus now grant it, the loud-thundering husband of Hera” and “then even there I would pray to you as to a god” (*Odyssey* 15.180–181 = 8.465, 8.467; see [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]]). The son takes leave of Helen in the words of his father's farewell to Nausicaa.

## Outside Homer

**Inspiration and augury.** Plato's Socrates sets two arts of the future against each other: prophecy, μαντική, which comes to the seer by divine dispensation, and the inquiry into the future that men in their senses make “through birds and the other signs”, which the old name-givers called οἰονοϊστική and the young now call οἰωνιστική, augury; by as much as prophecy is more perfect and more honorable than augury, by so much is madness from a god finer than the sanity of men (*Phaedrus* 244c–d; see [[plato]] and [[divination]]). Plato does not cite the scene, but it shows both kinds: Menelaus ponders how to read the sign “with understanding”, νοήσας, and Helen prophesies as the immortals put it in her heart.

**Statius.** In Statius' *Thebaid* Melampus himself, now aged, and Amphiaraus read birds of prey before the war of the Seven. The two seers take the auspices from a mountain (*Thebaid* 3.452–470); Melampus sees no bird of good omen, only a vulture and hawks exulting in their plunder (3.508–509), and Amphiaraus shows him a host of swans, which he is to take for Thebes, set upon by seven tawny eagles, *armigeras summi Iovis*, “the armor-bearers of highest Jupiter”, which are to be the Argive kings; the eagles kill and die, and Amphiaraus knows himself in the one that falls (3.524–547; see [[statius]], [[amphiaraus]] and [[the-seven-against-thebes]]). No ancient writer sets the scene beside Helen's omen; in Statius the eagles that fall on the white birds are the doomed. For the eagles of Aeschylus' Aulis, which Calchas reads as the two sons of Atreus, see [[aeschylus-aulis]].
