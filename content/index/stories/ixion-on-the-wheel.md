## The story

Pindar tells it as a warning to repay a benefactor. Bound to his winged wheel, Ixion cries out to mortals to pay back kindness with kindness. He was the first to shed a kinsman's blood, and that by treachery; Zeus took pity on him and received him among the gods, but he lusted after Hera. Zeus made a cloud in her likeness, and Ixion lay with it; the cloud bore Centaurus, who mated with the mares of Pelion and fathered the Centaurs. Ixion himself was bound to the four-spoked wheel (Pindar, *Pythian* 2.21–48; see [[ixion]] and [[centaurs]]). Later writers name the kinsman as his father-in-law, whom he lured into a pit of fire when he came to claim the bride-price. The Iliad knows only that Zeus loved “the wife of Ixion”, who bore Peirithous (14.317–318; see [[the-loves-of-zeus]]), the Lapith king at whose wedding the Centaurs were driven out (see [[lapiths-drive-out-the-centaurs]]). Aeschylus and Euripides each wrote an *Ixion* (see [[ixion-plays]]).
