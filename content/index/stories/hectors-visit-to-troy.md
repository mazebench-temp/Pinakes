## The story

**The errand.** With the gods gone from the field, the Trojans are giving way, and the seer Helenus tells Aeneas and Hector to hold the army before the gates (6.73–85; see [[helenus]]). Then: “But you, Hector, go to the city” (6.86). Hecuba is to take the old women to Athena's temple, lay a robe on the goddess's knees, and promise her twelve cows if she will keep Diomedes from the city (6.86–101; see [[the-robe-for-athena]]). Hector rallies the Trojans, tells them that he is going to ask the elders and the wives to pray, and goes, the black hide at the rim of his shield knocking against his ankles and his neck (6.102–118). While he walks, Glaucus and Diomedes meet on the plain (6.119–236; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]).

**At the gates.**

{{quote:e1e6f770-9724-596c-b20a-af6b4affeada}}

**Hecuba.** At Priam's palace, with its fifty chambers for his sons and twelve for his daughters (6.242–250; see [[priams-palace]]), his mother comes to meet him with Laodice. She takes his hand and offers him wine, to pour to Zeus and then to drink, for wine restores a weary man (6.251–262; see [[hecuba]] and [[laodice]]). He refuses: it would unnerve him, and he will not pour a libation with hands spattered with blood (6.263–268; see [[hand-washing]]). He tells her to take the robe to Athena, and says he will go after Paris, “if he is willing to listen to what I say. There and then may the earth gape for him!” (6.269–285; see [[may-the-earth-gape]]).

**Paris and Helen.** He finds Paris in his house on the height of the city, handling his armor and his bow, with Helen directing her women at their work (6.313–324). He rebukes him: the people are dying around the wall, and it is for Paris' sake that the war blazes around the city (6.325–331). Paris answers that he sat in his chamber from grief, not from anger at the Trojans, and that his wife has just persuaded him to go (6.333–341). Helen calls herself a dog, wishes she had died at birth or had a better husband, and asks Hector to sit, since Zeus has set an evil fate on her and Alexander “so that hereafter we may be a theme of song for men who are yet to be” (6.343–358; see [[helen]]). Hector will not sit. The Trojans long for him, and he wants to see his household:

{{quote:1cf53407-7778-5d38-bb60-0b1fcf4f82c8}}

**Andromache.** She is not at home. The housekeeper tells him she has gone to the great tower “like a woman out of her mind”, and he meets her at the Scaean gates with the nurse and their son (6.369–502; see [[hector-and-andromache]] and [[like-a-woman-out-of-her-mind]]). When she has gone home, her handmaids mourn him in his own house while he is still alive (6.497–502).

**Paris.** Paris comes running down from Pergamus in his armor like a stalled horse broken loose, and catches up with Hector at the place where he talked with his wife (6.503–516; see [[the-stallion]]). Hector tells him that he is brave but holds back of his own will, and that they will make things right “if ever Zeus grants us … to set up the mixing bowl of freedom in our halls” (6.521–529; see [[mixing-bowl]]). The two brothers go out through the gates together, and the Trojans welcome them like a wind sent to weary rowers (7.1–7).

## In the poem

**Why Hector goes.** The errand could have been carried by a herald. The poem sends the Trojans' best fighter from the battlefield to the city, and his errand opens the city to view. The rite for which he comes is carried out without him (6.286–311). What he does in Troy is meet his family. It is the last time the poem shows him inside the walls among his own people. His parents next speak to him from the wall, begging him to come inside before Achilles kills him (22.33–91); his wife next sees him dragged in the dust behind Achilles' chariot (22.462–465); and he comes home as a corpse (24.719–720).

**Three women.** Each of the three women he meets offers something that would keep him, and each is refused with a reason. His mother offers wine, which would weaken him and which he cannot pour to Zeus with unwashed hands (6.258–268). Helen offers a seat, and he answers that the Trojans are waiting for him (6.354–362). Andromache asks him to stay on the tower, and he answers that he would feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women if he shrank from the war (6.431–446). The order is of nearness, from mother to sister-in-law to wife. The refused seat belongs to the pattern of the visit (see [[visit-scene]]); Patroclus will refuse Nestor's seat in the same words, “you will not persuade me” (11.647–648 ~ 6.360).

**The city seen from inside.** Book 3 showed Troy's wall and its old men. Book 6 shows its houses: Priam's palace (6.242–250), Athena's temple on the citadel (6.88, 6.297; see [[temple]]), the house that Paris built with the best builders in Troy, near Priam's and Hector's on the height of the city (6.313–317), Hector's own house (6.370), the streets (6.391), the great tower of Ilios (6.386; see [[the-tower-of-ilios]]), the Scaean gates and the oak before them (6.237, 6.392–393; see [[scaean-gates]] and [[the-oak-of-zeus]]), and the height of Pergamus (6.512; see [[pergamus]]). The city is full of women and old men, and its men are on the plain (see [[troy]] and [[trojan-women]]).

**Foreboding.** The visit is full of the end of Troy. The women at the gate ask after their men, “and sorrows were in store for many” (6.241). Hector tells Helen that he does not know whether he will come back (6.367–368), tells Andromache that a day will come when Ilios will perish (6.447–449), and is mourned in his own house as if he were already dead (6.500–502; see [[lament]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). Athena refuses the Trojans' prayer (6.311). Yet the book ends in hope, with the two brothers going out together and Hector speaking of the mixing bowl of freedom (6.526–529).

**Time.** The narrative moves between the plain and the city. Glaucus and Diomedes meet while Hector walks to the gates (6.119–236), and Paris arms while Hector talks with Andromache (6.503–505). The day does not end with the book: the brothers' return rouses the Trojans, and the fighting goes on in Book 7 (7.1–16).
