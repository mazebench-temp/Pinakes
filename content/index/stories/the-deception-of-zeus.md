## The story

**The plan.** From a peak of Olympus Hera sees Poseidon busy among the Achaeans and is glad; she sees Zeus sitting on the topmost peak of Ida, and he is hateful to her (14.153–158). She decides to go to him adorned, to see if he will long to lie with her, so that she can pour sleep on his eyes and his shrewd mind (14.159–165; see [[hera]] and [[zeus]]).

**The adornment.** In the chamber Hephaestus made her, behind a secret bolt, she cleanses her body with ambrosia, anoints it with oil whose fragrance reaches earth and heaven, braids her hair and puts on the robe Athena worked for her, golden pins, a girdle with a hundred tassels, earrings, a veil and sandals (14.166–186; see [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]]). Then she lies to Aphrodite: she is going to the limits of the earth to end a quarrel between Oceanus and Tethys, who reared her, and asks for love and longing. Aphrodite gives her the embroidered strap that holds all her enchantments (14.187–223; see [[aphrodite]], [[aphrodites-band]], [[oceanus]] and [[tethys]]).

**Sleep.** Hera flies over Pieria, Emathia, the Thracian mountains and Athos to Lemnos and asks Sleep to lull Zeus, offering a golden throne (14.225–241). He refuses: once before he lulled Zeus for her, when she drove Heracles to Cos, and only Night saved him from Zeus' anger (14.242–262). She promises him Pasithea, one of the younger Graces, and he makes her swear by the Styx, naming the Titans as witnesses (14.263–280; see [[sleep]], [[night]], [[pasithea]], [[styx]] and [[titans]]). They come to Ida by Lectum, and Sleep hides in a tall fir in the likeness of a bird (14.281–291; see [[lectum]] and [[chalcis-and-cymindis]]).

**Gargarus.** Zeus sees her, and desire wraps his mind as when they first lay together “without the knowledge of their dear parents” (14.292–296). She tells him the same false story. He answers that she can go later, and swears that he has never desired goddess or woman so much, naming his loves one by one (14.297–328; see [[the-loves-of-zeus]]). She pretends to fear that a god might see them on the open peak and asks to go to her chamber; he wraps them in a golden cloud, and the earth sends up grass and flowers beneath them (14.329–351; see [[the-golden-cloud]]).

**While Zeus sleeps.** Sleep runs to tell Poseidon to help the Danaans “while he still sleeps” (14.354–360). Poseidon leads the Argives openly, holding a sword like lightning; Ajax strikes Hector down with a stone, and the Trojans are routed (14.361–522; see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]], [[hector]] and [[ajax]]).

**Zeus wakes.** On the peaks of Ida, beside Hera, Zeus wakes and sees the Trojans in flight, Poseidon among the Argives and Hector lying on the plain, vomiting blood. He calls her trick κακότεχνος, “evil-working”, and reminds her how he hung her from the sky when she drove Heracles to Cos. Hera swears by Earth and Heaven, the water of Styx, his head and their marriage bed that Poseidon acts of his own accord; Zeus smiles, sends her to fetch Iris and Apollo, and sets out his plan to the death of Hector and the fall of Ilios (15.4–77).

## In the poem

**Against the plan of Zeus.** Hera cannot overpower Zeus, and Poseidon would not fight him when she asked (8.198–211). What force cannot do, sleep and love can (14.353). The deception holds back Zeus' promise to Thetis for part of a day and turns the battle for the Achaeans; when Zeus wakes, he sends Apollo to restore Hector and states his plan in full (15.49–77; see [[plan-of-zeus]], [[the-battle-at-the-ships]] and [[the-third-day-of-battle]]).

**A marriage seen whole.** The quarrels of Zeus and Hera in Books 1, 4 and 8 are fought in words, in public. Here the poem shows their marriage from inside: a memory of their first love in secret from their parents, desire stronger than any he has felt, the bed, and a sleep in each other's arms (see [[husbands-and-wives]] and [[quarrel-on-olympus]]). Zeus woos her in the words of Paris to Helen after the duel: “as I love you now, and sweet longing takes hold of me” (14.328 = 3.446; see [[helen-and-aphrodite]]).

**Deceit in set pieces.** The episode is built from the poem's set pieces: a goddess dressing, a gift, a bargain sealed with an oath, a journey through the sky, a catalogue of loves. Each serves a lie, and the lie works (see [[apate]] and [[truth-and-deception]]).

## Outside Homer

The book is traditionally called Διὸς ἀπάτη, “the Deception of Zeus” (see [[title-of-book-14]]). Plato's Socrates would not let the young hear how Zeus forgot all his plans in his desire, refused to go to the chamber and lay with Hera on the ground, more possessed than when they first met “without the knowledge of their dear parents” (*Republic* 3, 390b–c; see [[plato-zeus-deceived]]).
