## The story

Helen tells the story years later in Sparta, to Telemachus and Menelaus. Odysseus marked himself with cruel blows, threw wretched rags over his shoulders and slipped into the city like a servant or a beggar. She alone recognized him and questioned him, but he evaded her questions with his cunning; when she had bathed and anointed him and sworn not to betray him, he told her all the Achaeans' plan, and went back to the camp after killing many Trojans with the long bronze (*Odyssey* 4.242–258; see [[helen]] and [[odysseus]]). The Little Iliad told it in the same way: Odysseus, disfigured, entered Troy as a spy, was recognized by Helen, and made an agreement with her about the taking of the city (Proclus, summary of the *Little Iliad*; see [[the-beggary]]).

## Outside Homer

Euripides gives the story to Hecuba. In the *Hecuba* she reminds Odysseus how he came to Ilium as a spy, disfigured by ragged clothes, with drops of blood running from his eyes down his cheek; Helen knew him and told her alone, and he clasped her knees, humbled, and she saved him and sent him out of the land (*Hecuba* 239–250; see [[hecuba]] and [[euripides]]). In the *Rhesus*, which has come down under Euripides' name, Hector says that Odysseus once entered the towers as a begging priest in a beggar's clothes, calling down curses on the Argives, sent into Ilium as a spy, and went out again after killing the guards of the gates; and the chorus remember him going through the city with a sword hidden in his ragged robe and his head squalid and filthy, begging his living like a vagrant servant and abusing the house of the Atreidae as though he were their enemy (*Rhesus* 501–507, 710–719). Merry cites both plays on Helen's story (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 4.245, 4.246).
