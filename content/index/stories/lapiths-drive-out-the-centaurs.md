## The story

{{quote:569ff1e4-c3ab-59dc-8f9d-45eaacd2915f}}

Three verbs tell the whole war. Peirithous "took vengeance" (ἐτίσατο) on the Centaurs, "drove them" (ὦσε) out of [[Pelion]], and "brought them near" (πέλασσεν) the [[Aethices]]. ἐτίσατο implies that the Centaurs had wronged him first, and the Odyssey says how (see below). The result is an expulsion: the Centaurs lose their mountain.

## In the poem

The story is told to date a birth. Polypoetes was born "on the day" of the victory, as if the Lapiths' future leader and their triumph belonged together. In Book 1 Nestor named Peirithous and Caeneus among the mightiest men he had ever known and said that they destroyed the beasts of the mountains (1.262–268; see [[nestor-and-the-centaurs]]). Book 2 brings on their son and grandson, [[Polypoetes]] and [[Leonteus]], as leaders at Troy. The heroes whom Nestor held up as a standard are the fathers of men in the army.

## Outside Homer

The Odyssey gives the cause. The Centaur Eurytion, drunk with wine in the house of Peirithous, did evil there, and the Lapiths cut off his ears and nose and threw him out; "from this the quarrel began between the Centaurs and men" (21.295–304). Later tradition set the outrage at Peirithous' wedding to Hippodameia, where the Centaurs tried to carry off the bride, and gave Theseus a part in the fight. The battle was a favorite subject of Greek sculpture, on the west pediment of the temple of Zeus at Olympia and on the south metopes of the Parthenon. Ovid gives Nestor a long account of it (*Metamorphoses* 12.210–535).
