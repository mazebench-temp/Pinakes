## The story

**Thetis' promise.** Achilles has resolved to kill Hector, though his mother has told him that his own death will follow (18.88–126; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). Thetis does not hold him back. His armor, she says, is held among the Trojans, and Hector wears it, but not for long; he must not go into the toil of Ares until he sees her again:

{{quote:761dcee4-2725-58ad-ae36-fc760af404d9}}

She sends the Nereids back to their father's house and goes to Olympus (18.138–147; see [[nereids]]). While she is away Achilles cannot arm, for his mother has forbidden it and he knows of no armor he could put on, and he goes to the ditch as he is (18.188–231; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]]).

**The house of Hephaestus.** That night she comes to the house of Hephaestus, “imperishable, starry, standing out among the immortals, / of bronze, which the crook-footed god had built for himself” (18.370–371; see [[houses-of-the-gods]] and [[crook-footed]]). She finds him sweating among his bellows, making twenty tripods on golden wheels, “so that of themselves they might enter the divine assembly / and go back again to his house, a wonder to see”; only their handles are still to be fixed (18.372–379; see [[tripod]]). His wife Charis comes out, clasps Thetis' hand, seats her on a silver-studded throne, and calls Hephaestus (18.380–392; see [[charis-wife-of-hephaestus]]).

**The debt.** Hephaestus tells Charis why the goddess is welcome. When he had fallen far, by the will of his mother, who wanted to hide him away because he was lame, Thetis and Eurynome took him to their bosom, and for nine years he forged jewelry with them in a hollow cave by the stream of Oceanus, known to no one else; so he must now “pay Thetis of the lovely tresses the full price for my life” (18.394–407; see [[fall-of-hephaestus]] and [[eurynome]]). He puts away his bellows and his tools, wipes his face, his hands, his neck and his chest with a sponge, puts on a tunic, takes a thick staff and comes out limping, held up by golden handmaids “like living young women”, who have mind, voice and strength (18.409–421; see [[the-golden-handmaids]]). He sits down by Thetis, clasps her hand, and asks what she wants (18.421–427).

**The supplication.** Thetis answers with her sorrows. Zeus subdued her, of all the goddesses of the sea, to a mortal man, and Peleus now lies worn out by old age in his halls (18.429–435; see [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]]). She bore and reared a son, preeminent among heroes, who will not come home (18.436–443). She tells his story since *Iliad* 1: the girl taken from him by Agamemnon, the Achaeans penned in at the sterns, the elders' entreaties and his refusal, Patroclus sent out in his armor, and Apollo, who killed Patroclus among the champions and gave the glory to Hector (18.444–456; see [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). “That is why I come now to your knees”: she asks for a shield, a helmet, greaves and a corselet for her swift-fated son, since his trusted companion lost what he had (18.457–461; see [[supplication]]).

**Hephaestus' promise.**

{{quote:a4519b03-5f64-50cb-8f9d-656910f65115}}

**The forge.** He turns his twenty bellows to the fire, casts bronze, tin, gold and silver into it, sets the anvil on its block, and takes up the hammer and the tongs (18.468–477). First he makes the shield, great and heavy, of five layers, and works on it the earth and the sky, two cities, the fields and the vineyard, herds and a pasture, a dance, and Oceanus around the rim (18.478–608; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]]); then a corselet brighter than the light of fire, a heavy helmet with a golden crest, and greaves of soft tin (18.609–613).

**The delivery.** He lifts the armor and sets it before Thetis, and “she like a hawk leapt down from snowy Olympus, / carrying the gleaming armor from Hephaestus” (18.614–617). At dawn she reaches the ships with the god's gifts and finds her son lying over Patroclus, weeping; she tells him to let the dead man lie and to take the armor from Hephaestus, and lays it before him (19.1–13). The arms ring out, the Myrmidons tremble and none dares look at them, and Achilles' anger rises, his eyes blaze, and he takes the god's gifts in his hands and delights in them (19.12–19). Such arms, he tells his mother, are the work of immortals, not of a mortal man; now he will arm (19.21–23).

## In the poem

**Why new armor.** The new arms are needed because of the old ones' story: the gods' gift to Peleus, lent to Patroclus, stripped from his body and worn by Hector (see [[the-armor-of-peleus]] and [[exchange-of-armor]]). Without armor Achilles cannot fight: “Those men have my armor”, and there is no one else's he could put on but the shield of Ajax (18.188–193). The making costs one night. Thetis promised to come back at dawn, with the rising sun (18.136), and she does (19.1–3; see [[the-third-day-of-battle]]).

**Thetis' second journey.** Thetis has gone up to Olympus for her son once before. In *Iliad* 1 she sat before Zeus, took his knees and asked honor for Achilles, and the honor was the Achaeans' defeat (1.495–530; see [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). Now she comes to Hephaestus' knees (τὰ σὰ γούναθʼ ἱκάνομαι, 18.457), and the defeat she asked for has cost her son his companion. Achilles has said so himself: the Olympian has fulfilled it for him, “but what pleasure have I in them” (18.79–80).

**The welcome.** Thetis is received as a goddess receives a visiting god. Charis' greeting and invitation, and Hephaestus' words after them, are the words with which Calypso receives Hermes in the *Odyssey* (18.385–387, 18.424–427 ≈ *Odyssey* 5.87–91; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.386): “Why, Thetis of the long robes, have you come to our house, / respected and dear? Before now you have not come often” (18.385–386 = 18.424–425; see [[xenia]]). Hephaestus bids Charis set guest-gifts before her while he puts away his tools (18.408–409).

**Gifts and debts.** The armor repays a debt. Hephaestus owes Thetis the price of his life, ζῳάγρια, a word that first meant the price paid to a warrior who took a prisoner alive, and which Leaf calls a playful extension here (18.407; Leaf on 18.407; see [[zoagria]]). Yet Thetis does not claim the debt; she comes as a suppliant, to his knees (18.457). The old armor was the gods' gift to Peleus on his wedding day (18.84–85), the new is a god's gift to his son, and the poem calls both δῶρα, “gifts” (18.84; 19.3, 19.18).

**The god's limit.** Hephaestus can promise armor that men will marvel at, but not that it will save its wearer. His wish that he could hide Achilles from death “as surely” as he will give him armor (18.464–466) sets the gift against the fate Thetis has just named, “my swift-fated son” (18.458; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).

**Wonders.** The house is full of marvels: the tripods that go of themselves (18.373–377) and the golden handmaids, “like living young women. / There is a mind in their hearts, and voice in them, / and strength” (18.418–420). Leaf compares the gold and silver dogs that Hephaestus made to guard the palace of Alcinous (*Odyssey* 7.91–94), and observes how sparingly such marvels are used even on Olympus (on 18.376). The shield carries the wonder further: its soldiers fight “like living mortals” (18.539; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]] and [[thaumaston]]).

## Outside Homer

The traditional title of *Iliad* 18, Ὁπλοποιία, “the Making of the Arms”, is already used by Strabo and Athenaeus for the passage in which Hephaestus forges the armor (see [[title-of-book-18]]). Aristotle cites the tripods of Hephaestus, “which, the poet says, of their own accord enter the divine assembly”, with the works of Daedalus, as tools that would do their own work and make servants needless (*Politics* 1.4, 1253b33–1254a1; see [[daedalus]] and [[aristotle-the-tripods-of-hephaestus]]). In the Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles* Hephaestus makes Heracles' shield “by the counsels” of Zeus (*Shield* 318–320; see [[shield-of-heracles]]). In Virgil, Venus asks Vulcan for arms for her son Aeneas, reminding him that the daughter of Nereus and the wife of Tithonus had moved him with their tears; his Cyclopes forge them, and she brings them to Aeneas, who marvels at the shield (*Aeneid* 8.370–386, 8.439–453, 8.608–625; see [[virgil-shield-of-aeneas]]).
