## The story

**In Homer.** Achilles tells it to Priam, to persuade him to eat: even Niobe of the lovely hair remembered food, though her twelve children, six daughters and six sons in their prime, had died in her halls. She used to liken herself to fair-cheeked Leto, and said that the goddess had borne two children while she herself had borne many; “and so those two, though they were only two, destroyed them all”, Apollo the sons with arrows from his silver bow, in his anger, and Artemis the daughters. For nine days they lay in their blood with no one to bury them, for the son of Cronus had turned the people to stones; on the tenth the heavenly gods buried them. Niobe remembered food when she was worn out with weeping; and now, a stone among the rocks of the lonely mountains of Sipylus, where the nymphs who dance around the Achelous have their beds, she broods over the sorrows the gods gave her (*Iliad* 24.602–617; see [[niobe]], [[apollo]], [[artemis]], [[leto]] and [[zeus]]). Homer gives no place for the killing but her halls, and no name to her husband or her children.

**Later tellings.** In Apollodorus Niobe is the daughter of Tantalus and the wife of Amphion of Thebes. She bore seven sons and seven daughters, whom he names; Hesiod, he reports, gave her ten of each, Herodorus two sons and three daughters, and Homer six and six. Leto, stung by her boast that she was more blessed in children, set Artemis and Apollo on them: Artemis shot the daughters in the house, and Apollo the sons together as they hunted on Cithaeron; one son and one daughter, Chloris, survived. Niobe left Thebes for her father's home at Sipylus, and there, at her prayer, Zeus turned her to stone, and tears flow from the stone night and day (*Library* 3.5.6). Walter Leaf, who reconciles the two Homeric moments of the story in the same way, reports that the tragedians gave her seven sons and seven daughters, and Hesiod, Pindar and Mimnermus ten of each (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.602, 24.613).

Aeschylus wrote a *Niobe* (see [[niobe-play]]). In a speech preserved on papyrus she sits on her children's tomb, brooding over the dead (PSI 1208), and Euripides in Aristophanes' *Frogs* mocks Aeschylus for seating a veiled figure, “an Achilles or a Niobe”, without showing the face and without a word (*Frogs* 911–913). Plato's Socrates will not have it said that “the sufferings of Niobe” were the work of a god, unless the poet can give a reason why the god's work was just (*Republic* 2.380a–b; see [[plato-broken-oaths]]). Ovid tells the story at length (see [[ovid-niobe]]).
