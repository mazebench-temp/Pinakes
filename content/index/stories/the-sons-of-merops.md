## The story

{{quote:954203b1-5b84-5d49-87e2-68a2b934c60d}}

[[Merops]] "knew prophecy above all men", and he would not let his sons go "into man-destroying war" ([[man-destroying]]). They would not obey. κῆρες, the "fates" of death ([[ker-doom]]), were leading them: the brothers who lead their men to Troy are themselves being led to their deaths.

## In the poem

The same lines return in Book 11. As Diomedes and Odysseus hold back the Trojan advance, they take a chariot and "two men, the best of their people, the two sons of Merops of Percote", and Diomedes strips their armor (11.328–335). The Catalogue's foreshadowing is fulfilled in its own words.

Other sons of seers die at Troy. The Corinthian seer Polyidus told his son Euchenor that he would die of a painful sickness at home or be killed by the Trojans at the ships, and Euchenor chose Troy (13.663–672). Diomedes kills the sons of the old dream-reader Eurydamas, "but the old man read no dreams for them when they set out" (5.148–151). Knowing the future does not save these men. Compare [[ennomus]], the augur whose birds did not save him (2.858–861).
