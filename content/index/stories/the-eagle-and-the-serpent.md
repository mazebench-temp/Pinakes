## The story

**The portent.** While the Lapiths strip the men they have killed at the left gate, the young men who follow Polydamas and Hector, “the most and the best”, still stand hesitating beside the ditch (12.195–199; see [[the-fight-at-the-wall]]).

{{quote:98754e30-088a-5ea6-84a3-4f9a590cffd2}}

The eagle comes ἐπʼ ἀριστερά, “on the left”, the side of ill omen. The serpent fights back, the eagle drops it among the Trojans, and they shudder at “the portent of aegis-bearing Zeus” (see [[eagle]], [[serpent]] and [[teras]]).

**Polydamas' reading.** Polydamas begins with a grievance: Hector always rebukes him in the assemblies when he gives good counsel, “since it is not fitting at all for a man of the people to speak against you” (12.211–214; see [[demos]]). He repeats the narrator's description of the eagle (12.218–220 = 12.200–202, but for the opening words) and adds what the narrator did not say: the eagle let the serpent go before it reached its home, and did not bring it to its young (12.221–222). **Hector's answer.** Hector looks at him darkly and answers in the words with which Paris refused Antenor's proposal to give Helen back: if Polydamas means what he says, the gods have destroyed his wits (12.231–234 = 7.357–360). Then he sets the counsels of Zeus against the birds:

{{quote:390f4e53-ec32-5e6c-b60e-3e6e82713603}}

Even if all the rest are killed at the ships, Hector adds, Polydamas need not fear for his life, since his heart is not staunch against the enemy; but if he holds back from the fight or turns another man away from it, he will die at once by Hector's spear (12.244–250). Hector leads the way, Zeus raises a storm of dust from Ida against the ships, and “trusting in his portents and in their own strength” the Trojans set to breaking the wall (12.251–257).

## In the poem

**A sign against the attack.** The eagle is the bird of Zeus, and the omen is called his. It comes on the left; the heron Athena sends to Odysseus and Diomedes, the eagle that flies up as Ajax challenges Hector, and the eagle that answers Priam's prayer all come on the right (10.274–277, 13.821–823, 24.315–321), and Hector names both sides in his scorn (12.238–240). It is the only eagle of omen in Homer that is hurt by its prey. The serpent fights like a warrior: it “had not yet forgotten the joy of battle” (12.203), as Sarpedon on the wall “did not forget the joy of battle” (12.393). Polydamas makes the eagle the Trojans, and the serpent the Achaeans fighting “in defense of the ships” (12.227).

**No seer.** Polydamas is not a seer; he says how “a prophet” would read the sign (θεοπρόπος, 12.228), and the Trojans' own reader of birds, Helenus, “by far the best of the bird-seers” (6.76), leads another column (12.94; see [[helenus]]). His reading has the shape of Calchas' at Aulis, a likeness drawn from the creatures and then “so we” (ὣς ἡμεῖς, 12.223; 2.328), and the two serpent portents of the Iliad are mirror images (see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]).

**Zeus' promise.** Hector trusts the promise that Zeus sent him earlier that day by Iris, power to kill “until you come to the well-benched ships, and the sun goes down” (11.200–209). His words for it, “promised me and nodded his assent” (ὑπέσχετο καὶ κατένευσε, 12.236), are the words in which Agamemnon complained of the false dream (2.112 = 9.19), and Asius has just called Zeus “a lover of lies” (12.164; see [[lover-of-lies]]). Both readings come partly true. Zeus does mean glory for Hector, who breaks the gate and reaches the ships (12.173–174, 12.437–471). But when Patroclus drives the Trojans back in Book 16, they cross the ditch again “not in due order” (οὐδὲ κατὰ μοῖραν), and many chariots are wrecked in it (16.364–376): the return that Polydamas foresaw.

**Hector and Polydamas.** This is the second of Polydamas' counsels and the first that Hector refuses. He accepted the first and will accept the third (12.60–81, 13.723–753); he refuses the last, on the night Achilles returns, and remembers it before he dies (18.249–313, 22.99–103; see [[polydamas]] and [[hector]]). In the Odyssey the suitor Eurymachus dismisses the seer Halitherses in the same spirit: many birds go to and fro under the rays of the sun, and not all are fateful (*Odyssey* 2.181–182). Hector's own line became a maxim (see [[one-omen-is-best]]).

## Outside Homer

In the *Ion* Socrates shows the rhapsode that each art has its own passages in Homer. For the seer he gives Theoclymenus' vision of the suitors' doom (*Odyssey* 20.351–357) and, “in many places in the Iliad too, as at the Teichomachia” (ἐπὶ τειχομαχίᾳ), the eagle and the serpent, quoting 12.200–207: these and the like belong to the seer to examine and to judge (*Ion* 538e–539d; see [[plato-ion-the-seers-passages]] and [[title-of-book-12]]). Aristotle cites Hector's line among the well-worn common maxims, for one who urges men to face danger without having sacrificed (*Rhetoric* 2.21.11, 1395a; see [[one-omen-is-best]]).
