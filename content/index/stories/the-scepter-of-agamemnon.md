## The story

{{quote:778cced3-e225-5437-8786-4ee584223e25}}

[[Hephaestus]] made the scepter, and the verb (κάμε τεύχων, “toiled to make”) gives it the labor of a god's craft. He gave it to [[Zeus]], and Zeus gave it to [[Hermes]], “the guide, the slayer of Argus” (διάκτορος ἀργεϊφόντης; see [[argeiphontes]]). Hermes gave it to [[Pelops]], “driver of horses”; Pelops to [[Atreus]]; Atreus, when he died, left it to his brother [[Thyestes]], “rich in flocks”; and Thyestes left it to [[Agamemnon]] to carry, with the rule that goes with it: “to be lord of many islands and of all Argos” (see [[argos]]).

The passage is a single sentence of eight lines. It moves from gods to men in a chain of giving: δῶκε (“gave”) four times, then ἔλιπεν and λεῖπε (“left”) for the two handings-on at death. Each holder has his epithet, and none has a story. There is no quarrel between the brothers, and no crime.

## In the poem

The history is told at a precise moment. Agamemnon has called the army to assembly, and he rises holding the scepter (2.100–101). Then he leans on it (τῷ ὅ γʼ ἐρεισάμενος, 2.109) and tells the army that Zeus has deceived him and that they must sail home. The speech is a test, and a lie (see [[the-test-of-the-army]]). The pedigree gives the king's word the full weight of Zeus just when he is about to misuse it, and just before the army takes him at his word and runs for the ships.

It also answers Book 1. Achilles swore by a scepter cut from a tree and carried by the judges, and threw it down (1.234–246). Agamemnon's is a god's work and an heirloom. Earlier that morning he took it up as “the scepter of his fathers, imperishable forever” (2.46). Its descent from Zeus is the ground of what Odysseus tells the commoners a little later: one king, “to whom the son of crooked-counseling Cronus has given the scepter and the ordinances” (2.205–206; see [[one-ruler]]). Odysseus carries this same scepter through the camp and beats [[Thersites]] with it (see [[scepter]] and [[odysseus-and-thersites]]).

## Outside Homer

Thucydides took the last line as evidence for Agamemnon's power. He cites “lord of many islands and of all Argos”, “in the handing down of the scepter”, to show that Agamemnon, a mainland king, must have had a fleet (1.9.4; see [[thucydides-and-the-catalogue]]).

Later poets made the house of Pelops a house of crimes. In Aegisthus' speech at the end of Aeschylus' *Agamemnon*, Atreus drove out his brother Thyestes and served him the flesh of his own children at a feast (1577–1611). Homer's handing-on, from brother to brother and then to Atreus' son, knows nothing of this, or passes over it.

Pausanias reports that the people of Chaeronea honored above all gods a staff they took to be this scepter. They called it “the Spear”, kept it in the house of that year's priest, and made offerings to it every day (9.40.11–12; see [[scepter]]).
