## The story

**The land of the Cyclopes.** From the Lotus-eaters the ships came to the land of the Cyclopes, “overbearing and lawless”, who neither plant nor plow, have no assemblies and no ordinances, and live each in his cave on the mountains, giving the law to his children and his wives (*Odyssey* 9.105–115; see [[the-lotus-eaters]], [[cyclopes]] and [[themis]]). Off their harbor lies a wooded island, full of wild goats and empty of men, for the Cyclopes have no ships to settle it; “some god guided us” into its harbor through a dark and misty night (*Odyssey* 9.116–151; see [[the-island-of-goats]]). At dawn the nymphs roused the goats, and the men hunted them, nine for each of the twelve ships and ten for Odysseus, and feasted all day, looking across at the smoke of the Cyclopes and hearing their voices and their flocks (*Odyssey* 9.152–169; see [[nymphs]] and [[goats]]).

**To make trial of these men.** The next morning Odysseus called an assembly and said that he would go with his own ship and “make trial of these men, who they are, / whether they are arrogant and savage and unjust, / or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods” (*Odyssey* 9.170–176; see [[peira]] and [[xenia]]). Near the sea they found a high cave overhung with laurels, with a high fold of stones, pines and oaks around it, the home of a huge man who herded his flocks alone, “and knew lawless ways” (*Odyssey* 9.181–192; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]] and [[fold]]).

**The cave.** He went up with twelve chosen companions and a goatskin of the dark wine that Maron, priest of Apollo at Ismarus, had given him, for his spirit foreboded a savage man (*Odyssey* 9.193–215; see [[maron]], [[wine]] and [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]]). The owner was out at pasture. In the cave they found racks laden with cheeses, pens crowded with lambs and kids, and vessels swimming with whey; the companions begged him to take the cheeses and the young of the flock and sail away, “but I did not listen—it would have been far better—”, for he wished to see the man himself, and whether he would give him guest-gifts (*Odyssey* 9.216–230; see [[cheese]] and [[guest-gifts]]).

**The Cyclops comes home.** The Cyclops came home with a heavy load of dry wood to light his supper, drove his flocks in, and set in the doorway a stone that twenty-two wagons could not have heaved (see [[door-stone]]); he milked and curdled, kindled the fire, and asked the strangers who they were (*Odyssey* 9.231–255; see [[strangers-who-are-you]]). Odysseus answered that they were Achaeans driven off course from Troy, the people of Agamemnon, and suppliants, and bade him respect the gods, since Zeus is “the god of guests” (*Odyssey* 9.259–271; see [[agamemnon]] and [[supplication]]). The Cyclops answered that the Cyclopes care nothing for Zeus or the blessed gods, and asked where his ship was moored; Odysseus said that Poseidon had shattered it (*Odyssey* 9.272–286). Then the Cyclops seized two of the companions, dashed them to the ground like puppies and ate them like a mountain-bred lion. Odysseus planned to stab him where he lay, but held back, for he and his men could not have moved the stone from the doorway (*Odyssey* 9.287–306).

**The stake.** In the morning the Cyclops ate two more and drove his flocks out, setting the stone back “as one sets the lid on a quiver” (*Odyssey* 9.307–316). Odysseus cut a fathom's length from the Cyclops' club of green olive wood, had it smoothed, sharpened the point, hardened it in the fire and hid it in the dung; four companions were chosen by lot to help him, “and I counted myself in with them as the fifth” (*Odyssey* 9.316–335; see [[stake]] and [[mace]]). In the evening the Cyclops brought all his flocks into the cave and ate two more (*Odyssey* 9.336–344).

**Nobody.** Odysseus offered him the wine in an ivy-wood bowl. He drank it off, asked for more and for the stranger's name, so that he might give him a guest-gift, and called it “a branch of ambrosia and nectar”; three times Odysseus gave it, and then he told him his name (*Odyssey* 9.345–365; see [[nobody]]):

{{quote:850ac1ed-4ca5-55ee-abfe-6dea20f770d1}}

The Cyclops fell asleep, heavy with wine. Odysseus heated the stake in the ashes, and with four companions drove it into the eye and twirled it, “as when a man bores a ship's timber / with a drill”, and the eye hissed around it as iron hisses in a smith's cold water (*Odyssey* 9.371–398; see [[augers]]). The other Cyclopes came at his cry and asked whether anyone was killing him “by trickery or by force”; “Friends, Nobody is killing me by trickery, and not by force”, he answered, and they told him to pray to his father, lord Poseidon, and went away. Odysseus' heart laughed “how my name and my flawless plan had deceived him” (*Odyssey* 9.399–414; see [[metis]]).

**Under the rams.** The blind Cyclops took the stone from the door and sat in the doorway with his hands stretched out. Odysseus bound the rams together in threes with willow shoots, a man under each middle ram, and himself clung beneath the belly of the best ram of the flock. At dawn the Cyclops felt the backs of the sheep as they went out, and spoke to his ram, the last of the flock, wishing that it could tell him where Nobody was hiding (*Odyssey* 9.415–461; see [[ram]] and [[sheep]]).

**The taunts.** Out of the cave, the men drove the flocks to the ship and rowed away. When they were as far off as a shout carries, Odysseus taunted the Cyclops: Zeus and the other gods had punished him for eating strangers in his own house. The Cyclops broke off a mountain peak and hurled it, and the backwash carried the ship back to the shore (*Odyssey* 9.462–486). Odysseus pushed off, and at twice the distance, though his companions begged him not to provoke a savage man, he called out again and gave his name: “say that Odysseus, sacker of cities, blinded you, / the son of Laertes, who has his home in Ithaca” (*Odyssey* 9.487–505; see [[say-that-odysseus-blinded-you]] and [[sacker-of-cities]]).

**The curse.** The Cyclops remembered the prophecy of Telemus, son of Eurymus, the seer of the Cyclopes: he would lose his sight at the hands of Odysseus; but he had always expected a tall and handsome man, and “now one who is small and a nobody and feeble” had blinded him (*Odyssey* 9.506–516; see [[telemus]], [[thesphaton]] and [[small-and-of-no-account]]). He invited Odysseus back for good cheer and an escort from Poseidon, who alone could heal him, and Odysseus answered that he wished he could send him to the house of Hades, as surely as not even the earth-shaker would heal his eye (*Odyssey* 9.517–525). Then he prayed to his father:

{{quote:24d8dea1-9845-56cb-b017-fc735ce6ef09}}

He hurled a far greater stone, which fell behind the ship, and the wave carried the ship forward to the island (*Odyssey* 9.537–542; see [[poseidon]] and [[curses]]).

**The ram for Zeus.** On the island the men divided the Cyclops' flocks, and the companions gave Odysseus the ram as a special gift. He sacrificed it to Zeus on the shore:

{{quote:99aedea8-f663-5e99-8757-b845d320b9c9}}

They feasted until sunset, slept on the shore, and at dawn sailed on, “glad to have escaped death, though we had lost our dear companions” (*Odyssey* 9.556–566; see [[zeus]] and [[sacrifice]]).

## In the poem

**The anger of Poseidon.** The adventure is the cause of the wanderings. In the first council of the gods Zeus names it: Poseidon is angry “because of the Cyclops, whom he blinded of his eye, / godlike Polyphemus, whose power is the greatest / among all the Cyclopes”, and from that time he drives Odysseus wandering from his native land (*Odyssey* 1.68–75). Teiresias in the underworld and Athena on Ithaca give the same reason: the earth-shaker is angry “because you blinded his dear son” (*Odyssey* 11.103; 13.343; see [[poseidon]] and [[athena]]). Aristotle's outline of the Odyssey keeps the god's watch, “watched closely by Poseidon”, and leaves the Cyclops, with all the wanderings, among the episodes (*Poetics* 1455b17–23; see [[aristotle-the-story-of-the-odyssey]]).

**The curse fulfilled.** Teiresias foretells the end of the prayer in its own words: Odysseus will come home late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions, on someone else's ship, and will find bane in his house, overbearing men who devour his livelihood and woo his wife (*Odyssey* 11.114–117). So it happens: the companions are lost (see [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]), the Phaeacians carry him home asleep (see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]), and he finds the suitors in his house (see [[the-suitors]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).

**The teller's recklessness.** Odysseus tells the adventure against himself. He would not listen to his companions, “it would have been far better” (*Odyssey* 9.228), a verse that Pandarus and Hector speak in the Iliad of advice they did not take, and Hector goes on to own that he has destroyed the army “by my own recklessness” (*Iliad* 5.201, 22.103–104). On Circe's island Eurylochus will blame the death of the companions in the cave on the recklessness of Odysseus (*Odyssey* 10.435–437; see [[atasthalie]] and [[odysseus-and-circe]]). From the ship he twice provokes the Cyclops, the second time against his companions' pleas, and then names himself (*Odyssey* 9.473–505). Merry thought the hero altered for the occasion: clever in the cave, but foolhardy and thoughtless, the mantle of his wisdom fallen for once on his comrades, who advised him to make use of the Cyclops' absence and drive his flocks away, and reckless again when he taunts the Cyclops from shipboard (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix II). What saves the men in the cave is his μῆτις, “plan”, the word that the Cyclopes' μή τις, “no one”, sounds like (*Odyssey* 9.405–414; see [[metis]] and [[nobody]]).

**Hospitality.** The adventure turns on the guest's rights. Odysseus stayed to see whether the Cyclops would give him guest-gifts (*Odyssey* 9.229), came to his knees as a suppliant in the name of Zeus, “the god of guests” (*Odyssey* 9.266–271), and received as his guest-gift the promise that Nobody would be eaten last (*Odyssey* 9.369–370); he taunts the Cyclops that he “did not shrink from eating strangers / in your own house” (*Odyssey* 9.478–479), and the Cyclops, too late, offers good cheer and an escort (*Odyssey* 9.517–518; see [[xenia]], [[guest-gifts]] and [[pompe]]). The tale is told to hosts who have promised Odysseus an escort, the Phaeacians, who once lived near the Cyclopes and were harmed by them (*Odyssey* 6.4–6, 7.317–328; see [[phaeacians]], [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[aristotle-the-cyclops-and-poseidon]]).

**Remembered.** The poem recalls the adventure again and again. In the second book the Ithacan elder Aegyptius has a son who sailed with Odysseus, Antiphus, and “the savage Cyclops killed him / in his hollow cave, and made ready of him the last of his suppers” (*Odyssey* 2.17–20; see [[antiphus-son-of-aegyptius]]); the companions weep on Circe's island, remembering the Laestrygonian Antiphates and “the mighty, great-hearted Cyclops, the eater of men” (*Odyssey* 10.199–200; see [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]]); Odysseus encourages his men before Scylla with the memory of the cave, from which “by my prowess and my counsel and my mind, / we escaped” (*Odyssey* 12.208–212); in his own house he bids his heart endure, as it endured “on the day when the Cyclops, uncontrollable in his fury, ate my strong companions”, until cunning led him out of the cave (*Odyssey* 20.18–21, a plain rendering); and in his tale to Penelope he tells “all that the Cyclops did” (*Odyssey* 23.312, a plain rendering).

## Outside Homer

**A folk tale.** Merry gathered, from J. F. Lauer and Wilhelm Grimm, other forms of the story of an ogre blinded or slain by his intended victim, which he found in many countries and judged too unlike in their details to be copies of the Homeric account. In the *Historia septem sapientum* of the monk John of Haute-Seille, written between 1184 and 1212 and soon put into French verse as *Li Romans de Dolopathos*, a robber captain blinds a giant with a molten mixture poured into his eyes, hangs all day and night from the hen-roost while the giant lays about him with his club, and escapes among the sheep under a ram's skin. In a tale of the Oghuz the one-eyed Depé Ghöz, fed by lot on two men a day, is blinded with a red-hot knife, and Bissat slips past him in a ram's skin. In the third voyage of Sindbad the blinded ogre and two other giants hurl stones at the escaping rafts; Serbian and Romanian tales have the escape in a sheep's skin; and in an Estonian tale a man tells the Devil that his name is Issi, “Self”, so that the blinded Devil can only cry that Self did it. Of the tales he summarizes, Merry notes, only the Estonian keeps the device of Nobody, though Grimm quotes a similar trick from the Vorarlberg and from Hesse; in the *Dolopathos* the giant has two eyes and the robber saves none of his companions, but his night on the hen-roost has a real counterpart in Odysseus holding on to the fleece of the ram (Merry, Appendix II, “On some various forms of the legend of the blinded Cyclops”).

**Later poets.** Euripides set the adventure under Etna in Sicily in his satyr play, the *Cyclops* (*Cyclops* 106–114); for the play, see [[euripides-cyclops]], and for the Cyclops in Cicero and the Latin poets, [[cicero-polyphemus-and-the-ram]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]].
