## The story

**The meeting.** Patroclus has driven the Trojans from the ships and is killing them between the ships, the river and the wall (16.394–418). When Sarpedon sees his Lycians, “who wear their tunics without the band”, brought down at the hands of Patroclus, he rebukes them for running and leaps from his chariot to meet the man, “so that I may learn / whoever he is who is prevailing here” (16.419–425; see [[sarpedon]] and [[lycians]]). Patroclus leaps down on the other side, and the two rush at each other with screams, like two vultures fighting on a high rock (16.426–430; see [[like-vultures]]).

**The death.** Patroclus kills Sarpedon's attendant Thrasymelus (16.462–465; see [[thrasymelus]]). Sarpedon misses him and strikes the trace horse Pedasus, which falls screaming in the dust and tangles the team until Automedon cuts it loose (16.466–475; see [[automedon]]). Sarpedon misses again, and Patroclus does not:

The five verses of the fall were said in Book 13 of Asius, whom Idomeneus killed (13.389–393; see [[the-felled-tree]]). A second simile follows: he dies raging under Patroclus as a bull dies groaning under the jaws of a lion that has come upon the herd (16.487–491; see [[bull-above-the-herd]]). Dying, he calls on his cousin:

{{quote:eabe2e8c-75bb-54e4-8ae1-f8d9781a8718}}

“As he spoke, the end of death covered / his eyes and nostrils” (16.502–503; see [[the-end-of-death]]). Patroclus sets his heel on his chest and draws out the spear, and the midriff follows it: “and he drew out his soul and the point of the spear together” (16.503–505; see [[psyche]]).

**The fight over the body.** Glaucus, still in pain from the arrow Teucer shot him at the wall, prays to Apollo, who stops the pain and dries the blood (16.508–531; see [[glaucus]] and [[apollo]]). He rouses the Lycians and goes to the Trojan leaders, and to Hector: “Sarpedon lies dead, the leader of the shield-bearing Lycians, / who guarded Lycia with his judgments and his strength” (16.541–542), and the Myrmidons will strip and defile him (16.538–547). Grief seizes the Trojans, for Sarpedon “was a prop of their city, / foreigner though he was” (16.549–550), and Hector leads them on, angered for him (16.552–553; see [[hector]]). On the other side Patroclus calls on the two Ajaxes: “There lies the man who was the first to leap inside the wall of the Achaeans, / Sarpedon” (16.558–559), and he wishes to take his armor and defile him (16.559–561; see the translation's note on 16.558). Zeus stretches deadly night over the fight, “so that there might be deadly toil of battle around his dear son” (16.567–568). Men fall on both sides: Hector kills the Myrmidon Epeigeus as he takes hold of the corpse, and Glaucus kills Bathycles (16.569–600; see [[epeigeus]] and [[bathycles]]).

“Nor could even a sharp-eyed man any longer have recognized brilliant Sarpedon, / since with missiles and blood and dust / he was wrapped from his head to the tips of his feet” (16.638–640). The fighters swarm around the body like flies around the milk pails in spring (16.641–644; see [[flies-around-the-milk-pails]]). Zeus, who never turns his eyes from the battle, decides that Patroclus will not yet be killed over Sarpedon but will first drive the Trojans to the city; he puts a craven heart into Hector, who flees, “for he knew the sacred scales of Zeus”, and the Lycians flee from their king “lying in the heap of the dead” (16.644–662; see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). The Achaeans strip the bronze armor from Sarpedon's shoulders, and Patroclus gives it to his companions to carry to the ships (16.663–665).

**Sleep and Death.** Zeus tells Apollo to take Sarpedon out of range of the missiles, cleanse the dark blood from him, wash him in the river, anoint him with ambrosia, clothe him in immortal garments, and send him with Sleep and Death, the twins, to Lycia, where his kin will bury him with a tomb and a pillar, “for that is the prize of the dead” (16.666–675; see [[the-prize-of-the-dead]]). ## In the poem

**Foretold and delayed.** Zeus named Sarpedon among Patroclus' victims when he told Hera the course of the war: Hector will kill Patroclus “before Ilios, after Patroclus has destroyed many young men, / others besides, and among them my son, brilliant Sarpedon” (15.66–67; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). Twice before, Zeus kept his son alive. When Tlepolemus' spear went into his thigh in Book 5, “his father still kept ruin away”, it was not fated for Odysseus to kill him, and when his soul left him under the oak of Zeus, the North Wind breathed him back to life (5.660–698; see [[tlepolemus-and-sarpedon]]). At the wall, when Teucer's arrow struck his shield-strap, “Zeus warded off the fates / from his son”, so that he should not be beaten down at the sterns of the ships (12.400–403). In Book 5 he begged Hector not to leave him a prey for the Danaans, since he would not return home to gladden his wife and infant son (5.684–688). In Book 12 he told Glaucus that since the fates of death stand over men in thousands, which no mortal can escape, they must go and either win glory or give it to another (12.322–328; see [[sarpedons-speech]]). In Book 16 the fates are not kept off, and his last words, like his plea in Book 5, are about what the enemy will do to his body (16.498–500).

**Zeus and fate.** The scene is the Iliad's fullest discussion of what Zeus may do against fate. Zeus speaks of Sarpedon's death as μοῖρα, “fated” (16.434), and yet asks whether he should snatch him out of the battle alive (16.435–438; see [[moira]]). Hera does not deny that he could. Her objection is that a mortal “long since ordained to his portion” (16.441; see [[aisa]]) should not be set free from death, that the other gods will not approve (see [[not-all-the-gods-approve]]), and that every god with a son at Troy will want the same (16.443–449). Zeus gives way, and keeps for his son what can be given to the dead: burial at home, “the prize of the dead” (16.457 = 16.675; see [[the-prize-of-the-dead]] and [[tomb]]). The same exchange returns when Achilles chases Hector round the walls: Zeus asks the gods whether they should save him from death, and Athena answers in Hera's words (22.168–181). Both times the father of gods and men lets the man he loves die (see [[zeus]] and [[hera]]).

**Signs and honors.** Zeus' grief shows itself in the sky: “bloody raindrops”, αἱματοέσσας … ψιάδας (16.459), as at the start of the long day of battle he sent down dewdrops wet with blood, “because he meant / to hurl down to Hades many mighty heads” (11.53–55). Over the body he stretches “deadly night” (16.567). And the son of Zeus, unrecognizable under missiles, blood and dust (16.638–640), is washed, anointed with ambrosia and clothed in immortal garments by Apollo, and carried home by Sleep, whom Hera had enlisted to put Zeus to sleep, and his brother Death (14.231; see [[sleep]], [[death]] and [[ambrosia]]). Hector's body will receive like care from the gods in the last books, when Aphrodite anoints it with ambrosial oil and Apollo keeps it from disfigurement (23.184–191, 24.18–21).

**The turn toward Patroclus.** The killing of Sarpedon is the height of Patroclus' victory and the beginning of his end. Over the body Zeus considers whether Hector should kill him there and then, and chooses to let him drive the Trojans to the city first (16.644–655). The Achaeans strip Sarpedon's armor and Patroclus sends it to the ships (16.663–665), as Hector will strip Patroclus of Achilles' armor (17.125–131; see [[stripping-the-dead]] and [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]]). Sarpedon is the first of three great deaths told in the same words: “As he spoke, the end of death covered” him (16.502, 16.855 = 22.361), and the long fight over his body comes before the longer fight for Patroclus' body in Book 17 (see [[the-death-of-patroclus]] and [[fighting-over-the-body]]).

**Not recognized.** Patroclus is fighting in Achilles' armor, and the Trojans at first take him for Achilles (16.278–283). Sarpedon goes to meet him “so that I may learn / whoever he is who is prevailing here” (16.423–424). Walter Leaf reports a scholium that took the words to mean that Sarpedon knew the man was not Achilles but did not know he was Patroclus (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.423).

## Outside Homer

**The ancient critics.** Walter Leaf reports that Zenodotus removed the whole conversation of Zeus and Hera (16.431–461), on the ground that Hera had gone from Ida to Olympus in Book 15, and that Aristarchus answered that the poet often leaves events to be inferred. Zenodotus also athetized the scene of Apollo and the body (16.666–683), objecting that it was unfitting for Apollo, a god untouched by mourning, to serve a corpse, and Aristarchus seems to have been inclined to agree (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.432 and 16.666). Leaf himself thought the verses on Sleep and Death of interest for the early vase paintings that show a dead hero carried by Death and Sleep (on 16.454).

**Plato.** Socrates would strike from the poets the laments of the gods, and above all of “the greatest of the gods”, who should not be made to say “Ah, truly a man dear to me, pursued around the city, I see with my eyes; and my heart grieves” (22.168–169) or “Alas, alas, that Sarpedon, dearest of men to me, / is fated to be slain by Patroclus son of Menoetius”: if the young took such verses seriously, they would sing dirges over small sufferings (*Republic* 3.388c–d; see [[plato]] and [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]]). At 22.168 Plato's text reads περὶ ἄστυ, “around the city”, where the Iliad has περὶ τεῖχος, “around the wall”; at 16.433 it reads αἲ αἲ ἐγών, “alas, alas”, where the Iliad has ὤ μοι ἐγών.

**Europa's son.** Later tradition made Sarpedon the son of Zeus and Europa, and brought his death into tragedy. In Aeschylus' lost *Carians or Europa*, Europa tells of her three sons by Zeus, Minos, Rhadamanthys and Sarpedon, and fears for the third, now that an enemy has come with the choicest men of a whole land and boasts that he will sack the city of the Trojans: she fears that, raging with his spear, her son may do and suffer the utmost evil, “for this hope is slender, and I have stepped onto a razor's edge” (fr. 99 Nauck, from a papyrus; see [[aeschylus]] and [[europa]]).

**Virgil.** In the *Aeneid* Hercules weeps when young Pallas prays to him before facing Turnus, and Jupiter consoles his son: each man has his day; beneath the walls of Troy so many sons of gods fell, “and Sarpedon, my own offspring, fell with them”; Turnus too is called by his fates (*Aeneid* 10.464–472; see [[virgil]] and [[jupiter-and-sarpedon]]). Hera's argument that many sons of gods fight around Priam's city (16.448–449) has become Jupiter's consolation.

**Hesiod's Shield.** When Heracles meets Cycnus in the Hesiodic *Shield*, Zeus thunders and sends down bloody raindrops from the sky as a sign of war for his own bold son (*Shield* 383–385; see the Book 16 note on [[hesiod]]).
