## The story

{{quote:784f5a0e-f17d-5278-b45b-4dc956e7d56e}}

Sleep tells it. The day is fixed by the first fall of Troy: “that high-hearted son of Zeus / was sailing from Ilios, when he had sacked the city of the Trojans” (14.250–251; see [[heracles]] and [[heracles-sacks-troy]]). At Hera's command Sleep lulled the mind of Zeus, and Hera raised the winds against Heracles and carried him off to “well-peopled Cos, / far from all his friends” (14.252–256; see [[cos]]). Zeus woke in anger, “flinging the gods about the house”, and looked for Sleep above all. He would have thrown him “from the upper air into the sea, out of sight”, but Sleep fled to Night, and Zeus held back (14.256–261; see [[sleep]] and [[night]]):

{{quote:482d4510-28b0-5de5-bd01-af8beba663de}}

Zeus' own account, when he wakes on Ida in Book 15, tells the rest. Hera won over the storm winds with the help of the North Wind, sent Heracles over the barren sea with evil intent and carried him off to Cos (15.26–28; see [[boreas]]), and Zeus rescued his son from there and brought him back to Argos, though he had suffered many labors (15.29–30). For the storm Zeus hung Hera from the sky, with two anvils on her feet and an unbreakable golden bond about her hands, and hurled from the threshold any god who tried to set her free; even so the pain for “divine Heracles” did not leave his heart (15.18–25; see [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]], [[hera]] and [[threshold]]).

## In the poem

**Sleep's refusal.** Hera has offered Sleep a golden throne made by Hephaestus if he will lull Zeus (14.233–241). He answers that he could lull any other god, even the streams of Oceanus, but not Zeus unless Zeus himself bids him, and the story is his reason: “now again you bid me accomplish this other impossible thing” (14.244–262). It is an example from the past, like the stories Nestor and Phoenix tell to persuade, here told to refuse (see [[mythological-paradigm]]). Sleep never names Heracles; he is “that high-hearted son of Zeus” (14.250).

**Hera's answer.** Hera names him, and turns the example against Sleep:

{{quote:3fd83925-6d46-5315-9335-a6feba7c7ca8}}

Zeus raged then for his own son; he will not care so much for the Trojans. Then she offers a better price than the throne, one of the younger Graces, Pasithea, as his wife, and Sleep makes her swear to it by the Styx (14.267–280; see [[pasithea]] and [[graces]]).

**The second time.** The story is told twice, by the deceiver's helper and by the deceived, and each time it is about to come true again. Sleep lulls Zeus once more for Hera (14.352–360), and when Zeus wakes and sees Hector lying on the plain he reminds Hera of Heracles, Cos and the anvils, so that she may see whether love and bed will help her (15.14–33; see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]). This time he says nothing of Sleep.

## Outside Homer

[[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] tells the story with its sequel. As Heracles sailed from Troy, Hera sent grievous storms, and Zeus in his anger hung her from Olympus (see [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]]). Heracles put in at Cos; the Coans took him for a pirate and drove him off with stones, but he forced his way in by night, took the city and killed its king Eurypylus, son of Poseidon and Astypalaea. Wounded by Chalcodon, he was snatched away by Zeus, and from Cos he went by Athena's guidance to Phlegra, to fight on the side of the gods against the Giants (*Library* 2.7.1). The scholium on 14.255 found the story in Pherecydes, whom Apollodorus probably follows (Frazer on Apollodorus, *Library* 2.7.1). Apollodorus also joins it to the fall of Hephaestus: Zeus threw Hephaestus out of heaven because he came to help Hera in her bonds, when Zeus had hung her from Olympus for the storm, and Hephaestus fell on Lemnos (*Library* 1.3.5; see [[fall-of-hephaestus]]).

Walter Leaf reports that Zenodotus left Zeus' whole account out of his text (15.18–31), and that some copies added verses in which Zeus threw the anvils down at Troy (see [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]] and [[zenodotus]]).
