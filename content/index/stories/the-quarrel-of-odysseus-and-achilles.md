## The story

On the morning of his first full day among the Phaeacians, Odysseus sits at the feast in the house of Alcinous, and the blind singer Demodocus has been led in and given his lyre (*Odyssey* 8.62–70; see [[demodocus]] and [[lyre]]). When they have put away the desire for drink and food, the Muse sets him to sing:

{{quote:cc38a8f0-c398-55a5-bed9-a5e6793ade53}}

That is all that the poem tells of it. The quarrel took place at a feast of the gods, “a rich feast” (see [[rich-feast]]); the two contended with “terrible words”; Agamemnon rejoiced “in his mind”, because Apollo had told him at Pytho that the best of the Achaeans would contend, when he crossed the stone threshold of the temple to consult the oracle (see [[agamemnon]], [[apollo]] and [[pytho]]); and it was the time when the beginning of bane was rolling upon both peoples, through the counsels of Zeus (see [[the-beginning-of-evils]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). The song does not say what the two quarreled about, nor when, nor how the quarrel ended. The singer is not given a word of his own: his song is reported in the narrator's words.

## In the poem

**Sung to its hero.** The singer sings the famous deeds of men, κλέα ἀνδρῶν (*Odyssey* 8.73), as Achilles sings them to his lyre in his hut (*Iliad* 9.189; see [[song-and-fame]] and [[kleos]]), and the lay he chooses is one “whose fame had at that time reached the wide sky” (*Odyssey* 8.74). He does not know that one of its two heroes sits at the feast. Odysseus draws his purple mantle over his head and weeps, and whenever the song begins again he covers his head again; Alcinous alone notices, and turns the company to the games (*Odyssey* 8.83–103; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]] and [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]]). In the evening Odysseus praises the singer for singing “the fate of the Achaeans … as if perhaps you had been present yourself, or heard it from another”, and asks him to “change your theme” and sing of the wooden horse (*Odyssey* 8.487–498). W. W. Merry takes the theme to be changed as the quarrel, not the song of Ares and Aphrodite, and the two lays as the beginning and the end of the ten years' siege (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.492; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]]).

**The other quarrel.** The Iliad also begins with a quarrel of the best of the Achaeans, “from the time when first the two of them stood apart in strife, / the son of Atreus, lord of men, and brilliant Achilles”, and in it too the plan of Zeus was being fulfilled (*Iliad* 1.5–7; see [[quarrel-of-achilles-and-agamemnon]]). Achilles there calls himself “the best of the Achaeans”, to whom Agamemnon paid no honor (*Iliad* 1.244, 1.412), and the wrath that follows lays countless sorrows on the Achaeans (*Iliad* 1.1–2; see [[menis]]). In Demodocus' song the same lord of men, ἄναξ ἀνδρῶν Ἀγαμέμνων, rejoices at a quarrel between the best of the Achaeans, and the bane it begins falls on Trojans and Danaans alike (*Odyssey* 8.77–82).

**Strength and understanding.** In the Iliad Odysseus and Achilles disagree openly before the last battle: Achilles would lead the army out at once, and Odysseus insists that they eat first, and tells him: “you are stronger than I, and better by no small amount / with the spear, but I would surpass you in understanding / by far, since I was born first and know more” (*Iliad* 19.216–219; see [[odysseus]] and [[achilles]]). The ancient story of the song's quarrel turns on the same contrast of courage and intelligence (see Outside Homer).

**The oracle.** Apollo is the god of the oracle at Pytho, and the Homeric Hymn to Apollo gives him his own claim, “I will proclaim to humankind the unerring will of Zeus” (*Hymn* 3.132; Merry on 8.79); in the song the oracle and the “counsels of great Zeus” are joined in the same sentence (*Odyssey* 8.79–82). The song does not give the oracle's words, nor say whether Agamemnon read it rightly. Merry takes the oracle to have been given before the war, since τότε, “then”, is the time of the consultation, when the war with Troy was just beginning (on 8.81).

## Outside Homer

**The scholia and Eustathius.** The scholia tell a fuller story. When Agamemnon consulted Apollo at Delphi about the end of the war, the god answered that Ilium would be sacked when the best of the Greeks quarreled; after the death of Hector, Odysseus and Achilles disputed over their wine, Achilles praising courage and Odysseus intelligence, the one urging that Troy be taken by force, for which he was killed, the other by guile; and Agamemnon rejoiced, thinking that the oracle was being fulfilled. Eustathius tells the same story and notes that the event proved Odysseus right, since Troy was taken by subtlety and Achilles fell in fight (Merry on 8.75). Merry finds nothing to corroborate it: after Hector's death there was no room for such an event, Nitzsch points to the *Aethiopis* as showing that Odysseus was then friendly to Achilles, and Merry asks whether, after the wrath of Achilles, any quarrel of chiefs could have made Agamemnon recall the oracle with pleasure (on 8.75; see [[menis]]). The scholia on *Odyssey* 3.267 give another form of the consultation, from Demetrius of Phalerum: Menelaus went to Delphi with Odysseus and asked the god about the coming expedition to Ilium (Merry on 8.81).

**Tenedos.** Nitzsch placed the quarrel before the time of the Iliad, at the feast the Greeks held when they first landed on Tenedos in expectation of success (Merry on 8.75; see [[tenedos]]). Merry finds the most trustworthy evidence in the fragments of a satyr play of Sophocles, the *Gathering of the Achaeans* or *Diners* (on 8.75; see [[sophocles]]). He refers to it Aristotle's example of a fallacy from the accidental: if one were to say “that being invited to dinner is the greatest honor; for it was because he was not invited that Achilles conceived his wrath against the Achaeans at Tenedos; but he conceived his wrath as one dishonored, and this happened to come about through his not being invited” (*Rhetoric* 1401b16–19; see [[aristotle]]). From Plutarch he quotes the Odysseus of Sophocles' play, who, to provoke Achilles, says that his anger is not over the dinner: now that he sees the seats of Troy, he is afraid (Plutarch, *Moralia* 74a, as Merry quotes him on 8.75; see [[plutarch]]).
