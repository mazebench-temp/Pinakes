## The story

**The terms.** At the fourth coming to the springs Zeus weighs the fates of the two men, Hector's sinks, and Apollo leaves him (22.208–213; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]] and [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]). Athena tells Achilles to stand and catch his breath, and goes to Hector in the likeness of his brother Deiphobus, who says that he has come out of the wall to fight beside him: “So saying, Athena led the way by craft” (22.214–247; see [[athena]] and [[deiphobus]]). When the two men have come close, Hector speaks first:

{{quote:e6646f5d-6b74-5feb-832c-ddf8c0a16a2f}}

Achilles answers “looking at him darkly” (22.260; see [[looking-darkly]]). There are no agreements between them, as there are no trusty oaths between lions and men, nor hearts at one between wolves and lambs, and no friendship or oath before one of them has fallen and glutted Ares with blood (22.261–267; see [[no-oaths-between-lions-and-men]]). Hector must now be a spearman and a bold warrior, for “there is no escape for you any longer, but at once Pallas Athena / will bring you down by my spear; and now you will pay back all at once / the sorrows of my companions, whom you killed raging with your spear” (22.270–272; see [[achilles]]).

**The spears.** Achilles throws first. Hector sees the spear in time and sinks down, and it flies over him and sticks in the ground; Athena snatches it up and gives it back to Achilles, “unseen by Hector, shepherd of the people” (22.273–277). Hector taunts him. Achilles did not know his fate from Zeus after all, but was “a glib talker and a cheat with words, / so that in fear of you I might forget my might and my courage” (22.279–282). He will not die with a spear in his back as he runs, but in the chest as he comes straight on, if a god allows it; now let Achilles avoid his spear, “for you are their greatest bane” (22.283–288). His spear strikes the middle of Achilles' shield and glances far off, “and he stood downcast, for he had no other ash spear” (22.289–293; see [[shield]] and [[spear]]). He calls to Deiphobus of the white shield for a long spear, “but he was nowhere near him” (22.294–295).

**The recognition.**

{{quote:a9418be1-c4b4-5be4-810d-aae07352ef00}}

**The charge.** He draws his sword, “great and heavy”, and swoops like an eagle that drops through the clouds on a lamb or a hare (22.306–311; see [[sword]] and [[the-eagle-flying-high]]). Achilles rushes to meet him behind his shield, nodding his four-ridged helmet with the plumes of gold that Hephaestus set on it, and the point of his spear shines like the evening star as he looks for the place where Hector's skin “would best give way” (22.312–321; see [[helmet]], [[the-making-of-the-arms]] and [[the-evening-star]]).

{{quote:bb3354aa-fd71-5a0b-89bd-bf0e79f28404}}

**The boast and the plea.** Hector falls in the dust, and Achilles boasts over him. When Hector was stripping Patroclus, he thought that he would be safe, the fool, and gave no thought to the helper left behind at the ships; now the dogs and the birds will tear him shamefully, “but him the Achaeans will give burial rites” (22.330–336; see [[patroclus]]). Hector, speaking “feebly”, begs by Achilles' life, his knees and his parents: let him take the bronze and gold that Priam and Hecuba will give, and give the body back, so that the Trojans and their wives may give him his portion of fire (22.337–343; see [[supplication]] and [[apoina]]). Achilles calls him a dog and wishes his own fury would let him carve Hector's flesh and eat it raw. No one will keep the dogs from his head, not if they bring ten and twenty times the ransom, not if Priam bids them weigh out his body in gold, and his mother will not lay him on a bier and lament him (22.344–354; see [[dogs]]).

**The prophecy and the death.**

{{quote:06ecc1eb-d7ce-56b6-bd11-384c1400e4fc}}

Achilles draws out his spear and strips the armor, and the Achaeans run up to gaze at Hector's body and to stab it (22.367–375; see [[stripping-the-dead]]). Then Achilles ties him to his chariot and drags him toward the ships (22.395–404; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]).

## In the poem

**Patroclus' death answered.** Book 16 told the death that turned Achilles against Hector, and Book 22 tells this one in many of the same words (see [[the-death-of-patroclus]] and [[death-in-battle]]). Zeus foretold both to Hera in Book 15: Hector would kill Patroclus before Ilios, “And in anger for him brilliant Achilles will kill Hector” (15.64–68; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). He watched each fight and weighed each death, Hector's in his scales (16.644–655; 22.166–187, 22.209–213), and Apollo struck Patroclus from behind and leaves Hector (16.788–804; 22.213). Euphorbus, who wounded Patroclus first, was killed by Menelaus' spear “straight through the soft neck” (17.49), the verse of Hector's wound (22.327; see [[euphorbus]]). Hector's boast over Patroclus began “Patroclus, I suppose you said that you would ravage our city” and called him a fool (16.830–833); Achilles' begins “Hector, you thought, I suppose, when you were stripping Patroclus” and calls him a fool too (22.331–333; see [[nepios]]). Both dying men speak “feebly”, ὀλιγοδρανέων (16.843 = 22.337), both foretell the death of the man who kills them (16.851–854; 22.358–360), and the same three verses take each soul to Hades (16.855–857 = 22.361–363; see [[the-end-of-death]] and [[leaving-manhood-and-youth]]). Each victor speaks to the dead in the same verse (16.858 ≈ 22.364). Hector answered with a question, “Who knows if Achilles … / may not first be struck by my spear and lose his life?” (16.860–861). Achilles answers in the words with which he chose his own death in Book 18, “and I will accept my doom then, whenever / Zeus is willing to bring it to pass” (22.365–366 ≈ 18.115–116; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). Each victor then draws his spear from the body (16.862–863; 22.367).

**The armor.** The armor that covers Hector is Achilles' own, the immortal armor of Peleus that Patroclus wore and that Hector stripped from him and put on (17.125, 17.192–197; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]] and [[exchange-of-armor]]). The narrator names it in Hector's own words, “the fine armor I stripped from mighty Patroclus when I killed him” (17.187 ≈ 22.323). Zeus, watching him put it on, said that he had taken it “against all order”, and that he would not come home from the battle for Andromache to receive it (17.205–208). Walter Leaf finds it unclear why stripping a slain enemy should be against order, unless because the armor came from the gods (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.205). Achilles, in the new armor that Hephaestus made, finds the one place the old armor leaves bare (22.321–325).

**The terms refused.** Hector's terms are those of his challenge in Book 7. There he called Zeus to witness that the victor would strip the armor and give back the body, his own to the Trojans and their wives for burning, the other's to the Achaeans for a mound by the Hellespont (7.76–86); the last two verses of his plea to Achilles are the verses of that challenge (22.342–343 = 7.79–80; see [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]). That duel ended at nightfall in an exchange of gifts, so that men would say of the two that they fought and “then parted in friendship, reconciled” (7.299–302; see [[parted-in-friendship]]). Achilles will hear neither of agreements nor of friendship (22.261–267; see [[oath]]). Nor will he take a ransom: not even the weight of Hector's body in gold (22.351). In the last book he takes Priam's ransom and gives the body back (24.560–595; see [[apoina]]). The poem does not remind its hearers here that Hector, who promises not to defile Achilles' body (22.256), dragged Patroclus' body meaning to cut off its head and give it to the dogs (17.125–127, 18.175–177). Achilles' ancient defenders did remind them (see [[the-dragging-of-hector-defended]]).

**The gods.** Athena fights the duel at Achilles' side. Achilles knows it before he throws (22.270–271); Hector learns it only when Deiphobus is not there (22.299; see [[truth-and-deception]] and [[divine-intervention]]). The narrator says it once more when Andromache hears the wailing: “gray-eyed Athena had brought him down at the hands of Achilles” (22.446; see [[andromache]]). Apollo, who in Book 20 snatched Hector away from Achilles in a mist (20.443–444), has left him (22.213). Hector's prophecy names the same god as Achilles' killer.

**The prophecy.** Achilles' death has been foretold before, but not like this. Thetis told him that it would follow straight after Hector's (18.95–96), and Achilles remembers her saying that he would die under the wall of the Trojans by the swift arrows of Apollo (21.277–278). The horse Xanthus said that he was fated to be brought down “by a god and a man” (19.416–417). Hector names the man, Paris, and the place, the Scaean gates (22.359–360; see [[paris]], [[apollo]] and [[scaean-gates]]). The Iliad does not tell that death. In the Odyssey the shade of Agamemnon tells the shade of Achilles how he fell in Troy and how he was mourned and buried (*Odyssey* 24.36–94).

**The city.** Priam had told Hector that the people's grief for his lost sons would be shorter-lived unless Hector died too (22.52–55), and Hecuba and Andromache say what Hector was to the city: its glory, honored as a god, the one man who guarded its gates and walls (22.433–436, 22.506–507; see [[priam]], [[hecuba]] and [[astyanax]]). When Hector is dragged in the dust, the city wails as if all Ilios were burning from top to bottom (22.408–411; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).

## Outside Homer

The book was called Ἕκτορος ἀναίρεσις, “the Death of Hector” (see [[title-of-book-22]]). Plato would strike out of poetry the two verses of the soul's flight to Hades, said of Patroclus and of Hector (*Republic* 3.386c–d; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]). For Virgil's reworking of the chase and the death at the end of the *Aeneid*, see [[virgil-the-death-of-turnus]].
