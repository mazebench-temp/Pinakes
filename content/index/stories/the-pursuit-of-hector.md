## The story

When Achilles comes at him, Hector, who has waited alone before the Scaean gates and resolved to fight, loses his nerve and runs (*Iliad* 22.131–137). Achilles pursues him like a falcon after a dove, past the lookout and the windy wild fig tree, along the wagon road under the wall, to the two springs of the Scamander, one warm and one cold, and the stone troughs where the Trojan women washed their clothes in time of peace (22.138–156; see [[the-lookout-at-troy]], [[the-fig-tree]] and [[the-springs-of-the-scamander]]). They run not for a prize of the games but for Hector's life, three times around the city, while the gods look on. Zeus asks whether to save him, and when Athena objects he lets her go (22.157–187; see [[zeus]]). Whenever Hector makes for the Dardanian gates, Achilles cuts him off and turns him back toward the plain, and as in a dream the one cannot catch and the other cannot escape (22.188–201; see [[dardanian-gates]]). Apollo strengthens Hector for the last time, and Achilles shakes his head at his own men, forbidding them to throw at Hector, so that no one else may win the glory and he come second (22.202–207). At the fourth coming to the springs Zeus weighs the fates of the two men, and Hector's sinks; Apollo leaves him, and Athena, in the likeness of his brother Deiphobus, persuades him to stand (22.208–247; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]] and [[deiphobus]]). Then Achilles kills him (see [[the-death-of-hector]]).

## In the poem

**Achilles' feet.** Achilles is “swift-footed”, and the chase puts his speed to the test that Idomeneus named in *Iliad* 13, when he said that Ajax would not give ground even to Achilles in a standing fight, “but in speed no one can contend with him” (13.324–325; see [[swift-footed]] and [[achilles]]). It follows straight on another chase. At the end of Book 21 Apollo, in the likeness of Agenor, ran a little ahead of Achilles across the plain, “so that he always hoped to catch him with his own feet”, while the Trojans escaped into the city, and at the start of Book 22 he turns and asks Achilles why he pursues a god “with your swift feet” (21.599–611, 22.8; see [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]). The god could not be caught, and the man cannot be caught either until a god intervenes.

**Hector's resolve.** In the assembly after Achilles shouted at the ditch, Hector said that he would not flee from Achilles but stand face to face (18.306–308), and alone before the gates he decided again to fight (22.129–130). When Achilles comes near, he runs (22.136–137; see [[hector]] and [[pondering-two-ways]]). When at last he stands, it is because Athena has deceived him, and his first words are “I will no longer flee from you, son of Peleus” (22.250).

**Three times and a fourth.** The chase goes three times around the city and ends at the fourth coming to the springs (22.165, 22.208), the pattern of three attempts and a decisive fourth with which Patroclus' last charges were told (16.702–706, 16.784–787; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]). The circling returns in the next books: mourning Patroclus, the Myrmidons drive their horses three times around his body (23.13), and for days afterward Achilles drags Hector three times around Patroclus' tomb (24.14–18).

**The games.** The narrator sets the chase beside the games: “it was no beast for sacrifice or ox-hide / they strove to win, such as are prizes for the feet of men, / but they ran for the life of Hector, tamer of horses” (22.159–161), and they whirled around the city like racehorses rounding the turning post for a great prize, “a tripod or a woman, in honor of a man who has died” (22.162–166; see [[racehorses-at-the-turning-post]]). In the next book a woman and a tripod are the first prize of the chariot race at Patroclus' funeral games (23.262–265; see [[contests]]).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle uses the scene twice in the *Poetics*. Epic, he says, admits the irrational more than tragedy, since we do not see the person acting: “The scenes of Hector's pursuit would seem ridiculous on the stage, with the men standing still and not pursuing, and Achilles waving them back; but in epic it goes unnoticed” (*Poetics* 1460a14–17). Achilles' gesture, ὁ δὲ ἀνανεύων, “waving them back”, is the narrator's ἀνένευε, “shook his head at his men” (22.205). An impossibility, he says later, is an error, but it is correct if it makes this or another part more astonishing: “An example is the pursuit of Hector” (*Poetics* 1460b23–26; see [[aristotle]], [[alogon]] and [[adynaton]]). Plato's text of Zeus' grief has Hector chased “around the city” where Homer has “around the wall” (*Republic* 3.388c; see [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]]). Strabo, setting out the case of Demetrius of Scepsis that Homer's Ilium did not stand where the Ilium of his own day stood, argues from the chase: the city of his day could not be run around, because of the continuous ridge, but the old one could (*Geography* 13.1.37; see [[strabo-on-the-chase-around-troy]]). For Virgil's reworking of the chase in the death of Turnus, see [[virgil-the-death-of-turnus]]. For the ancient title of the book, see [[title-of-book-22]].
