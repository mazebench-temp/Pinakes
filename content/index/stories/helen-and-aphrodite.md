## The story

Aphrodite finds Helen on the high tower among the Trojan women, plucks at her robe, and speaks in the likeness of an old wool-comber who used to work fine wool for her in Lacedaemon and loved her most of all (3.383–389).

{{quote:ca5838d5-b994-51ba-8b95-184b2e7e5340}}

Helen's heart is stirred. Then she sees the goddess' beautiful neck, her lovely breasts and her flashing eyes, and is amazed (θάμβησεν), as Achilles was when he knew Athena (3.395–398; 1.199). She answers with a question: “Strange one, why do you long to deceive me like this?” Will the goddess lead her still farther on, to some city in Phrygia or Maeonia, to another mortal she loves? Is it because Menelaus has won and wants to take her home that she has come with treachery in her heart (3.399–405)?

{{quote:a597c362-d6e6-5694-adf8-d958a691e854}}

The goddess answers in anger.

{{quote:79d9e453-2269-5f6e-a419-69b8f47a58ee}}

Helen is afraid. She wraps herself in her shining robe and goes in silence, unseen by the Trojan women, “and the divinity led the way” (ἦρχε δὲ δαίμων, 3.418–420). In Alexander's house the handmaids go to their work, and Helen goes up to the high-roofed chamber. “Laughter-loving” Aphrodite herself takes a chair and sets it facing Alexander, and Helen sits down with her eyes turned away and rebukes him: “You have come back from the war. I wish you had died there, beaten by a stronger man, who was my former husband” (3.421–429). Go and challenge him again, she says, and then takes it back: better not, or he will kill you (3.430–436). Paris does not take offense. Menelaus won this time with Athena's help, he says, and another time he will win, “for we have gods on our side too” (3.438–440).

{{quote:e4a4c8f4-648d-5cde-9fe8-3b71699aad7e}}

He leads the way to the bed, and his wife follows (3.447).

## In the poem

**The goddess resisted.** Few mortals in the Iliad speak to a god as Helen does, and those who do are warriors with a spear in hand: Diomedes, who has just wounded Aphrodite, asks her whether it is not enough to deceive weak women (ἠπεροπεύεις, 5.348–351), and Achilles tells Apollo that he would punish him if he could (22.15–20). Helen has no weapon. She names the goddess' deceit (ἠπεροπεύειν, 3.399; see [[eperopeuo]]), guesses her motive, and tells her to give up Olympus and become Paris' wife or slave herself. The address δαιμονίη (“strange one”; see [[strange-one]]) is the word with which Zeus and Hector speak to their wives (1.561, 6.486). What Helen refuses is shame: sharing Paris' bed now would be “a thing for blame” (νεμεσσητόν), and the Trojan women would reproach her (3.410–412; see [[nemesis]]). Aphrodite answers with the one thing Helen cannot face, the loss of her favor. Hated by both peoples, Helen is safe only as long as the goddess loves her.

**Compulsion and consent.** The scene puts the question of Helen's guilt in its sharpest form. She goes to Paris' bed in fear of a goddess, and the narrator says that “the divinity led the way”. Her feelings in the book come from gods: Iris put in her heart “sweet longing … for her former husband, and her city, and her parents” (3.139–140), and Aphrodite sends her back to Paris. What she says in her own voice is regret and refusal. Readers have found here a Helen forced by the gods, a Helen whose own desire the goddess stands for, or both at once. The poem shows the threat and the obedience and does not settle the question. See [[who-is-to-blame]], [[eros]] and [[himeros]].

**The first night again.** Paris remembers the island of Cranae, where he first lay with Helen after carrying her off from Lacedaemon (see [[the-abduction-of-helen]] and [[cranae]]). The duel was to have ended what began there. Instead the day of the oaths ends with the beginning replayed, the same man and woman, and the same goddess bringing them together. Aphrodite had told Helen that Paris looked as if he were going to a dance, not coming from a fight (3.392–394).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Paris' invitation returns almost word for word in the mouth of Zeus, when Hera has borrowed Aphrodite's embroidered band to seduce him: “come, let us go to bed and take our pleasure in love” (14.314), “never yet has desire so overwhelmed me”, and “as I love you now, and sweet longing takes hold of me” (14.328 = 3.446). In Book 6 Helen says that the gods devised these evils, and wishes she were at least the wife of a better man (6.349–353), and in the Odyssey she remembers the “blind folly” (ἄτη) that Aphrodite gave her when she led her from her native land (Odyssey 4.261–264).

## Outside Homer

Gorgias, defending Helen, argued that if a god drove her to Troy she cannot be blamed, since a mortal cannot resist a god's will (*Encomium of Helen* 6; see [[gorgias-helen]]). In Euripides' *Trojan Women* Helen makes the same defense before Menelaus, and Hecuba answers that when Helen saw Paris, “your own mind became Cypris” (987–988; see [[euripides-trojan-women]]).
