## The story

{{quote:62440dba-bb59-5316-aa0a-b6d83385cbf7}}

The story is told in outline. There is no wedding, no wine, and no description of the fight, only its heroes, its enemies, and its result: "they destroyed them terribly". What matters to Nestor is that he was there.

## In the poem

This is the first story from the past told inside the Iliad, and it sets the pattern for Nestor's many later tales (see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]] and [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). It is a paradigm, a story told to persuade. Its logic runs:

1. those men were greater than you;
2. they took my advice;
3. so you should take it too.

The list of names is the proof. It is also a small catalogue of heroes (1.263–265), and one of its lines, the one naming Theseus, is probably a later addition. See [[theseus-line]].

The advice fails. Neither Achilles nor Agamemnon yields.

## Outside Homer

The Odyssey gives the cause: the Centaur Eurytion got drunk at Peirithous' house and outraged the host, and the Lapiths cut off his ears and nose (21.295–304). Iliad 2.740–744 dates Polypoetes' birth to the day Peirithous "punished the shaggy beasts". Later tradition set the battle at Peirithous' wedding to Hippodamia. Ovid gives Nestor his full tale at last, a long account of the battle told at a feast (*Metamorphoses* 12.182–535). The fight was carved on the west pediment of the temple of Zeus at Olympia and on the south metopes of the Parthenon.
