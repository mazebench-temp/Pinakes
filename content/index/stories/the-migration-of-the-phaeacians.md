## The story

While Odysseus sleeps on the shore, Athena goes “to the land and the city of the Phaeacian men” (*Odyssey* 6.3), and the poet tells who they are:

{{quote:7b8539c2-f7e0-504c-856e-ada97b22339b}}

**The old home.** Hypereia is named nowhere else in the Odyssey, and its only epithet, εὐρυχόρῳ, “with its wide dancing floors”, is one the poems give to many cities and lands (see [[hypereia-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[with-wide-dancing-floors]]). Its neighbors were the Cyclopes, ἀνδρῶν ὑπερηνορεόντων, “overweening men”, the word the Odyssey usually gives the suitors and the Iliad only the Trojans and Deiphobus (*Iliad* 4.176, 13.258; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.5; see [[cyclopes]] and [[overweening]]). σινέσκοντο, “used to plunder them”, is an iterative form, found only here in Homer, and the half-verse that follows, “and were the stronger in strength”, gives the reason they could oppress their Phaeacian neighbors (Merry on 6.6).

**The new city.** In ἄγε … εἷσεν, “led them away … and settled them”, the change from the imperfect to the aorist shows that the settling was the result and completion of the leading (Merry on 6.7; see [[nausithous]], [[godlike]] and [[scheria]]). Scheria lies ἑκὰς ἀνδρῶν ἀλφηστάων, “far from toiling men” (see [[toiling-men]]). Then the founder's works follow in four clauses: the wall, the houses, the temples of the gods, the division of the fields (see [[house]] and [[temple]]). The wall comes first, Merry thinks, not only because walls mark the site and size of a city, but because their old experience had taught the Phaeacians to live in a fenced city, where they could defend themselves against dangerous neighbors (on 6.9). ἐδάσσατʼ ἀρούρας is “allotted them for cultivation”, ἄρουραι being land as an inheritance, as in Andromache's fear that others will take away her orphan's lands (*Iliad* 22.489; Merry on 6.10).

**The founder's death.** Nausithous is dead when the poem comes to his city, and his son rules in his place, in the words used at Pylos, where Nestor sits on the polished stones on which his father once sat: “But he had already been overcome by his doom and gone to the house of Hades” (*Odyssey* 6.11 = 3.410). Neleus there was “a counselor equal to the gods” (*Odyssey* 3.409), and Alcinous here is the king “who knew counsels from the gods” (*Odyssey* 6.12; see [[neleus]], [[nestor]], [[alcinous]], [[who-knew-counsels]] and [[hades]]).

**The founder's family.** Nausithous was a son of Poseidon and of Periboea, the youngest daughter of Eurymedon, king of the Giants; his sons were Rhexenor and Alcinous, and Alcinous married Arete, the only child of Rhexenor (*Odyssey* 7.56–66; see [[poseidon]], [[periboea-mother-of-nausithous]], [[eurymedon-king-of-the-giants]], [[rhexenor]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). So the king who led the Phaeacians away from the Cyclopes was a son of Poseidon, the father of the Cyclops Polyphemus (*Odyssey* 1.68–73; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]), and Poseidon calls the Phaeacians “of my own line” (*Odyssey* 13.130, a plain rendering).

**The founder's prophecy.** Alcinous remembers what his father used to say (*Odyssey* 8.564–571; the renderings of *Odyssey* 8 and 13 here are plain renderings, not the translation): that Poseidon begrudged the Phaeacians because they are safe escorts of all men, and that one day he would wreck a fine ship of theirs as it came back from an escort over the misty sea, and throw a great mountain around their city; “so the old man used to say”, and the god would bring it to pass or leave it unfulfilled, as his heart pleased. Merry takes the grudge as Poseidon's jealousy of their immunity from harm, which amounted to independence of his authority and power (on 8.566). When the Phaeacians have carried Odysseus home, Poseidon turns their ship to stone within sight of the city, and Alcinous recognizes the prophecies spoken long ago by his father; he bids the people stop escorting mortals, and they make ready twelve chosen bulls for Poseidon, that he may pity them and not hide their city behind a great mountain (*Odyssey* 13.125–187; see [[phaeacians]]). The poem leaves their leaders praying around his altar.

## In the poem

**What it explains.** The backstory is told as Athena reaches the city, and the book that follows bears it out. Nausicaa tells her handmaids that no man will come to the land of the Phaeacians bringing combat, “for we are very dear to the immortals. / And we live far apart in the sea of many waves, / the farthest of men, and no other mortal mingles with us” (*Odyssey* 6.203–205; see [[nausicaa]]). The city she describes to Odysseus has the high rampart around it, a harbor on either side, the ships drawn up along the road and the place of assembly around the precinct of Poseidon (*Odyssey* 6.262–267; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]]). Odysseus, who does not know what people he has come to, asks whether they are “arrogant and savage and unjust, / or kind to strangers, with a mind that fears the gods” (*Odyssey* 6.120–121; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]] and [[xenia]]).

**The Cyclopes.** In the ninth book Odysseus tells the Phaeacians of the people they left. The Cyclopes neither plant nor plow, have no assemblies to give counsel and no laws, and live in hollow caves on the mountain peaks, each giving law to his own children and wives (*Odyssey* 9.106–115); they have no ships and no shipwrights (*Odyssey* 9.125–129). The things Nausithous gave his people in Scheria are the things the Cyclopes lack: a wall, houses and temples, fields divided for plowing (*Odyssey* 6.9–10), and, in Nausicaa's account, a place of assembly and ships (*Odyssey* 6.263–272). Alcinous names the two peoples together when he says that the gods come among the Phaeacians openly, as they do among the Cyclopes and the Giants (*Odyssey* 7.205–206; see [[near-kin-to-the-gods]]).

## Outside Homer

**Aristotle's problem.** The Homeric problems asked why Odysseus was not afraid to tell the Phaeacians, who were descended from Poseidon, that he had blinded Poseidon's son. Aristotle's answer was that he knew they were hostile to the Cyclopes, who used to plunder them and had driven them to Scheria (*Homeric Problems* fr. 396 Gigon, from the scholia on *Odyssey* 9, citing *Odyssey* 6.4–8; see [[aristotle]]).

**No place on the map.** Merry would not put the story on a map. When one tries to identify Scheria with Corcyra, “the story melts into romance”: Hypereia and Scheria are “merely topographical descriptions”, the “Highlands” and the “Coast” (on 5.34), and Hypereia, like Apeire, the land from which Nausicaa's old nurse was brought (*Odyssey* 7.8–9; see [[apeire]]), is “purely mythical”, a land of which the limits are unknown (on 7.8). For the later identification of Scheria with Corcyra, see [[scheria]].
