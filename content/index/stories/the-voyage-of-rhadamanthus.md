## The story

{{quote:f835f064-df01-5638-8e2a-e9bb9aeb6f77}}

The voyage is known only from these verses. The Phaeacians carried Rhadamanthys to Euboea to look upon Tityus, the son of Earth (ἐποψόμενον, “to visit”), went there and back without weariness, and were home “on the same day”: ἤματι τῷ αὐτῷ, “on that very day”, an expression close to the Attic use of ὁ αὐτός (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.326). Euboea is the farthest of lands, as those of the Phaeacians say who saw it on that voyage (*Odyssey* 7.321–323; see [[euboea]] and [[phaeacians]]). Γαιήιον υἱόν, “the son of Earth”, names the mother with an adjective, as Ποιάντιον υἱόν names Philoctetes the son of Poias (Merry on 7.324; *Odyssey* 3.190; see [[earth]]).

Homer says nothing else of the two travelers together. Rhadamanthys is a son of Zeus and of the daughter of Phoenix, and the brother of Minos (*Iliad* 14.321–322; see [[rhadamanthus]] and [[minos]]); in the Odyssey he dwells in the Elysian plain at the limits of the earth (*Odyssey* 4.563–564; see [[elysium]]). Tityus, “son of glorious Earth”, Odysseus sees in the underworld, lying on the ground over nine plethra while two vultures tear at his liver, because he assaulted Leto, the wife of Zeus, as she went to Pytho through Panopeus (*Odyssey* 11.576–581, a plain rendering; see [[tityus]] and [[leto]]).

## In the poem

**A boast and a promise.** Odysseus has asked to be set on his native land at dawn (*Odyssey* 7.222–225). Alcinous fixes the escort for the next day: the stranger will lie asleep while the young men row over the calm to his native land and his house, “even if it is very much farther away than Euboea” (*Odyssey* 7.317–321), and the king ends with a sea king's pride: “And you yourself will know in your mind how far the best / are my ships, and my young men at tossing up the sea with the oar-blade” (*Odyssey* 7.327–328; see [[alcinous]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). The voyage to Euboea and back in a day bears out what Athena told Odysseus on his way into the city, that the Phaeacians cross the great gulf trusting in the speed of their ships, “swift as a wing or a thought” (*Odyssey* 7.34–36), and the voyage to Ithaca bears it out again: the ship that carries him runs on so steadily that not even a falcon could keep up with her, and comes to the island as the morning star rises (*Odyssey* 13.81–95; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]).

**The farthest.** To a people who live “far apart in the sea of many waves, / the farthest of men” (*Odyssey* 6.204–205), the farthest of lands is Euboea, an island of the Aegean beside the Greek mainland (see [[scheria]]).

**No clue.** Merry finds no clue to the early form of the legend: Rhadamanthys lives in Elysium (*Odyssey* 4.564) and Tityus suffers in Hades for his outrage in Phocis (*Odyssey* 11.576). It is just possible to imagine that Scheria was not far from Elysium, so that the Phaeacian sailors were at hand to carry Rhadamanthys from there on his visit; but the object of the visit is equally obscure (Merry on 7.323; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 7.323–324).

## Outside Homer

**The cave in Euboea.** Strabo, coming to Panopeus in Phocis, where the story of Tityus was told, recalls that Homer has the Phaeacians bring Rhadamanthys to Euboea to see Tityus, the son of Earth, and adds that a cave called Elarion is shown on the island, named after Elara, the mother of Tityus, with a hero's shrine of Tityus and some honors paid to him (*Geography* 9.3.14; see [[strabo]]).

**Eustathius' guess.** Eustathius, as Merry reports him, suggested that Rhadamanthys is imagined as going to look upon Tityus either in wonder at his size, or, being just, as the story made him, to bring Tityus to his senses (Merry on 7.323).
