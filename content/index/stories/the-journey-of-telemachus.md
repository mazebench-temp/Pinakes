## The story

In the council of the gods Athena proposes to go to Ithaca, rouse Odysseus' son to call an assembly, and send him to Sparta and sandy Pylos “to learn of his dear father's homecoming” (*Odyssey* 1.88–95; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). In the likeness of Mentes she gives him the plan as counsel: a ship with twenty oarsmen, Nestor first and then Menelaus (*Odyssey* 1.279–292; see [[athena-visits-telemachus]]), and he lies awake all night planning the journey (*Odyssey* 1.443–444).

The assembly of the next day achieves nothing, and the suitors go back to his father's house (*Odyssey* 2.1–259). Athena comes to him in the likeness of Mentor and promises him a ship (*Odyssey* 2.267–295). His old nurse Eurycleia fills twelve jars with wine and sewn skins with barley meal, and he makes her swear not to tell his mother until the eleventh or twelfth day (*Odyssey* 2.348–380; see [[eurycleia]]). The goddess, now in Telemachus' own likeness, goes through the town gathering a crew and borrows a ship from Noemon, son of Phronius; at nightfall they sail, Athena sitting at the stern beside him and sending a west wind (*Odyssey* 2.382–434).

At Pylos they find the people sacrificing on the shore (*Odyssey* 3.4–5). Nestor tells what he knows of the returns, though he came home “without tidings” and knows nothing of the rest (*Odyssey* 3.184–187), and Athena leaves them in the likeness of a sea eagle, to the old man's amazement (*Odyssey* 3.371–373; see [[nestor]]). With Nestor's son Peisistratus driving (see [[peisistratus]]), Telemachus goes on by chariot to Sparta (*Odyssey* 3.481–486, 4.1–2), where Menelaus is feasting at a double wedding, and Helen knows him at once for the son of Odysseus, whom his father left newborn (*Odyssey* 4.3, 4.141–146; see [[menelaus]] and [[helen]]). Menelaus tells him what the Old Man of the Sea told him in Egypt: Odysseus is alive, weeping on an island in the halls of the nymph Calypso, with no ships or companions to bring him home (*Odyssey* 4.555–560; see [[calypso]]).

Meanwhile the suitors learn that he has gone. Antinous takes twenty men to lie in wait for him in the strait between Ithaca and Samos, at the little island of Asteris (*Odyssey* 4.660–672, 4.842–847; see [[antinous]]). Athena goes to Sparta to send him home, finds him sleepless with cares for his father, and warns him of the ambush (*Odyssey* 15.1–42). At Pylos he takes on board the seer Theoclymenus, a fugitive from Argos (*Odyssey* 15.222–286); he sails by night, lands on Ithaca at dawn, and goes to the hut of the swineherd Eumaeus, where his father, disguised as a beggar, is waiting (*Odyssey* 15.495–500, 16.1–16; see [[eumaeus]]). There Odysseus makes himself known to his son (*Odyssey* 16.186–219). The renderings of later books here are plain renderings, not the translation.

## In the poem

**Why he goes.** Athena's purpose is double: that Telemachus may hear of his father's homecoming, and “that noble fame may be his among men” (*Odyssey* 1.94–95; see [[nostos]] and [[kleos]]). The news he brings home is Menelaus' report of what Proteus saw, and he tells it to his mother in the same verses (*Odyssey* 17.142–146, where 17.143–146 = 4.557–560, the verses Athena speaks in the second council, 5.14–17).

**The two councils.** The journey fills the books between the two councils of the gods (*Odyssey* 1.26–95, 5.1–42; see [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). At the second, Athena's last plea is for the son, and Zeus bids her bring him home unharmed; then the poem leaves him at Sparta until *Odyssey* 15.

**Twenty oarsmen.** Athena bids Telemachus man his ship with twenty oarsmen (*Odyssey* 1.280), Telemachus asks the assembly for “a swift ship and twenty companions” (*Odyssey* 2.212), and Antinous asks the suitors for them in the same verse, to lie in wait for him, and chooses twenty of the best (*Odyssey* 4.669 = 2.212, 4.778).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle's one-sentence outline of the Odyssey keeps, of these books, only that the son is plotted against: “his property is being wasted by suitors and his son plotted against”; the rest, he says, is episodes (*Poetics* 1455b19–23; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).
