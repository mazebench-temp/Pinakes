## The story

**Nausicaa comes home.** While Odysseus prays in the grove of Athena, the mules carry Nausicaa to her father's house. Her brothers, “like the immortals”, unyoke them at the doorway and carry the clothing inside, and in her chamber the old Eurymedusa, a woman from Apeire whom the curving ships once brought as a prize for Alcinous, and who reared the girl, lights her fire and makes ready her supper (*Odyssey* 7.1–13; see [[nausicaa]], [[eurymedusa]], [[apeire]] and [[the-grove-of-athena]]).

**The mist and the guide.** Then Odysseus sets out for the city, and Athena pours a thick mist around him, “lest any of the great-hearted Phaeacians, meeting him, / should taunt him with words and ask him who he was” (*Odyssey* 7.14–17; see [[athena]] and [[achlys]]). As he is about to enter the city she meets him herself, “like a young girl, a maiden, carrying a water jar”, and he asks her the way to the house of Alcinous (*Odyssey* 7.18–26). She leads him, and bids him look at no one and question no one: the Phaeacians do not much put up with strangers, but trust in the speed of their ships, a gift of the earth-shaker, and “their ships are swift as a wing or a thought” (*Odyssey* 7.27–36; see [[phaeacians]] and [[xenia]]). The Phaeacians famed for ships do not see him as he goes among them, and he marvels at the harbors and the balanced ships, the places of assembly and the long walls (*Odyssey* 7.37–45; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**Arete.** At the palace the goddess bids him go in without fear, “for a bold man is better in all / deeds, even if he comes from some other place”, and find the queen first: “Arete is her name, a name with a meaning” (*Odyssey* 7.46–55; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]], [[a-bold-man-is-better]] and [[tharsos]]). She tells him the queen's descent. Poseidon begot Nausithous on Periboea, the youngest daughter of Eurymedon, who once ruled the high-hearted Giants and destroyed his reckless people; Nausithous ruled the Phaeacians and had two sons, Rhexenor and Alcinous; Apollo struck down Rhexenor, newly married and without a son, and Alcinous made his only daughter, Arete, his wife (*Odyssey* 7.56–66; see [[nausithous]], [[periboea-mother-of-nausithous]], [[eurymedon-king-of-the-giants]], [[rhexenor]] and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]). He honors her as no other woman on earth is honored, and so do her children and the people, who look on her as a god; she lacks nothing in good sense, and settles quarrels even among men (*Odyssey* 7.67–74). The goddess ends with Nausicaa's promise: “If she thinks of you in friendship in her heart, / then there is hope that you will see your friends and reach / your high-roofed house and your native land” (*Odyssey* 7.75–77). Then she leaves lovely Scheria for Marathon and wide-streeted Athens, and enters the well-built house of Erechtheus (*Odyssey* 7.78–81; see [[marathon]], [[athens]] and [[athena-and-erechtheus]]).

**The house and the garden.** Odysseus stands before the bronze threshold, and his heart ponders much, “For there was a gleam as of the sun or of the moon / throughout the high-roofed house of great-hearted Alcinous” (*Odyssey* 7.82–85). Its walls are bronze, with a coping of dark blue enamel; golden doors shut it in, on silver doorposts set in the bronze threshold; on either side stand gold and silver dogs that Hephaestus made to guard it, immortal and ageless; chairs run along the walls, where the leaders of the Phaeacians sit at their feasts, and golden young men on pedestals hold torches to light the banqueters by night; fifty slave women grind the grain and weave, for Athena gave the Phaeacian women skill at the loom, as their men have skill at sea (*Odyssey* 7.86–111; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]], [[the-gold-and-silver-dogs]], [[hephaestus]] and [[weaving]]). Outside the court lies a great orchard whose fruit never fails, winter or summer, ripened by the West Wind, with a vineyard, garden beds and two springs: “Such were the splendid gifts of the gods at the house of Alcinous” (*Odyssey* 7.112–132; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]]). He gazes at it all, then quickly steps over the threshold (*Odyssey* 7.133–135).

**The suppliant.** Inside he finds the leaders and rulers of the Phaeacians pouring the last libation of the evening to Hermes (*Odyssey* 7.136–138; see [[hermes]] and [[libation]]). Still wrapped in the mist, he goes through the hall to Arete and King Alcinous and throws his arms around the knees of the queen; the god-sent mist flows back from him, and they fall silent and marvel as they look (*Odyssey* 7.139–145; see [[supplication]] and [[thesphaton]]). Then he sits down at the hearth in the ashes beside the fire, and they all fall hushed in silence (*Odyssey* 7.153–154; see [[hearth]]). At last the old hero Echeneus tells Alcinous that it is not fitting for a stranger to sit on the ground in the ashes: let the king raise him to a chair, and bid the heralds mix wine for a libation to Zeus, “who attends on revered suppliants” (*Odyssey* 7.155–166; see [[echeneus]] and [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]]). Alcinous takes Odysseus by the hand, raises him from the hearth and seats him on a shining chair, making his son Laodamas stand up, “who sat beside him, and whom he loved most of all” (*Odyssey* 7.167–171; see [[alcinous]] and [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]]).

**The feast and the promise.** A handmaid brings water for the hands, the housekeeper sets out bread and many dainties, and Odysseus eats and drinks; the herald Pontonous mixes the wine, and they pour their libation (*Odyssey* 7.172–184; see [[water-for-the-hands]] and [[pontonous]]). Then Alcinous sends the leaders home to bed. At dawn they will call more of the elders, entertain the stranger and sacrifice to the gods, and then see to his escort, so that he may reach his native land and suffer no harm before he sets foot on it (see [[pompe]]); there he shall suffer “whatever his fate and the heavy Spinners / spun for him with their thread at his birth” (*Odyssey* 7.185–198; see [[fates]] and [[no-man-escapes-his-fate]]). But if he is one of the immortals come down from heaven, the gods are devising something else; for the gods have always appeared to the Phaeacians in plain sight, at their hecatombs and on the road, “since we are near to them, / as are the Cyclopes and the savage tribes of the Giants” (*Odyssey* 7.199–206; see [[seeing-the-gods]], [[cyclopes]] and [[giants]]).

**The belly.** Odysseus answers that he is not like the immortals but like mortal men, and in sorrows could liken himself to those who bear the most misery. But let them leave him to his supper, “for there is nothing more shameless than the hateful belly”, which bids him eat and drink and forget all he has suffered; and at dawn let them set him on his native land: “let life leave me, once I have seen / my wealth and my slaves and my great high-roofed house” (*Odyssey* 7.207–225; see [[gaster]]). They all approve, pour their libations and go home to bed (*Odyssey* 7.226–229).

**Arete's question.** Odysseus is left in the hall with Arete and godlike Alcinous while the handmaids clear away the gear of the feast. The queen knows the mantle and the tunic he is wearing, for she made them herself with her handmaids, and she speaks first: “Who are you among men, and from where? Who gave you these clothes?” (*Odyssey* 7.230–239; see [[who-are-you-among-men]] and [[clothing]]). He answers the second question only. His sorrows are hard to tell from beginning to end; but he tells of Ogygia, where the wily Calypso lives, the daughter of Atlas (see [[wily]]); of the ship that Zeus struck with his thunderbolt, and the keel that carried him for nine days to her island; of seven years with the goddess, who offered to make him immortal and ageless; of the raft on which she sent him in the eighth year, the seventeen days at sea and the storm that Poseidon roused; of his swim to the river and his sleep among the leaves; and of Nausicaa, who “did not fall short of good sense”, gave him food and wine, bathed him in the river and gave him these clothes (*Odyssey* 7.240–297; see [[ogygia]], [[calypso]], [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]], [[immortality]], [[the-river-of-scheria]] and [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]]).

**The offer.** Alcinous finds one fault in his daughter: she did not bring the stranger home with her handmaids, though it was to her first that he came as a suppliant. Odysseus takes the blame on himself: she bade him follow, but he would not, out of fear and shame, “for we tribes of men on the earth are quick to anger” (*Odyssey* 7.298–307; see [[quick-to-anger]]). The king is not one to be angry for no reason, “what is due is better in all things” (*Odyssey* 7.309–310; see [[what-is-due-is-better]]), and he wishes that such a man, thinking as he does, might have his daughter and be called his son-in-law, staying there with a house and possessions; but no Phaeacian will keep him against his will. He fixes the escort for the next day: Odysseus will lie asleep while the young men row over the calm to his native land, even if it is much farther than Euboea, the farthest of lands, where they once carried fair-haired Rhadamanthys to visit Tityus and came back the same day (*Odyssey* 7.308–328; see [[nausicaas-wish]], [[euboea]] and [[the-voyage-of-rhadamanthus]]). Odysseus is glad, and prays: “Father Zeus, would that he might accomplish all that he has said, / Alcinous!” Then the king's fame would be unquenchable, and Odysseus would reach his native land (*Odyssey* 7.329–333; see [[kleos]] and [[prayer]]).

**The bed.** White-armed Arete bids the handmaids set a bed under the colonnade with fine purple blankets, rugs and woolly cloaks. They go out from the hall with torches in their hands, spread the bed in haste and urge him: “Rise and go to bed, stranger; your bed has been made.” It seems welcome to him to lie down, and he sleeps there on a corded bed beneath the loud-resounding colonnade, while Alcinous lies down in the inner part of the high house, and the queen beside him (*Odyssey* 7.334–347; see [[beds-beneath-the-colonnade]], [[bed]] and [[handmaids]]).

## In the poem

**Nausicaa's directions.** The book carries out what Nausicaa told Odysseus at the end of the sixth: to wait in the grove until she had reached home, then to ask for her father's house, “It is easy to know, and even a child could lead you”, to go quickly through the hall, pass by the king and throw his arms around the knees of her mother (*Odyssey* 6.295–315). She reaches home first (*Odyssey* 7.1–13), a girl leads him to the house (*Odyssey* 7.18–20), and he passes the king and clasps the queen's knees (*Odyssey* 7.139–142). The goddess who leads him repeats the girl's promise in the girl's own verses, with “high-roofed house” for “well-built house” (*Odyssey* 7.75–77, after 6.313–315), and Alcinous promises the escort in his daughter's words, “rejoicing, and quickly, even if he is from very far away” (*Odyssey* 7.194, as 6.312; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.194; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).

**Unseen, then seen.** The mist spares him the question that a Phaeacian in the street might have put to him (*Odyssey* 7.17), and the goddess bids him ask no one himself (*Odyssey* 7.31). It lifts only at the queen's knees, so that the Phaeacians see him first as her suppliant (*Odyssey* 7.142–145). The question comes at the end of the evening, from the queen herself: “Who are you among men, and from where?” (*Odyssey* 7.238). In αὐτή … πρῶτον Merry hears the queen taking the initiative, “I will begin (the conversation) by asking thee this” (on 7.237). Odysseus answers only her second question, and the Phaeacians do not learn his name until the end of the next day's feast, when Alcinous asks for it again (*Odyssey* 8.548–556) and he gives it at the beginning of his great tale (*Odyssey* 9.16–20; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).

**God or mortal.** Alcinous wonders whether the stranger is a god (*Odyssey* 7.199–206), as Odysseus wondered whether Nausicaa was a goddess (*Odyssey* 6.149–152); Zeus had said that the Phaeacians would honor him “with all their hearts like a god” (*Odyssey* 5.36). It was the stranger's majestic looks and his sudden appearance among them, Merry thinks, that put the thought into the king's mind (on 7.200, citing *Odyssey* 6.229 and following; see [[divine-epiphany]]). Odysseus denies it, and his answer turns from his sorrows to the needs of the belly (*Odyssey* 7.208–221).

**His story.** Odysseus told Nausicaa only that he had been twenty days at sea from Ogygia (*Odyssey* 6.170–172); to the queen he gives his first long account of himself (*Odyssey* 7.241–297). It retells the fifth book in the first person, often in its own verses (*Odyssey* 7.250 = 5.132, 7.257 = 5.136, 7.266 = 5.268, 7.268 = 5.279, 7.282 = 5.443; see [[retelling]]), with the wreck that brought him to Ogygia before it and the meeting with Nausicaa after it. It also stands at the two ends of the tale of his wanderings: one of its verses comes again in the opening of that tale, “my sorrows, since the heavenly gods have given me many” (*Odyssey* 7.242 = 9.15), and the tale ends with the nine days' drift to Ogygia in the verses he speaks here (*Odyssey* 12.447–449, after 7.253–255), breaking off because he told the rest to the king and his wife the day before (*Odyssey* 12.450–453; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). The scholia report that eight verses here were athetized because the story is told later (Merry's critical note on 7.251–258; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 7.251–258).

**The clothes.** The clothes that Nausicaa's handmaids laid beside him at the river, “a mantle and a tunic” (*Odyssey* 6.214), are the queen's own work, and they give him away to her (*Odyssey* 7.234–235). The thread runs on: among the gifts of the next day Arete herself lays a fine mantle and tunic in his chest (*Odyssey* 8.438–441), and when he sails she sends slave women with him to the ship, one of them carrying a mantle and a tunic (*Odyssey* 13.66–67; see [[clothing]], [[tunic]] and [[cloak]]).

**The promise kept.** The day after this one Alcinous does what he said: he calls the Phaeacians to the place of assembly, has a ship and fifty-two young men chosen for the escort, and entertains the stranger with feasting, songs and games (*Odyssey* 8.1–384; see [[the-quarrel-of-odysseus-and-achilles]], [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]]); the leaders and the king give him gifts (*Odyssey* 8.385–448), and at the evening feast the stranger's tears at the song of the wooden horse bring the question of his name (*Odyssey* 8.469–586; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]). The voyage waits one day more, for in a pause of his tale the queen asks the leaders not to hurry him away or stint their gifts, and the king asks him to stay until the morrow (*Odyssey* 11.336–353). Then the promise is kept in the words in which it was given. Pontonous mixes the wine again, for a prayer to Zeus and a libation (*Odyssey* 13.49–56, as 7.179–183); Odysseus puts a cup in Arete's hands and takes leave of her, and steps over the threshold (*Odyssey* 13.56–63, as 7.135); he lies down in the ship, the young men toss up the sea with the oar-blade, and sleep falls on him (*Odyssey* 13.73–80, after 7.318–319 and 7.328); and they set him on the shore of Ithaca still overcome by sleep (*Odyssey* 13.116–119, as 7.318; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]).

**One evening.** The book fills the evening and night after the meeting with Nausicaa (the structure file's night of Day 32). It opens with Odysseus at prayer in the grove, “So he prayed there, much-enduring brilliant Odysseus” (*Odyssey* 7.1), a verse made on the first verse of the sixth book, and it closes with that verse itself, “So he lay sleeping there, much-enduring brilliant Odysseus” (*Odyssey* 7.344 = 6.1).

## Outside Homer

**Virgil.** Aeneas comes to Carthage as Odysseus comes to the Phaeacians. His mother meets him and Achates in the wood with the face and dress of a girl, a Spartan huntress (*Aeneid* 1.312–320), as Athena meets Odysseus in the likeness of a girl with a water jar. As they walk on, Venus walls them in a dark mist and pours a thick cloak of cloud around them, so that no one may see or touch them, delay them, or ask why they have come (*Aeneid* 1.411–414), an imitation, Merry notes, of Athena's mist (on 7.15); and Aeneas marvels at the city, its gates, its din and its paved streets (*Aeneid* 1.421–422). His tale to Dido begins *Infandum, regina, iubes renovare dolorem*, “Unspeakable, queen, is the grief you bid me renew” (*Aeneid* 2.3), which Merry compares with Odysseus' first words to Arete, “It is hard, queen, to tell from beginning to end / my sorrows” (*Odyssey* 7.241–242; Merry on 7.241; see [[virgil]]).
