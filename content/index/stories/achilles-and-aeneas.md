## The story

**Apollo's errand.** The gods have gone down to the war, and Achilles longs above all to meet Hector, “But Apollo, who rouses armies, roused Aeneas straight / to face the son of Peleus” (20.75–80; see [[apollo]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). In the likeness of Priam's son Lycaon the god asks Aeneas where the threats have gone that he made to the Trojan kings over his wine (20.81–85; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). Aeneas is unwilling. Achilles once put him to flight from Ida, when he came upon his cattle and sacked Lyrnessus and Pedasus, and only Zeus saved him; a god is always at Achilles' side, and even without one his spear flies straight (20.87–102; see [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]]). Apollo answers that Aeneas' mother is the greater goddess, a daughter of Zeus, while Achilles' is the daughter of the Old Man of the Sea, and breathes great might into him (20.104–111; see [[aphrodite]] and [[thetis]]).

**Hera.** Hera sees him go and calls Poseidon and Athena: they must turn him back, or one of them must stand by Achilles, “so that he may know that the best of the immortals love him” (20.112–131; see [[hera]]). Poseidon will not drive the gods into strife. He leads the gods of the Achaean side to the wall of Heracles, and the gods of the Trojans sit on the brows of Callicolone; neither side will begin (20.132–155; see [[poseidon]], [[the-wall-of-heracles]] and [[callicolone]]).

**The meeting.** The plain fills with men and horses, “And two men, by far the best, / came together between the two armies, eager to fight, / Aeneas, son of Anchises, and brilliant Achilles” (20.158–160). Aeneas strides out first, threatening, tossing his heavy helmet, his shield before his chest, shaking his bronze spear. Achilles comes against him like a lion that a whole people has gathered to kill: struck by a spear, it lashes its ribs with its tail and rushes straight on, to kill or to perish (20.161–175; see [[like-a-lion]] and [[wounded-lion]]).

**Achilles' taunt.** Achilles speaks first:

{{quote:f7e2cbe5-7e82-5be2-a10f-d4172b8347c5}}

Once already, he says, he put Aeneas to flight. He drove him from the cattle down the mountains of Ida, and Aeneas never turned around; he escaped to Lyrnessus, and Achilles sacked it and led its women away. The gods saved him then, but they will not save him now. Let him go back into the crowd before he suffers harm: “a fool learns a thing once it is done” (20.187–198; see [[achilles]] and [[a-fool-learns-once-it-is-done]]).

**Aeneas' answer.** Aeneas will not be frightened with words, as if he were a child; he too knows how to speak taunts (20.200–202). Each knows the other's parents only by report, “hearing the tales mortal men have told of old”: Achilles is the son of Peleus and Thetis, Aeneas of Anchises and Aphrodite, and one pair of them will weep for a son today (20.203–212). Then he tells his lineage, from Zeus and Dardanus through Erichthonius and Tros to the two branches of Tros' house, Priam's and his own (20.213–241; see [[dardanus]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]). “But it is Zeus who increases and diminishes the prowess of men” (20.242; see [[zeus-increases-and-diminishes]]). There are more insults on both sides than a ship of a hundred benches could carry, and the tongue of mortals turns every way; whatever word a man speaks, such he will hear back (20.246–250; see [[oneidos]], [[the-tongue-of-mortals]] and [[such-will-you-hear-back]]). Let them not quarrel like women in the street, but “taste of each other with our bronze-fitted spears” (20.251–258; see [[women-quarreling-in-the-street]]).

**Spears.** Aeneas' spear strikes the shield of Hephaestus, and the shield groans. Achilles holds it away from him in fear, “the fool”, not knowing “that the glorious gifts of the gods are not easy / for mortal men to master”; the spear drives through two of the five layers and is stopped in the gold (20.259–272; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]]). Achilles' spear strikes Aeneas' shield at the rim, where the bronze and the hide are thinnest, and goes through it. Aeneas crouches and holds the shield up away from him, and the spear passes over his back and stands in the ground; grief is poured over his eyes (20.273–283; see [[the-pelian-ash]]).

**The sword and the stone.** Achilles draws his sword and springs at him, shouting terribly, and Aeneas takes up a stone “which two men could not carry, / such as mortals are now” (20.283–287; see [[stone-throwing]] and [[men-of-today]]). He would have struck Achilles' helmet or shield with it, and Achilles would have killed him with the sword, “had not Poseidon the earth-shaker been quick to see it” (20.288–291; see [[would-have-had-not]]).

**Poseidon.** Poseidon speaks among the gods. Aeneas will go down to Hades because he obeyed Apollo, though he is not to blame and always gives the gods pleasing gifts. Let them lead him out of death, lest Zeus be angry: it is fated that he escape, so that the lineage of Dardanus may not perish, for Zeus has come to hate the lineage of Priam, and Aeneas and his children's children shall be lords over the Trojans (20.292–308; see [[the-rule-of-aeneas]]). Hera leaves it to him, since she and Athena have sworn never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans (20.309–317).

**The rescue.** Poseidon goes through the battle to where the two men stand (20.318–320):

## In the poem

**The first fight of the return.** Achilles has come back to the war for Hector (20.75–78; see [[the-fourth-day-of-battle]]). The first man he meets is the Trojan whom the seer Helenus named with Hector as the best “both in fighting and in counsel” (6.77–79). Walter Leaf notes that the combat is introduced as if it were a chance meeting, as though Apollo's errand had not prepared it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.158). It ends with no one wounded. Achilles' killing begins when Aeneas is gone (20.381–503; see [[the-aristeia]]).

**Glaucus and Diomedes.** The meeting is told in the verses of another. Glaucus and Diomedes too “came together between the two armies, eager to fight”, and “when they had come close, advancing against each other”, one spoke first (20.159 = 6.120; 20.176–177 ≈ 6.121–122). There too a man answers with his lineage, in the same words: “But if you wish to learn this too, so that you may know well / our lineage—and many men know it—” (20.213–214 = 6.150–151), and “This is the lineage and the blood I claim” (20.241 = 6.211; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]). That meeting ended in guest-friendship and an exchange of armor; this one ends in spears and a god's rescue.

**Iliad 5 turned around.** In Book 5 Aeneas stood over Pandarus' body like a lion, “shouting terribly”, and Diomedes took up against him a stone that two men could not carry, which crushed his hip (5.299–310). Here Achilles is the lion, Achilles shouts terribly, and Aeneas takes up the stone in the same three verses (20.285–287 = 5.302–304). Both times a god carries him out of the fight: Aphrodite and then Apollo in Book 5, Poseidon here (5.311–346; see [[the-phantom-of-aeneas]] and [[aeneas]]).

**Hector after Aeneas.** Later in the book the meeting is played again with Hector. He answers Achilles' threat in the verses with which Aeneas began his answer (20.431–433 = 20.200–202), and when Achilles springs at him Apollo snatches him away and hides him in thick mist (20.428–444; see [[hector]]). Both Trojans escape Achilles in this book. Aeneas, Poseidon says, will outlive him (20.337–339); Hector meets him again in *Iliad* 22 and dies.

**Why Poseidon.** Poseidon is a god of the Achaean side (20.33–34), and it was he who proposed that the gods sit apart and leave the war to men (20.133–143). Leaf finds his speech and his action glaringly inconsistent with his attitude elsewhere in the Iliad: Aeneas should naturally have been saved by Apollo, who urged him on, or by his mother, as in Book 5. He suggests that the episode was composed not only for the glory of Aeneas but perhaps to explain some cult of Poseidon among families who claimed descent from him (on 20.293; see [[poseidon]]). In the poem Poseidon gives his own reasons, Aeneas' innocence, his gifts and his fate (20.297–308). Poseidon's grievance against Troy began with Laomedon, who cheated him of his wage for building its wall (21.441–460; see [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]]), and Aeneas descends from Assaracus, not from Laomedon (20.231–240; see [[laomedon]] and [[assaracus]]).

**The critics.** Aristarchus athetized seven passages of the episode: Hera's words on the thread of fate (20.125–128); Achilles' taunt on the honor of Priam (20.180–186); the end of his speech, taken from Menelaus' words to Euphorbus (20.195–198 = 17.30–32); the lines on the two men's parents (20.205–209); the women in the street (20.251–255); the five layers of the shield (20.269–272); and Poseidon's drawing of the spear out of the shield (20.322–324; see [[aristarchus]] and the translation's notes on these lines). Leaf takes the genealogy for “the kernel of this ‘Aeneid’”, to which the rest of the speech was written up: its repeated refusals to waste time on words, he argues, can be excused only by a long digression like the genealogy (on 20.215).

## Outside Homer

Virgil's Neptune, consoling Venus, recalls the day when Achilles was driving the Trojans against their walls and choking the rivers with the dead: “Pelidae tunc ego forti / congressum Aenean nec dis nec viribus aequis / nube cava rapui”, “then, when Aeneas met the brave son of Peleus with neither gods nor strength equal to his, I snatched him away in a hollow cloud”, though he longed to overthrow the walls of perjured Troy that his own hands had built (*Aeneid* 5.803–811; see [[virgil-the-house-of-aeneas]] and [[virgil]]). Strabo quotes Achilles' reminder of the flight from Ida to Lyrnessus (20.188–192) in his account of Demetrius of Scepsis, who held that Scepsis had been the royal seat of Aeneas (*Geography* 13.1.53; see [[strabo-on-the-rule-of-aeneas]] and [[the-rule-of-aeneas]]).
