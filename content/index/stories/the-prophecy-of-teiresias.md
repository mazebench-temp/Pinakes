## The story

**The seer.** The soul of Theban Teiresias came to the pit “holding a golden scepter”, knew Odysseus, and asked why he had left the light of the sun to see the dead and that joyless place: “But draw back from the pit, and hold away your sharp sword, / so that I may drink of the blood and tell you truly” (*Odyssey* 11.90–96; see [[teiresias]] and [[odysseus-in-the-land-of-the-dead]]). Odysseus sheathed his sword, and when the seer had drunk the dark blood he spoke:

{{quote:9118b837-8cdc-5363-8f42-7e709f46b440}}

**The cattle of the Sun.** “But even so you may still come home, though suffering evils, / if you will restrain your own heart and your companions'”, when they brought the ship to the island of Thrinacia and found the cattle and sheep of the Sun grazing there. If they left them unharmed they might still reach Ithaca; if they harmed them, he foretold destruction for the ship and the companions, and Odysseus, if he escaped, would “come home late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions, / on someone else's ship, and find bane in your house”: overbearing men devouring his substance and wooing his wife (*Odyssey* 11.104–117; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]], [[helios]] and [[the-suitors]]). “Yet you will surely take vengeance for their violence when you come” (*Odyssey* 11.118).

## In the poem

**What Circe promised.** Circe sent Odysseus to the dead to hear this: the seer, she said, “will tell you the way and the measures of your path / and your homecoming, how you shall go over the fish-filled sea” (*Odyssey* 10.538–540; see [[odysseus-and-circe]]). He tells instead what will happen, and how the homecoming may be lost; the way past the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis Circe herself gives when the ship comes back to Aeaea, and she repeats the warning about the cattle in five of Teiresias' verses (*Odyssey* 12.37–141; 12.137–141 = 11.110–114).

**The curse fulfilled.** The seer's words about the homecoming are the Cyclops' curse: Polyphemus prayed to his father that Odysseus might “come late and in evil plight, having lost all his companions, / on someone else's ship, and find bane in his house” (*Odyssey* 9.534–535), and Teiresias puts the same two verses in the future (*Odyssey* 11.114–115; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). Poseidon's anger, which he names first, is the anger the gods speak of at their first council (*Odyssey* 1.68–79). The warning about the cattle comes back on Thrinacia, where Odysseus, hearing the lowing of the herds, remembers “the word / of the blind seer, Theban Teiresias, / and of Circe of Aeaea” and tells his companions “the prophecies of Teiresias / and of Circe” (*Odyssey* 12.266–275); the companions kill the cattle, and the ship and all of them are lost (see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). The rest follows as the seer said: Odysseus comes home alone on a Phaeacian ship, finds the suitors in his house, and kills them by guile (see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]).

**Told to Penelope.** On the night of his return Odysseus tells Penelope that there is still a measureless labor before him, “for so the soul of Teiresias prophesied to me / on the day when I went down into the house of Hades” (*Odyssey* 23.248–253, a plain rendering). At her wish he tells her the journey inland and the gentle death in the seer's verses, turned to the first person, adding that the seer bade him go to very many cities of men with the oar in his hands (*Odyssey* 23.264–284, Greek only); and Penelope answers that if the gods will bring about a better old age, there is hope that he will escape from his evils (*Odyssey* 23.286–287, a plain rendering). The poem ends before the journey is made.

## Outside Homer

**The meaning of the journey.** Merry reads the commands as serious ones: as the wrath of Poseidon was the prime cause of Odysseus' trials, a last reconciliation with him must come before the peaceful close of his life; in the most unlikely place, among a people who do not know the sea or the name of its god, he is to publish that name and sacrifice to him, leaving his oar as a memorial, and afterward the sea will do him no more harm (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.119). He accepts Eustathius' account of its purpose, that Poseidon might be honored as a god of the mainland, and reports that the ancients took the men who do not know the sea to be the Epirots or Thesprotians, a description that fits them very badly, since they have a long coast (on 11.122, 11.134). For a people that knows no salt he quotes Sallust on the Numidians, who lived mostly on milk and the flesh of wild beasts and sought neither salt nor any other relish (*Jugurtha* 89; Merry on 11.122). Sophocles, as Merry reports, copied the winnowing fan in his *Odysseus Struck by the Spine*, where the oar is “an implement that eats the chaff” (on 11.128; see [[odysseus-wounded]]).

**The death from the sea.** Interpreters, Eustathius among them, have taken ἐξ ἁλός as “away from the sea”, and the translation follows them; Ptolemy of Ascalon read ἔξαλος, which the scholia explain as “of the mainland and not of the sea”. Merry argues that the natural sense is “out of the sea”, and that the verses allude to the later story of Odysseus' death at the hands of Telegonus, whose spear was tipped with the spine of a sea fish; for him the whole passage is a later addition (on 11.134; see the translation's note on 11.134–137 and [[telegonus-kills-odysseus]]).
