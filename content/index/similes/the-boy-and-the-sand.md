## The simile

{{quote:c69a3dcb-7535-581e-b728-2a746ef835d1}}

ἔρειπε δὲ τεῖχος Ἀχαιῶν / ῥεῖα μάλʼ, ὡς ὅτε τις ψάμαθον πάϊς ἄγχι θαλάσσης, / ὅς τʼ ἐπεὶ οὖν ποιήσῃ ἀθύρματα νηπιέῃσιν / ἂψ αὖτις συνέχευε ποσὶν καὶ χερσὶν ἀθύρων: “and he threw down the wall of the Achaeans very easily, as when a boy beside the sea throws down the sand, who, when he has made playthings in his childishness, scatters them again with his feet and hands in play” (15.361–364).

ψάμαθος is sand, the sand of the shore. ἀθύρματα are playthings, from ἀθύρω, “play”, which Homer has only here; in the Odyssey the word is used of the trinkets that Phoenician traders bring in their ship (*Odyssey* 15.416). νηπιέη is childishness, the condition of a νήπιος, a little child: Phoenix uses the word of the infant Achilles who spat out his wine on Phoenix's tunic (9.490–491), and the narrator uses it of Polydorus, the youngest son of Priam, who in his childishness showed off his speed of foot among the champions until Achilles killed him (20.407–412). συνέχευε, “pours together, confounds”, is the boy's verb for knocking his work down, and the tenor gives the same verb to the god (σύγχεας, 15.366).

The tenor: ὥς ῥα σὺ ἤϊε Φοῖβε πολὺν κάματον καὶ ὀϊζὺν / σύγχεας Ἀργείων, αὐτοῖσι δὲ φύζαν ἐνῶρσας, “so you, archer Phoebus, scattered the long labor and misery of the Argives, and roused panic among them” (15.365–366). The narrator speaks to Apollo in the second person, as he speaks to Menelaus and Patroclus (see [[apostrophe]]), and calls him ἤϊε, a title of uncertain meaning, rendered “archer” (see the translation's note on 15.365 and [[phoebus]]). The labor is the building of the wall, which the Achaeans raised in a single day in Book 7 (7.433–441, 7.465; see [[the-achaean-wall]]).

The point of likeness is ease: ῥεῖα μάλα, “very easily”, as Apollo kicked down the banks of the ditch “easily” a few verses before (15.356; see [[the-cast-of-a-spear]]). The boy makes his toys and knocks them down for the pleasure of it; nothing he does with the sand matters to him. The likeness also measures what the god destroys. The wall that cost the army its toil and misery, κάματον καὶ ὀϊζύν, is to the god what a child's sand is to a child. The simile looks back to Book 7, where the gods watched the Achaeans build it and Poseidon complained that its fame would spread as far as the dawn (7.442–453), and forward to its end.

## In context

Apollo has bridged the ditch for the Trojan chariots, and he goes before them holding the aegis (15.352–361). After the simile the Achaeans halt beside the ships and pray with their hands lifted to the gods, and Nestor prays to Zeus to remember the thigh-pieces burned to him in Argos; Zeus thunders, and the Trojans, hearing it, spring on the Argives more fiercely and come down over the wall like a wave over the side of a ship (15.367–384; see [[the-wave-over-the-ships-side]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The wall ends in sand. In Book 7 Zeus promised Poseidon that when the Achaeans had sailed home he could break the wall, pour it all into the sea, and cover the great beach again with sand (7.459–463). In Book 12 the narrator tells how it was done: Poseidon and Apollo turned the rivers of Ida against it, Apollo turning their mouths together for nine days while Zeus rained, and Poseidon swept the foundations into the waves, made the shore smooth and covered the great beach again with sand (12.17–33). The boy's sand on the shore is the wall's last state.

Other children of the Iliad's similes are smaller and more helpless: a sleeping child whose mother brushes away a fly (4.130–131; see [[mother-and-fly]]), a child who runs back under its mother (8.271; see [[like-a-child-under-its-mother]]), and a little girl who tugs at her mother's dress to be picked up (16.7–11). This one is not helpless. He plays, and his play is a destruction (see [[childhood]]).
