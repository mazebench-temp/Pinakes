## The simile

{{quote:626f5f0a-4a31-5fab-904a-4c0e2677647e}}

οἵη δʼ ἐκ νεφέων ἐρεβεννὴ φαίνεται ἀὴρ / καύματος ἐξ ἀνέμοιο δυσαέος ὀρνυμένοιο: “as a murky mist appears out of the clouds when after heat a stormy wind arises”. ἐρεβεννός, “dark as Erebus”, is a word for night, the night that covers the eyes of the dying (5.659) or of a woman who faints (22.466). ἀήρ in Homer is not the clear air but haze and mist, the thick air that hides things: a god hides a man in it (3.381), Ares rested his spear and horses in it (5.356), and Hera pours it around her horses (5.776). καῦμα, “heat”, occurs only here in Homer, and δυσαής, “ill-blowing, stormy”, is said of the West Wind (23.200). ἐκ may be “after” or “because of”: the mist appears after the heat, or out of it, when a hard wind rises.

What weather is meant has been disputed. It may be the dark cloud of a storm building up after a hot day, or a column of dust or darkness raised by a hot wind, or a dark squall driven across the sky. The Greek allows each. What is clear is a dark shape that rises out of a heavy sky as a wind gets up.

The tenor: τοῖος Τυδεΐδῃ Διομήδεϊ χάλκεος Ἄρης / φαίνεθʼ ὁμοῦ νεφέεσσιν ἰὼν εἰς οὐρανὸν εὐρύν, “so to Diomedes, son of Tydeus, bronze Ares appeared as he went up with the clouds into the wide sky” (5.866–867). The verb of the simile, φαίνεται, “appears”, is repeated in the tenor, and the dative says to whom: to Diomedes, who can see gods since Athena took the mist from his eyes (5.127–128). The god who sat resting “in a mist” (ἠέρι) at the left of the battle (5.355–356) leaves it as a dark mist going up into the clouds.

## In context

The simile follows the bellow of the wounded god (5.859–863; see [[ten-thousand-men]]). It is the last sight of Ares on the battlefield in the book. “Swiftly he came to the seat of the gods, steep Olympus, and sat down beside Zeus, son of Cronus, grieving in his heart, and showed him the immortal blood flowing down from the wound” (5.868–870; see [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]). Aphrodite, wounded earlier by the same man, went up to Olympus in Ares' chariot with Iris at the reins (5.363–369). Ares goes up alone, like weather.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The gods' comings and goings are often given images of weather. Apollo comes down from Olympus “like the night” (1.47; see [[like-night]]), Thetis rises from the sea “like a mist” (1.359; see [[like-a-mist]]), and Athena comes down like a shooting star (4.75–79; see [[shooting-star]]). Iris flies down like snow or hail under the blast of the North Wind (15.170–172), and Athena, wrapped in a purple cloud, comes like the rainbow that Zeus stretches from heaven as a portent of war or of cold storm (17.547–552). Mist also hides men from the fighting. Aphrodite hides Paris in it, and Apollo hides Hector and Agenor (3.381, 20.444, 21.597). When a mist covers the fight over Patroclus, Ajax prays to Zeus to make the sky clear: “destroy us in the light, since that is your pleasure” (17.645–647).
