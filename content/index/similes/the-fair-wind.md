## The simile

{{quote:99780865-2820-5014-968f-e53d664b39f4}}

ὡς δὲ θεὸς ναύτῃσιν ἐελδομένοισιν ἔδωκεν / οὖρον, ἐπεί κε κάμωσιν ἐϋξέστῃς ἐλάτῃσι / πόντον ἐλαύνοντες, καμάτῳ δʼ ὑπὸ γυῖα λέλυνται, / ὣς ἄρα τὼ Τρώεσσιν ἐελδομένοισι φανήτην. An οὖρος is a fair wind, one that blows from astern and fills the sail, so that the crew can ship their oars. In Homer it is a god's gift: Apollo sends one to the ship coming home from Chryse (ἴκμενον οὖρον, 1.479), and Athena one to Telemachus (*Odyssey* 2.420–421). ἐλάτη is the fir, and here the oar made from it, “well-polished” (ἐϋξέστῃς); in the Odyssey the rowers “whitened the water with polished firs” (ξεστῇς ἐλάτῃσιν, *Odyssey* 12.172). πόντον ἐλαύνοντες, “driving over the sea”, is rowing. κάματος is the weariness of hard work: the same words tell of the exhausted Achaeans in Book 13, “their limbs were loosened by grievous weariness” (13.85).

The point of likeness is longing and relief. The participle ἐελδόμενος, “longing”, is used on both sides of the comparison: the sailors long for the wind, the Trojans long for the two men. The likeness describes the Trojans' feelings, not the heroes' looks. The brothers are the wind; the Trojans are the rowers, and the relief comes as a gift, as if from a god. φανήτην, “the two appeared”, is dual, and the verb is the one of the Odyssey's similes of welcome, where land “appears” to swimmers saved from the sea (φανήῃ, *Odyssey* 23.233) and a father's recovery to his children (*Odyssey* 5.394–398).

## In context

The brothers left the city together at the end of Book 6, Paris running down from Pergamus like a stallion that has broken loose (6.503–516; see [[the-stallion]]). Book 7 opens with them going out through the gates, and “both were eager to make war and to fight” (7.1–3). The Trojans had reason to long for them. In Book 6 they were on the point of fleeing into Ilios before the Achaeans (6.73–74), and Hector left the field to go to the city (6.116; see [[hectors-visit-to-troy]]).

The relief of one side is death for the other. Paris kills Menesthius, Hector kills Eioneus, and Glaucus kills Iphinous (7.8–16; see [[menesthius]], [[eioneus]] and [[iphinous]]). The simile's rowers have their limbs loosened by weariness (γυῖα λέλυνται, 7.6). Within ten lines the same words close two deaths: “and his limbs were loosened” (λύντο δὲ γυῖα, 7.12, 7.16). Then Athena sees the Argives dying and comes down from Olympus (7.17–18; see [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]).

The sea returns in the book's later images. The armies sit like the sea darkening under the West Wind (7.63–66; see [[the-ripple-of-the-west-wind]]), Hector imagines a sailor passing the tomb of his victim (7.87–91), and at the end ships from Lemnos come in with wine (7.467).

## Elsewhere in Homer

A wind from Zeus stands in another sea simile: Nestor, torn between two courses, is like the sea that heaves without moving either way “before some settled wind comes down from Zeus” (14.16–21).
