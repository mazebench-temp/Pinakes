## The simile

{{quote:abbd2b97-d7c1-5e93-90fb-82680b955514}}

σευάμενος ὥς θʼ ἵππος ἀεθλοφόρος σὺν ὄχεσφιν, / ὅς ῥά τε ῥεῖα θέῃσι τιταινόμενος πεδίοιο: “rushing like a prize-winning horse with its chariot, / that runs easily at full stretch over the plain” (22.22–23). ἀεθλοφόρος, “carrying off prizes”, is the epithet of racehorses (see [[prize-winning]]). Agamemnon offers Achilles twelve horses, “strong prize-winners, which have won prizes with their feet” (9.124), and Nestor tells of the debt owed to his father in Elis, “four prize-winning horses with their chariot”, which had gone there “to race for a tripod” and which Augeas kept (11.698–702). σὺν ὄχεσφιν answers αὐτοῖσιν ὄχεσφιν, “with their chariot”, there (11.699): the horse of the simile is a chariot horse in a race, not a horse running loose. τιταίνω is the word for a horse straining at the chariot it pulls (2.390, 12.58), and in the chariot race of *Iliad* 23 the horse that draws its master over the plain runs τιταινόμενος σὺν ὄχεσφι, “at full stretch with the chariot” (23.517–518). Walter Leaf takes πεδίοιο with θέῃσι, “runs … over the plain”, and thinks the reading ὅς τε ῥεῖα, without ῥα, may be right (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 22.23; see the translation's note on 22.23).

ῥεῖα, “easily”, is the word for what the gods do without effort: Aphrodite and Apollo snatch their men from the fight “very easily, as a god can” (3.381, 20.444). The horse runs so, and the point of likeness is speed without strain. The tenor gives the speed to the man's own legs: ὣς Ἀχιλεὺς λαιψηρὰ πόδας καὶ γούνατʼ ἐνώμα, “so Achilles plied his nimble feet and knees” (22.24). The verse is that of Hector in *Iliad* 15, running back to the battle like the stalled horse that breaks its halter (15.269; see [[the-stallion]]); Leaf notes the repetition: λαιψηρά may be an adverb, or an epithet carried over from the familiar λαιψηρὰ … γοῦνα, “nimble knees” (on 22.24), which *Iliad* 22 has twice more, of Hector in flight and of the strength that Apollo rouses in him for the last time (22.144, 22.204; see [[nimble]]). The stallion of Books 6 and 15 breaks loose and runs to the pasture of the horses. This horse runs in harness, and for a prize.

## In context

Apollo, in the likeness of Agenor, has drawn Achilles away across the plain and along the Scamander, running a little ahead of him, while the routed Trojans crowded into the city (21.599–611; see [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]). Now he turns and mocks him: “Why, son of Peleus, do you pursue me with your swift feet, / you who are mortal, me, an immortal god?” (22.8–9; see [[apollo]]). Achilles answers that the Far-Worker has foiled him and taken great glory from him, and that he would take vengeance on him if he had the strength (22.15–20; see [[kydos]]). Then he goes toward the city μέγα φρονέων, “in high spirits” (22.21), and the simile carries him over the plain. Priam sees him first, and in the next simile Achilles is a star (22.25–32; see [[the-autumn-star]]).

The chase of a god who cannot be caught gives way to the chase of a man who will be, and the racehorse is its first image.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The race returns at the center of the book. When Achilles chases Hector around the walls, the two run as prize-winning single-hoofed horses race around the turning posts for a tripod or a woman, the prize set out in honor of a dead man; but this race is run “for the life of Hector, tamer of horses” (22.157–166; see [[racehorses-at-the-turning-post]]). The horses of the simile pull a chariot; at the end of the book Achilles' own horses pull his chariot back to the ships with Hector's body dragging behind it (22.395–404, 22.463–465; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). At the games for Patroclus he does not race them, for they are immortal and would win, and they stand grieving for their charioteer (23.274–286).
