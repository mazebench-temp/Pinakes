## The simile

{{quote:3bf5b467-19b1-543f-9290-57f16fafee18}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ὄνος παρʼ ἄρουραν ἰὼν ἐβιήσατο παῖδας / νωθής: “and as when a donkey going past a field has had his way against the boys, a sluggish beast”. ὄνος, “donkey”, occurs nowhere else in Homer, and νωθής, “sluggish”, only here (see [[donkey]]). The boys have been guarding the field, and many cudgels, ῥόπαλα, have been broken around the donkey's sides (περὶ … ἀμφὶς ἐάγῃ). It goes in and crops βαθὺ λήϊον, “the deep field of grain”, the phrase of the standing crop that the West Wind bows in Book 2 (2.147; see [[wind-in-the-wheat]]). The boys beat it with their cudgels, βίη δέ τε νηπίη αὐτῶν, “but their strength is childish”, and drive it out with effort, “once he has had his fill of fodder” (φορβή, 11.560–562). ἐβιήσατο and ἐξήλασσαν are gnomic aorists, the tense of what always happens (see the translation's note on 11.558–562).

The tenor: “so then great Ajax, the son of Telamon, the high-hearted Trojans and their allies gathered from many lands kept following, stabbing with their spears at the middle of his shield” (11.563–565). The Trojans are the boys, their spears the cudgels, and the blows do not hurt: the spears stick in the great shield or in the ground short of his flesh (11.571–574). The points of likeness are slowness, stubbornness and indifference to blows. The donkey leaves only when it is ready, and Ajax gives ground at his own pace.

## In context

The donkey follows at once on the lion driven from the cattle yard, and the two similes describe one retreat (11.548–557; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]). The lion gives Ajax's courage and his unwillingness to go, the donkey the slowness of his going and the uselessness of the Trojan attack. Homer often sets two similes side by side for one moment, but rarely two so unlike. The narrative that follows joins them: Ajax at one time remembers his furious courage and turns to hold off the Trojan battalions, and at another turns to flee, and in this way he keeps them all from the ships (11.566–570; see [[ajax]] and [[retreat]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The beast of burden of the Iliad is the mule, ἡμίονος, “half-donkey”, whose name implies the donkey (see [[mules]]). Another creature that will not be driven off is the fly whose daring Athena puts into Menelaus: kept off, it still longs to bite (17.570–573; see [[flies]]). Children are a measure of strength in another simile. Apollo knocks down the Achaean wall as easily as a boy by the sea scatters with his hands and feet the sand he has heaped up in his childish play (νηπιέῃσιν, 15.362–364). There the child's play is the measure of ease; here the boys' strength is νηπίη, “childish”, and it is the measure of failure.
