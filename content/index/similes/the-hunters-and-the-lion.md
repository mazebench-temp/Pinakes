## The simile

{{quote:656b7e6e-f0db-5a93-8e20-a0fd5aa21f17}}

οἳ δʼ ὥς τʼ ἢ ἔλαφον κεραὸν ἢ ἄγριον αἶγα / ἐσσεύαντο κύνες τε καὶ ἀνέρες ἀγροιῶται: “And as when an antlered stag or a wild goat is chased by dogs and country men” (15.271–272). The quarry is the pair of beasts that the hungry lion of Book 3 finds and devours, “an antlered stag or a wild goat”, in the same half-verse (3.24; see [[lion-on-a-carcass]] and [[antlered]]). The second verse is the one in which dogs and country men chase a lion from the farmyard, when Ajax gives ground before the Trojans (11.549; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]).

The hunt fails twice. First the quarry escapes: τὸν μέν τʼ ἠλίβατος πέτρη καὶ δάσκιος ὕλη / εἰρύσατʼ, “but a lofty rock or a shady forest shelters it”. ἠλίβατος is always said of rocks and cliffs, and its derivation is unknown; δάσκιος is “thick-shaded”. εἰρύσατο is from ἔρυμαι, “protect, keep safe”. The escape is put in the language of fate: οὐδʼ ἄρα τέ σφι κιχήμεναι αἴσιμον ἦεν, “and it is not their lot to catch it” (15.274). Then their own noise brings out something worse: τῶν δέ θʼ ὑπὸ ἰαχῆς ἐφάνη λὶς ἠϋγένειος / εἰς ὁδόν, “and at their shouting a bearded lion appears in their path”, and turns them all back, “for all their eagerness” (15.275–276). λίς is a short form of λέων, used only in comparisons, and ἠϋγένειος, “well-bearded”, is the lion's mane (see [[lion]]).

The tenor: ὣς Δαναοὶ εἷος μὲν ὁμιλαδὸν αἰὲν ἕποντο, “so the Danaans for a while kept following in a mass”, stabbing at the Trojans with swords and double-pointed spears; “but when they saw Hector going along the ranks of men, they were afraid, and the hearts of all sank to their feet” (15.277–280).

The hunters set out after one beast and meet another. The Danaans have been pursuing the routed Trojans since Hector was carried from the field (14.440–441; 15.1–8), and the pursuit has become a hunt with an easy end in view. The quarry that it is “not their lot to catch” answers to what Thoas says next: every Achaean had hoped that Hector was dead at Ajax's hands, but once again one of the gods “has rescued and saved” him (15.286–293). The lion is the same Hector, restored. In Book 11 the dogs and country men of verse 272 drove a lion away from the cattle; here a lion turns them back.

## In context

Apollo has breathed great might into Hector, and he has run back to the battle like a stallion that has broken its halter, urging on the horsemen (15.243–270; see [[the-stallion]] and [[like-a-hawk]]). The Achaeans see him and lose heart. Thoas, son of Andraemon, the best of the Aetolians, speaks among them: “Ah, truly this is a great wonder that I see with my eyes, how he has risen up once more, escaping the fates, Hector!” He advises that the multitude go back to the ships, while the best men stand with their spears held up, in the hope that Hector will be afraid to plunge into the throng of the Danaans (15.281–299; see [[thoas]]). They do as he says, and the leaders set the battle in order around Ajax, Idomeneus, Teucer, Meriones and Meges (15.300–305).

## Elsewhere in Homer

A lion that appears and scatters smaller hunters is the turn of the simile of the wounded Odysseus. Jackals press around a stag that a hunter's arrow has hit, until a god brings a lion and they scatter in fear; the lion is Ajax coming to the rescue (11.474–484; see [[the-jackals-and-the-stag]]). Later in the poem the bearded lion is a lion that loses: Menelaus leaves the body of Patroclus turning back again and again, like a bearded lion that dogs and men drive from a farmyard (17.108–112), and Achilles groans over Patroclus like a bearded lion whose cubs a hunter has stolen (18.318–322).
