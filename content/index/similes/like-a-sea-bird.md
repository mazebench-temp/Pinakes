## The simile

{{quote:e4a669b9-3c77-5d49-bfa4-bb0780b2f125}}

τὴν μὲν ἔπειτα γυναῖκα βάλʼ Ἄρτεμις ἰοχέαιρα, / ἄντλῳ δʼ ἐνδούπησε πεσοῦσʼ ὡς εἰναλίη κήξ: “then Artemis of the showering arrows struck the woman, / and she fell into the bilge with a splash, like a sea bird” (*Odyssey* 15.478–479). κήξ is named only here in Homer, and the poem says nothing of the bird but that it belongs to the sea; the translation renders it “a sea bird”, and which bird it was is not known (see the translation's row εἰναλίη κήξ; ἐνδουπέω). εἰνάλιος, “of the sea”, is the word of the κορῶναι εἰνάλιαι, the “crows / of the sea” that roost in the wood around Calypso's cave (*Odyssey* 5.66–67; see [[birds]]), and of the seal, “a beast of the sea” beside which no one would choose to sleep (*Odyssey* 4.443; see [[seals]]). ὣς after its noun is the short comparison, as in ὄρνις δʼ ὣς, “like a bird”, of Athena's flight from Ithaca (*Odyssey* 1.320; see [[like-a-bird]]).

ἄντλος is the bilge, the water in the hold of a ship: when the West Wind broke the forestays of Odysseus' last ship, the mast fell backward “and all the tackle / was spilled into the bilge” (*Odyssey* 12.410–411; see the translation's row πρότονοι; ἄντλος). ἐνδουπέω, “fall with a splash”, is found once more in Homer, when Odysseus, hanging from the fig tree above Charybdis, lets go and “fell with a splash in the middle, beyond the very long timbers” (*Odyssey* 12.443). W. W. Merry renders that verse “I plumped down in the middle”, and compares this one (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 12.442). The compound is made on the verb of the Iliad's deaths in battle. δούπησεν δὲ πεσών, “and he fell with a thud”, is the half-verse of a warrior struck down, nineteen times in the Iliad and twice in the Odyssey, for Amphinomus and for Eupeithes (4.504, 5.42; *Odyssey* 22.94, 24.525; see [[fell-with-a-thud]]), and ἐνδούπησε πεσοῦσα is, in D. B. Monro's words, “an adaptation, perhaps a parody, of the conventional δούπησεν δὲ πεσών of the Iliad” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 15.479; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 15.479). The Iliad's warrior falls to the ground with a thud, and his armor clatters upon him; the woman falls into water with a splash.

What the likeness gives is the sudden drop and its sound, as of a sea bird coming down on the water. The poem has a bird of the sea for her death and the beasts of the sea for her body, which the sailors throw overboard “to become a spoil for seals and fish” (*Odyssey* 15.480–481; see [[seals]] and [[fish]]).

## In context

The woman is the Phoenician of Eumaeus' story, “beautiful and tall and skilled in splendid handiwork” (*Odyssey* 15.418; see [[the-phoenician-nurse]]), who said that she came from Sidon, the daughter of [[arybas|Arybas]], and had been seized by Taphian pirates and sold into the house of Eumaeus' father, the king of the island Syrie (*Odyssey* 15.403–429; see [[syrie]], [[sidon]], [[taphians]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). Phoenician traders seduced her and swore to take her home unharmed, and when their ship was laden she led the king's small son out of the house, with three goblets hidden in her bosom, and went aboard at nightfall (*Odyssey* 15.415–475; see [[phoenicians]]). “For six days we sailed on, night and day alike, / but when Zeus, son of Cronus, added the seventh day, / then Artemis of the showering arrows struck the woman” (*Odyssey* 15.476–478; see [[artemis]] and [[of-the-showering-arrows]]). The first verse is the verse of the voyage to the Laestrygonians, and the second that of the seventh day on Thrinacia, after which the companions put to sea and were lost (*Odyssey* 10.80, 12.399).

Artemis is the giver of sudden death to women. On [[syrie|Syrie]] itself, Eumaeus has said, no sickness comes, but when men grow old Apollo comes with Artemis “and comes upon them with his gentle arrows and kills them” (*Odyssey* 15.409–411; see [[gentle-arrows]]), and among the dead Odysseus asked his mother whether it was a long sickness that brought her down, “or did Artemis of the showering arrows / come upon you with her gentle arrows and kill you?” (*Odyssey* 11.172–173). [[the-phoenician-nurse|The Phoenician woman]] is struck not in old age on the island but at sea, on the way to the home she was promised. The boy is left “grieving at heart”, and the wind and the water carry the ship to Ithaca, where Laertes buys him (*Odyssey* 15.481–483; see [[eumaeus]] and [[laertes]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Odyssey's other birds of the sea are likenesses of gods and of drowning men. Hermes skims the waves to Calypso's island “like a bird, a gull” (*Odyssey* 5.51; see [[the-gull]]); Ino rises from the sea to the broken raft “like a diving bird” and plunges back in the same likeness (*Odyssey* 5.337, 5.353; see [[like-a-diving-bird]]); Athena leaves Pylos “in the likeness of a sea eagle” (*Odyssey* 3.372; see [[phene]]); and the companions thrown from Odysseus' last ship are carried on the waves “like sea crows”, in verses that the false tale told in the hut repeats (*Odyssey* 12.418, 14.308; see [[like-sea-crows]]). The gull, the diving bird and the sea eagle are gods coming and going, and the sea crows are men in the water after a wreck; the κήξ is the only one of them that falls. The fall of a man struck dead is the diver's in both poems: Epicles falls from the tower and Cebriones from the chariot “like a diver”, and so does the helmsman of Odysseus' last ship, falling from the deck when the mast smashes his skull (12.385–386, 16.742–743; *Odyssey* 12.412–414; see [[like-a-diver]]).
