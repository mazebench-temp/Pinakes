## The simile

τὰς Εὔμηλος ἔλαυνε ποδώκεας ὄρνιθας ὣς / ὄτριχας οἰέτεας σταφύλῇ ἐπὶ νῶτον ἐΐσας: “which Eumelus drove, swift-footed like birds, alike in coat and in age, their backs as level as a builder's line”. ποδώκεας is “swift-footed” ([[swift-footed]]), and ὄρνιθας ὣς “like birds”, with ὡς (“as”) placed after its noun. ὄτριχας is “with the same coat” (θρίξ, “hair”), and οἰέτεας “of the same age” (ἔτος, “year”). σταφύλη is a builder's level, and ἐΐσας “equal”: laid along their backs, the level shows the two mares to be of one height.

There are two measures, then. The simile gives the mares the speed of birds; the builder's tool shows that they are exactly matched. A pair that runs under one yoke must be matched, and these are.

## In context

{{quote:3d3eb9d9-432c-5e1a-8b5b-b5977726e84c}}

The Catalogue is over, and the poet asks the Muse one more question: who was the best “of the men and of the horses who followed the sons of Atreus?” (2.760–762). The horses come first. Eumelus' mares were reared in [[pereia|Pereia]] by Apollo himself, “both of them mares, carrying the panic of Ares” (φόβον Ἄρηος); see [[the-mares-of-eumelus]] and [[eumelus]]. Then the men: the best was Telamonian Ajax, “while Achilles kept up his wrath; for he was by far the strongest, and so were the horses that carried the noble son of Peleus” (2.768–770; [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). Both answers hold only while Achilles stays out of the war.

Birds appear in one other simile of the book, the flocks on the Asian meadow, where they give the noise and movement of the army ([[birds-on-the-asian-meadow]]). Here they give speed.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The mares run again in the chariot race at the funeral games of Patroclus, in the same words: Φηρητιάδαο ποδώκεες … ἵπποι, “the swift-footed horses of the grandson of Pheres” (Iliad 23.376). They lead until Athena breaks their yoke and Eumelus is thrown from the chariot (23.375–397).
