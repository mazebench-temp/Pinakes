## The simile

{{quote:b6f1b632-0cda-59ba-9c22-92b315d54fd0}}

ὁ δʼ ἐρεμνῇ νυκτὶ ἐοικώς, / γυμνὸν τόξον ἔχων καὶ ἐπὶ νευρῆφιν ὀιστόν, / δεινὸν παπταίνων, αἰεὶ βαλέοντι ἐοικώς: “and he was like dark night, / holding his naked bow, with an arrow on the string, / glancing terribly around, like one ever about to shoot” (*Odyssey* 11.606–608). Two comparisons with ἐοικώς frame the figure, night at the start and the archer at the end (see [[like-night]]). βαλέοντι is a future participle, “about to shoot”, and W. W. Merry joins it with αἰεί, “ever as if about to shoot”, comparing the horses of Diomedes in the chariot race, which “always seemed about to mount the chariot” of Eumelus, αἰεὶ γὰρ δίφρου ἐπιβησομένοισιν ἐίκτην (*Iliad* 23.379), and the fisherman on the shield of Heracles in the Hesiodic *Shield*, “like one about to throw” his net (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.608; *Shield* 213–215). The comparison holds the archer at the moment before the shot. παπταίνων, “glancing terribly around”, Merry thinks, always suggests anxiety in the look, here caution blended with keen expectation (on *Odyssey* 11.608).

The four participles, ἐοικώς, ἔχων, παπταίνων and again ἐοικώς, have no finite verb, and the English supplies “was”. Merry finds no variant in the manuscripts; one might conjecture πάπταινεν or ἔχεν, or take ἐοικώς as ἐοικὼς ἦν with Fäsi, but if the need had been felt, he says, nothing would have been simpler than to write ἐῴκει. He suspects a real rhetorical art in the abrupt, unconnected clauses: “The very passage has, as it were, the expression or attitude of uneasy expectation” (on *Odyssey* 11.608; see the Odyssey translation's note on 11.606–608).

## In context

The phantom of mighty Heracles stands in the land of the dead while Heracles himself feasts among the gods (*Odyssey* 11.601–604; see [[heracles]] and [[eidolon]]). The dead scatter around him like birds (*Odyssey* 11.605–606; see [[a-clamor-as-of-birds]]), and below the bow is the terrible baldric with its bears, boars and lions, and its battles and slaughters (*Odyssey* 11.609–614; see [[the-baldric-of-heracles]], [[bow]] and [[arrows]]). The archer who is always about to shoot never does: he knows Odysseus, speaks to him of his labors and goes back into the house of Hades (*Odyssey* 11.615–627).

## Outside Homer

Merry sets beside the verse Virgil's sinners in Tartarus, over whom a black rock “is always about to slip, and hangs like one falling”, *quos super atra silex iam iam lapsura cadentique / imminet adsimilis* (*Aeneid* 6.602–603; see [[virgil]]).
