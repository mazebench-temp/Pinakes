## The simile

{{quote:7a7c7a84-91e3-5889-a6c3-97debd367d41}}

ἐγὼ δʼ ἐφύπερθεν ἐρεισθεὶς / δίνεον, ὡς ὅτε τις τρυπῷ δόρυ νήιον ἀνὴρ / τρυπάνῳ, οἱ δέ τʼ ἔνερθεν ὑποσσείουσιν ἱμάντι / ἁψάμενοι ἑκάτερθε, τὸ δὲ τρέχει ἐμμενὲς αἰεί: “and I, throwing my weight on it from above, / twirled it, as when a man bores a ship's timber / with a drill, and those below keep it spinning with a strap, / holding it at either end, and it runs round without letting up” (*Odyssey* 9.383–386). δόρυ νήιον, “a ship's timber”, is the shipwright's beam of the Iliad's similes (*Iliad* 3.61–62, 15.410; see [[heart-like-an-axe]] and [[the-chalk-line]]). The τρύπανον is a large drill, the kind that one man guides from above while others spin it; the τέρετρα with which Odysseus bores the timbers of his raft are small borers or gimlets that can be worked with one hand (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.246; *Odyssey* 5.246; see [[augers]]). The strap, ἱμάς, does the work of the string of an ordinary bow-drill: it took a turn round the shaft, and pulling each end in turn spun the drill a turn or two one way and then the other. Merry warns that these particulars belong to the drill of the simile, not to the stake (on 9.383).

ἐρεισθείς, “throwing my weight on it”, is the reading of two important manuscripts, said to have been preferred by Aristarchus, “as a man presses with his body upon the stock of a drill as it turns round”; the common ἀερθείς, “raised up”, only repeats the notion of ἐφύπερθεν, “from above” (Merry on 9.383; see the translation's note on 9.383). τρυπῷ, which is taken as an optative, is strange after ὡς ὅτε, since a simile so introduced never has the optative elsewhere; Draco read τρυπᾷ, and Ameis proposed the participle τρυπῶν (Merry on 9.383–384; see the translation's note on 9.384).

The point of likeness is the work and the attitudes of the workers: Odysseus above, bearing down and turning, and his men around the stake, as a ship's carpenter and his mates at the drill (Merry on 9.383). The tenor follows in the same verb: “So we took the fire-pointed stake and twirled it in his eye, / and the blood flowed round the stake, hot as it was” (*Odyssey* 9.387–388). θερμὸν ἐόντα, “hot as it was”, belongs to the stake: the thought is the intense heat of its point, so hot that it hissed in the blood and juices of the eye, and Voss's ἰόντα is a mere conjecture (Merry on 9.388; see the translation's note on 9.387–388).

## In context

The Cyclops lies drunk and asleep (*Odyssey* 9.371–374). Odysseus heats the stake of olive wood under the ashes, and when it is about to catch fire, “green though it was, and glowed terribly”, he brings it out; his companions stand around him, “and a god breathed great daring into us” (*Odyssey* 9.375–381; see [[stake]], [[olive]] and [[tharsos]]). “They took the stake of olive wood, sharp at the point, / and thrust it into his eye” (*Odyssey* 9.382–383). The drill gives the boring, and a second simile, of a smith tempering an axe, gives the hissing of the eye (*Odyssey* 9.389–394; see [[the-smith-tempering-an-axe]]). The two likenesses of the blinding are both taken from a craftsman's work. The man who made his own raft, boring its timbers and fitting them together (*Odyssey* 5.243–251; see [[the-building-of-the-raft]]), puts out the Cyclops' eye as a shipwright works.

## Outside Homer

Euripides kept the simile in his *Cyclops*, as Merry notes (on 9.383): before the blinding Odysseus tells the chorus that “as a man fitting together a ship's timbers works the drill with a double strap”, so he will whirl the brand in the Cyclops' eye and dry up its pupils (*Cyclops* 460–463; see [[euripides]]).
