## The simile

{{quote:83ceec21-cf65-597b-b6f1-7b2ff250ce4d}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἀνὴρ ἵπποισι κελητίζειν ἐῢ εἰδώς, / ὅς τʼ ἐπεὶ ἐκ πολέων πίσυρας συναείρεται ἵππους, / σεύας ἐκ πεδίοιο μέγα προτὶ ἄστυ δίηται / λαοφόρον καθʼ ὁδόν· πολέες τέ ἑ θηήσαντο / ἀνέρες ἠδὲ γυναῖκες· ὃ δʼ ἔμπεδον ἀσφαλὲς αἰεὶ / θρῴσκων ἄλλοτʼ ἐπʼ ἄλλον ἀμείβεται, οἳ δὲ πέτονται: “And as a man well skilled in riding horses, who, when he has harnessed together four horses out of many, drives them from the plain and speeds toward a great city along the public road; and many gaze at him, men and women; and he, always steadily and safely, leaps from one horse to another as they fly on” (15.679–684).

κελητίζω, “ride horseback”, from κέλης, a riding horse, is found only here; in the Odyssey Odysseus, his raft broken, sits astride a single beam “as though riding a horse” (κέληθʼ ὡς ἵππον ἐλαύνων, *Odyssey* 5.371; see [[like-a-rider]]). πίσυρες is the Aeolic form of “four”, and συναείρω is “yoke together”: the man picks four horses from many and harnesses them side by side. λαοφόρος, “carrying people”, is a highway, and is found only here. θηέομαι is to gaze in wonder, and the gazers are “men and women” together. ἔμπεδον ἀσφαλὲς αἰεί, “always steadily and safely”: the rider never slips, though the horses under him πέτονται, “fly”.

The tenor: ὣς Αἴας ἐπὶ πολλὰ θοάων ἴκρια νηῶν / φοίτα μακρὰ βιβάς, “so Ajax went over many decks of the swift ships, ranging with long strides”, and his voice reached the upper air as he shouted to the Danaans to defend the ships and the huts (15.685–688). The ἴκρια are the half-decks at the bow and the stern of the beached ships, and Ajax steps from one to the next as the rider steps from horse to horse. μακρὰ βιβάς, “with long strides”, is Ajax's gait in the Iliad more than any other man's (7.213, 13.809, 15.676; see [[like-huge-ares]]).

The point of likeness is skill, balance and display. The ships stand in a row as the four horses run side by side, and the man who crosses them never misses his footing. The setting is peace: a plain, a public road, a great city, a crowd of men and women watching a feat. Riding on horseback is rare in Homer. Its heroes fight from chariots, and the only riders in the Iliad's story are Diomedes and Odysseus, who ride the horses of Rhesus back to the ships (10.513–514, 10.527–531; see the translation's note on 10.513).

## In context

Athena has thrust the mist from the Achaeans' eyes, and both armies are in the light (15.668–673). “Nor did it please the heart of great-hearted Ajax any longer to stand where the other sons of the Achaeans had stood back; but he went up and down the decks of the ships with long strides, and wielded in his hands a great spear for fighting at sea, joined with bands, twenty-two cubits long” (15.674–678; see [[ajax]] and [[spear]]). The simile follows. Hector at the same moment darts at a ship like an eagle at a flock of birds (15.688–695; see [[the-eagle-and-the-birds]]), and seizes the stern of the ship of Protesilaus (15.704–706). Driven back to the seven-foot bench of a ship, Ajax tells the Danaans that there is no city near, fitted with towers, where they could defend themselves; they sit with their backs to the sea, far from their native land, “so in our hands lies the light, not in softness in war” (15.727–741). The rider of the simile speeds toward a great city along a public road; the man he stands for has no city behind him.

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the Iliad's other horse similes the horse runs free or races. Hector runs back to the battle like a stalled horse that breaks its halter (15.263–268; see [[the-stallion]]), and Achilles runs toward Troy like a prize-winning horse with its chariot (22.21–24). The skill of horsemanship is shown in the chariot race of Book 23, where Nestor teaches Antilochus how to take the turning post (23.306–348).
