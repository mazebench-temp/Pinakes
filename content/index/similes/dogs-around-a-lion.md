## The simile

{{quote:67bcc58c-80d6-539e-a425-c1a491eb70f1}}

ἀλλὰ καταπτώσσουσι κύνες ὣς ἀμφὶ λέοντα: “they cower like dogs around a lion”. καταπτώσσω is “crouch down, cower”, a compound of πτώσσω, which is used of animals crouching in fear. κύνες ὥς is “like dogs”, with ὥς after its noun, and ἀμφὶ λέοντα “around a lion”.

The dogs of Homer's lion similes are the herdsmen's and hunters' dogs, and they bark at the lion from a distance but do not close with it. On the shield of Achilles, when two lions seize a bull, the dogs “shrank from biting the lions, but stood very close and barked, and kept out of their way” (18.585–586); in Book 17 dogs and herdsmen shout at a lion from far off and do not dare to come near (17.65–67). That is the point of likeness: the Trojan princes stay around the enemy and do not engage. Sarpedon does not name the lion. It can only be the Achaeans, or Diomedes, who has been killing Trojans all morning, two sons of Priam among them (5.159–165).

The line is a single comparison inside a speech of reproach, and it is an insult. The allies, Sarpedon goes on, are the ones who fight, and he has come from far away and has nothing at Troy that the Achaeans could carry off or drive away (5.477–484).

## In context

After Apollo has rescued Aeneas, Ares goes among the Trojans in the likeness of Acamas and urges on the sons of Priam (5.461–470). Then Sarpedon turns on Hector (5.471–492). “Where has the might gone that you used to have?”, he begins, and reminds him of a boast: that he would hold the city without the people and the allies, “alone, with your brothers-in-law and your brothers” (5.472–474). The boast is not reported anywhere else in the poem, and Sarpedon gives it with a hedge (φῆς που, “you said, I think”). Then the dogs. He ends with a second image, of the Trojans caught as in the meshes of an all-catching net (5.487–489; see [[the-net]]). The rebuke stings Hector to the heart; he leaps from his chariot in his armor and rouses the army (5.493–497). See [[sarpedon]], [[hector]] and [[trojan-allies]].

**Cowering.** καταπτώσσω is a word of reproach in Books 4 and 5. Agamemnon, going through the army, asks Menestheus and Odysseus why they stand apart “cowering” (4.340), and Diomedes why he “cowers” (πτώσσεις, 4.371), and the narrator says that Agamemnon himself was not cowering (4.224). Diomedes answers in Book 5 that it is not in his blood “to cower” (5.253–254). Sarpedon throws the word at Hector's kin, and a few hundred lines later Tlepolemus throws it at Sarpedon: “what need do you have to cower here?” (5.633–634; see [[tlepolemus-and-sarpedon]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The dogs and the lion appear together in many similes. Dogs and young men rush at a feeding lion in vain (3.23–28; see [[lion-on-a-carcass]]). Hector turns among his enemies like a boar or a lion among dogs and hunters, who stand against it in a wall and throw their spears (12.41–48). Menelaus, driven from Patroclus' body, goes like a lion driven from a farmyard by dogs and men (17.657–664). See [[dogs]] and [[lion]].
