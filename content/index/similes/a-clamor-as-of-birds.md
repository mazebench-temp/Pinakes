## The simile

{{quote:b6f1b632-0cda-59ba-9c22-92b315d54fd0}}

ἀμφὶ δέ μιν κλαγγὴ νεκύων ἦν οἰωνῶν ὥς, / πάντοσʼ ἀτυζομένων: “And around him rose a clamor of the dead, as of birds / scattering in terror on every side” (*Odyssey* 11.605–606). ὥς follows its noun, οἰωνῶν, as in σύες ὣς ἀργιόδοντες, “like white-tusked hogs” (*Odyssey* 11.413; see [[like-white-tusked-hogs]]); the participle in the next verse, ἀτυζομένων, may go with the birds or with the dead, and the English gives it to the birds, but either way it is the dead who scatter (see [[birds]]). κλαγγή is a bird's cry. The Iliad's third book opens with it: the Trojans came on “with clamor and with cries, like birds, / as the clamor of cranes rises under the face of heaven”, flying toward the streams of Oceanus to bring death to the Pygmies (*Iliad* 3.2–6; see [[cranes-and-pygmies]]), and the birds of the Asian meadow settle “with loud cries”, κλαγγηδόν (*Iliad* 2.463; see [[birds-on-the-asian-meadow]]). The word is also the twang of Apollo's bow: “and terrible was the twang of the silver bow” (*Iliad* 1.49). ἀτυζόμενος is “in terror”, said of bolting horses and of men driven in rout across the plain (*Iliad* 6.38, 18.7); W. W. Merry renders πάντοσʼ ἀτυζομένων “flying scared every way” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.606).

The dead are like birds in their voices as well as their flight. The soul of Patroclus goes beneath the earth “gibbering”, τετριγυῖα (*Iliad* 23.101; see [[like-smoke]]), the verb of the sparrow's nestlings that the serpent devours (*Iliad* 2.314), and in the last book the suitors' souls follow Hermes “gibbering”, as bats fly gibbering in the depths of a cave when one has fallen from the rock (*Odyssey* 24.5–9, a plain rendering; see [[psyche]]).

## In context

Odysseus sees the phantom of mighty Heracles; Heracles himself is among the gods, the husband of Hebe (*Odyssey* 11.601–604; see [[heracles]], [[eidolon]] and [[hebe]]). The dead scatter around him, and he stands among them “like dark night, / holding his naked bow, with an arrow on the string, / glancing terribly around, like one ever about to shoot” (*Odyssey* 11.606–608; see [[like-night]], [[bow]] and [[like-one-about-to-shoot]]); the terrible baldric with its beasts and battles is described, and then he speaks (*Odyssey* 11.609–626; see [[the-baldric-of-heracles]]). Even his phantom puts the souls to flight. Their noise frames the book: they came to the pit “with a wondrous cry” (*Odyssey* 11.43), and at the end the countless tribes of the dead gather “with a wondrous din”, and pale fear sends Odysseus back to his ship (*Odyssey* 11.632–636; see [[the-tribes-of-the-dead]]).

## Outside Homer

[[virgil|Virgil]] gives the souls the likeness of birds and the dead warriors Homer's panic. The crowd of souls at the river is as many as the leaves that fall in the woods at the first chill of autumn, or the birds that flock to land from the deep sea when the cold of the year drives them over the waters to sunny lands (*Aeneid* 6.309–312); and when the Greek leaders and the ranks of Agamemnon see Aeneas and his shining arms among the shades, they tremble with great fear: some turn their backs, as once they ran to the ships, and some raise a thin voice, and the cry they begin mocks their gaping mouths, *inceptus clamor frustratur hiantis* (*Aeneid* 6.489–493).
