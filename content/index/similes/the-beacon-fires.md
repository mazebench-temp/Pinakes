## The simile

{{quote:5e273bdf-e97a-5740-aeeb-e6bbd055df3c}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτε καπνὸς ἰὼν ἐξ ἄστεος αἰθέρʼ ἵκηται / τηλόθεν ἐκ νήσου, τὴν δήϊοι ἀμφιμάχωνται: “And as when smoke goes up from a city and reaches the upper air / far off, from an island that enemies are fighting around” (18.207–208). The αἰθήρ is the bright upper air above the clouds. The town is on an island and is besieged, and its men fight all day “from their own city”, ἄστεος ἐκ σφετέρου, that is, from the walls (18.209–210). Walter Leaf reports that the manuscripts' οἵ τε at 18.209 would make the fighters the besiegers, while ἄστεος ἐκ σφετέρου belongs to the besieged, so that most editors adopt Heyne's οἱ δέ; he thinks the picture is taken from a sudden descent of pirates on a small island town (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.209; see the translation's note on 18.207–214). The fighting is told in a formula: πανημέριοι στυγερῷ κρίνονται Ἄρηϊ, “all day long the men try their strength in the hateful work of Ares”, nearly the words that Agamemnon used of the battle to come in Book 2 (2.385; see [[ares]]).

ἅμα δʼ ἠελίῳ καταδύντι / πυρσοί τε φλεγέθουσιν ἐπήτριμοι, ὑψόσε δʼ αὐγὴ / γίγνεται ἀΐσσουσα περικτιόνεσσιν ἰδέσθαι, / αἴ κέν πως σὺν νηυσὶν ἄρεω ἀλκτῆρες ἵκωνται: “and as the sun goes down / the beacon fires blaze in rows, and the glare goes darting / up high, for those who live around to see, / in the hope that they may come with their ships to defend them against disaster” (18.210–213). πυρσοί, “beacon fires”, are named only here in Homer; ἐπήτριμοι is “in rows, one after another”, a word that returns for the handfuls of grain on the Shield (Leaf on 18.211; 18.552; see [[the-kings-harvest]]). The περικτίονες are the people who live round about, on the other islands and the coast, and the beacons are a call for help. ἄρεω ἀλκτῆρες, “to defend them against disaster”, is the phrase that Achilles used of himself a hundred verses before, when he said that Patroclus perished “and lacked me to be his defender against disaster” (ἀρῆς ἀλκτῆρα, 18.100; see the translation's note on 12.334).

The tenor: ὣς ἀπʼ Ἀχιλλῆος κεφαλῆς σέλας αἰθέρʼ ἵκανε, “so from the head of Achilles the blaze reached the upper air” (18.214). Leaf finds the point of the simile in the words “as the sun goes down”. The beacons are only smoke by day and only fire by night, but at sunset the fire flames out and both are seen together for a time, as the dark cloud and the fire are seen together over Achilles' head; he notes that Aristarchus read a variant because he thought it absurd to compare fire to smoke, a reading that looks to Leaf like a conjecture, and that the fire is “depicted not as bright but as lurid”, as when Athena in a purple cloud is likened to a rainbow in *Iliad* 17 (on 18.207; 17.547–552; see [[like-rainbows]]).

## In context

Iris has told Achilles to show himself to the Trojans at the ditch, unarmed as he is (18.197–201; see [[iris]]). He rises, and Athena flings the tasseled aegis around his shoulders, sets a golden cloud on his head as a crown and kindles a flame from him:

{{quote:0de772a7-c95e-5511-b8db-2748ef82afa7}}

ἐκ δʼ αὐτοῦ, “from him”, is from his own body, as the fire that Athena kindled from Diomedes' head and shoulders (5.4–7; Leaf on 18.206; see the translation's note on 18.206, [[the-autumn-star]] and [[aegis]]). The simile follows, and after it Achilles goes from the wall to the ditch and shouts, and a second simile makes his voice a trumpet (18.215–221; see [[the-trumpet]]). The fire does its work as the voice does. The Trojan charioteers are struck with terror “when they saw the tireless fire / blazing terribly above the head of the great-hearted son of Peleus”, and the narrator says again whose fire it is: “the gray-eyed goddess Athena kindled it” (18.225–227; see [[athena]]). Three times Achilles shouts, twelve of the best Trojans perish among their own chariots and spears, and the Achaeans draw the body of Patroclus out of the missiles (18.228–233; see [[achilles]] and [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]]).

The simile's sunset comes true a few verses later. Hera sends the unwilling sun down to the streams of Oceanus, and the day of battle that began in *Iliad* 11 is over (18.239–242; see [[helios]] and [[sunset-lines]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The fire on Achilles' head is the beginning of a series of lights around him. When he arms in the next book, the gleam of his shield goes up like the moon, and like a fire that sailors see from the sea, burning high on the mountains in a lonely farmstead, while the storm carries them away from their friends; the tenor repeats this simile's verse with the shield in place of the head, ὣς ἀπʼ Ἀχιλλῆος σάκεος σέλας αἰθέρʼ ἵκανε (19.373–380; see [[moon]] and [[the-fire-seen-by-sailors]]). His helmet shines like a star (19.381; see [[like-a-star]]), and as he runs at Hector his bronze gleams like the star called Orion's dog (22.25–32; see [[orion]]).

Smoke from a city is the opening of a later simile for Achilles, in the same words, ὡς δʼ ὅτε καπνὸς ἰὼν: the toil he brings on the Trojans is like the smoke that goes up to the sky from a burning city, sent by the wrath of the gods (21.522–525). At nightfall in *Iliad* 8 Hector ordered fires to be burned on the plain all night, so that their glare might reach the sky (σέλας δʼ εἰς οὐρανὸν ἵκῃ, 8.508–509; see [[stars-around-the-moon]]). Earlier in this book Achilles has said that anger swells in men's breasts “like smoke” (18.110; see [[cholos]]).
