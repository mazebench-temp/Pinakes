## The simile

{{quote:63cd51ff-19a6-5536-9421-f310d712a415}}

ὡς δʼ ἄνεμοι δύο πόντον ὀρίνετον ἰχθυόεντα / Βορέης καὶ Ζέφυρος, τώ τε Θρῄκηθεν ἄητον: “As two winds stir up the fish-filled sea, the North Wind and the West Wind, which blow from Thrace” (see [[fish-filled]]). The verbs ὀρίνετον and ἄητον are duals: the winds work as a pair (see [[boreas]] and [[zephyrus]]). ὀρίνω, “stir, rouse”, is a verb of the sea and of the heart alike; when Meleager hears his wife's account of a captured city, “his heart was stirred” (ὠρίνετο θυμός, 9.595). The winds come suddenly (ἐξαπίνης), and at once (ἄμυδις) the dark wave “rears up in a crest” (κορθύεται) and casts much “seaweed” (φῦκος) along the shore. Homer has both words only here. The wave of an earlier simile, driven by the West Wind, “rears its head” on the open sea (κορύσσεται, 4.424) and “curls and crests” around the headlands (κορυφοῦται, 4.426; see [[wave-on-the-headland]]).

The tenor is one line: ὣς ἐδαΐζετο θυμὸς ἐνὶ στήθεσσιν Ἀχαιῶν, “so the heart in the breasts of the Achaeans was torn” (9.8). δαΐζω is to cleave or rend. The simile does not say what the two winds stand for. They may answer the two feelings that the opening lines name, the heaven-sent Panic that holds the army and the grief that strikes its best men (9.2–3), or picture only the violence of a sea struck from two sides at once. Another sea simile pictures a mind pulled two ways: Nestor wavers between two courses as the sea heaves with a silent swell and rolls neither way until a wind comes down from Zeus (δαϊζόμενος κατὰ θυμὸν διχθάδια, 14.16–21).

The winds “blow from Thrace”, Θρῄκηθεν. Seen from the Troad, Thrace lies north and northwest across the sea, and the North Wind and the West Wind are at home there (see [[thrace]] and the translation's note on 9.4–7). In Book 23 the same pair, called by Achilles' prayer, fan the pyre of Patroclus all night and at dawn go home “over the Thracian sea” (23.192–230).

## In context

The night after the second day of battle begins with the two camps. The Trojans keep their watch on the plain, and the Achaeans are held by “heaven-sent Panic, companion of chilling Rout”, and all the best of them are stricken with unbearable grief (9.1–3; see [[the-second-day-of-battle]]). The simile carries the scene from the Trojan fires to the Achaean hearts. Agamemnon calls the assembly in silence and stands up weeping like a spring of dark water, the second simile of the book (9.9–16; see [[the-spring-of-dark-water]]).

In Book 2 two other winds, the East Wind and the South Wind, raise the long waves of the Icarian sea, and the assembly is stirred like them when Agamemnon proposes flight as a test (2.144–146; see [[waves-of-the-icarian-sea]] and [[the-test-of-the-army]]). There the sea is the army roused by his words. Here it is stirred before anyone speaks, and Agamemnon makes the same proposal in earnest (9.17–28). The closing line returns once more, when Hector falls on the Achaeans at the ships like a wind-fed wave on a swift ship and the sailors tremble: “so the heart in the breasts of the Achaeans was torn” (15.624–629; 15.629 = 9.8). There the Achaeans are the men in the ship, and Hector the wave.
