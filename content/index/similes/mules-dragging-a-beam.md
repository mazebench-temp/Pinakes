## The simile

{{quote:a52af6a8-5c41-5871-ae5d-1b000d6ab89a}}

οἳ δʼ ὥς θʼ ἡμίονοι κρατερὸν μένος ἀμφιβαλόντες / ἕλκωσʼ ἐξ ὄρεος κατὰ παιπαλόεσσαν ἀταρπὸν / ἢ δοκὸν ἠὲ δόρυ μέγα νήϊον, “and they, as mules that have put their strong might into it / drag down from the mountain along a rocky path / a beam or a great ship's timber” (17.742–744). ἡμίονοι, “half-asses”, are mules, the draft animals that pull a plow better than oxen (10.351–353; see [[mules]]). ἀμφιβαλόντες is literally “throwing around”. Walter Leaf takes it as putting their strength on both sides of the beam, “into the work”, as though their strength were something with which they clasped the load; others render “clothing themselves with strength”, but in that sense Homer uses only the middle voice (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.742). παιπαλόεις is “rocky, rugged” (see [[rocky]]), and ἀταρπός a path. A δοκός is a roof beam, and δόρυ νήϊον is ship's timber, the wood for which the builders of another simile cut down the tallest trees in the mountains (13.389–391; see [[the-felled-tree]]).

ἐν δέ τε θυμὸς / τείρεθʼ ὁμοῦ καμάτῳ τε καὶ ἱδρῷ σπευδόντεσσιν, “and their hearts / are worn down by labor and sweat together as they strain” (17.744–745). κάματος and ἱδρώς, “labor” and “sweat”, are the pair that told the fighting over the body in the mist (καμάτῳ δὲ καὶ ἱδρῷ, 17.385; see [[the-stretched-hide]]). The tenor: ὣς οἵ γʼ ἐμμεμαῶτε νέκυν φέρον, “so the two eagerly carried the corpse” (17.746). The point of likeness is heavy labor done with a will: two beasts, a great weight, a long rough way down, and no stopping. The dead man is a load like a timber.

## In context

The two men carrying the body are Menelaus and Meriones, sent by Ajax to lift it while the two Ajaxes fight behind them (17.715–724; see [[menelaus]] and [[meriones]]). Around them the war blazes like a fire that falls on a city (17.735–741; see [[the-burning-city]]), and behind them the Ajaxes hold back the Trojans like a wooded ridge that turns aside the floods of rivers (17.746–753; see [[the-wooded-ridge]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Mules come back to the dead in the last books of the poem. For the funeral of Patroclus, Agamemnon sends men and mules to bring wood from Ida, with Meriones in charge; the men fell the high-leafed oaks and bind the logs to the mules, which make for the plain through the thick brush (23.110–124). The Meriones who here carries the body like a mule dragging timber leads the mules that drag the timber for its pyre. In the last book mules draw Priam's wagon with the ransom (24.324), and they carry Hector's body home to Troy, ἡμίονοι δὲ νέκυν φέρον, in the words that carry the body of Patroclus here (24.697).
