## The simile

{{quote:32b1e342-5888-54a2-877a-19a96799b7c2}}

Automedon throws, and his spear goes through the shield of Aretus and through his belt into the lower belly (17.516–519). Then the simile: ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἂν ὀξὺν ἔχων πέλεκυν αἰζήϊος ἀνὴρ / κόψας ἐξόπιθεν κεράων βοὸς ἀγραύλοιο / ἶνα τάμῃ διὰ πᾶσαν, ὃ δὲ προθορὼν ἐρίπῃσιν, “And as when a man in his prime, holding a sharp axe, / strikes behind the horns of an ox of the fields / and cuts clean through the sinew, and it leaps forward and falls” (17.520–522). αἰζήϊος, “in his prime”, is found only here and once in the Odyssey, both times with ἀνήρ (*Odyssey* 12.83; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.520). The ox is ἄγραυλος, “of the fields”, a beast that lives out at pasture (see [[of-the-fields]]). ἶνα is the accusative of ἴς, “sinew”: the blow behind the horns cuts the sinew of the neck, and the beast leaps forward and drops. πέλεκυς is the axe (see [[axe]]).

The stroke is the first act of a slaughter. Leaf compares the sacrifice at Pylos in the Odyssey, where Thrasymedes, son of Nestor, strikes the cow with the axe and cuts through the sinews of her neck, and the throat is cut afterward (*Odyssey* 3.442–454). In the Iliad's sacrifices only the drawing back of the victims' heads and the cutting of their throats are told (1.459, 2.422; see [[sacrifice]]), and Leaf thinks the blow of the axe was given to the ox only for convenience, with no meaning in the rite (on 17.521).

The tenor takes up the participle: ὣς ἄρʼ ὅ γε προθορὼν πέσεν ὕπτιος· ἐν δέ οἱ ἔγχος / νηδυίοισι μάλʼ ὀξὺ κραδαινόμενον λύε γυῖα, “so he leapt forward and fell on his back; and the spear, / very sharp, quivering in his bowels, loosened his limbs” (17.523–524). προθορών, “leaping forward”, is said of the ox and of the man, but the ox falls forward and the man on his back. The point of likeness is a strong body brought down by a single stroke, with one convulsive spring before it falls.

## In context

Hector sees the horses of Achilles in the field with what he takes for poor charioteers, and goes out with Aeneas to take them, with Chromius and godlike Aretus beside them, hoping to kill the two men and drive off the horses, “the fools” (17.483–498; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[hector]]). Automedon prays to Zeus, tells Alcimedon to keep the horses breathing on his back, and calls the two Ajaxes and Menelaus to help, for “these things lie on the knees of the gods” (17.498–515; see [[automedon]] and [[alcimedon]]). His spear kills Aretus; Hector's misses him and sticks quivering in the ground (17.525–529). The Ajaxes come up and part them before they fight with swords, and Automedon strips the dead man, saying that he has eased his heart a little for Patroclus, though it is a lesser man he has killed. Then he mounts the chariot with his feet and hands bloody, “like a lion that has devoured a bull” (17.530–542; see [[lion-among-cattle]]). Aretus' death is framed by the two likenesses of cattle: he falls like an ox under the axe, and his killer leaves him like a lion over a bull.

## Elsewhere in Homer

Other men die like cattle. Adamas, son of Asius, writhes around the spear in his belly as an ox writhes that herdsmen in the mountains have bound with twisted ropes (13.570–573; see [[the-roped-ox]]); Hippodamas bellows as he breathes out his life, as a bull bellows when young men drag it around the altar of Poseidon (20.403–406); and Sarpedon dies under Patroclus like a bull that a lion kills among the herd (16.487–491; see [[lion-among-cattle]]). In the Odyssey Aegisthus kills Agamemnon at the feast “as one kills an ox at the manger” (*Odyssey* 4.535, 11.411).
