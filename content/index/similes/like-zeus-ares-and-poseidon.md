## The simile

μετὰ δὲ κρείων Ἀγαμέμνων / ὄμματα καὶ κεφαλὴν ἴκελος Διὶ τερπικεραύνῳ, / Ἄρεϊ δὲ ζώνην, στέρνον δὲ Ποσειδάωνι: “and among them was powerful Agamemnon, in eyes and head like Zeus who delights in thunder, in waist like Ares, and in chest like Poseidon”. ὄμματα are the eyes and κεφαλή the head; ζώνη is the belt, and so the waist it goes round; στέρνον is the chest. One adjective, ἴκελος (“like”), serves for all three gods, and the accusatives say in which part he resembles each.

It is a comparison of appearance, of stature and bearing, not of deeds. The poem does not explain the choice of parts, but two are easy to read: the eyes and head of [[Zeus]], the king of the gods, whose nod decides (1.528–530), and the waist of [[Ares]], the god of war, where a fighter's belt is fastened. [[Poseidon]] lends the chest.

## In context

{{quote:f4d7205e-a9c2-590b-97df-ff5395c7a1ba}}

The run of similes has moved from the army to its leaders ([[goatherds-sorting-their-flocks]]). Now, in the same sentence, it moves from the leaders to one man. The next simile completes the picture: a bull above the herd ([[bull-above-the-herd]]). Then the Muses are asked for the names of the leaders ([[invocation-of-the-muses]]).

Agamemnon's first simile in the poem was “and his eyes were like blazing fire”, as he rose in anger against Calchas (1.104; [[eyes-like-fire]]). Now his eyes are like Zeus'. The splendor comes on the day of the lying Dream ([[agamemnons-dream]]), soon after Zeus has accepted his sacrifice and refused his prayer to take Troy before sunset (2.412–420).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the next book Priam, watching from the wall, picks Agamemnon out by his bearing. Others are taller by a head, but he has never seen a man so handsome and so majestic; he looks like a king (Iliad 3.166–170). Odysseus is measured against him in the same scene, part by part: shorter by a head, but broader in the shoulders and the chest (στέρνοισιν, the word used here of Poseidon's chest; 3.193–194).
