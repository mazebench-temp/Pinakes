## The simile

{{quote:c48aa658-b12a-5e12-905b-3d1c496b631b}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτε τίς τε κύων συὸς ἀγρίου ἠὲ λέοντος / ἅπτηται κατόπισθε: “as when some hound catches at a wild boar or a lion from behind”. ἅπτομαι with the genitive is “lay hold of”, and ἰσχία τε γλουτούς τε, “hips and buttocks”, are the parts it snaps at as it runs. ἑλισσόμενόν τε δοκεύει: the hound “watches for it as it wheels”. ἑλίσσομαι is to turn round, and δοκεύω to keep an eye on: the dog watches for the moment when the beast turns on it. ὤπαζε, “pressed hard on”, is the word of pursuit. αἰὲν ἀποκτείνων τὸν ὀπίστατον, “always killing the hindmost”: ὀπίστατος, a superlative formed from ὄπισθε, “behind”, is the last man in the flight.

The quarry is the point of the comparison. A boar or a lion is a beast that fights: the boar stands at bay and the lion attacks, and in the similes they are the pictures of a warrior's courage (see [[boar]], [[lion]] and [[lions-and-boars]]). The dog does not face them. It runs behind and bites, and it must look out for the turn. So the Achaeans are still dangerous, and they are running; Hector harries them from behind and picks off the last. The turn the hound watches for comes only at the ships. When the Achaeans have crossed the stakes and the ditch, they halt beside the ships, call to one another and to all the gods, and lift up their hands in prayer (8.343–347; see [[the-achaean-wall]]).

## In context

The second Trojan advance of the day begins as the first did, with Zeus: “And again the Olympian roused might in the Trojans” (8.335; see [[zeus]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). The rally of the Achaeans, with Teucer shooting from under Ajax's shield, has ended when Hector felled Teucer with a stone (8.266–334; see [[teucer]]). The dog has already been named. Teucer, when eight of his arrows had found their men, said that he could not hit “this mad dog” (κύνα λυσσητῆρα, 8.299), and the narrator now makes a hound of Hector in earnest (see [[dogs]]). Hector ends the day by calling the Achaeans dogs in turn, “dogs borne along by the fates” (8.527).

The phrase that introduces him belongs to the beasts. Hector “went among the foremost, exulting in his strength” (σθένεϊ βλεμεαίνων, 8.337; see [[exulting-in-strength]]). In Book 12 the same words describe a boar or a lion that turns among dogs and huntsmen, and there the beast is Hector (12.41–49); in Book 17 they describe a lion standing over its cubs (17.133–136). Here the beast's phrase is given to the hound. The next lines give Hector another face, as he wheels his horses this way and that “with the eyes of the Gorgon or of Ares, ruin of mortals” (8.348–349; see [[gorgon]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The hunting scene of this simile recurs from both sides. Hector sets the Trojans on the Achaeans as a hunter sets his white-toothed dogs on a wild boar or a lion (11.292–295). In the fight over the body of Patroclus the Trojans rush at the retreating Achaeans like dogs that run at a wounded boar ahead of the young huntsmen, until it wheels on them in its strength (ἑλίξεται, 17.728) and they scatter; so the Trojans fall back when the two Ajaxes turn (17.725–734). The line that ends the pursuit here, “always killing the hindmost”, ends a lion simile for Agamemnon in Book 11, where the Trojans flee like cattle (11.172–178).

Other dogs of the similes chase weaker game. Antilochus springs on a Trojan like a dog on a wounded fawn (15.579–581), and Diomedes and Odysseus run down Dolon like two sharp-toothed hounds after a fawn or a hare (10.360–364). The last of these pursuits reverses this one. Achilles chases Hector around the walls of Troy as a dog chases a fawn through the glens and ravines, tracking it when it hides under a bush (22.188–193), and the hound of Book 8 has become the fawn (see [[deer]]).
