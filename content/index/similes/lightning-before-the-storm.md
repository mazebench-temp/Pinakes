## The simile

{{quote:ed793849-5d8b-5874-9ef8-e9fa2df7e338}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἂν ἀστράπτῃ πόσις Ἥρης ἠϋκόμοιο / τεύχων: “as when the husband of Hera of the lovely hair flashes his lightning, making ready …”. ἀστράπτω, “flash lightning”, is the act of Zeus wherever Homer uses the verb (2.353, 9.237). τεύχων, “making ready”, looks forward: the flashes come before what they announce, a heavy, endless rain (ὄμβρον ἀθέσφατον, as at 3.4), hail, or snow, “when the snowflakes sprinkle the fields” (ἐπάλυνεν, a gnomic aorist). παλύνω is the verb for sprinkling barley meal (11.640, 18.560), and the snow is strewn over the plowland in the same way. The last member leaves the weather: ἠέ ποθι πτολέμοιο μέγα στόμα πευκεδανοῖο, “or somewhere the great mouth of bitter war”. στόμα is the mouth of battle, where the fighting is joined (πολέμου στόμα, 19.313; ὑσμίνης στόμα, 20.359), and πευκεδανός, “bitter”, occurs only here (see the translation's note on 10.5–8).

The tenor is short: ὣς πυκίνʼ ἐν στήθεσσιν ἀνεστενάχιζʼ Ἀγαμέμνων / νειόθεν ἐκ κραδίης, “so thick and fast did Agamemnon groan in his breast, from the depths of his heart”. πυκινά, “thick, close-set”, is the point of the likeness: the groans come one on another like flashes in a storm sky. ἀνεστενάχιζε is formed from στενάχω, “groan”, with ἀνά, “up, aloud”, and νειόθεν, “from the bottom”, occurs only here. Then the king's mind trembles within him (10.10).

The comparison is of frequency, but the vehicle says more than the tenor needs. Lightning in the Iliad is a sign. Zeus flashed on the right for the Achaeans when they sailed (2.353), and on this very night, Odysseus has told Achilles, he flashes on the right for the Trojans (9.236–237). The storm that Agamemnon's groans foretell is not named, and the list ends in war.

## In context

The book opens with the chiefs asleep and their king awake (10.1–4; see [[the-doloneia]]). The simile follows at once, and the next lines give its cause. When Agamemnon looks out over the plain he marvels at the many Trojan fires and at the sound of flutes and pipes; when he looks at his own ships and army, he tears out his hair “to Zeus on high”, and his glorious heart groans greatly (10.11–16; see the translation's note on 10.15–16). His fear is the same when he tells it to Nestor: his heart leaps out of his breast and his limbs tremble beneath him (10.93–95; see [[agamemnon]]).

The two night books both measure the king's grief by a force of nature. In Book 9 he stood up in the assembly shedding tears like a spring of dark water and spoke “groaning heavily” (9.14–16; see [[the-spring-of-dark-water]]). Here the groans are lightning, and the god whose flashes they match is the god whose mind, he says, “has turned” (10.45; see [[zeus]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The bronze of the Achaeans shines “like the lightning of father Zeus” later in the same book, and so does Hector's armor in Book 11 (10.153–154, 11.65–66; see [[like-lightning]]). Snow is Zeus' work in the similes too. Stones fly in battle as thick as the snowflakes that Zeus pours down on a winter's day, showing his shafts, until he has covered mountains, fields and shores (12.278–286), and the helmets and shields pour out from the ships like snowflakes that fly from Zeus under the blast of the North Wind (19.357–360). Iris flies from Ida as snow or hail flies from the clouds (15.170–172). See [[storm-cloud]] and [[snowflakes-of-winter]].
