## The simile

{{quote:29539010-7a4a-576b-94ed-e42b7a61efb0}}

ὅσση δʼ ὑψορόφοιο θύρη θαλάμοιο τέτυκται / ἀνέρος ἀφνειοῖο ἐῢ κληῗσʼ ἀραρυῖα, / τόσσʼ ἄρα τοῦ ἑκάτερθεν ἔσαν πτερά: “As wide as the door of the high-roofed chamber / of a wealthy man is made, well fitted with bolts, / so wide were its wings on either side” (24.317–319). It is a simile of measure, built on the pair ὅσση … τόσσα, “as wide … so wide”, and it says nothing of the bird's flight; that follows in plain words. Homer takes such measures from the works and doings of men. The Odyssey measures Odysseus' raft by the floor of a broad freight ship that a man well skilled in carpentry marks out (*Odyssey* 5.249–251; see [[the-building-of-the-raft]]) and the Cyclops' club by the mast of a black ship of twenty oars (*Odyssey* 9.322–324); the Iliad measures distance by a throw (23.431–433; see [[the-cast-of-a-spear]]) and the leap of Hera's horses by what a man can see from a lookout (5.770–772; see [[the-lookout]]).

ὑψόροφος, “high-roofed”, is said in the Iliad of only two other rooms, Helen's chamber (3.423) and the chamber from which Priam has just taken the ransom: “And he himself went down into the fragrant chamber, / high-roofed, of cedar, which held many bright trinkets” (24.191–192). A θάλαμος is an inner room, a bedchamber or a storeroom, and its door is shut with κληῗδες, “bolts”: Hephaestus fitted Hera's chamber “with a secret bolt, which no other god could open” (14.168), and the gates of the Achaean wall had “a single bolt” over their two bars (12.456). In the Odyssey the high-roofed θάλαμος of Odysseus, where gold and bronze and clothing and oil are stored, is shut by close-fitted double doors and kept night and day by a housekeeper (*Odyssey* 2.337–345). The text has ἐῢ κληῗσʼ, “well (fitted) with bolts”, the reading of Trypho; Aristarchus preferred ἐϋκλήϊς, “well-bolted”, as the manuscripts have it, and Walter Leaf finds little to choose between them, though he notes that elsewhere a door has only one κληΐς (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.318). The wealthy man, ἀνὴρ ἀφνειός, comes back in the book: the exile who has killed a man comes “to the house of a wealthy man” (24.482; see [[the-killer-in-exile]]), and the father that Hermes invents for himself is “wealthy, and an old man”, like Priam (24.398).

The point of likeness is size. Hecuba told Priam to ask for the bird “whose strength is greatest” (24.293), and Priam asked for it in the same words (24.311); the breadth of its wings shows that Zeus has sent it. The measure comes from the house that Priam is leaving. The doors and their bars are also what his journey must pass: Hermes will open the gates of the camp and push back the bars (24.446), and then the single bar of fir on the door of Achilles' courtyard, which it took three Achaeans to drive home and Achilles drove home alone (24.453–457); a mortal, Achilles tells him, could not “easily push back the bar of our doors” (24.567).

After the simile: εἴσατο δέ σφι / δεξιὸς ἀΐξας διὰ ἄστεος· οἳ δὲ ἰδόντες / γήθησαν, καὶ πᾶσιν ἐνὶ φρεσὶ θυμὸς ἰάνθη, “and it appeared to them / darting on the right across the city; and when they saw it / they rejoiced, and the heart in the breasts of all was warmed” (24.319–321). A bird on the right is a good sign (see [[eagle]] and [[divination]]). Most manuscripts have ὑπὲρ ἄστεος, “over the city”; Leaf prefers διὰ ἄστεος because ἄστυ keeps its initial digamma so regularly, and he compares the two eagles of Ithaca, which darted “on the right through the houses and the city” (on 24.320; *Odyssey* 2.154). ἰάνθη, “was warmed”, is the verb of what the ransom is for: Priam is to bring Achilles “gifts … that may warm his heart” (24.119, 24.147, 24.176, 24.196).

## In context

Before Priam sets out, Hecuba comes to him with wine in a golden cup and tells him to pour a libation and pray to Zeus that he may come home, and to ask for a bird of omen on the right; if Zeus will not send it, she would not urge him to go, however eager he is (24.283–298; see [[hecuba]]). Priam agrees, “for it is good to lift up one's hands to Zeus, in the hope that he may have pity” (24.301). He washes his hands, prays standing in the middle of the enclosure, pours the wine, and asks for the sign in Hecuba's words (24.302–313; see [[libation]] and [[prayer]]). “So he spoke in prayer, and Zeus the counselor heard him, / and at once he sent an eagle, surest in omen of winged birds, / the dark hunter, which men also call the dusky eagle” (24.314–316; see [[surest-of-birds]]). The simile follows, and then the journey: Priam mounts his chariot and drives out through the city behind the mule wagon, his family lamenting him as if he were going to his death (24.322–328). Zeus, who sent the sign, sees the two old men come out on the plain, pities Priam, and sends Hermes to guide him (24.331–338; see [[hermes]]).

Walter Leaf reports Bergk's remark that praying for an omen is otherwise found only in the Odyssey, and that the Iliad knows only signs that come unasked (on 24.290). In the Odyssey Nestor tells how the Achaeans sailing home asked the god to show them a sign (*Odyssey* 3.173), and Odysseus asks Zeus for a word from within the house and a sign from outside it; the verse that follows is the verse of Priam's prayer, “So he spoke in prayer, and Zeus the counselor heard him” (*Odyssey* 20.98–102; 24.314).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The verse of the sign, “at once he sent an eagle, surest in omen of winged birds”, is used once more in the Iliad, when Zeus answers Agamemnon's prayer with an eagle that drops a fawn beside his altar (8.247; see [[surest-of-birds]]). The eagle of Book 12 flies on the left and drops a serpent among the Trojans (12.200–209; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]]), and the eagle that answers Ajax' challenge flies on the right (13.821–823). The joy that greets Priam's eagle is told in the same verses in the Odyssey, when an eagle with a goose in its talons darts on the right in front of the horses of Telemachus and Peisistratus as they leave Sparta: “and when they saw it / they rejoiced, and the heart in the breasts of all was warmed” (*Odyssey* 15.164–165; Leaf on 24.320). There too the sign is given at a departure, and Helen reads it as the return of Odysseus (*Odyssey* 15.172–178).

## Outside Homer

[[aristotle|Aristotle]], listing the kinds of eagle, names a second kind, the πλάγγος, next in size and strength to the first, which lives in glens and ravines and by lakes and is also called the duck-killer and the μορφνός, “which Homer too mentions in the going out of Priam” (*History of Animals* 9.32; see [[eagle]]).
