## The simile

{{quote:adacfdfa-2949-5e57-9123-1e4ab1a36b1d}}

ἀλλὰ τί ἢ ἔριδας καὶ νείκεα νῶϊν ἀνάγκη / νεικεῖν ἀλλήλοισιν ἐναντίον ὥς τε γυναῖκας, / αἵ τε χολωσάμεναι ἔριδος πέρι θυμοβόροιο / νεικεῦσʼ ἀλλήλῃσι μέσην ἐς ἄγυιαν ἰοῦσαι / πόλλʼ ἐτεά τε καὶ οὐκί· χόλος δέ τε καὶ τὰ κελεύει: “But what need is there for strife and quarrels between us two, / to quarrel with each other face to face, like women / who in their anger over heart-devouring strife / go out into the middle of the street and quarrel with each other, / saying many things, true and not true; for anger bids them say these too” (20.251–255). ἔρις and νεῖκος, “strife” and “quarrel”, are the words of the Iliad's quarrels (see [[eris]] and [[neikos]]), and νεικέω, “quarrel with”, is said twice, of the heroes and of the women (20.252, 20.254). Walter Leaf takes γυναῖκας as drawn into the accusative by the infinitive, and thinks the variant γυναῖκες may be right (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.252). The women are χολωσάμεναι, “in their anger” (see [[cholos]]), and their strife is θυμοβόρος, “heart-devouring” (see [[heart-devouring]]): ἔριδος πέρι θυμοβόροιο ends the verse in which Hector imagines what men will say of his duel with Ajax, “They fought in heart-devouring strife” (7.301), and the verse in which Patroclus and Sarpedon come together again (16.476); Achilles gave the same name to his quarrel with Agamemnon, “that we two, grieving at heart, / raged in heart-devouring strife for the sake of a girl” (θυμοβόρῳ ἔριδι, 19.57–58). The ἀγυιά is the street of a city, as in the streets of Ilios that Heracles widowed and the well-built streets through which Hector hurries back from his house (5.642, 6.391).

The point of likeness is abuse given and returned, in public, with no care for the truth: πόλλʼ ἐτεά τε καὶ οὐκί, “many things, true and not true”, because anger bids them say even what is false. The heroes have been doing the same. Achilles has taunted Aeneas with his hopes and his flight, and Aeneas has answered that he too knows how to taunt, and set out his lineage; now he calls the exchange a thing for women in the street, and ends it. ἐναντίον, “face to face”, is said of the quarrel he refuses and, three verses on, of the fight he wants: “You will not turn me from courage with words, eager as I am, / until we have fought face to face with the bronze” (20.256–257). Then he throws his spear (20.259).

## In context

Achilles has asked Aeneas whether he hopes to win the honor of Priam or a domain from the Trojans, and reminded him of the day he chased him from his cattle on Ida (20.178–198; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]] and [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]]). Aeneas answers that he too knows how to speak taunts, and gives his lineage from Zeus and Dardanus (20.200–241; see [[aeneas]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]). Then he turns against talk itself. “But come, let us no longer talk like this, like children” (20.244; see [[children-and-widows]]); there are insults enough for both of them, more than a ship of a hundred benches could carry; the tongue of mortals is a twisting thing, and “Whatever kind of word you speak, such will you hear back” (20.246–250; see [[oneidos]], [[the-tongue-of-mortals]] and [[such-will-you-hear-back]]). The women in the street show that last saying at work: each answers the other's abuse with more.

Leaf reports from the scholia that Aristarchus athetized 251–255 as untimely and tiresome after “let us no longer talk like this” (244), and their words as unworthy of the speakers; the same note adds that among barbarians, as among the Egyptians, the women go out and abuse one another. Leaf himself finds the comparison the most vigorous passage in the speech, and as fitting to the heroic age, when women were comparatively free, as to the Egyptians; but he takes the contracted νεικεῦσι for a late form, and ἔριδος πέρι θυμοβόροιο, the strife of warriors, for almost a travesty of its use elsewhere (on 20.251; see the translation's note on 20.251–255). Aristarchus also read πολλά τʼ ἐόντα καὶ οὐκί for πόλλʼ ἐτεά τε καὶ οὐκί (Leaf on 20.255).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Women, and children, are the Iliad's likenesses of shame for men who talk instead of fighting. The Achaeans wail to go home “like young children or widowed women” (2.289–290; see [[children-and-widows]]), and Hector tells Ajax, “do not test me as if I were some feeble boy / or a woman, who does not know the works of war” (7.235–236); when Diomedes turns his chariot to flee, Hector taunts him, “you are no better than a woman after all” (8.163; see [[women-and-war]]). The quarrel is a scene from the life of peace, like the other quarrel of the similes, two men contending over the boundary stones of a common field with measuring rods in their hands (12.421–424; see [[the-boundary-dispute]]), and like the quarrel in the place of assembly of the city at peace on the Shield, where two men dispute the penalty for a man who has perished, while the women stand at their doors to watch a wedding (18.495–508; see [[the-two-cities]]). Before his last fight Hector, alone outside the gate, thinks that there is no way now to chat with Achilles from an oak or a rock, as a girl and a young man chat with each other (22.126–128): another picture of ordinary speech that has no place between these enemies.
