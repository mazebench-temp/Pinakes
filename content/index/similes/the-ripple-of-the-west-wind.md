## The simile

{{quote:685c9c73-21af-57fb-a5c4-081263896f79}}

οἵη δὲ Ζεφύροιο ἐχεύατο πόντον ἔπι φρὶξ / ὀρνυμένοιο νέον, μελάνει δέ τε πόντος ὑπʼ αὐτῆς, / τοῖαι ἄρα στίχες εἵατʼ Ἀχαιῶν τε Τρώων τε / ἐν πεδίῳ. A φρίξ is the ripple that a breeze raises on smooth water, the rough, dark surface that comes before the waves. The word belongs with φρίσσω, “bristle”, and the line before the simile has already used the verb of the men: their ranks sat close-packed, “bristling with shields and helmets and spears” (πεφρικυῖαι, 7.61–62). The metaphor turns into the simile. Spear points and helmet crests roughen the mass of men as the ripple roughens the sea. ἐχεύατο, “spreads”, is literally “pours itself”. ὀρνυμένοιο νέον, “when it has just risen”, fixes the moment when the wind first touches the water, and μελάνει, “grows dark”, found only here in Homer, gives the color. οἵη … τοῖαι, “as … so”, joins the two halves.

The points of likeness are the dark mass, the rough surface and the stillness before movement. The armies are not marching. They sit and wait, as the sea darkens before the waves rise.

## In context

The scene is a picture of an audience. Hector stops the Trojan battalions, Agamemnon makes the Achaeans sit, and Athena and Apollo sit on the oak of Zeus “like birds, like vultures” (7.54–61; see [[like-vultures]]). The two similes follow each other, and the second is a sight for eyes that look down: a broad surface darkening under the wind, as the gods on the high oak might see the ranks below. Then Hector speaks “between the two armies” (7.66; see [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]).

The West Wind is the wind of the Iliad's army similes. It bends a deep field of grain as the assembly rushes for the ships (2.147–149; see [[wind-in-the-wheat]]), drives a storm cloud over the sea as the Ajaxes lead their battalions (4.275–282; see [[storm-cloud]]), and drives breaker after breaker on the shore as the Danaans move into battle (4.422–428; see [[waves-on-the-shore]]). In Book 4 the armies are moving and the wind is a gale. Here it has only just risen, and the armies are sitting still. The storm is the fight to come.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The dark ripple returns in other seas. In Book 9 the West Wind and the North Wind stir up the fish-filled sea and heap up the dark wave, as the Achaeans' hearts are torn by grief (9.4–8). Achilles tells the dead Lycaon that a fish will dart up “under the dark ripple” to feed on him (21.126–127), and another leaps up “under the ripple of the North Wind” in the simile for Euryalus felled by a boxer's blow (23.692–694). In the Odyssey Proteus comes out of the sea “under the breath of the West Wind, covered by the dark ripple” (*Odyssey* 4.402). The bristling armies have a parallel in Book 13: “the man-destroying battle bristled with the long spears” that the men held (ἔφριξεν, 13.339–340).
