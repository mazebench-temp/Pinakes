## The simile

{{quote:9cfee6b1-8244-56d1-b556-ed5a9fd89b57}}

δὴ τότε μιν τρὶς τόσσον ἕλεν μένος ὥς τε λέοντα: “but now three times as great a fury seized him, like a lion”. μένος is the fury of battle here (see [[menos]]). The lion is one ὅν ῥά τε ποιμὴν ἀγρῷ ἐπʼ εἰροπόκοις ὀΐεσσι / χραύσῃ μέν τʼ αὐλῆς ὑπεράλμενον οὐδὲ δαμάσσῃ, “that a shepherd in the fields, watching over his woolly sheep, has grazed as it leapt over the wall of the fold, but has not killed”. χραύω, “graze, scratch”, occurs only here in Homer. The αὐλή is the walled yard of a farm, here the sheepfold, and the lion is wounded as it leaps over the wall (ὑπεράλμενον).

The wound does not stop the lion. The shepherd “has roused its strength” (σθένος ὦρσεν), and then he does not defend the flock but “slips into the farm buildings” (κατὰ σταθμοὺς δύεται). τὰ δʼ ἐρῆμα φοβεῖται: the sheep, “left undefended” (ἐρῆμα, “deserted, alone”), flee in panic. Some take the shepherd as the subject, fleeing the lonely places (see the translation note on 5.140). The ewes “lie heaped close one upon another” (ἀγχιστῖναι … κέχυνται), huddled in fright or killed; the same adjective is used of men falling dead one beside another (17.361; Odyssey 22.118). Then the lion, ἐμμεμαώς, “in his fury”, leaps back out of the deep, high-walled fold.

The tenor takes up the lion's word: ὣς μεμαὼς Τρώεσσι μίγη κρατερὸς Διομήδης, “so furiously did mighty Diomedes close with the Trojans” (5.143). It also looks back to the line before the simile, where he was eager to fight the Trojans “even before” (καὶ πρίν περ, 5.135). The lion was hungry before it was hurt.

The point of likeness is a wound that does not kill and only makes the beast more dangerous. The shepherd who hurts the lion and then hides is Pandarus, whose arrow hit Diomedes in the shoulder: “but the swift shaft had not overcome him” (5.106). The verb of the simile, οὐδὲ δαμάσσῃ, “has not killed” (5.138), is the verb of the archer's failure, which Pandarus himself repeats: “and yet I did not overcome him” (5.191), and to Diomedes' face, “so my swift shaft did not overcome you after all” (5.278). The flock left undefended is the Trojan army.

## In context

Pandarus shot Diomedes in the right shoulder through the plate of his corselet and boasted that he would not hold out long (5.95–105). Diomedes stepped back to his chariot, and Sthenelus drew out the arrow while the blood spurted through his tunic (5.106–113). He prayed to Athena. She made his limbs light, put his father's might in his breast, took the mist from his eyes so that he could tell god from man, and forbade him to fight any god but Aphrodite (5.114–132; see [[prayer-type-scene]] and [[diomedes]]). The simile follows at once, and so does the killing: Astynous and Hypeiron, the two sons of the dream-reader Eurydamas, the two sons of old Phaenops, and two sons of Priam, where a second lion simile marks the last pair (5.144–165; see [[lion-among-cattle]]).

Wound, prayer and renewal belong to the pattern of a hero's great day (see [[the-aristeia-of-diomedes]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Diomedes is a lion among the flocks again in Book 10, when he falls on the sleeping Thracians “as a lion, coming on flocks that have no herdsman, on goats or sheep, leaps on them with evil intent” (10.485–486). Sarpedon goes against the Achaean wall like a mountain lion long without meat, which makes for the close-built fold; even if it finds herdsmen there guarding the sheep with dogs and spears, it will not be driven off untried, but either seizes a sheep or is itself struck by a javelin among the first (12.299–306).

The wounded lion returns when Achilles goes against Aeneas in Book 20. He is like a ravaging lion that a whole people gathers to kill. At first it goes on disdainfully, but when a young man strikes it with a spear, it crouches with gaping jaws and foam about its teeth, lashes its flanks with its tail, and rushes straight on in its fury, to kill or be killed (20.164–175).
