## The simile

{{quote:537bd8aa-05ca-50af-b8a4-ab70dbc49059}}

τὼ μὲν ἄρα προπάροιθε πυλάων ὑψηλάων / ἕστασαν ὡς ὅτε τε δρύες οὔρεσιν ὑψικάρηνοι: “These two, in front of the high gates, stood like oaks in the mountains, with lofty crowns” (12.131–132). δρῦς is the oak, and ὑψικάρηνος, “high-headed”, occurs only here. The oaks αἵ τʼ ἄνεμον μίμνουσι καὶ ὑετὸν ἤματα πάντα, “withstand the wind and the rain all their days”, ῥίζῃσιν μεγάλῃσι διηνεκέεσσʼ ἀραρυῖαι, “made fast by great roots that reach far down” (12.133–134). διηνεκής is “running on, unbroken”. ἀραρυῖα, “fitted, made fast”, is the participle of joinery: the Achaeans were to build “gates that fit well” in their wall (πύλας … εὖ ἀραρυίας, 7.339), and the doors that Hector breaks at the end of the book are “fitted close and strong” (12.454).

The tenor repeats the verb of the trees. The oaks μίμνουσι, “withstand”, and the two men μίμνον ἐπερχόμενον μέγαν Ἄσιον οὐδὲ φέβοντο, “awaited great Asius as he came on, and did not flee” (12.136). They trust “in their hands and their strength” (χείρεσσι πεποιθότες ἠδὲ βίηφι, 12.135), as the trees hold by their roots. The point of likeness is steadfastness. The oaks do not move in the storm, and the men do not move from the gate.

## In context

Asius alone of the Trojans would not leave his chariot, and drove for the gate on the left of the ships, which men held open for companions fleeing from the plain; the narrator has already said that he will never go home (12.108–126; see [[asius]]). At the gate he finds the two Lapiths, Polypoetes, son of Peirithous, and Leonteus (12.127–130; see [[polypoetes]], [[leonteus]] and [[lapiths]]). The oaks are the first of three images for the pair. When the Trojans rush the wall, the two dart out and fight in front of the gates like two wild boars, and Asius complains to Zeus that they hold the gate like wasps defending their nest (12.141–172; see [[wild-boars-in-the-mountains]] and [[wasps-at-the-roadside]]). They hold it, and bring down eight Trojans (12.182–194).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Steadfastness has two close likenesses in the Iliad, and both end in the verse “so the Danaans awaited the Trojans steadfastly and did not flee” (5.527 = 15.622), which ends like 12.136. In Book 5 the Danaans stand like clouds that Zeus has set “in windless weather on the tops of the mountains”, while the winds sleep (5.522–527). In Book 15 they hold like a great sheer rock by the gray sea that withstands (μένει) the swift paths of the shrill winds and the swelling waves (15.618–622). The oaks withstand the wind and the rain in the same way.

Asius himself falls like a tree. In Book 13, on foot in front of his horses, he is struck in the throat by Idomeneus' spear and falls as an oak falls, or a white poplar, or a tall pine that carpenters cut in the mountains to be a ship's timber (13.384–393). The same verses tell the fall of Sarpedon (16.482–486), and Hector, struck by Ajax's stone, goes down like an oak uprooted by the thunderbolt of Zeus (14.414–418). In the similes trees usually fall; these two stand.
