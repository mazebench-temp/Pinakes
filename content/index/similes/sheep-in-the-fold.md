## The simile

Τρῶες δʼ, ὥς τʼ ὄϊες πολυπάμονος ἀνδρὸς ἐν αὐλῇ / μυρίαι ἑστήκασιν ἀμελγόμεναι γάλα λευκὸν / ἀζηχὲς μεμακυῖαι ἀκούουσαι ὄπα ἀρνῶν, / ὣς Τρώων ἀλαλητὸς ἀνὰ στρατὸν εὐρὺν ὀρώρει: “But the Trojans, like sheep in the fold of a man of many possessions, that stand in their thousands while they are milked of their white milk, bleating ceaselessly as they hear the voices of their lambs, so the shouting of the Trojans rose through the wide army”.

ὄϊες are sheep ([[sheep]]), and the words that go with them are feminine: these are ewes. πολυπάμων, “of many possessions”, from πᾶμα, “possession”, occurs only here in Homer. αὐλή is the yard or fold, and μυρίαι “countless”. μεμακυῖαι is “bleating”, ἀζηχές “without pause”, and ὄψ the voice of the lambs ([[lambs]]). ἀλαλητός is the shouting of an army.

The sentence begins with the Trojans and breaks off: “But the Trojans, like sheep…”. After the picture the subject is taken up again as “the shouting of the Trojans”. The point of likeness is sound, a noise made of many voices, each calling on its own. At milking the ewes are kept apart from their young; the Cyclops milks his ewes and goats and only then puts a young one under each (Odyssey 9.244–245). Every mother calls to her own lamb, and the fold is loud with a thousand cries that do not make one shout.

Then the narrator explains: οὐ γὰρ πάντων ἦεν ὁμὸς θρόος οὐδʼ ἴα γῆρυς, / ἀλλὰ γλῶσσα μέμικτο, πολύκλητοι δʼ ἔσαν ἄνδρες, “for not all of them had the same speech or one language, but their tongues were mingled, and the men were called from many lands”. θρόος is noise or talk, γῆρυς voice or speech, γλῶσσα tongue. πολύκλητοι, “called from many places”, are the allies summoned to defend Troy (see [[trojan-allies]]).

## In context

{{quote:8e90d374-4124-5071-8bdb-c4ae78bdeca9}}

The simile answers the silent advance of the Danaans, who move like waves driven onto a shore (4.422–432; see [[waves-on-the-shore]]). The Achaeans are silent because they fear their commanders, and the Trojans are loud because they share no language. The contrast of Book 3's opening returns with new pictures. There the Trojans came on “with clamor and with cries, like birds”, and the Achaeans in silence (3.1–9; see [[cranes-and-pygmies]]). But the Trojan noise of Book 3 was a threat: the cranes fly to bring slaughter to the Pygmies. The noise of Book 4 is the noise of a farm, of tame animals and a rich man's wealth.

Iris had warned Hector of the difficulty in Book 2: many are the allies in Priam's city, “and the tongues of men scattered far and wide are many and various”, so let each leader give orders to his own men (2.803–806). The Catalogue calls the Carians “of barbarous speech” (2.867). The Trojan army is a coalition, and the simile hears it as a flock. The Achaean army has its own divisions, by tribe and by clan, as Nestor advised (2.362–363), but here it moves as one.

## Elsewhere in Homer

In Book 3 the flock was Priam's picture of the Achaean ranks, with Odysseus going through them like a thick-fleeced ram through a great flock of white sheep (3.196–198; see [[ram-among-the-sheep]]). The Trojans are a flock again in Book 13, following Aeneas as sheep follow the ram to water (13.491–494). In the similes of the later books the flock is prey: a lion leaps into a fold of woolly sheep (5.136–142), and wolves fall on lambs and kids scattered through a shepherd's carelessness (16.352–355). The Odyssey has a noise of the same kind: on Circe's island Odysseus' companions crowd around him as calves run lowing around their mothers when the cows come back to the fold (Odyssey 10.410–415).
