## The simile

{{quote:99962af3-b84d-52c3-a71a-320e97d226e2}}

ὠρέξατο χερσὶ φίλῃσιν / οὐδʼ ἔλαβε· ψυχὴ δὲ κατὰ χθονὸς ἠΰτε καπνὸς / ᾤχετο τετριγυῖα: “and reached out with his own hands, / but did not take hold of it; and the soul went beneath the earth like smoke, / gibbering” (23.99–101). The ghost had asked for his hand (23.75), and Achilles had asked it to stand nearer, “let us put our arms around each other, if only for a little while” (23.97). His hands close on nothing. Smoke is what can be seen and not held; it thins and is gone. This smoke goes down, κατὰ χθονός, “beneath the earth”, the way of a soul to Hades: when Patroclus died, “his soul flew from his limbs and went to Hades” (16.856 = 22.362, of Hector). τετριγυῖα, “gibbering”, is the soul's thin cry, the sound the suitors' souls make in the Odyssey as they follow Hermes down like bats in a cave (*Odyssey* 24.5–9; see the translation's note on 23.101). Achilles springs up and strikes his hands together: “Ah, truly even in the house of Hades there is some / soul and phantom, but there is no mind in it at all” (23.103–104; see [[psyche]], [[eidolon]] and [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]]).

The Odyssey gives the same lesson to Odysseus in the land of the dead. Three times he tries to take hold of his mother's soul, and three times it flies from his hands “like a shadow or a dream” (σκιῇ εἴκελον ἢ καὶ ὀνείρῳ), and she tells him that when life leaves the white bones the soul flies off like a dream (ψυχὴ δʼ ἠύτʼ ὄνειρος, *Odyssey* 11.204–208, 11.221–222).

## The other smoke

The only other “like smoke” in Homer is also spoken by Achilles in his grief for Patroclus. In Book 18 he wishes that strife might perish from among gods and men, and anger, “which, far sweeter than dripping honey, / swells in the breasts of men like smoke” (18.107–110; see [[sweeter-than-honey]]). There the smoke rises and fills a man, the anger that kept Achilles from the war while Patroclus died; here it sinks away, the friend he cannot hold. Elsewhere smoke goes up from a besieged city to the sky, a likeness for the blaze about Achilles' head at the trench and for the sorrows he brings on the Trojans (18.207–214, 21.522–525; see [[smoke-from-a-burning-city]]), and Odysseus on Calypso's island longs only to see the smoke leaping up from his own land (*Odyssey* 1.57–59).

## Outside Homer

[[plato|Plato]]'s Socrates counts the soul that went beneath the earth like smoke, gibbering, among the verses about Hades that he would strike out of poetry for his guardians (*Republic* 3.387a; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]). [[virgil|Virgil]] gives the simile to Anchises, whose likeness glides down from the sky to speak to his son (*Aeneid* 5.722–723). When the dawn is near, “he spoke, and fled like thin smoke into the air” (*dixerat et tenuis fugit ceu fumus in auras*, *Aeneid* 5.739–740), and Aeneas cries after him, “Who keeps you from our embrace?” (*quis te nostris complexibus arcet?*, *Aeneid* 5.741–742).
