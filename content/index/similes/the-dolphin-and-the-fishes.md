## The simile

{{quote:37b22ff1-fe2f-5361-ba51-6ecb22dc3c8d}}

δελφίς, the dolphin, is named only here in the Iliad; in the *Odyssey* Scylla fishes for dolphins and dogfish from her cave (*Odyssey* 12.95–96). μεγακήτης, “huge-bellied”, is said of ships, “huge in the hull” (8.222, 11.5, 11.600), and of the sea once in the *Odyssey* (*Odyssey* 3.158). Walter Leaf takes it as “with mighty hollow”, here “with mighty maw”, and reports Jordan's derivation from κῆτος in its ordinary sense, “monster”, the word for the sea monster of Troy (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 8.222; 20.147; see [[sea-beasts]]). The fishes are ἄλλοι, “the other fishes”: the poet counts the dolphin among them, as Leaf notes, rejecting van Leeuwen's conjecture ἐλλοί, “mute” (on 21.22; see [[fish]]). λιμὴν εὔορμος, “a harbor with good anchorage”, is a harbor of the *Odyssey* too, on Pharos and on the island of the goats (*Odyssey* 4.358, 9.136), and μάλα γάρ τε κατεσθίει, “for he devours greedily”, are the words of the hungry lion that comes upon a carcass (3.25; see [[lion-on-a-carcass]]).

The simile follows the locusts (21.12–16; see [[the-locusts-and-the-fire]]), and it takes the reader under the water. Achilles is in the river now with his sword, striking on every side, and the water is red with blood (21.17–21). The dolphin is Achilles, the small fish the Trojans; the recesses of the harbor are the banks under which they cower, πτῶσσον ὑπὸ κρημνούς (21.26), and their hiding is no safer than the fish's. The tenor turns from the hunter to his quarry: the fish flee “in fear” (δειδιότες, 21.24), and the Trojans cower (see [[scamander]] and [[achilles]]).

## In context

The likeness becomes fact as the book goes on. Achilles throws the body of Lycaon into the river to lie “among the fishes, who will lick the blood / from your wound” (21.122–123; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]), and the eels and the fishes feed on the fat of Asteropaeus (21.203–204; see [[eels]] and [[asteropaeus]]). When Achilles' hands grow weary he takes twelve young men alive out of the river, as the penalty for Patroclus (21.26–32; see [[human-sacrifice]]).

## Outside Homer

The harbor of the Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles* has the same picture in metal: in a harbor with good anchorage, λιμὴν ἐύορμος, of refined tin, dolphins dart this way and that hunting fish, two silver dolphins feast on the mute fishes, the bronze fishes tremble beneath them, and a fisherman sits on the shore with a net, about to cast (*Shield of Heracles* 207–215; see [[shield-of-heracles]]). In the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* the god leaps onto a Cretan ship in the form of a dolphin, and bids the sailors pray to him as Delphinius (*Homeric Hymn to Apollo* 399–403, 493–496; see [[apollo]]).

[[aristotle|Aristotle]] found the dolphin hard to class. It takes in the sea and lets it out through its blowhole, but it breathes air with a lung, and if it is caught in nets it soon chokes; so it is not easy to call it either an animal of the water or one of the land, for it shares in both. Of its hunting he says that the dolphins, like the sharks and all the whale-like animals, must turn on their backs to take their prey, since their mouths are underneath; that is why the smaller fish escape, “for the swiftness of the dolphin and its power of eating seem to be marvelous” (*History of Animals* 8.2).
