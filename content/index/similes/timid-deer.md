## The simile

{{quote:bf0f7cb8-4f68-5145-8b98-9de7b41d8262}}

φυζακινῇς ἐλάφοισιν ἐοίκεσαν, αἵ τε καθʼ ὕλην / θώων παρδαλίων τε λύκων τʼ ἤϊα πέλονται / αὔτως ἠλάσκουσαι ἀνάλκιδες, οὐδʼ ἔπι χάρμη: “were like deer quick to flee, which through the forest are the prey of jackals and leopards and wolves, straying aimlessly, without courage, with no joy of battle in them” (13.102–104). φυζακινός, “quick to flee”, occurs only here. It is made from φύζα, the panic flight that Book 9 calls the “companion of chilling Rout” (9.2), and the Trojans who have fled into the city at the start of Book 22 are πεφυζότες ἠΰτε νεβροί, like fawns that have fled (22.1). ἤϊα are provisions, the food a ship carries in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 2.289, 4.363), and here the food of beasts: jackals, leopards and wolves, three hunters of the deer (see [[deer]], [[jackal]], [[leopard]] and [[wolves]]). ἠλάσκω, “wander, stray”, is the word of the flies that swarm around the milk pails (2.470). ἀνάλκιδες is “without ἀλκή”, the strength to stand and defend oneself (see [[alke]]), and οὐδʼ ἔπι χάρμη is “there is no χάρμη in them”, no will to fight (see the translation's note on 13.104).

The tenor gives the deer's flight to the Trojans of the past: ὣς Τρῶες τὸ πρίν γε μένος καὶ χεῖρας Ἀχαιῶν / μίμνειν οὐκ ἐθέλεσκον ἐναντίον, οὐδʼ ἠβαιόν, “so before this the Trojans would not await the might and the hands of the Achaeans face to face, not even a little” (13.105–106). ἐθέλεσκον is an iterative, “they were never willing”. The simile is spoken as a wonder. Poseidon opens it with the verse “Ah, truly this is a great wonder that I see with my eyes” (13.99; see [[o-popoi]]): the deer now come to the hunters, “fighting far from the city, by the hollow ships” (13.107).

## In context

The men in the rear are weeping as they watch the Trojans come over the wall (13.83–90). Poseidon goes among them and calls on Teucer, Leitus, Peneleos, Thoas, Deipyrus, Meriones and Antilochus (13.91–94), and his speech begins “Shame, Argives, young men!” (13.95; see [[aidos]]). The deer serve his rebuke. If the enemy were once deer, the Achaeans who will not face them now are worse. He lays the change on “the baseness of their leader and the slackness of the army” (13.108), and grants that Agamemnon may be wholly to blame for dishonoring Achilles; even so, there is no holding back from war (13.111–114; see the translation's note on 13.108–114). He ends with Hector at the ships, who “has broken the gates and the long bar” (13.123–124), and the battalions form around the two Ajaxes (13.126–135; see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The deer is the Iliad's picture of cowardice. Achilles calls Agamemnon a man “with the eyes of a dog and the heart of a deer” (1.225; see [[dog-eyes-deer-heart]]), Agamemnon asks the Argives who hang back why they “stand like this, dazed, like fawns” (4.243; see [[like-fawns]]), and Achilles leads the twelve young Trojans out of the river “dazed like fawns” (21.29). Deer are prey in the similes: of lions (3.23–24, 11.113–119), of jackals (11.474–481; see [[the-jackals-and-the-stag]]), of wolves (16.156–159) and of a hound (22.189–192). Paris uses Poseidon's word for the deer later in this book, when he tells Hector that his mother did not bear him “wholly without courage either” (ἀνάλκιδα, 13.777; see [[paris]]).
