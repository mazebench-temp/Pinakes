## The simile

{{quote:cd7b3a69-6764-530b-b625-a57a3e5cd7c6}}

νῦν δέ μοι ἑρσήεις καὶ πρόσφατος ἐν μεγάροισι / κεῖσαι, τῷ ἴκελος ὅν τʼ ἀργυρότοξος Ἀπόλλων / οἷς ἀγανοῖσι βέλεσσιν ἐποιχόμενος κατέπεφνεν: “But now, fresh as dew and as if newly slain, in my halls / you lie, like one whom Apollo, god of the silver bow, / has come upon and killed with his gentle arrows” (24.757–759). ἑρσήεις, “dewy”, is formed from ἕρση, ἐέρση, “dew”. Its only other use in the Iliad is for the “dewy lotus” that the earth sends up under Zeus and Hera on Ida, where glistening drops of dew fall from their golden cloud (14.348–351); Hermes used it of the same body earlier in the book, in the form ἐερσήεις (24.419). Walter Leaf glosses it “fresh”, comparing the Latin *roscidus*, and explains that the body is not parched by the sun because Apollo has spread a mist over it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.419; 23.188–191). πρόσφατος, found only here in Homer, means “fresh”. It is generally taken as “just slain”, from the root of φόνος, but in that literal sense it would miss the point, which is that Hector has lain twelve days; Leaf notes an ancient alternative that refers it to plants newly sprung from the earth, and that later Greek uses the word of anything recent with no thought of death (on 24.757; see the translation's note on 24.757).

The last verse of the simile is one that the Iliad uses only here, while the Odyssey has it five times. οἷς ἀγανοῖς βελέεσσιν ἐποιχόμενος κατέπεφνε, “came upon him and killed him with his gentle arrows”, is said in the Odyssey of Apollo, who killed Menelaus' helmsman as he held the steering oar (*Odyssey* 3.279–281), and of Apollo and Artemis together, who come to the people of the island Syria when they grow old (*Odyssey* 15.407–411); in the feminine, of Artemis, who killed Orion (*Odyssey* 5.121–124; see [[dawn-and-orion]]), and in the question that Odysseus asks his mother among the dead and in her answer: was it a long illness, or did Artemis come upon her with her gentle arrows? Neither; it was longing for her son, and for his ἀγανοφροσύνη, his gentleness (*Odyssey* 11.171–173, 11.197–203). Leaf, quoting Merry and Riddell's note on *Odyssey* 3.280, explains the gentle arrows as a sudden death without suffering, brought as a rule to women by the goddess and to men by the god, and set against the long illness of *Odyssey* 11.172 and the hateful illness of *Odyssey* 15.408; such a death was easy, and Penelope prays that pure Artemis may give her one as soft (*Odyssey* 18.202). He also prefers the subjunctive καταπέφνῃ, “as one whom Apollo kills”, to the printed κατέπεφνεν (on 24.759; see the translation's note on 24.759).

The point of likeness is the look of the body. A man killed by the gentle arrows dies whole and at once, with no wound to mar him and no wasting; Hector, who was stabbed by every Achaean who came near him (22.371) and has been dragged in the dust day after day, lies as fair as if he had died so. ἀγανός, “gentle”, and its noun ἀγανοφροσύνη are the words that Helen uses a few verses later for Hector himself, “your gentleness and your gentle words” (24.772); Anticleia used the noun of Odysseus. Apollo's arrows in the Iliad are not gentle. They brought the plague, falling first on the mules and the swift dogs, with the same verb, ἐπῴχετο, “went after” (1.48–52; see [[apollo]]), and in Achilles' story of Niobe, told in this book, they killed her six sons “with arrows from his silver bow” (24.605; see [[the-children-of-niobe]] and [[god-of-the-silver-bow]]).

## In context

The freshness of the body is told twice, by a god and by a mother. Priam, meeting Hermes in the likeness of a young Myrmidon, asks whether Achilles has already cut his son limb from limb and set him before his dogs (24.406–409). Hermes answers that the dogs and birds have not touched him: “his flesh does not rot at all, nor do the worms / eat him” (24.414–415; see [[maggots]]), and though Achilles drags him around the mound of Patroclus at every dawn, “yet he does not disfigure him; you yourself would gaze, if you came, / at how fresh as dew he lies, and the blood is washed from him, / and he is nowhere foul; and all the wounds are closed / where he was struck; for many drove their bronze into him” (24.418–421). μιαρός, “foul”, is found only here in Homer. “So do the blessed gods care for your own son, / corpse though he is” (24.422–423; see [[hermes]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector]]).

Hecuba's lament for Hector is the second of the three at the end of the poem (24.747–760; see [[hecuba]] and [[lament]]). She begins with the gods' love for him, “and they have cared for you even in the portion of death” (24.750), sets the fate of her other sons, whom Achilles sold across the sea to Samos, Imbros and Lemnos, against Hector's, dragged many times around the mound of Patroclus, “and even so he did not raise him up” (24.751–756), and ends with the simile. Leaf takes νῦν δέ μοι, “but now”, as the turn of the whole speech: harsh as Achilles' treatment of Hector has been, it has ended only in his honor, since the freshness of the body is a mark of the gods' favor (on 24.752).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The gods care for other bodies. Zeus has Apollo wash Sarpedon in the river, anoint him with ambrosia and put immortal clothing on him before Sleep and Death carry him home to Lycia (16.676–683; see [[sarpedon]]); Thetis drips ambrosia and red nectar into the nostrils of Patroclus, “so that his flesh might be sound” (19.37–39; see [[ambrosia]]). Hector's body is guarded by two gods at once: Aphrodite keeps the dogs from it day and night and anoints it with rose-sweet oil, and Apollo brings a dark cloud over it, “lest the might of the sun should first / parch the skin around his sinews and limbs” (23.184–191), and covers it with the golden aegis when Achilles drags it (24.18–21; see [[aegis]]).

Priam, begging Hector not to face Achilles, said that for a young man killed in battle “all that is seen of him is fair, dead though he is” (22.73), but that dogs defiling the gray head of an old man are the most pitiful sight for wretched mortals (22.74–76; see [[all-is-fitting-for-the-young]]). His mother's simile makes Hector, dead, fair in the way Priam meant.
