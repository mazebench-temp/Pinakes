## The simile

{{quote:1235ba38-4d06-5fc4-abc9-ab2ab9b1c14f}}

ὡς δὲ γυνὴ κλαίῃσι φίλον πόσιν ἀμφιπεσοῦσα, / ὅς τε ἑῆς πρόσθεν πόλιος λαῶν τε πέσῃσιν, / ἄστεϊ καὶ τεκέεσσιν ἀμύνων νηλεὲς ἦμαρ: “And as a woman weeps, flinging herself upon her dear husband, / who has fallen before his city and his people, / keeping off the pitiless day from his town and his children” (*Odyssey* 8.523–525). ἀμφιπεσοῦσα goes with πόσιν, and is answered two verses later by ἀμφʼ αὐτῷ χυμένη, “throws herself around him”, where the dative is used (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.523). For πρόσθεν πόλιος a scholium records the variant προπάροιθε, “in front of” (Merry's critical note on 8.524). The husband dies as a defender: ἀμύνω with νηλεὲς ἦμαρ, “keep off the pitiless day”, is a phrase of the Iliad's battles, and it is used of Odysseus himself, when the Trojans close around him and he “kept off the pitiless day” with his spear (*Iliad* 11.484; see [[pitiless]]).

The verses that follow give the end of a city in three moments. She sees him dying, “and wails shrilly”; the enemy, οἱ δέ, “they”, come from behind, “beating her back and shoulders with their spears”; and they “lead her away into slavery, to have toil and misery, / and her cheeks waste away with most pitiful grief” (*Odyssey* 8.526–530; Merry on 8.527). πόνον τʼ ἐχέμεν καὶ ὀιζύν, “to have toil and misery”, is the Iliad's half-verse for the armies that Zeus leaves to their toil by the ships (*Iliad* 13.2). εἴρερον, “slavery”, is found only here in Homer. Merry connects it with εἴρω, “bind”, and σειρά, “rope”, and compares Latin *servus*; though the idea is in the Iliad, as in Agamemnon's threat to Chryseis, the circumstances here seem to him to suggest customs more barbarous than Homer's, so that the passage “is not free from suspicion” (on 8.529; *Iliad* 1.29–31; see the translation's note on 8.529). εἰσανάγουσι may mean “take on shipboard”, but εἰσαναβαίνω, “go up into”, suggests that she is led up with the victors into the captured citadel (Merry on 8.529, comparing *Odyssey* 2.172).

The tenor is a single verse: ὣς Ὀδυσεὺς ἐλεεινὸν ὑπʼ ὀφρύσι δάκρυον εἶβεν, “so Odysseus let a pitiful tear fall from beneath his brows” (*Odyssey* 8.531; see [[tears]] and [[pity]]). ἐλεεινοτάτῳ is “most pitiful”, and ἐλεεινόν is not idle with δάκρυον: it marks tears that spring from feeling, as Thersites' tears of pain do not (Merry on 8.530; *Iliad* 2.266). The likeness is in the weeping and in its cause. Odysseus has asked for the song of his own stratagem, the horse “which once brilliant Odysseus brought up into the citadel as a snare” (*Odyssey* 8.494), and has heard himself sung going to the house of Deiphobus “like Ares” (*Odyssey* 8.517–518; see [[sacker-of-cities]] and [[the-fall-of-troy]]). The sacker of the city weeps as one of its women weeps.

## In context

At the feast in the evening Odysseus asks Demodocus for the song of the wooden horse (*Odyssey* 8.487–498; see [[demodocus]]). The singer tells how the Argives sailed away and the Trojans dragged the horse into the citadel and debated what to do with it, how the Achaeans poured out of it and sacked the city, and how Odysseus and Menelaus fought at the house of Deiphobus and conquered “through great-hearted Athena” (*Odyssey* 8.499–520; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and [[deiphobus]]). “This the renowned singer sang; but Odysseus / was melting, and tears wet his cheeks beneath his eyelids” (*Odyssey* 8.521–522). After the simile the poem repeats the verses of the first song: he escaped the notice of all the others, “but Alcinous alone noticed him and observed him” (*Odyssey* 8.532–534 = 8.93–95; see [[alcinous]]). This time the king stops the singer and asks the stranger his name and why he weeps at the fate of the Argives and of Ilios (*Odyssey* 8.535–586; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The simile's words are those of the Iliad's women. When Briseis sees Patroclus dead, ἀμφʼ αὐτῷ χυμένη λίγʼ ἐκώκυε, she “threw herself around him and wailed shrilly” (*Iliad* 19.284), and she tells how she had seen her own husband “before the city, torn by the sharp bronze”, when Achilles sacked the city of Mynes (*Iliad* 19.291–296; see [[briseis]]). Hector foresees the day when an Achaean will lead Andromache away in tears, “taking away your day of freedom”, and grieves that she will lack a man “to ward off the day of slavery” (*Iliad* 6.454–463; see [[slavery-and-freedom]]); in her lament she says that he kept the city safe, its wives and little children, “who soon now will be carried off in the hollow ships, / and I among them” (*Iliad* 24.729–732; see [[andromache]]). Meleager's wife reminds him of all the sorrows that come to men whose city is taken: “they kill the men, and fire levels the city, / and strangers lead away the children and the deep-girdled women” (*Iliad* 9.592–594; see [[captive-women]]).

The Odyssey has the same scene from the other side. Helen tells how Odysseus slipped into Troy in the likeness of a beggar and, as he went back to the ships, killed many Trojans: “Then the other Trojan women wailed shrilly” (*Odyssey* 4.244–259; see [[helen]]). The tenor's verse returns once more, when Odysseus and Telemachus know each other at last and weep more shrilly than sea eagles or vultures whose young the countrymen have taken before they could fly: “so they let a pitiful tear fall from beneath their brows” (*Odyssey* 16.216–219, a plain rendering). And the weeping is turned around in *Odyssey* 19. Odysseus here “was melting”, τήκετο (*Odyssey* 8.522); there Penelope, hearing the beggar's tale of her husband, melts, τήκετο, as snow melts on the mountains, “weeping for her own husband, who sat beside her” (*Odyssey* 19.204–209, a plain rendering; see [[penelope]]). Here Odysseus weeps like a wife for a husband who is dead; there his wife weeps for him as lost while he sits beside her.
