## The simile

{{quote:59525024-f74c-5df3-abe6-8ff1160cd0d0}}

αὐτὰρ ὃ θυμὸν ἄϊσθε καὶ ἤρυγεν, ὡς ὅτε ταῦρος / ἤρυγεν ἑλκόμενος Ἑλικώνιον ἀμφὶ ἄνακτα / κούρων ἑλκόντων· γάνυται δέ τε τοῖς ἐνοσίχθων: “And he gasped out his life and bellowed, as when a bull / bellows as he is dragged around the Heliconian lord / by young men dragging him; and the earth-shaker is glad of them” (20.403–405). ἤρυγε, “bellowed”, is said three times in four verses, of the man, of the bull and of the man again (20.403, 20.404, 20.406), and this aorist is found nowhere else in Homer. It belongs with ἐρύγμηλος, the “bellowing” bull that two lions drag from the herd on the Shield (18.580; see [[bellowing]]), and with ἐρεύγομαι, said of the salt sea that “belches out” against the shore (17.265). θυμὸν ἄϊσθε, “he gasped out his life”: the verb is found again only of the horse Pedasus, which “screamed, gasping out its life” under Sarpedon's spear (16.468).

The bull is dragged ἀμφὶ ἄνακτα, “around the lord”. Walter Leaf takes ἀμφί literally, “dragged round (the altar of) Poseidon” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.404). The lord is called the Heliconian only here in Homer (see [[heliconian-poseidon]] and [[helice]]), and in the next verse he is the earth-shaker, ἐνοσίχθων, who “is glad of them”: γάνυται, the verb of the shepherd glad at heart in the simile of the ram (13.493), is said here of the god, and τοῖς of the young men who drag the victim (see [[poseidon]] and [[earth-shaker]]). The bull is Poseidon's own victim (11.728; *Odyssey* 3.5–8; see [[bulls]] and [[sacrifice]]).

The point of likeness is the sound, the bellow of the victim as it is dragged to the god. What the bellow meant to those who sacrificed is not said in Homer. Leaf reports from the A scholia that the god was thought to accept the sacrifice when the oxen bellowed, and that their silence grieved the sacrificers, who took it for a sign of his anger (on 20.404); Strabo reports the same belief of the Ionians (see Outside Homer below).

The tenor: ὣς ἄρα τόν γʼ ἐρυγόντα λίπʼ ὀστέα θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ, “so as he bellowed, his proud spirit left his bones” (20.406). The words are those of a death formula, λίπε δʼ ὀστέα θυμός, “and his spirit left his bones”, said of men who fall in battle (12.386, 16.743) and, in the *Odyssey*, of the heifer that Nestor's household sacrifices to Athena (*Odyssey* 3.455). The simile tells the death in battle as a sacrifice: the man bellows like the victim, and a god is glad.

## In context

Hippodamas is the third man Achilles kills after Poseidon has carried Aeneas out of his reach, after Iphition, son of Otrynteus, and Demoleon, son of Antenor (20.381–400; see [[iphition]] and [[demoleon]]). He has leapt down from his chariot and is fleeing when Achilles stabs him in the back (20.401–402; see [[hippodamas]]). Both verses are formulaic: the first is that of Chersidamas, killed by Odysseus (11.423), the second that of Scamandrius, killed by Menelaus (5.56). Next comes Polydorus, the youngest son of Priam (20.407–418; see [[polydorus-son-of-priam]]). The god of the simile is the god who has just saved Aeneas from Achilles (20.291–340). Two more similes end the book, a fire and a second team of bulls: Achilles rages like a forest fire, and his horses trample the dead as bulls tread out barley (20.490–503; see [[forest-fire]] and [[bulls-on-the-threshing-floor]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Other similes give a dying man the death of an ox. Adamas writhes around Meriones' spear like an ox that herdsmen in the mountains have bound with ropes (13.571–573; see [[the-roped-ox]]), and Aretus leaps forward and falls like an ox struck behind the horns with an axe (17.520–523; see [[the-ox-and-the-axe]]). On the Shield two lions drag off a bellowing bull at the head of a herd (18.579–581; see [[the-herd-and-the-lions]]), and in the next book the river Scamander bellows like a bull as he throws the dead out of his stream (μεμυκὼς ἠΰτε ταῦρος, 21.237). In the *Odyssey* the doors of the storeroom roar like a bull grazing in a meadow when Penelope opens them (*Odyssey* 21.48–49).

## Outside Homer

Strabo reports that some took these verses for the Ionians' sacrifice to Heliconian Poseidon at the Panionia, in the territory of Priene, where they thought the omens favorable when the bull bellowed as it was sacrificed, and inferred that the poet lived after the Ionian migration; others referred the bull and the sacrifice to Helice itself, the old seat of the cult, which the sea had drowned (*Geography* 8.7.2). Herodotus names the Panionium on Mycale as the place the Ionians set apart in common for Poseidon Heliconius (1.148.1). See [[helice-or-the-panionia]].
