## The simile

{{quote:5b759767-da09-52b1-8848-48ec45d0af49}}

ὃ δʼ ἑσπόμενος περὶ δουρὶ / ἤσπαιρʼ ὡς ὅτε βοῦς τόν τʼ οὔρεσι βουκόλοι ἄνδρες / ἰλλάσιν οὐκ ἐθέλοντα βίῃ δήσαντες ἄγουσιν: “and he, following it, bent around the spear and writhed, as an ox writhes that herdsmen in the mountains have bound by force with twisted ropes and lead along against its will” (13.570–572). ἀσπαίρω is the struggle of the dying body: the lambs of the oath sacrifice laid on the ground “gasping” (3.293), the serpent in the eagle's talons “still struggling” (12.203), and earlier in this book the heart of Alcathous, “still quivering” around Idomeneus' spear (13.443). ἰλλάς, a twisted rope or bond, occurs only here. The βουκόλοι ἄνδρες are herdsmen (βοῦς, “ox”), who bind the beast βίῃ, “by force”, and lead it οὐκ ἐθέλοντα, “against its will”. Where they are taking it the simile does not say.

The point of likeness is a body that struggles against what holds it and cannot get free. The spear is the rope: Adamas, ἑσπόμενος, “following” the spear, bends around it, as the ox is led on its rope. The tenor marks the struggle as short: ὣς ὃ τυπεὶς ἤσπαιρε μίνυνθά περ, οὔ τι μάλα δήν, “so, struck, he writhed for a little while, not long at all” (13.573). The same words close the hanging of the faithless maids in the Odyssey, who writhed with their feet “for a little while, not long at all” (*Odyssey* 22.473). When Meriones comes close and pulls the spear out, “darkness covered his eyes” (13.574–575; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]).

The wound is placed with care: “between the genitals and the navel, where most of all Ares is painful for miserable mortals” (13.568–569). The comment is the narrator's own, and Ares here is the god's name for war and its wounds.

## In context

Adamas, son of Asius, has stabbed the middle of Antilochus' shield, but Poseidon, guarding the son of Nestor, made the spear-point powerless, and half the spear stayed in the shield “like a fire-hardened stake” (13.560–565; see [[like-a-fire-hardened-stake]]). Adamas draws back among his companions, “avoiding doom” (13.566), and Meriones follows him (13.567; see [[meriones]] and [[adamas]]). His father Asius has fallen before his horses like a felled tree (13.384–393; see [[the-felled-tree]]). At the end of the book Hector looks for “Adamas, son of Asius, and Asius, son of Hyrtacus” among the champions and finds them dead (13.758–764, 13.771).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Cattle die in the similes of death. Aretus, struck by Automedon's spear, falls on his back as a young ox leaps forward and falls when a man strikes it behind the horns with an axe (17.520–524). Hippodamas bellows as he breathes out his life, like a bull dragged around the altar of the lord of Helice by young men, while the earth-shaker rejoices in them (20.403–405). In the Odyssey Agamemnon was killed at the feast “as one kills an ox at the manger” (*Odyssey* 4.535 = 11.411). Elsewhere a lion kills a bull among the herd (16.487–489), and on the shield of Achilles two lions drag a bellowing bull away from the herdsmen (18.579–586). See [[cattle]].
