## The simile

{{quote:324b99c4-6764-519a-b770-919659c691d0}}

οἴματα, “the swoop”, is the rush of a beast of prey: Patroclus goes at the fallen Cebriones “with the spring of a lion” (οἶμα λέοντος, 16.751–752), and in *Iliad* 22 Hector, drawing his sword for his last charge, swoops (οἴμησεν) like a high-flying eagle on a lamb or a hare (22.306–310; see [[the-eagle-flying-high]]). μέλανος τοῦ θηρητῆρος, “the black hunter”, has its match in Book 24, where the eagle that Zeus sends to Priam is μόρφνος θηρητήρ, the dark hunter, whom men also call the dusky one (24.316; see [[eagle]]). The article τοῦ is unusual in Homer, and the ancients read the verse in several ways. Walter Leaf reports μελανόστου, “black-boned”, ascribed to Aristotle but more likely Democritus', μελανόσσου, “black-eyed”, and Ahrens' conjecture μελανόρσου, “black-tailed”, and Philetas' ὄμματα, “eyes”, for οἴματα (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.252; see the translation's note on 21.252).

ὅς θʼ ἅμα κάρτιστός τε καὶ ὤκιστος πετεηνῶν, “which is at once the strongest and the swiftest of birds” (21.253). The eagle is the bird of superlatives, “surest in omen of winged birds” (8.247) and the one which, as men say, “has the sharpest sight of all the winged birds under heaven” (17.674–675; see [[surest-of-birds]] and [[the-eagle-and-the-hare]]). ὤκιστος πετεηνῶν is said of the hawk too, the swift dove-killer whose likeness Apollo has when he goes down from Ida (15.237–238; see [[like-a-hawk]]).

The eagle's swoop is usually a hunter's: it falls on a flock of birds feeding by a river, or on a hare under a bush (15.690–692, 17.674–678; see [[the-eagle-and-the-birds]]). Here the hunter is in flight. Achilles, who has been the dolphin among the fishes (21.22–26), springs away from the water with all the eagle's speed and strength, and the bronze on his chest rings terribly as he swerves from under the river and runs (21.254–256). Strength and speed do not save him. The next simile shows the water outrunning even a man who leads it (21.257–264; see [[the-channel-and-the-gardener]]).

## In context

The river has risen against Achilles, beaten on his shield, and torn away the bank with the elm he clutched; he has leapt out of the eddy and run over the plain in fear, and the great god rises after him, darkening at the crest, to stop him and save the Trojans (21.233–250; see [[scamander]] and [[achilles-and-the-river]]). ὅσον τʼ ἐπὶ δουρὸς ἐρωή, “as far as the cast of a spear” (21.251), is the measure of a short distance (see [[the-cast-of-a-spear]]).

## Outside Homer

[[aristotle|Aristotle]] describes several kinds of eagle. One is black in color and the smallest in size but the strongest of them; it lives in mountains and forests and is called the black eagle and the hare-killer, and it alone rears its young and leads them out; it is swift to strike, fearless and combative, and of good omen, for it neither whimpers nor screams. Another kind is the μορφνός that Homer mentions at Priam's going out (*History of Animals* 9.32). In the *Agamemnon* of [[aeschylus|Aeschylus]] the portent of the two eagles that tear a pregnant hare by the palace shows “the black one, and the one white behind” (ὁ κελαινός, ὅ τʼ ἐξόπιν ἀργᾶς, *Agamemnon* 109–120), which Leaf compares with the reading “black-tailed” (on 21.252).
