## The comparison

κακὸς ὣς is two words: κακός, “bad”, which of a fighting man means “cowardly” and as a noun “a coward”, and ὥς, “like”, written with an accent when it follows its noun. It is the shortest kind of comparison, a man set beside a type of man (see [[like-a-boy-or-a-woman]]). The Iliad has it twice.

**Hector in Book 6.** Andromache begs Hector to stay on the wall. He answers that all this is on his mind too, “but most terribly I would feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes, if like a coward I shrink away from the war”; he has learned to be brave always and to fight among the foremost (6.441–446; see [[aidos]] and [[hector]]). The coward is the man he refuses to be, and the shame is his own.

**Diomedes in Book 8.** When Zeus' thunder has put the Achaean leaders to flight, Nestor is caught on the field, and Diomedes calls on Odysseus to stand with him:

{{quote:e4baf5b5-6a70-5718-92f6-80e4a8f926cb}}

Here the coward is another man, and the comparison is a rebuke. μετὰ νῶτα βαλών, “turning your back”, can also mean “throwing your shield behind your back” (see the translation's note on 8.94). The warning that follows names the coward's death: a spear “in your back” (μεταφρένῳ, 8.95). Odysseus “did not hear” (οὐδʼ ἐσάκουσε, 8.97) and darts past to the ships. The verb is found only here; it may also be taken as “did not heed”, which would make his flight a refusal (see the translation's note on 8.97 and [[odysseus]]).

## In Book 8

The word turns back on the man who used it. Soon afterward Zeus' thunderbolt falls before Diomedes' own horses, and Nestor turns them to flight (8.130–157; see [[diomedes]]). Diomedes fears what Hector will say, “The son of Tydeus fled before me to the ships” (8.148–150), and Nestor consoles him: even if Hector calls him “a coward and a weakling” (κακὸν καὶ ἀνάλκιδα), the Trojans will not believe it (8.152–156). Hector calls him worse, “no better than a woman” and a “wretched doll” (8.163–164; see [[wretched-doll]]). Later in the day Diomedes kills Agelaus as he turns to flee, planting his spear in his back (μεταφρένῳ ἐν δόρυ πῆξεν, 8.258), in the words of his warning to Odysseus (μεταφρένῳ ἐν δόρυ πήξῃ, 8.95; see [[agelaus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

When Zeus routs the Achaeans again in Book 11, it is Odysseus who is left alone, and he argues the case with himself: “For I know that it is cowards who leave the fighting, but whoever would be best in battle must by all means stand his ground” (11.401–410; see [[retreat]]). Hector, facing Achilles at last, tells him: “you will not plant your spear in my back as I flee, but drive it through my chest as I charge” (22.283–284).
