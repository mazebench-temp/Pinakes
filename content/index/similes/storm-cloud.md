## The simile

The line before the simile ends in a metaphor: ἅμα δὲ νέφος εἵπετο πεζῶν, “and with them followed a cloud of foot soldiers” (4.274). The simile opens the metaphor into a scene: ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἀπὸ σκοπιῆς εἶδεν νέφος αἰπόλος ἀνὴρ / ἐρχόμενον κατὰ πόντον ὑπὸ Ζεφύροιο ἰωῆς· / τῷ δέ τʼ ἄνευθεν ἐόντι μελάντερον ἠΰτε πίσσα / φαίνετʼ ἰὸν κατὰ πόντον, ἄγει δέ τε λαίλαπα πολλήν, / ῥίγησέν τε ἰδών, ὑπό τε σπέος ἤλασε μῆλα: “as when a goatherd from a lookout sees a cloud coming over the sea before the blast of the West Wind; and to him, being far away, blacker than pitch it seems as it goes over the sea, and it brings a great storm, and he shudders at the sight and drives his flocks under a cave”.

A σκοπιή is a lookout, a height for watching. ἰωή is a rush or blast, of wind or of fire, and the West Wind (Ζέφυρος; see [[zephyrus]]) drives the cloud in from the sea. λαῖλαψ is a squall of wind and rain. πίσσα is pitch, the blackest thing the herdsman knows, and μελάντερον ἠΰτε πίσσα, “blacker, as pitch is”, puts the comparison inside the comparison. μῆλα are the flock, here the goatherd's goats ([[goats]]). The picture is seen from far off (ἄνευθεν ἐόντι, “being far away”), and to the distant watcher the cloud looks blacker than it is.

The tenor: τοῖαι ἅμʼ Αἰάντεσσι διοτρεφέων αἰζηῶν / δήϊον ἐς πόλεμον πυκιναὶ κίνυντο φάλαγγες / κυάνεαι, σάκεσίν τε καὶ ἔγχεσι πεφρικυῖαι: “so with the two Ajaxes the battalions of young men nurtured by Zeus moved thick into destructive war, dark, bristling with shields and spears”. πυκιναί, “thick, close-packed”, and κυάνεαι, “dark” (κύανος, a dark blue-black), answer the dense black cloud. πεφρικυῖαι, “bristling”, is from φρίσσω, used of a surface roughened by wind. The points of likeness are darkness, mass and menace, all seen from a distance.

The watcher in the simile shudders; the watcher in the story rejoices. “And seeing them, powerful Agamemnon rejoiced” (4.283). What the goatherd fears is on Agamemnon's side. The verb of the goatherd's fear, ῥίγησεν, is the one used of Agamemnon himself when he saw his brother's blood (4.148).

## In context

{{quote:7717aa52-0413-53c3-924a-c95195ff5b2d}}

After Idomeneus (see [[like-a-boar]]), Agamemnon comes to the two Ajaxes as they arm (κορυσσέσθην, 4.274). He praises them without an order: “to you two I give no orders, since it is not fitting to urge you on”. If all had such a spirit, the city of Priam would soon bow its head (4.285–291; see [[ajax]], [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[the-review-of-the-army]]). The Ajaxes lead a mass of infantry: their cloud is made of πεζοί, foot soldiers, a dense formation rather than a line of champions. In Book 13 the battalions around the two Ajaxes stand shield to shield and helmet to helmet, man pressed against man (13.126–133).

Two herdsmen watch the approach to battle in Book 4. The goatherd sees the cloud from his lookout. When the armies meet, a shepherd far off in the mountains hears the roar of torrents rushing together (4.455; see [[torrents-meeting]]). The West Wind drives both the cloud and the waves to which the Danaan battalions are compared later in the book (4.276, 4.423; see [[waves-on-the-shore]]). The goatherd of Book 2 sorted out his mingled flocks as the leaders marshaled the army (2.474–477; see [[goatherds-sorting-their-flocks]]), and the mist of Book 3 was “no friend to shepherds” (3.10–11; see [[mist-on-the-mountain]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The metaphor returns: at Patroclus' funeral the horsemen go first, “and a cloud of foot soldiers followed” (νέφος εἵπετο πεζῶν, 23.133), and Patroclus sees “a dark cloud of Trojans” around the ships (κυάνεον Τρώων νέφος, 16.66). When Hector challenges the Achaeans in Book 7, both armies sit in their ranks, “bristling with shields and helmets and spears” (πεφρικυῖαι), like the ripple that the West Wind spreads over the sea when it begins to rise, darkening the water (7.61–66). A still cloud gives the opposite picture: the Danaans stand firm like clouds that Zeus sets on the mountain peaks in windless weather (5.522–527). Storm images grow larger in the later books. Hector falls on the Achaeans like a squall that stirs up the violet sea (11.297–298), and the Trojans come on like a blast of winds that falls on the sea and heaps up the waves (13.795–800).
