## The simile

σκῆπτρον δʼ οὔτʼ ὀπίσω οὔτε προπρηνὲς ἐνώμα, / ἀλλʼ ἀστεμφὲς ἔχεσκεν ἀΐδρεϊ φωτὶ ἐοικώς: “and he would not move the scepter backward or forward, but held it stiff, like a man who knows nothing”. ἀΐδρις (ἀ-, “not”, and the root of εἰδέναι, “know”) means ignorant or unskilled; it occurs only here in the Iliad. ἀστεμφής is “unmoving, firm”. The iterative verbs, στάσκεν (“he would stand”), ἴδεσκε (“he would look”) and ἔχεσκεν (“he would hold”), describe what he did each time he rose to speak. The next line gives the verdict of an onlooker: φαίης κε ζάκοτόν τέ τινʼ ἔμμεναι ἄφρονά τʼ αὔτως, “you would have said he was some surly fellow, and a fool as well”. ζάκοτος (“very resentful, surly”, from ζα-, “very”, and κότος, “grudge”; see [[kotos]]) is found only here.

The comparison is of appearance only. A speaker in the assembly held the [[scepter]] and, it seems, moved it as he spoke. Odysseus stood with his eyes fixed on the ground and the staff held still, and looked like a man with nothing to say.

## In context

{{quote:563b562b-8df5-5c11-aeb7-e42ca1052aae}}

Antenor is remembering the embassy of Menelaus and Odysseus to Troy (see [[the-embassy-to-troy]]). Menelaus spoke briefly and clearly; Odysseus seemed a fool until he opened his mouth, and then “no other mortal could rival Odysseus” (3.221–224; see [[snowflakes-of-winter]]). The first comparison exists for the sake of the second. The less he looked like a speaker, the greater the surprise of the voice, and Antenor ends by saying that the Trojans then “did not so much marvel at Odysseus' looks” (3.224).

Looks and worth are a theme of the book. Hector opened it by telling Paris that the Achaeans laugh at a champion who has a handsome face and no strength in his heart (3.43–45; see [[eidos]] and [[beauty-and-its-price]]). Odysseus is the reverse: the look of a fool, and the power within.

## Elsewhere in Homer

Deceptive looks are Odysseus' art in the Odyssey. Helen tells how he entered Troy disfigured with blows and dressed as a slave, made himself look like another man, and was recognized by her alone (Odyssey 4.244–251), and he comes home to Ithaca disguised as a beggar. The noun of the same root, ἀϊδρείη (“ignorance”), is used there of his companions, who follow Circe into her house “in their ignorance” (Odyssey 10.231, 10.257).

## Outside Homer

Ovid gives the scene to his Ulysses at the contest for the arms of Achilles. He stands, lets his eyes rest a little on the ground, then raises them to the chiefs and begins (*Metamorphoses* 13.123–127).
