## The simile

{{quote:0b666abe-9cb0-5969-9710-c45c0634029d}}

Odysseus has asked Nausicaa whether she is a goddess, and if so likened her to Artemis, and if a mortal, called her parents, her brothers and her bridegroom blessed (*Odyssey* 6.149–159; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]] and [[three-times-blessed]]). Then he turns to his own wonder: “For never yet have I seen with my eyes such a mortal, / neither man nor woman; awe holds me as I look” (*Odyssey* 6.160–161; see [[awe-holds-me]] and [[sebas]]). The simile is an instance of that awe, drawn from his own memory: Δήλῳ δή ποτε τοῖον Ἀπόλλωνος παρὰ βωμῷ / φοίνικος νέον ἔρνος ἀνερχόμενον ἐνόησα, “Once in Delos, beside the altar of Apollo, such a thing / I saw, a young sapling of a palm tree springing up” (*Odyssey* 6.162–163; see [[delos]], [[apollo]], [[altar]] and [[palm]]). Delos is named nowhere else in Homer, and the palm, φοῖνιξ, nowhere else.

ἔρνος is a young shoot or sapling, the word of the Iliad's plant similes for the young: Euphorbus falls like the olive sapling a man has reared with care, and Thetis' son “shot up like a sapling” (*Iliad* 17.53, 18.56; see [[the-olive-sapling]] and [[like-a-sapling]]). Odysseus has come to it by way of a plant word already: he imagined Nausicaa's family watching τοιόνδε θάλος, “such a scion”, going into the dance (*Odyssey* 6.157), as Hecuba calls Hector φίλον θάλος, “dear scion” (*Iliad* 22.87). From the young shoot of a house he passes to the young shoot of a tree, νέον, “young”, and ἀνερχόμενον, “springing up”, tall and slender.

The occasion is told in a parenthesis: “for I went there too, and many people followed me, / on that journey on which I was to have evil sorrows” (*Odyssey* 6.164–165). Merry takes the visit to belong to the voyage from Aulis to Troy, when the Greeks passed through the Cyclades to Delos (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.162; see the translation's note on 6.162–163). The journey of sorrows is the war (see [[the-fall-of-troy]]), and the young palm is a memory from before it.

The comparison then runs, as Merry says, “at the wrong end”: ὣς δʼ αὔτως καὶ κεῖνο ἰδὼν ἐτεθήπεα θυμῷ / δήν … ὡς σέ, γύναι, ἄγαμαί τε τέθηπά τε, “And in the same way, when I saw that, I was astounded in my heart / for a long time … as I marvel at you, lady, and am astounded” (*Odyssey* 6.166–168). The natural order would be “as I was astounded at that, so I marvel at you”; the verse gives the reverse, which Merry renders *tantum, illud conspicatus, obstupui quantum te iam nunc admiror*, “I was as astounded when I saw that as I marvel at you now” (on 6.166; see the translation's note on 6.166–168). ἄγαμαι is “marvel”; τέθηπα, “be astounded”, is said twice, of the tree and of the girl, and the translation keeps it apart from θαυμάζω, the “marvel” of Nausicaa at her dream (*Odyssey* 6.49; see its table of key words). The reason given for the wonder is the tree's uniqueness, ἐπεὶ οὔ πω τοῖον ἀνήλυθεν ἐκ δόρυ γαίης, “since never yet had such a tree come up from the earth” (*Odyssey* 6.167; see [[earth]]); δόρυ, the spear shaft or ship's timber of the Iliad, is here the living tree. The preposition ἐκ stands apart from its noun γαίης, a licence unusual in Homer, which Merry compares with ἐν … οἴκῳ at *Odyssey* 9.535 and 11.115 (on 6.167; see the translation's note on 6.167).

The tenor ends where Odysseus' choice began. He had decided not to clasp her knees but to speak from a distance, lest she be angry (*Odyssey* 6.141–148); now he gives the reason as reverence: “and I am terribly afraid / to clasp your knees; and hard grief has come upon me” (*Odyssey* 6.168–169; see [[supplication]] and [[deos]]).

## In context

The simile closes the first half of the supplication speech. The second half tells his need: “Yesterday, on the twentieth day, I escaped from the wine-dark sea” (*Odyssey* 6.170), the shipwreck, the request for the city and a rag to wear, and the wish that the gods may give her a husband, a home and oneness of mind (*Odyssey* 6.170–185; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]). Nausicaa answers with clothes, food and the way to the city (*Odyssey* 6.186–210; see [[nausicaa]]).

The words of wonder return at the end of the poem, in the other direction. When Penelope cannot yet believe that the stranger is her husband, she says “my heart within me is astounded” (θυμός μοι ἐνὶ στήθεσσι τέθηπεν, *Odyssey* 23.105, a plain rendering), and Athena makes him beautiful for her in the verses of the sixth book (*Odyssey* 23.156–162; see [[gold-poured-around-silver]]).

## Outside Homer

Merry thinks that a palm may have been kept always in the precinct of Delos, like the sacred olives of the Academy at Athens (on 6.162). A scholium refers Odysseus' palm to the first palm of Delos, by which Leto gave birth (Merry on 6.162). In the *Hymn to Apollo* Leto, in labor on Delos, throws her arms around the palm and presses her knees into the soft meadow, and the god leaps forth into the light (*Hymn to Apollo* 117–119; see [[leto]]); in Euripides' *Hecuba* the captive Trojan women wonder whether they will be carried to the island “where the first-born palm and the bay raised their sacred branches for dear Leto” (*Hecuba* 458–461, the passage Merry cites; see [[euripides]]).

In Cicero the palm is among the things that live longer in memory than in nature. In *On the Laws* his brother Quintus, defending the oak of Arpinum that Cicero's poem *Marius* made famous, says that the place will never lack an oak called Marian as long as Latin letters speak, “unless perhaps your Athens has been able to keep an everlasting olive on the citadel, or they show today the same tall and slender palm that Homer's Ulysses said he saw at Delos”; many things in many places, he goes on, last longer in remembrance than they could stand by nature (Cicero, *On the Laws* 1.2; see [[cicero-the-palm-of-delos]], [[cicero]] and [[olive]]). Merry reads the passage as saying that the palm was still shown in Cicero's day (on 6.162), but the words stand under “unless perhaps”, and Quintus' point is that such trees live on in the memory of men.
