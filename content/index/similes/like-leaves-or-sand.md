## The simile

λίην γὰρ φύλλοισιν ἐοικότες ἢ ψαμάθοισιν / ἔρχονται πεδίοιο μαχησόμενοι προτὶ ἄστυ: “for like leaves or like the sands, far too many, they are coming across the plain to fight against the city”. φύλλα are leaves and ψάμαθοι grains of sand. ἐοικότες (“like”) is the participle of ἔοικα (“be like”), and λίην means “exceedingly, too much”.

The comparison is of number, and of number past counting: the leaves of the trees, the sand of the shore. λίην makes it a warning. They are too many.

## In context

{{quote:2d2f4651-59fc-5fdc-a6ba-ef08bcd826e2}}

Zeus sends [[iris|Iris]] to the Trojans with “grievous news” while they hold an assembly at Priam's doors. She speaks in the voice of [[polites|Polites]], the Trojan lookout, and begins with a rebuke: “endless words are always dear to you, as once in time of peace; but relentless war has come” (2.786–797). Her report repeats what the narrator has shown. The Achaeans stood in the meadow of the Scamander “numberless, as the leaves and flowers” (2.468; [[leaves-and-flowers]]); now a Trojan voice sees leaves again, and sand.

She has never seen “so fine and so great an army” (τοιόνδε τοσόνδέ τε λαόν, 2.799). These are the words Agamemnon used when he called it shameful that so fine and so great an army should fight in vain against fewer men (2.119–122). Then she tells [[Hector]] to let each leader marshal his own people, and the Trojan catalogue follows ([[trojan-catalogue]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Sand is the measure of the countless when Achilles refuses Agamemnon's gifts: not even if he gave him as many as the grains of sand and dust (Iliad 9.385).
