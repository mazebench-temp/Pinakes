## The simile

{{quote:ddc81568-ff71-507f-9f69-909397431671}}

ὡς δʼ ὅθʼ ὑπὸ λιγέων ἀνέμων σπέρχωσιν ἄελλαι / ἤματι τῷ ὅτε τε πλείστη κόνις ἀμφὶ κελεύθους, / οἵ τʼ ἄμυδις κονίης μεγάλην ἱστᾶσιν ὀμίχλην: “As when storms rush on before shrill winds on a day when the dust lies thickest on the roads, and they raise together a great mist of dust” (13.334–336). ἄελλαι are squalls, the storm winds of Hector's simile in Book 11 (see [[the-storm-wind-on-the-sea]]), and λιγύς, “shrill”, is the whistle of a wind, as in the winnowing simile later in this book (πνοιῇ ὕπο λιγυρῇ, 13.590). The day is a dry one, when the roads are deepest in dust. ὀμίχλη is the mist that the South Wind pours over the mountain peaks in Book 3, where it pictures the dust raised by the marching Achaeans (3.10–14; see [[mist-on-the-mountain]]). ἄμυδις, “together”, is the word of the meeting. The tenor takes it up: ὣς ἄρα τῶν ὁμόσʼ ἦλθε μάχη, “so their battle came together” (13.337).

The point of likeness is the confused meeting of forces from many sides, and the cloud it raises. The lines that follow turn the cloud into a sight. The battle “bristled with the spears, long and flesh-cutting” (see [[flesh-cutting]]), and eyes were blinded by the gleam of bronze from helmets, corselets and shields (13.339–343). The dust that hides and the bronze that dazzles are the same confusion. The passage ends with an imagined onlooker: “he would be very bold of heart who could then have rejoiced at the sight of the toil, and not grieved” (13.343–344).

## In context

Idomeneus and Meriones have gone out like Ares and Rout and chosen the left of the army (13.295–329; see [[ares-and-rout]]). When the Trojans see Idomeneus “like a flame in his courage”, with his attendant, they all come at him, “and the quarrel stood even among them at the sterns of the ships” (13.330–333; see [[like-a-flame]]). After the simile the narrator turns from the fighting to the gods who direct it, the two sons of Cronus, Zeus willing victory to the Trojans and Poseidon secretly rousing the Achaeans (13.345–360; see [[the-rope-of-war]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Dust rises wherever armies move. It stands in the air as the Achaeans rush to their ships in Book 2 (2.150–151), it rises from under the marching Achaeans like mist on a mountain (3.10–14), and it whitens the Achaeans as chaff whitens the heaps on the threshing floor (5.499–505; see [[winnowing]]). Zeus raises a storm of wind from Ida that carries the dust straight against the ships (12.252–255). When Patroclus drives the Trojans back from the ships, a storm (ἄελλα) spreads up under the clouds as their horses strain for the city (16.374–376). Ranks bristle with spears in earlier books too, “bristling with shields and spears” (4.282; compare 7.62).
