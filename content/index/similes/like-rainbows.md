## The simile

{{quote:b8ea0b3e-f828-53ea-9113-e0aba767faec}}

τρεῖς ἑκάτερθʼ ἴρισσιν ἐοικότες, ἅς τε Κρονίων / ἐν νέφεϊ στήριξε, τέρας μερόπων ἀνθρώπων: “three on each side, like the rainbows that the son of Cronus sets in the clouds, a portent for mortal men”. ἶρις is the rainbow, the same word as the name of Iris, the messenger of the gods. στήριξε is a gnomic aorist of στηρίζω, “fix, set firm”: Zeus plants the bow in the cloud. τέρας is a sign sent by a god, and μέροψ, an old epithet of men, is usually rendered “mortal”; its meaning is uncertain.

The serpents are κυάνεοι, of dark blue enamel, like the ten bands of the same substance on the corselet (11.24–26; see [[kyanos]] and [[corselet]]). What they share with rainbows is not said. The likeness may lie in their arching shape as they rise toward the neck, or in their sheen, or in both (see the translation's note on 11.19–28). The comparison also gives the armor a meaning. The rainbow is set in the sky by Zeus as a sign, and on the morning of this day Zeus has sent Strife to the ships “holding in her hands a portent of war”, πολέμοιο τέρας (11.4). The king puts on signs of war along with his armor.

## In context

The serpents belong to the corselet that Cinyras of Cyprus sent Agamemnon as a guest-gift, the second piece of his armor after the greaves in the fullest arming scene of the poem (11.17–28; see [[cinyras]] and [[arming-scene]]). A third serpent, three-headed, coils on the strap of his shield (11.39–40; see [[serpent]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The rainbow is a portent once more in the Iliad. When Athena comes down to rouse the Danaans over the body of Patroclus, wrapped in a purple cloud, she is likened to the purple rainbow (πορφυρέην ἶριν) that Zeus stretches from heaven for mortals as a portent of war or of a wintry storm, which stops men's work and troubles the flocks (17.547–552). The verse of the serpents is built like the verse of another simile of Zeus' sky: the Danaans stood their ground νεφέλῃσιν ἐοικότες ἅς τε Κρονίων, “like clouds that the son of Cronus” sets motionless on the mountain tops (5.522–523; see [[clouds-on-the-mountains]]). Homer does not say whether he thought of the goddess Iris, who carries Zeus' message to Hector later in this book, as the rainbow (11.185–210; see [[iris]]).
