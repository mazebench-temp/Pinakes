## The simile

{{quote:be4b675c-4840-56e8-addc-74eb868513a4}}

οἷος δʼ ἀστὴρ εἶσι μετʼ ἀστράσι νυκτὸς ἀμολγῷ / ἕσπερος, ὃς κάλλιστος ἐν οὐρανῷ ἵσταται ἀστήρ: “And as a star goes among the stars in the dead of night, / the evening star, which stands in the sky the fairest star of all” (22.317–318). ἕσπερος is the evening and the star of evening. As a star it is named only here in Homer; in the Odyssey ἕσπερος is the dark evening that comes on (μέλας ἐπὶ ἕσπερος ἦλθε, *Odyssey* 1.423 = 18.306). The star of morning has its own name, ἑωσφόρος, “the morning star”, the bringer of dawn, which goes out to announce light upon the earth as the pyre of Patroclus dies down (23.226–228). εἶσι, “goes”, is the verb of both, and of the star of late summer at the start of this book (22.27).

νυκτὸς ἀμολγῷ, “in the dead of night”, is the phrase of that star too (22.28; see [[the-autumn-star]]). Walter Leaf, noting that Sirius, the star of late summer, is seen at night only in winter and spring, finds the precisely similar difficulty in this verse, and takes the two together to suggest that the words really mean the twilight, of morning or of evening (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 22.27; see the translation's note on 22.26–31). κάλλιστος, “the fairest”, answers that star's superlative, λαμπρότατος, “the brightest” (22.30). The star of late summer was a sign of evil and of fever; the evening star is only beautiful.

The tenor: ὣς αἰχμῆς ἀπέλαμπʼ εὐήκεος, ἣν ἄρʼ Ἀχιλλεὺς / πάλλεν δεξιτερῇ φρονέων κακὸν Ἕκτορι δίῳ, / εἰσορόων χρόα καλόν, ὅπῃ εἴξειε μάλιστα, “so light shone from the well-sharpened point which Achilles / brandished in his right hand, with evil intent against brilliant Hector, / eyeing his fair skin, where it would best give way” (22.319–321). ἀπέλαμπε has no subject: “there was a gleam”, with a word such as σέλας, “a blaze”, understood, a use that Leaf calls very curious and hardly to be paralleled in Homer (on 22.319; see the translation's note on 22.319). The verb is the one of the robe and of the helmet that shone like a star (ἀστὴρ ὣς ἀπέλαμπεν, 6.295, 19.381; see [[like-a-star]]). εὐήκης, “well-sharpened”, occurs only here. The point of likeness is the light and its beauty, and the simile sets beauty against killing. κάλλιστος is said of the star, χρόα καλόν, “his fair skin”, of the place the point is looking for, and καλά of the armor that Hector stripped from Patroclus, which covers all the rest of him (22.318, 22.321, 22.323).

## In context

Hector, knowing that the gods have called him to his death, has drawn his sword and swooped like an eagle flying high (22.296–311; see [[the-eagle-flying-high]]). Achilles rushes at him, “and filled his heart with savage / fury, and covered his chest in front with his shield, / fine and intricately worked, and nodded with his shining helmet / with its four ridges”, while the plumes of gold that Hephaestus set about the crest wave around it (22.312–316; see [[shield]] and [[helmet]]). Then the star, on the spear that Athena has given back to him (22.276–277; see [[the-pelian-ash]]). Hector is wearing Achilles' own armor, taken from Patroclus, and his flesh shows only where the collarbones hold the neck away from the shoulders, “at the throat, where the destruction of life is swiftest”; there Achilles drives the spear in, “and straight through the soft neck went the point” (22.322–327; see [[the-death-of-hector]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Stars in the Iliad are the flash of bronze and the sign of danger. Athena darts down like a shooting star, a portent for sailors or for an army (4.75–78; see [[shooting-star]]); a fire like the star of late summer blazes from Diomedes' helmet and shield (5.4–7; see [[the-autumn-star]]); Hector moves along the ranks like a baneful star that shows itself out of the clouds and sinks into them again (11.62–65; see [[the-baleful-star]]); Achilles' new helmet shines like a star (19.381; see [[like-a-star]]); and Priam sees him come over the plain like the star of late summer, the brightest and a sign of evil (22.25–31). Twice a star is a likeness of beauty alone: the watch-fires of the Trojans shine like the stars around the moon on a windless night (8.555–559; see [[stars-around-the-moon]]), and Hector's infant son in his nurse's arms is “like a lovely star” (6.401; see [[astyanax]]). The fairest star of the poem shines on the spear that kills his father.

## Outside Homer

[[virgil|Virgil]] gives a star to Pallas, the young son of Evander, riding out in the middle of the column to his first war, conspicuous in his cloak and his painted armor: he is like Lucifer, “the star that Venus loves above the other fires of the stars”, when he has bathed in the wave of Ocean and lifts his sacred face in the sky and dissolves the darkness (*Aeneid* 8.587–591). The bath in Ocean is that of Homer's star of late summer (5.6), and Venus' star, which rises as Lucifer in the morning and shines as the evening star, is the fairest in the sky. Pallas is killed by Turnus, and his belt costs Turnus his life (see [[virgil-the-death-of-turnus]]).
