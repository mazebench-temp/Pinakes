## The simile

{{quote:1cff4163-7ecb-513e-9a83-b73759fef637}}

τῷ δὲ μάλιστʼ ἄρʼ ἔην ἐναλίγκιον ὡς εἰ ἅπασα / Ἴλιος ὀφρυόεσσα πυρὶ σμύχοιτο κατʼ ἄκρης: “And it was most like as if all / beetling Ilios were smoldering with fire from top to bottom” (22.410–411). The likeness is between what is and what has not yet happened. The sound of the city mourning is most like (ἐναλίγκιον) the sound it would make if it were burning, and ὡς εἰ, “as if”, with the optative σμύχοιτο, puts the burning as a supposition. ἐναλίγκιος, “like”, is the word of the star of late summer and of the lightning (5.5, 13.242). σμύχω is to burn with a slow, smoldering fire. Homer has it once more, in Achilles' answer to the embassy: he will not think of bloody war until Hector comes to the huts and ships of the Myrmidons and “burns the ships with fire” (κατά τε σμῦξαι πυρὶ νῆας, 9.650–653). ὀφρυόεσσα, “beetling”, the city on its brow of rock, occurs only here.

κατʼ ἄκρης, “from top to bottom”, from the citadel down, is the phrase of a city's taking. Hector, reproaching Paris, tells him that “from top to bottom all of steep Ilios / has perished” (13.772–773), and tells Melanippus that they must fight until they kill the Achaeans or the Achaeans “from top to bottom … take steep Ilios and kill its citizens” (15.557–558). Andromache uses it in her last lament, when she tells the dead Hector that the city will be sacked from top to bottom before their son grows up, for he has perished, its guardian, who kept it safe (24.728–730).

The point of likeness is the noise of grief and what it means. Hector was the city's guard. The Trojans call his son Astyanax, lord of the city, “for Hector alone was guarding Ilios” (6.403), and Andromache says it again in this book: “for you alone guarded their gates and long walls” (22.507; see [[astyanax]]). With the man who guarded it dead, the city mourns as if it were taken.

## In context

Achilles has stripped the body; the Achaeans have run up to gaze at Hector and to stab him; and Achilles has pierced the tendons of his feet, tied him to the chariot, and dragged his head through the dust before the city, “that once was handsome; but then Zeus gave him to his enemies / to defile in his own native land” (22.367–404; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). His mother tears her hair and flings away her head covering and wails, his father groans, and the people throughout the city are seized by wailing and groaning (22.405–409; see [[hecuba]], [[priam]] and [[lament]]). The simile follows. Then the people can hardly hold back the old man from going out through the Dardanian gates to beg Achilles for his son (22.412–428), Hecuba leads the lament of the Trojan women (22.430–436), and Andromache, at her loom in the inner part of the house, hears the wailing from the tower and runs to the wall (22.437–474; see [[like-a-woman-out-of-her-mind]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The poem foretells the burning of Troy and never shows it (see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). Hera and Athena have sworn never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans, “not even when all Troy blazes with ravening fire, / burning, and the warlike sons of the Achaeans are burning it”, and the river swears the same oath in the same words (20.313–317, 21.373–376; see [[troy]]). In the similes of the later books other cities burn. The war around the body of Patroclus is savage as a fire that rushes on a city of men and suddenly blazes up (17.736–741; see [[the-burning-city]]); the light about Achilles' head is like the smoke and the beacons of an island town that enemies are fighting around (18.207–214; see [[the-beacon-fires]]); and the toil and sorrows that Achilles brings on the Trojans are like smoke going up from a burning city, sent up by the wrath of the gods (21.522–525; see [[smoke-from-a-burning-city]]). In this simile the city is Ilios itself, and only its grief is real.

## Outside Homer

[[virgil|Virgil]] gives the likeness to the mourning for Dido. When she has fallen on the sword, Rumor runs wild through the shaken city; the houses roar with laments and groaning and the wailing of women, and the upper air resounds with great cries of mourning, “no otherwise than if all Carthage or ancient Tyre were falling to the enemy who had broken in, and raging flames were rolling over the roofs of men and of gods” (*Aeneid* 4.663–671). In both cities the one on whom everything rested is dead.
