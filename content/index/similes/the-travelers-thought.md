## The simile

{{quote:2d9b422f-6fe6-5a7e-832c-339757cfee39}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἂν ἀΐξῃ νόος ἀνέρος, ὅς τʼ ἐπὶ πολλὴν / γαῖαν ἐληλουθὼς φρεσὶ πευκαλίμῃσι νοήσῃ / “ἔνθʼ εἴην ἢ ἔνθα,” μενοινήῃσί τε πολλά: “As when the mind of a man darts, who has traveled over much of the earth, and in his shrewd mind he thinks, ‘If only I were there, or there,’ and longs for many things” (15.80–82).

νόος is the mind, and here the thought itself. ἀΐσσω, “dart”, is the verb of quick movement, of a bird's flight and of the gods: Iris and Apollo, sent by Hera, “darted off and flew” to Ida a few verses later (ἀΐξαντε πετέσθην, 15.150), and Hera “darted” from the peak of Olympus on her way to Lemnos and Ida (ἀΐξασα, 14.225). ἐληλουθώς, “having traveled”, the perfect of ἔρχομαι, gives the man a past: he has been to the places he now thinks of. φρεσὶ πευκαλίμῃσι, “in his shrewd mind”, is a formula of good sense (see [[shrewd-mind]]). His thought is given in his own words, a wish in the optative, ἔνθʼ εἴην ἢ ἔνθα, “if only I were there, or there”, which the Greek sets as a quotation (see the translation's note on 15.80–83). μενοινάω is “long for, have one's mind set on”, and πολλά, “many things”, leaves the wish open.

The tenor is one verse: ὣς κραιπνῶς μεμαυῖα διέπτατο πότνια Ἥρη, “so swiftly queen Hera flew in her eagerness” (15.83). κραιπνός, “swift”, is a word for quick feet and swift winds, and once for the mind itself: Antilochus, excusing himself to Menelaus after the chariot race, says that a young man's mind is the quicker (κραιπνότερος … νόος) and his counsel the thinner (23.589–590). μεμαυῖα, “eager”, is Zeus' word for Hera when he asks her where she is bound, coming down from Olympus to Gargarus (14.298), and διαπέτομαι is “fly through”.

The comparison is one of speed, and it measures the speed of a god by the quickest thing a mortal knows, his own thought, which is in one place and then in another with nothing between. The traveler's mind does not move through space; it is at once where it wishes to be. The vehicle is a movement of the mind rather than a thing in nature, and it has a mood: the man is not where he wants to be, and he longs for many places at once. Hera's flight has one goal, Olympus, and the errand is not her own. She has just been threatened by Zeus (15.14–33), and she arrives among the gods in anger (15.100–112).

## In context

Zeus, awake on Ida, has heard Hera's oath that Poseidon helps the Achaeans of his own will, and he sends her back to Olympus to call Iris and Apollo (15.36–77; see [[hera]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]). “So he spoke, and the white-armed goddess Hera did not disobey, and she went from the mountains of Ida to high Olympus” (15.78–79). After the simile she comes to steep Olympus, where the gods are gathered in the house of Zeus; they all spring up and greet her with their cups, and she takes the cup of Themis, who comes running first to meet her (15.84–88; see [[themis-the-goddess]]).

The simile is the first of three for the journeys of the gods in this part of the book. Its last half-verse, κραιπνῶς μεμαυῖα διέπτατο, “swiftly … flew in her eagerness”, returns for Iris, who goes down from Ida to Poseidon like snow or hail driven by the North Wind (15.170–172; see [[snow-and-hail]]), and Apollo goes down from Ida like a hawk (15.237–238; see [[like-a-hawk]]). Hera's journey from Olympus to Ida in *Iliad* 14 was told stage by stage, over Pieria and Emathia, the snowy mountains of the Thracians, Athos, the sea and Lemnos (14.225–230, 14.281–285). Her journey back is told by a single likeness.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The speed of the gods is measured elsewhere by what a man can do. Each leap of Hera's horses covers as much of the misty distance as a man can see from a lookout over the wine-dark sea (5.770–772; see [[the-lookout]]). In the Odyssey the ships of the Phaeacians are as swift as a wing or a thought (ὡς εἰ πτερὸν ἠὲ νόημα, *Odyssey* 7.36).

## Outside Homer

The Homeric Hymn to Apollo sends the god from earth to Olympus “like thought” (ὥστε νόημα, *Homeric Hymn* 3.186–187). The Hymn to Hermes has a simile close to this one for the young god's quickness of invention: as a swift thought passes through the breast of a man whom thronging cares beset, or as glances flash from the eyes, so Hermes devised word and deed together (*Homeric Hymn* 4.43–46; see [[hermes]]).
