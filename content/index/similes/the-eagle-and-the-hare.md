## The simile

{{quote:897fdfdc-6094-59ba-a947-7b3daee0d498}}

ὣς ἄρα φωνήσας ἀπέβη ξανθὸς Μενέλαος, / πάντοσε παπταίνων ὥς τʼ αἰετός, ὅν ῥά τέ φασιν / ὀξύτατον δέρκεσθαι ὑπουρανίων πετεηνῶν, “So he spoke, and fair-haired Menelaus went away, / looking about everywhere like an eagle, which, they say, / has the sharpest sight of all the winged birds under heaven” (17.673–675). The narrator gives the eagle's sight as something men say, as he gives the bed of Typhoeus in another simile, “where they say Typhoeus has his bed” (2.783). ὀξύτατον δέρκεσθαι is “to see most keenly”; it is one of the eagle's superlatives, with the surest of birds in omen and the strongest and swiftest of birds (8.247, 21.253; see [[eagle]]). The birds are ὑπουράνιοι, “under heaven”, all the birds the sky covers (see [[under-heaven]]).

ὅν τε καὶ ὑψόθʼ ἐόντα πόδας ταχὺς οὐκ ἔλαθε πτὼξ / θάμνῳ ὑπʼ ἀμφικόμῳ κατακείμενος, ἀλλά τʼ ἐπʼ αὐτῷ / ἔσσυτο, καί τέ μιν ὦκα λαβὼν ἐξείλετο θυμόν, “and even when he is high up, the hare, swift of foot, does not escape him, / crouching under a leafy bush, but he swoops / down on it, and quickly seizes it and takes its life” (17.676–678). πτώξ, “the cowerer”, is the hare (see [[hare]]). ἀμφίκομος is “leafy all round”: Walter Leaf notes that ἀμφι- has here gone beyond its first sense, “on both sides”, and that κόμη, “hair”, is used of foliage, as of the olive whose leafy top Odysseus lopped to make his bed (*Odyssey* 23.195; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.677). The words of the kill, ἐξείλετο θυμόν, are used of a man killed by a man: Teucer would have stopped Hector “if he had hit him while he was the best in the fighting, and taken away his life” (15.460).

The tenor turns to Menelaus himself: ὣς τότε σοὶ Μενέλαε διοτρεφὲς ὄσσε φαεινὼ / πάντοσε δινείσθην πολέων κατὰ ἔθνος ἑταίρων, / εἴ που Νέστορος υἱὸν ἔτι ζώοντα ἴδοιτο, “So then, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, your shining eyes / roved everywhere through the crowd of your many companions, / to see if somewhere they might find the son of Nestor still alive” (17.679–681). The narrator speaks to him in the second person, as he does again twenty verses later (17.702; see [[apostrophe]]). The point of likeness is the sight: the eagle's eye searching a wide field from above for one small creature, and finding it. The swoop and the kill belong to the bird alone. What Menelaus finds is Antilochus, to whom he must give the news of Patroclus' death. Leaf judged this simile much more apt than the lion simile that comes just before it, which he thought pointless where Menelaus goes of his own will (on 17.657; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]).

## In context

When Zeus has scattered the mist, Ajax tells Menelaus to look for Antilochus, son of Nestor, and send him to tell Achilles that Patroclus is dead (17.648–655). Menelaus goes from the body very unwillingly, like a lion leaving a farmyard at dawn, and charges the Ajaxes and Meriones to remember the gentleness of Patroclus (17.656–672). Then he goes looking (17.673–681). “And very quickly he saw him on the left of all the battle” (17.682), and he gives him the news; Antilochus is struck speechless, his eyes fill with tears, and he gives his armor to Laodocus and runs to Achilles (17.683–701; see [[antilochus]] and [[menelaus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The eagle is the hunter of Homer's similes. Hector makes for the ships as a tawny eagle swoops on a flock of geese or cranes or swans feeding beside a river (15.690–692; see [[the-eagle-and-the-birds]]); Achilles springs away from the river with the swoop of the black eagle, the hunter, the strongest and swiftest of birds (21.251–253); and Hector, drawing his sword for his last charge, swoops like a high-flying eagle that goes down through the dark clouds to the plain to seize a tender lamb or a cowering hare (22.306–311), a verse that the Odyssey gives to Odysseus falling on the suitors' kinsmen (*Odyssey* 24.538). The hare is the quarry in the Iliad's other hunting simile that has it, run down by two hounds in the chase of Dolon (10.360–364; see [[two-hounds]]).

## Outside Homer

In Aeschylus' *Agamemnon* the eagle and the hare become an omen. Two eagles, king of birds, appear to the army as it sets out, feeding on a hare big with young, and the army's prophet knows them for the two warlike sons of Atreus, the “hare-feasters” (λαγοδαίτας, *Agamemnon* 109–125; see [[hare]]).
