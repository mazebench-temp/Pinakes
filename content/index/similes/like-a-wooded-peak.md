## The simile

{{quote:ca6f6215-b9ad-50d0-a083-a77701f7d460}}

καὶ γὰρ θαῦμʼ ἐτέτυκτο πελώριον, οὐδὲ ἐῴκει / ἀνδρί γε σιτοφάγῳ, ἀλλὰ ῥίῳ ὑλήεντι / ὑψηλῶν ὀρέων, ὅ τε φαίνεται οἶον ἀπʼ ἄλλων: “For he was made a huge wonder, and was not like / a man who eats bread, but like a wooded peak / of high mountains, which stands out alone, apart from the others” (*Odyssey* 9.190–192). The comparison is made twice, first by denial, then by likeness: not like a man, but like a mountain. σιτοφάγος, “who eats bread”, marks a human being, as σῖτον ἔδοντες does when Odysseus sends his scouts to learn “what men they might be who eat bread upon the earth” (*Odyssey* 9.89; see [[who-eat-the-fruit-of-the-field]] and the translation's table of key words). The Cyclops is not of them, and the tale soon shows what he eats instead (*Odyssey* 9.288–298; see [[like-puppies]] and [[the-devouring-lion]]). ῥίον is a peak or a jutting height, ὑλήεις “wooded” (see [[wooded]]), and θαῦμα πελώριον, “a huge wonder”, gives him the word of the man himself, ἀνὴρ … πελώριος (*Odyssey* 9.187; see [[huge]]).

The point of likeness is first size. A scholium that W. W. Merry quotes says that the poet makes many comparisons of the Cyclops' size: he likened a man to a mountain, as one who exceeds the size of every living creature, and not simply to a mountain but to a wooded peak, a mountain higher still and wooded, which is hyperbole upon hyperbole (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.192). The second point is solitude. The peak “stands out alone, apart from the others”, as the man “herded his flocks / alone, far off; and he did not go among the others, / but lived apart” (*Odyssey* 9.187–189): οἶος, ἀπόπροθεν, οὐδὲ μετʼ ἄλλους, ἀπάνευθεν are so many repetitions, Merry notes, to stress the isolated, unsociable life of the monster (on 9.188), and the simile repeats it once more. The text has ὅ τε, “which”, the reading Wolf took from several manuscripts and Merry prefers, for the common ὅτε, “when”, which Bekker kept (Merry on 9.192 and his critical note).

## In context

Odysseus has crossed from the island of goats with his own ship to “make trial of these men, who they are” (*Odyssey* 9.173–176; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). Near the shore they see the cave, high and overhung with laurels, and the high fold around it (*Odyssey* 9.181–186; see [[the-cave-of-polyphemus]] and [[fold]]). Before the companions go up, before anyone has seen its master, the narrator describes him: “There a huge man used to sleep, who herded his flocks / alone, far off; and he did not go among the others, / but lived apart, and knew lawless ways” (*Odyssey* 9.187–189; see [[themis]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). Odysseus tells it as he learned it afterwards, and the simile prepares the size the story will need: the door-stone that twenty-two wagons could not have heaved (*Odyssey* 9.240–243; see [[door-stone]]), the club like a ship's mast (*Odyssey* 9.319–324; see [[the-mast-of-twenty-oars]]), and the peak of a great mountain that the blinded Cyclops breaks off and hurls at the ship (*Odyssey* 9.481).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The other Cyclopes “live on the peaks of high mountains / in hollow caves” (*Odyssey* 9.113–114; see [[cyclopes]]), the simile's ὑψηλῶν ὀρέων. In the next book the queen of the Laestrygonians is found to be “as big as a mountain peak” (ὅσην τʼ ὄρεος κορυφήν, *Odyssey* 10.112–113, a plain rendering), a measure that Merry sets beside the Cyclops' club (on 9.322). In the Iliad Hector rushes off “like a snowy mountain” (*Iliad* 13.754; see [[like-a-snowy-mountain]]).

## Outside Homer

Virgil keeps the mountain in the Cyclopes' size. His Polyphemus is so tall that he “strikes the high stars” (*ipse arduus, altaque pulsat / sidera*, *Aeneid* 3.619–620), and when the Cyclopes come down to the shore at his cry they stand there “like lofty oaks or cone-bearing cypresses that stand on a high summit, the high wood of Jupiter or the grove of Diana” (*quales cum vertice celso / aeriae quercus aut coniferae cyparissi / constiterunt, silva alta Iovis lucusve Dianae*, *Aeneid* 3.679–681; see [[virgil]]).
