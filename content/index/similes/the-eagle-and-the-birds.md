## The simile

{{quote:af20937f-6f49-5333-8e57-fdfcd80f1427}}

ἀλλʼ ὥς τʼ ὀρνίθων πετεηνῶν αἰετὸς αἴθων / ἔθνος ἐφορμᾶται ποταμὸν πάρα βοσκομενάων / χηνῶν ἢ γεράνων ἢ κύκνων δουλιχοδείρων: “but as a tawny eagle swoops on a tribe of winged birds as they feed beside a river, geese or cranes or long-necked swans” (15.690–692). αἴθων, “blazing, tawny”, is said also of lions, oxen, horses and iron, and of the eagle only here (see [[eagle]] and [[tawny]]). ἔθνος is a tribe or flock, and ἐφορμάομαι “rush upon”.

The birds are those of the Asian meadow. The first half-verse and the whole third verse come from the simile of Book 2, where the Achaean army poured out onto the plain like the many tribes of winged birds, geese or cranes or long-necked swans, flying over the meadow by the Cayster (2.459–463; see [[birds-on-the-asian-meadow]], [[geese]], [[cranes]] and [[swans]]). There the birds were the army going out to war in its numbers; here the same flock is feeding, and something falls on it from above.

The tenor: ὣς Ἕκτωρ ἴθυσε νεὸς κυανοπρῴροιο / ἀντίος ἀΐξας, “so Hector made straight for a dark-prowed ship, darting at it face to face” (15.693–694). ἰθύω is to press straight on, and ἀΐσσω “dart”, the verb of birds and of the gods' flight. The quarry is a ship, not a man, and the flock of birds is the crowd of ships and men drawn up on the shore. Behind the eagle stands its god: “and Zeus pushed him on from behind with his very great hand, and urged on the army with him” (15.694–695). The eagle is the bird of Zeus, and here Zeus' own hand is at Hector's back (see [[zeus]] and [[plan-of-zeus]]).

## In context

The simile answers the one before it. Ajax strides from deck to deck along the line of ships like a rider leaping from horse to horse (15.674–688; see [[the-rider-of-four-horses]]); “nor did Hector stay in the throng of the close-armored Trojans”, and he falls on the ships like an eagle (15.688–693). After it the battle at the ships is bitter, and both sides fight as if unwearied: the Achaeans think they will perish, the Trojans hope to burn the ships and kill the Achaean heroes (15.696–703). Hector takes hold of the stern of the ship that brought Protesilaus to Troy and calls for fire (15.704–725; see [[protesilaus]] and [[stern]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The eagle is Hector's likeness again at the end of his life: drawing his sword for his last charge against Achilles, he swoops like an eagle flying high that goes down to the plain through the murky clouds to snatch a tender lamb or a cowering hare (22.306–310; see [[the-eagle-flying-high]]). Menelaus looks about for Antilochus like the eagle, sharpest-sighted of the birds, that sees from on high a hare crouching under a bush and swoops on it (17.674–678), and Automedon drives against the Trojans like a vulture after geese (17.460). In the Odyssey Odysseus and his companions fall on the suitors as vultures with crooked talons fall on birds that cower in the plain, and men rejoice at the hunt (*Odyssey* 22.302–306); in Penelope's dream an eagle from the mountain breaks the necks of her twenty geese, and the geese are the suitors (*Odyssey* 19.535–553).
