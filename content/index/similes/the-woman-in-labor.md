## The simile

{{quote:945db8eb-1f57-5d97-bc58-edc10011c89f}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἂν ὠδίνουσαν ἔχῃ βέλος ὀξὺ γυναῖκα / δριμύ: “as when the sharp dart seizes a woman in labor, the piercing dart”. ὠδίνω is “be in labor”, and ὠδῖνες are the pangs. The pain is a missile, βέλος, the word for an arrow or a spear, and it is “sharp” (ὀξύ) and “piercing” (δριμύ, also “bitter”). The goddesses who send it are the Εἰλείθυιαι μογοστόκοι, “who bring hard birth” (μόγος, “toil”, and τόκος, “birth”), “the daughters of Hera, who have the bitter pangs in their keeping” (11.270–271; see [[eileithyia]] and [[hera]]).

The simile is framed by one verse said twice: ὀξεῖαι δʼ ὀδύναι δῦνον μένος Ἀτρεΐδαο, “sharp pains came over the might of the son of Atreus” (11.268), and again after the comparison, ὣς ὀξεῖʼ ὀδύναι, “so sharp pains” (11.272). ὀδύναι, “pains”, and ὠδῖνες, “pangs of labor”, sound alike, and the sharpness passes from the dart to the pains. The point of likeness is pain that comes from outside, sharp, sudden and not to be fought. Agamemnon has fought on while the wound bled; the pain that comes when it dries he cannot fight.

## In context

Coon's spear went through Agamemnon's forearm below the elbow. “While the blood still welled up warm from his wound” he ranged through the ranks with spear, sword and great stones, “but when the wound dried and the blood stopped”, the pains came (11.252–268). He mounts his chariot, orders his charioteer to the ships, and calls on the Argive leaders to defend them, because Zeus has not allowed him to fight all day (11.273–283; see [[the-aristeia-of-agamemnon]]). The king's day ends in the image of a woman's pain. The verse of the drying wound returns at the end of the book, when Patroclus' bitter root stops Eurypylus' pain: “and the wound dried, and the blood stopped” (11.848 = 11.267; see [[healing]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

No other simile in Homer takes its picture from a woman's labor; the nearest is the cow that stands over her first calf, “not knowing birth before”, as Menelaus stands over the body of Patroclus (17.4–5). Eileithyia appears again in the Iliad as the goddess of births: she brings Eudorus to the light (16.187), and on the day Heracles is to be born Hera holds back the Eileithyiai from Alcmene and hastens another birth (19.103–119). The verb ὠδίνω returns once, in the Odyssey, for the blinded Cyclops, “groaning and in agony with his pains” (ὠδίνων ὀδύνῃσι, *Odyssey* 9.415).

## Outside Homer

Euripides' Medea turns the comparison around. Men say, she tells the women of Corinth, that women live a life without danger at home while they fight with the spear: “I would rather stand three times beside a shield than give birth once” (*Medea* 248–251).
