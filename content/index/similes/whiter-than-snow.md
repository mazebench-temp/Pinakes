## The simile

{{quote:a83ae8a5-2390-5de2-a88f-063dc7d47822}}

λευκότεροι χιόνος, θείειν δʼ ἀνέμοισιν ὁμοῖοι: “whiter than snow, and like the winds in running”. One verse makes two comparisons, of color and of speed. λευκότεροι is a comparative, “whiter”, with snow as the measure; θείειν, “in running”, is an infinitive of respect, and the winds are the measure of their speed. Snow comes into other Homeric comparisons for its thickness, as words, stones and helmets fall or pour like snowflakes (3.222, 12.156, 12.278, 19.357), or for its speed, as Iris flies like snow or hail from the clouds (15.170). Only here is it the measure of a color. White horses are named nowhere else in Homer (see the translation's note on 10.437).

The verse belongs to a description that keeps raising its measure. The horses are the finest and biggest Dolon has seen; the chariot is worked with gold and silver; the armor is golden, “huge, a wonder to see”, and fit to be worn not by mortal men “but for the immortal gods” (10.436–441). The horses come first, and they too are given the measure of things beyond men, the snow and the winds.

## In context

Dolon has come out for the horses of Achilles, which Odysseus has told him are hard for any mortal to master and drive (10.401–404; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Asked about the Trojan watch and the allies' camps, he answers all, and then, unasked, he points his captors to another team: the Thracians, newly come and camped farthest out, and their king Rhesus with his horses (10.432–441; see [[dolon]], [[rhesus]] and [[the-horses-of-rhesus]]). The two do what his words invite. Odysseus knows the horses when he sees them, “here are the horses / that Dolon, whom we killed, told us about” (10.477–478), and drives them off while Diomedes kills (10.498–514). When they ride into the camp Nestor says that he has never seen or noticed horses like these, “terribly like the rays of the sun” (10.547–550; see [[like-the-sun]]), and asks whether some god gave them; Odysseus answers that a god could easily give even better horses (10.556–557; see [[horses]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Other horses have the winds for kin. Achilles' horses, Xanthus and Balius, “flew with the blasts of the wind”, for the Harpy Podarge bore them to the West Wind (16.149–151), and Xanthus tells Achilles that the two of them could run with the blast of Zephyrus, the swiftest of winds (19.415–416). The North Wind sired twelve foals on the mares of Erichthonius, which ran over the tips of the grain without breaking it and over the breakers of the sea (20.221–229). Eumelus' mares are “swift-footed like birds” (2.763–765; see [[swift-as-birds]]). The comparative of whiteness returns once, in the Odyssey, where Athena makes Penelope “whiter than sawn ivory” as she sleeps (*Odyssey* 18.196).

## Outside Homer

The tragedy *Rhesus* keeps the whiteness of the horses and finds its own comparisons for it. A shepherd, bringing Hector word of the king's arrival, tells of his horses under a golden yoke, “brighter than snow” (*Rhesus* 303–304), and Athena, sending Odysseus and Diomedes to him, describes his white mares tied beside the chariots, shining in the night like the wing of a river swan (616–618; see [[the-doloneia]]).
