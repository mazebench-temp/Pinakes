## The simile

{{quote:08b03d22-51f5-5a65-93b4-74c9eb64c725}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἂν ἄνδρʼ ἄτη πυκινὴ λάβῃ, ὅς τʼ ἐνὶ πάτρῃ / φῶτα κατακτείνας ἄλλων ἐξίκετο δῆμον / ἀνδρὸς ἐς ἀφνειοῦ, θάμβος δʼ ἔχει εἰσορόωντας: “As when a heavy blind folly seizes a man who in his native land / has killed a man, and he comes to the land of others, / to the house of a wealthy man, and amazement holds those who watch” (24.480–482). ἄτη is the blind folly that the gods send on a man's mind (see [[ate]]); the book has named it once before, as the cause of the war, “for Alexander's blind folly” (24.28). πυκινή, “heavy”, is the adjective of things packed close, ranks and clouds and well-fitted doors (4.281, 5.751, 14.167), and of a grief that falls thick on men (πυκινὸν ἄχος, 16.599); only here is it said of ἄτη. Walter Leaf takes the folly to be the force that drove the man to the killing, not the remorse or the confusion that came after it, as others had thought, and explains the syntax by this: the poet begins as if he would say “as when folly seizes a man so that he kills another”, and the main thought, that the killer comes to the land of others, takes over the clause (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.480).

The scene is one that the Iliad knows well. A man who has killed must pay the penalty to the dead man's kin or leave his country; Ajax tells Achilles that a man accepts the penalty even for a brother or a son, “and the killer stays there among his people, having paid much” (9.632–634; see [[poine]]). Those who could not pay fled, and the poem names several who did. Tlepolemus killed his father's uncle Licymnius and fled over the sea to Rhodes (2.661–667; see [[tlepolemus]]); Medon, the bastard son of Oileus, lived in Phylace, “away from his native land, since he had killed a man” (13.696; see [[medon]]); Lycophron came from Cythera to live with Ajax, “since he had killed a man in holy Cythera” (15.431–432; see [[lycophron]]). Leaf describes the custom: the homicide takes refuge with a chieftain and enters his following in return for food and protection, and ἀνδρὸς ἀφνειοῦ, “of a wealthy man”, is said because only a rich chief would attract or could keep such men (on 24.480). A wealthy man's house gives the measure of one other simile in the book, the eagle whose wings are as wide as the door of a wealthy man's chamber (24.317–319; see [[wings-as-wide-as-a-door]]).

The point of likeness is the amazement, θάμβος, of those who see the stranger. The man who has killed comes into a house where no one expects him, and those who see him are amazed; so Achilles and his companions look at Priam and at one another. θάμβος δʼ ἔχει εἰσορόωντας, “amazement holds those who watch”, is a formula of the battlefield, said when Paris and Menelaus stand ready for their duel and when Athena comes down like a shooting star (3.342, 4.79; see [[thambos]]), and θαμβέω is the verb of Achilles' astonishment when Athena takes hold of his hair (1.199). Leaf notes that this simile, unlike every other simile of its form, adds the point of comparison as an independent clause, θάμβος δʼ ἔχει, so that its minor touches come first and are in danger of overshadowing the essential elements (on 24.480).

## The reversal

The likeness is turned the wrong way round. In the simile the man who has killed comes to the house of a stranger; in the scene the stranger is Priam, and the killer is the man whose house he enters. The verse before has said it: Priam “clasped the knees of Achilles in his hands and kissed his hands, / the terrible, man-slaying hands that had killed many of his sons” (24.478–479), and Priam will end his plea with the same thought, “to put to my lips the hand of the man who killed my sons” (24.506).

The house of a wealthy man, to which such exiles came, is in the Iliad's own stories the house of Achilles' father. Peleus took in Patroclus when he was brought to Phthia “because of a grievous killing”, the death of the son of Amphidamas over a game of knucklebones, and made him Achilles' attendant (23.85–90; see [[patroclus]]); Epeigeus, “having slain a brave cousin of his”, came “as a suppliant to Peleus and to silver-footed Thetis” (16.573–574; see [[epeigeus]]); and Phoenix, fleeing from his father's house, came to Peleus, who received him gladly and made him wealthy (9.478–483; see [[phoenix]]). In the next verses Priam bids Achilles remember his father (24.486; see [[peleus]]). See also [[supplication]].

## In context

Hermes has brought Priam unseen through the camp and left him at the door of the hut, telling him to clasp Achilles' knees and beg him by his father, his mother and his child (24.440–467; see [[hermes]]). Priam goes straight in. Achilles has just finished his meal, and only Automedon and Alcimus are with him (24.471–476). “Great Priam came in unseen by them” (24.477), clasps his knees and kisses his hands. After the simile, “so Achilles was amazed when he saw godlike Priam; / and the others were amazed too, and looked at one another” (24.483–484), and Priam begins to speak (24.485–506; see [[priam]] and [[achilles]]). He has clasped the knees and kissed the hands, and his words make Achilles weep for his own father; the two weep together (24.507–512).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The arrival of a stranger who clasps the knees of the master or mistress of a house and strikes the household silent is told in the Odyssey, when Odysseus, hidden in a mist by Athena, comes unseen into the hall of Alcinous: he throws his arms around the knees of Arete, the mist is shed from him, and those in the hall fall silent at the sight of a man in the house and marvel as they look (*Odyssey* 7.139–145). Another exile is the seer Theoclymenus, who, having killed a man of his own tribe, flees the kinsmen of the dead and begs Telemachus to take him aboard his ship (*Odyssey* 15.271–278), and Odysseus tells his son that one who has killed even a single man, with few to avenge him, goes into exile, leaving his kin and his native land (*Odyssey* 23.118–120).

## Outside Homer

The ancient commentators took the exile to be seeking purification. Leaf reports that the T scholia speak of the one who purifies, and that the B scholia explain that the man goes to someone who will cleanse him, so that K. O. Müller conjectured a reading ἀνδρὸς ἐς ἁγνίτεω, “to the house of one who purifies”. Leaf replies that such a reading could only be late: Homer knows no expiation for blood except payment or exile, and ritual purification is unknown to him; the T scholia themselves allow that the idea may be an anachronism (on 24.482; see the translation's note on 24.480–482). In Herodotus the custom is full grown, and he says that the Lydians purify much as the Greeks do. Adrastus, son of Gordias, who has killed his brother without meaning to and has been driven out by his father, comes to the house of Croesus at Sardis, “not clean of hands”, and asks for purification; Croesus purifies him, and only then asks who he is and whom he has killed, and keeps him in his house (*Histories* 1.35).
