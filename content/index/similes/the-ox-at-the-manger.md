## The simile

{{quote:a61d5baf-8177-5966-9719-174ab41ce025}}

ἀλλά μοι Αἴγισθος τεύξας θάνατόν τε μόρον τε / ἔκτα σὺν οὐλομένῃ ἀλόχῳ, οἶκόνδε καλέσσας, / δειπνίσσας, ὥς τίς τε κατέκτανε βοῦν ἐπὶ φάτνῃ: “but Aegisthus, contriving death and fate for me, / killed me, with my ruinous wife, when he had called me to his house / and feasted me, as one kills an ox at the manger” (*Odyssey* 11.409–411). The last verse is Proteus' verse word for word (*Odyssey* 4.535). ὥς τίς τε stands for ὥς τέ τις, as in a lion simile of the Iliad (*Iliad* 17.61), and κατέκτανε is the aorist of what is always done, the tense of similes (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 4.535). In Agamemnon's mouth δειπνίσσας, “and feasted me”, follows καλέσσας without a connecting word: the asyndeton, Merry says, is intended, and befits the outburst of indignation and sorrow (on *Odyssey* 11.411).

The likeness lies in the meal. The ox stands at its manger, fed, and the host's feast is laid for the king; the beast is killed where it eats, and the man where he has been feasted. φάτνη, “manger”, is elsewhere in Homer the horses' manger: Anchises and Priam rear their horses at it, the horses of Diomedes stand at it, and the stalled horse of the simile breaks his halter there (*Iliad* 5.271, 24.280; 10.568; 6.506 = 15.263); only in this verse is an ox at the manger. The ox fed for slaughter is a beast of the house, not the ox of the fields that the Iliad's similes strike down with the axe (see [[the-ox-and-the-axe]] and [[cattle]]).

## In context

Proteus tells Menelaus of his brother's homecoming: Aegisthus posted a lookout, chose twenty men for an ambush, and bade others prepare a feast; then he went with horses and chariot to invite Agamemnon, “and he led him up, unaware of his destruction, and killed him / when he had feasted him, as one kills an ox at the manger”, and none of the companions of either survived (*Odyssey* 4.524–537; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]], [[aegisthus]] and [[feast]]). In the land of the dead Agamemnon tells it himself, and puts his wife beside Aegisthus at the killing, σὺν οὐλομένῃ ἀλόχῳ, “with my ruinous wife” (*Odyssey* 11.410; see [[clytemnestra]]); Merry observes that the Homeric story makes Aegisthus the murderer and the scene a banquet, while Aeschylus and Euripides have Clytemnestra kill him in the bath, yet even here she is his accomplice (on *Odyssey* 11.409). He also found Proteus' account, in which the ambush and the companions on both sides fall to a man, inconsistent with this later form of the story, in which Clytemnestra plays so large a part (on *Odyssey* 4.536). The ox is followed at once by another likeness of the feast, the companions killed around him “like white-tusked hogs / that are slaughtered in the house of a rich man of great power / for a wedding, or a shared feast, or a rich banquet” (*Odyssey* 11.413–415; see [[like-white-tusked-hogs]]), and by the sight that Odysseus would have grieved at most, the dead around the mixing bowl and the laden tables, and the floor seething with blood (*Odyssey* 11.416–420; see [[mixing-bowl]] and [[table]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Iliad's warriors die like cattle in other ways. Aretus leaps forward and falls like an ox struck behind the horns with an axe (*Iliad* 17.520–524; see [[the-ox-and-the-axe]]); Adamas writhes around the spear like an ox that herdsmen have bound with ropes (*Iliad* 13.570–573; see [[the-roped-ox]]); Hippodamas bellows as a bull bellows when young men drag it around the Heliconian lord (*Iliad* 20.403–405); and Sarpedon dies like a bull that a lion kills among the herd (*Iliad* 16.487–491; see [[lion-among-cattle]]). Those are beasts of the field, the altar and the herd; this one dies in its stall.

## Outside Homer

In Aeschylus' *Agamemnon* the beast becomes a bull killed by a cow. Cassandra, seeing the murder before it happens, cries: “Keep the bull away from the cow! In the robes she catches him with the black-horned device and strikes; he falls into the vessel of water. I tell you of the fortune of a treacherous, murdering cauldron” (*Agamemnon* 1125–1129; see [[aeschylus]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon-in-tragedy]]). The bath of the tragedy has replaced the feast, and the wife the lover.
