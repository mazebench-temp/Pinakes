## The simile

{{quote:f0a72b01-66da-5edc-9e11-d3864f51810f}}

ἀμφὶ δʼ ἄρʼ αὐτῷ βαῖνʼ ὥς τις περὶ πόρτακι μήτηρ / πρωτοτόκος κινυρὴ οὐ πρὶν εἰδυῖα τόκοιο: “and bestrode him, as a mother stands over her calf, / one that has borne her first, lowing plaintively, and never knew birth before” (17.4–5). ἀμφὶ … βαῖνε is the stance of a warrior over a fallen comrade, astride the body to keep the enemy from it (see [[fighting-over-the-body]]). The animal is not named: she is only μήτηρ, “mother”, and her calf is a πόρταξ, a word found only here (the related πόρτις is a heifer, 5.162). πρωτοτόκος, “bearing for the first time”, is also found only here, and the next words say it again, οὐ πρὶν εἰδυῖα τόκοιο, “and never knew birth before”. Walter Leaf calls the repetition thoroughly Homeric and defends the verse against the critics who would cut it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.5). κινυρή, “plaintive”, occurs nowhere else in Homer. Leaf accepts Eustathius' explanation of it, “plaintive-voiced, for the greatness of her love” (on 17.5; see the translation's note on 17.4–5).

The point of likeness is the mother's care. Leaf takes it to lie in her affection for her young alone, and doubts that she is to be pictured warding off a beast of prey (on 17.4). The young cow has never had a calf before, and she will not leave it; she stands over it and cries. The tenor: ὣς περὶ Πατρόκλῳ βαῖνε ξανθὸς Μενέλαος, “so fair-haired Menelaus bestrode Patroclus” (17.6). What he stands over is a dead man.

The next two verses arm him: he holds his spear and his shield in front of the body, “eager to kill whoever came against him” (17.7–8). They are the verses of Aeneas standing over the body of Pandarus (5.300–301), and there the verse before them gave him another likeness: ἀμφὶ δʼ ἄρʼ αὐτῷ βαῖνε λέων ὣς ἀλκὶ πεποιθώς, “He bestrode it like a lion trusting in his courage” (5.299; see [[like-a-lion]]). Leaf compares the two passages (on 17.4). The frame is the same, and Book 17 puts a mother where Book 5 put a lion.

## In context

The book begins with Menelaus' sight of the fall: “Nor did it escape the son of Atreus, Menelaus, dear to Ares, / that Patroclus had been brought down by the Trojans in the combat” (17.1–2). He comes through the champions and takes his stand (17.3–8; see [[menelaus]] and [[patroclus]]). The first man to come against him is Euphorbus, who struck Patroclus first and claims the spoils, and whom Menelaus kills (17.9–60; see [[euphorbus]] and [[the-olive-sapling]]). Driven off by Hector, Menelaus fetches Ajax, and the second defender of the body has the second parent's likeness: Ajax stands over Patroclus like a lion over its young, while Menelaus stands on the other side, “nursing great grief in his breast” (17.132–139; see [[the-lion-and-its-cubs]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Other similes give a hero the care of a parent for its young. Achilles likens himself to a bird that brings its unfledged chicks every morsel it catches and fares badly itself (9.323–324; see [[the-bird-and-her-chicks]]); Athena turns aside the arrow aimed at Menelaus as a mother keeps a fly from her sleeping child (4.130–131; see [[mother-and-fly]]); and Teucer ducks back behind Ajax's shield like a child under its mother (8.271–272; see [[like-a-child-under-its-mother]]). Achilles will groan over the body of Patroclus like a bearded lion whose cubs a hunter has stolen (18.318–322). In the Odyssey the image is turned round: when Odysseus comes back from Circe's house to the ship, his companions crowd weeping around him as calves in the country run lowing around their mothers when the cows come home from pasture (*Odyssey* 10.407–415).
