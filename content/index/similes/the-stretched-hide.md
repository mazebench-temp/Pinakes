## The simile

{{quote:b7bd82fd-5009-5d61-a954-e8f189e1e628}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἀνὴρ ταύροιο βοὸς μεγάλοιο βοείην / λαοῖσιν δώῃ τανύειν μεθύουσαν ἀλοιφῇ, “And as when a man gives the hide of a great bull / to his people to stretch, drenched with fat” (17.389–390). βοείη is an oxhide, the stuff of shields and of the hides on which men sleep (10.155, 11.843; see [[cattle]]), and this one comes from a bull (see [[bulls]]). The λαοί, “people”, are the man's own household, his servants or retainers, a use for which Walter Leaf finds no parallel in Homer (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.390). μεθύουσαν is literally “drunk”: the hide has drunk the fat. Leaf calls it “an almost grotesquely violent metaphor”, without parallel in Greek, since μεθύω never meant anything but to be drunk (on 17.390).

δεξάμενοι δʼ ἄρα τοί γε διαστάντες τανύουσι / κυκλόσʼ, ἄφαρ δέ τε ἰκμὰς ἔβη, δύνει δέ τʼ ἀλοιφὴ / πολλῶν ἑλκόντων, τάνυται δέ τε πᾶσα διὰ πρό, “and they take it and stand apart and stretch it / in a circle, and at once the moisture goes out and the fat sinks in / as many pull, and it is stretched all the way through” (17.391–393). The men stand at intervals all round, so that the hide is stretched equally in every direction (Leaf on 17.392). ἰκμάς, “moisture”, is found only here in Homer. Leaf takes it as the natural moisture of the skin, which goes out so that the grease can enter the pores; others take it of the oil itself, spreading over the hide (on 17.392). The work is a rough way of curing leather, and Leaf reports that hides were still cured so in India in his day, pegged out or stretched and rubbed with grease (on 17.390). The verb of the work is said three times, τανύειν, τανύουσι, τάνυται, “stretch”.

The tenor: ὣς οἵ γʼ ἔνθα καὶ ἔνθα νέκυν ὀλίγῃ ἐνὶ χώρῃ / εἵλκεον ἀμφότεροι, “so they kept pulling the corpse this way and that in a little space, / both sides” (17.394–395). The point of likeness is many men pulling at one thing from every side in a narrow space, so that it is stretched taut between them. The likeness is taken from the work of a farm, and the thing stretched is a dead man.

The verb goes on into the narrative. τοῖον Ζεὺς ἐπὶ Πατρόκλῳ ἀνδρῶν τε καὶ ἵππων / ἤματι τῷ ἐτάνυσσε κακὸν πόνον, “such was the toil of men and horses that Zeus over Patroclus / stretched tight that day, an evil toil” (17.400–401). Later in the book “the fierce battle was stretched tight” over Patroclus again (17.543), and at the end “the war was stretched tight over them” as the body is carried to the ships (17.736). The hide in the simile is pulled by men; the battle over the body is pulled tight by Zeus (see [[the-rope-of-war]]).

## In context

Around the body the best men fight in a mist, while the rest of the armies fight at their ease under a clear sky (17.366–383; see [[like-blazing-fire]]). All day long the struggle goes on, and the knees, shins, feet, hands and eyes of every man are spattered with sweat and blood as the two sides fight around Patroclus (17.384–388). After the simile both sides hope to drag the body away, the Trojans to Ilios and the Achaeans to the ships, and not even Ares or Athena could have made light of the fighting (17.395–399). Achilles, far off at the ships, does not yet know that Patroclus is dead (17.401–411; see [[achilles]] and [[patroclus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same verse-end, βοὸς μεγάλοιο βοείην, “the hide of a great bull”, returns on the shield of Achilles, where two lions have torn open the hide of the bull they have seized and gulp down its entrails and black blood (18.579–583). In *Iliad* 13 Zeus and Poseidon knot the rope of war and stretch it over both armies, “unbreakable and not to be loosed, which loosened the knees of many” (13.358–360; see [[the-rope-of-war]]).
