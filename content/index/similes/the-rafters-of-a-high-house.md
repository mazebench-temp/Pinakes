## The simile

{{quote:a274cbac-95f0-502f-8f82-9936669bcf5a}}

ἀγκὰς δʼ ἀλλήλων λαβέτην χερσὶ στιβαρῇσιν / ὡς ὅτʼ ἀμείβοντες, τούς τε κλυτὸς ἤραρε τέκτων / δώματος ὑψηλοῖο βίας ἀνέμων ἀλεείνων: “and took hold of each other in their arms with strong hands, / like rafters that a famous craftsman has fitted together / of a high house, keeping out the force of the winds” (23.711–713). ἀμείβοντες, from ἀμείβω, “exchange”, are timbers that answer one another. The scholia explain them as great beams that lean against each other to carry the roof, and Walter Leaf, after the scholia, takes them for the sloping rafters of a gabled roof, meeting like the letter Λ: the two wrestlers lean together at the shoulders with their feet planted wide apart (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.712; see the translation's note on 23.712–713). He compares ἐπημοιβοί, the two bars “crossing each other” that held the high double doors of the Achaean wall (12.455–456). ἤραρε, “fitted together”, is the builder's verb, ἀραρίσκω: a craftsman who worked in horn fitted together the horns of Pandarus' bow (ἤραρε τέκτων, 4.110). The τέκτων here is κλυτός, “famous”, a master builder (see [[house]]).

The second verse is borrowed whole from the simile of the Myrmidons closing their ranks, where a man fits together the wall of a high house with close-set stones (16.212–213; see [[the-stones-of-a-wall]]). Leaf allows that it may have been interpolated from there, in which case the ἀμείβοντες would be crossbeams without any particular reference to a roof, but he thinks it more likely that the line stood from the first, a loan in a late passage (on 23.713). The wall and the rafters are parts of the same house. In Book 16 the stones are many men packed together; here the rafters are two men, each holding the other up.

That is the point of likeness. Two rafters stand because they lean on each other, and neither can fall unless the other gives. The wrestlers are as evenly matched, and the simile foretells the end: “neither could Odysseus trip him and bring him to the ground, / nor could Ajax” (23.719–720), and Achilles stops them, “victory is for both; take equal prizes” (23.736). The verses that follow give the strain of the timbers: “And their backs creaked under bold hands, / tugged hard” (23.714–715). τετρίγει, “creaked”, is the verb of the sparrow's chicks that “cheeped pitifully” as the serpent ate them (2.314) and of the ghost of Patroclus that went beneath the earth “gibbering” (23.101); Leaf takes the creaking to be the sound of the hands slipping over the skin (on 23.714). Then the wet sweat runs down, and thick welts rise along their ribs and shoulders, “crimson with blood” (23.715–717).

## In context

For the third contest Achilles sets out a great tripod to stand on the fire, which the Achaeans valued at twelve oxen, for the winner, and a woman skilled in handiwork, valued at four, for the loser (23.700–705; see [[tripod]] and [[cattle]]). Great Telamonian Ajax rises, “and up stood Odysseus of many wiles, who knew crafty ways” (23.708–709; see [[ajax]], [[odysseus]] and [[kerdos]]). They gird themselves, go into the middle of the gathering, and take hold of each other: the simile. When the struggle begins to weary the watching Achaeans, Ajax proposes that each try to lift the other. He lifts Odysseus, but Odysseus does not forget his trickery: he strikes the hollow of Ajax's knee from behind and throws him backward, and falls on his chest. Then Odysseus tries to lift Ajax, moves him a little from the ground, and hooks his knee in, and both fall side by side in the dust (23.721–732). They would have wrestled a third time, had not Achilles stood up and held them back (23.733–737; see [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The builder's work is a likeness for men who stand fast together: the stones of a wall for the Myrmidons' ranks (16.212–217), and the chalk line that makes a ship's timber straight for the battle stretched evenly at the ships (15.410–413; see [[the-chalk-line]]). In the Odyssey Menelaus remembers Odysseus as a wrestler: on Lesbos he rose to wrestle Philomeleides, who had challenged him, and threw him mightily, and all the Achaeans rejoiced (*Odyssey* 4.341–344), and among the Phaeacians Euryalus is the best of them all at the wrestling (*Odyssey* 8.126–127).
