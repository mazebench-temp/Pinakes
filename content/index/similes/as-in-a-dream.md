## The simile

{{quote:aedffb2f-758d-5340-bad4-36e287bcc598}}

ὡς δʼ ἐν ὀνείρῳ οὐ δύναται φεύγοντα διώκειν· / οὔτʼ ἄρʼ ὃ τὸν δύναται ὑποφεύγειν οὔθʼ ὃ διώκειν: “As in a dream a man cannot pursue one who flees; / the one cannot escape, nor the other pursue” (22.199–200). The subject of the first δύναται is not expressed, and the translation supplies “a man”: the dreamer, who may be either, the pursuer who cannot gain or the fugitive who cannot get away. The words go round as the dream goes round: δύναται twice and δύνατο in the tenor, φεύγοντα and ὑποφεύγειν, διώκειν twice. The tenor: ὣς ὃ τὸν οὐ δύνατο μάρψαι ποσίν, οὐδʼ ὃς ἀλύξαι, “so the one could not catch him with his feet, nor the other escape” (22.201). μάρπτω is to seize: Agenor, deciding to stand, had feared that if he ran across the plain Achilles would “dart after me and catch me with his swift feet” (μάρψῃ ταχέεσσι πόδεσσιν, 21.564).

The point of likeness is effort without result. The other similes of the chase give speed and the nearness of the kill: the falcon close behind the dove, the horses at full stretch round the turning posts, the dog that tracks the fawn until it finds it (22.139–144, 22.162–166, 22.189–193; see [[the-falcon-and-the-dove]], [[racehorses-at-the-turning-post]] and [[the-dog-and-the-fawn]]). This one stops the race where it is. Both men run as hard as they can, and neither gains.

The dream is not a god's. ὄνειρος in the Iliad is otherwise a dream with a message, the Dream that Zeus sends to Agamemnon (2.6–35; see [[dream]]), or one that a seer reads, as Eurydamas, “the old interpreter of dreams”, read none for his sons when they set out to die at Diomedes' hands (5.148–151; see [[onar]]). Here it is the dream that everyone has.

## In context

Achilles has been driving Hector on relentlessly, like a dog that rouses a fawn from its lair and tracks it through the glens until it finds it (22.188–193; see [[the-dog-and-the-fawn]]). Then the narrator turns to Hector's tactics. Again and again he makes a rush for the Dardanian gates, “in the hope that from above they might defend him with their missiles”, and each time Achilles gets in front of him and turns him back toward the plain, “and he himself flew always on the side of the city” (22.194–198; see [[dardanian-gates]] and [[the-walls-of-troy]]). The simile follows, and then the question how Hector could have escaped the fates of death so long, “had not Apollo come to him for the very last time, / close by, who roused his might and his nimble knees” (22.202–204; see [[apollo]]). Achilles shakes his head at his men and lets no one throw at Hector, “lest someone win the glory by hitting him, and he himself come second” (22.205–207; see [[kydos]]). The deadlock is broken by the gods. At the fourth coming to the springs Zeus weighs the fates and Hector's day sinks, Apollo leaves him, and Athena comes to make him stand (22.208–247; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]] and [[truth-and-deception]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The chase that cannot end has a forerunner at the close of *Iliad* 21, where Apollo, in Agenor's likeness, ran a little ahead of Achilles over the plain, bewitching him by a trick “so that he always hoped to catch him with his own feet” (21.604–605; see [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]). That was a god's deception. The dream of Book 22 is a man's.

The chase ends in a dream. That night, as he lies groaning on the shore among the Myrmidons, sleep seizes Achilles (ὕπνος ἔμαρπτε), for his limbs are very weary from rushing at Hector toward windy Ilios, and the soul of Patroclus comes to him as he sleeps; he reaches out with his hands and cannot take hold of it, and it goes beneath the earth like smoke (23.59–65, 23.99–101). In the Odyssey a dream is the likeness of what cannot be held. Three times Odysseus rushes to embrace the shade of his mother, his heart bidding him take hold of her (ἑλέειν τέ με θυμὸς ἀνώγει, the half-verse of the falcon at 22.142), and three times she flies from his hands σκιῇ εἴκελον ἢ καὶ ὀνείρῳ, like a shadow or a dream (*Odyssey* 11.204–208); for when the body has been burned, the soul flies off and flits about like a dream (*Odyssey* 11.219–222).

## Outside Homer

[[virgil|Virgil]] keeps the dream for the end of his own duel. When Turnus' great stone falls short of Aeneas, “as in dreams at night, when languid rest has weighed down our eyes, we seem to want in vain to run on eagerly, and in the midst of our efforts we sink down helpless; the tongue has no power, the body's familiar strength fails, and neither voice nor words follow: so to Turnus, wherever he sought a way by his courage, the dread goddess denied success” (*Aeneid* 12.908–914; see [[virgil-the-death-of-turnus]]). Homer's dream holds both men; Virgil gives it to the man who is about to die.
