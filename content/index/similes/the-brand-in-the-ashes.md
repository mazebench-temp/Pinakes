## The simile

{{quote:e07cebb8-ddfa-5cfc-a373-ab230edf532d}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτε τις δαλὸν σποδιῇ ἐνέκρυψε μελαίνῃ / ἀγροῦ ἐπʼ ἐσχατιῆς, ᾧ μὴ πάρα γείτονες ἄλλοι, / σπέρμα πυρὸς σώζων, ἵνα μή ποθεν ἄλλοθεν αὔοι, / ὣς Ὀδυσεὺς φύλλοισι καλύψατο: “As when a man hides a brand in the black ashes / at the far edge of the fields, where he has no other neighbors, / keeping alive the seed of fire, so that he need not kindle it from elsewhere, / so Odysseus covered himself with the leaves” (*Odyssey* 5.488–491). δαλός is a brand, a burning stick, in the Iliad the brand that would set the ships on fire (*Iliad* 13.320, 15.421; see [[fire]]). σποδιή, “ashes”, and σπέρμα πυρός, “the seed of fire”, occur only here in Homer. ἀγροῦ ἐπʼ ἐσχατιῆς, “at the far edge of the fields”, is the place of lonely dwellings in the Odyssey, where Aegisthus lived in the house of Thyestes and where Eumaeus has his steading (*Odyssey* 4.517, 24.150); in this book Odysseus felled the trees for his raft νήσου ἐπʼ ἐσχατιῆς, “to the far edge of the island” (*Odyssey* 5.238).

W. W. Merry draws out the picture: a man so remote and lonely that he has no neighbors from whom to get a light if his fire goes out keeps a brand smoldering under a heap of ashes, to fan it into a flame when he pleases; “the point of comparison is that Odysseus kept up his spark of life under a warm covering of leaves” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.488; see the translation's note on 5.488–491). For the neighbor who gives a light Merry quotes Socrates in Xenophon, who asks whether one does not wish to please one's neighbor, so that he may kindle one's fire when one needs it (*Memorabilia* 2.2.12; on 5.490). αὔοι, “kindle”, is the reading of most manuscripts; Demetrius Ixion read the subjunctive αὔῃ, which Hermann and Nauck prefer, but Merry finds that the optative well expresses a merely possible case, and La Roche writes the word with the rough breathing (Merry's critical note on 5.490).

The man of the simile is alone, at the edge of the tilled land, with no one to give him fire; so Odysseus lies alone at the edge of an unknown land, naked, with no one to give him anything. What he keeps under the leaves is the last spark of his life.

## In context

Washed ashore at the river's mouth, swollen with sea water and breathless, Odysseus has kissed the grain-giving earth and pondered where to spend the night: by the river he fears the frost and the dew, and in the woods the wild beasts (*Odyssey* 5.453–473; see [[rivers]] and [[the-river-of-scheria]]). He chooses the woods, and finds near the water two bushes grown from the same spot, one of wild olive and one of olive, so thick that neither wind nor sun nor rain comes through them (*Odyssey* 5.474–481; see [[olive]]). He creeps under them and heaps up a bed of the fallen leaves, “enough to protect two men or three / in the winter season, however hard it might rage”, and lies down in the middle of it and heaps the leaves over himself (*Odyssey* 5.482–487; see [[bed]]). After the simile Athena pours sleep on his eyes, “so that it might free him as quickly as possible / from his grievous weariness” (*Odyssey* 5.491–493; see [[athena]] and [[hypnos]]).

The book that began with Dawn rising from her bed (*Odyssey* 5.1–2) ends with Odysseus in his bed of leaves, and the man who would not sleep on the raft as he watched the stars (*Odyssey* 5.271–272) sleeps at last.
