## The simile

{{quote:d558ec84-da33-514d-a33f-f92c6febc820}}

ὅσσον δὲ τροχοῦ ἵππος ἀφίσταται, ὅς ῥα ἄνακτα / ἕλκῃσιν πεδίοιο τιταινόμενος σὺν ὄχεσφι: “As far as a horse is from the wheel, a horse that draws its master / over the plain at full stretch with the chariot” (23.517–518). The horse is one of the team, and the wheel is that of its own chariot, with the driver standing in the car. Walter Leaf notes that on the monuments the front of the wheel of the Greek chariot is in line with the front of the car or a little ahead of it; Helbig concluded from this passage that Homer knew chariots drawn by a single horse, but Leaf thinks the words do not require it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.517). τιταινόμενος, “at full stretch”, is the racehorse to which Achilles was likened as he ran back toward Troy, “that runs easily at full stretch over the plain” (22.23; see [[like-a-racehorse]]).

τοῦ μέν τε ψαύουσιν ἐπισσώτρου τρίχες ἄκραι / οὐραῖαι· ὃ δέ τʼ ἄγχι μάλα τρέχει, οὐδέ τι πολλὴ / χώρη μεσσηγὺς πολέος πεδίοιο θέοντος: “and the tips of its hairs touch the tire, / the hairs of its tail; and the wheel runs very close, and there is no great / space between, as it runs over the wide plain” (23.519–521). The ἐπίσσωτρον is the metal tire on the rim of the wheel: Hera's chariot has “tires of bronze, a wonder to see” fitted over its golden rims (5.724–725), and in battle the drops of blood thrown up “from the horses' hooves / and from the tires” spatter the rails of Achilles' chariot (20.501–502). οὐραῖαι, “of the tail”, occurs only here in Homer. The verses pile up words of nearness, ἄγχι μάλα, “very close”, and οὐδέ τι πολλὴ χώρη μεσσηγύς, “no great space between”, and Leaf notes that πολέος, “wide”, adds the idea of an open course on which the greatest speed can be reached, as in the fawns worn out “from running over a wide plain” (4.244; on 23.521).

The tenor: τόσσον δὴ Μενέλαος ἀμύμονος Ἀντιλόχοιο / λείπετʼ, “so far was Menelaus behind noble Antilochus” (23.522–523). The distance is a hair's breadth, the tips of a tail on the rim of a wheel. The race supplies its own measure: the two teams are as close as the horse and the wheel of one chariot.

## In context

Diomedes has come in first, and Sthenelus has taken the woman and the tripod (23.499–513). “And after him Antilochus, of the line of Neleus, drove in his horses, / by crafty ways, not by speed, having got in ahead of Menelaus; / but even so Menelaus kept his swift horses close” (23.514–516; see [[kerdos]]). The simile measures how close, and the narrator goes on: “At first, it is true, he was left behind as far as the range of a discus, / but he quickly caught up with him; for the noble might was growing / of Agamemnon's mare, fine-maned Aethe” (23.523–525). If the race had been longer, Menelaus would have passed him (23.526–527). In the narrow place Antilochus had gained the range of a discus by driving off the road at Menelaus' side (23.417–437; see [[the-cast-of-a-spear]]); by the finish the gap is a tail's length. Meriones comes in a spear's cast behind Menelaus, and Eumelus last of all, dragging his chariot, whose yoke Athena had broken (23.528–533, 23.392). The quarrel over the second prize follows (23.534–613; see [[antilochus]], [[menelaus]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Iliad takes a few of its likenesses from the chariot and its parts. Eumelus' mares are matched so well that their backs are “as level as a builder's line” (2.765; see [[swift-as-birds]]); a potter tries a wheel that fits his hands to see whether it will run, as the dancers run on the Shield (18.599–601; see [[the-potters-wheel]]); and Ajax leaps from deck to deck like a rider who leaps from horse to horse of a team of four (15.679–684; see [[the-rider-of-four-horses]]). The measures of this race are taken from the games themselves: the range of a discus and the cast of a spear (23.431–433, 23.523, 23.529).
