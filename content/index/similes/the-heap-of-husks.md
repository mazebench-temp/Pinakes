## The simile

{{quote:e54acf1e-b37a-5693-a7e2-af84e01616ea}}

ὡς δʼ ἄνεμος ζαὴς ἠΐων θημῶνα τινάξῃ / καρφαλέων· τὰ μὲν ἄρ τε διεσκέδασʼ ἄλλυδις ἄλλῃ: “And as a blustering wind shakes a heap of husks, / dry ones, and scatters them this way and that” (*Odyssey* 5.368–369). ζαής, “blustering” (see [[blustering]]), is the wind that drives the shadowy clouds in the Iliad's simile of the stones that fall like snowflakes (*Iliad* 12.156–158; see [[a-snow-of-stones]]), and the wind that Zeus rouses on the night Odysseus' ship comes to the island of the Sun (*Odyssey* 12.312–315, a plain rendering). θημών, “heap”, occurs only here in Homer. καρφαλέος, “dry”, is said once more, in the Iliad, of the dry ring of a shield grazed by a spear (*Iliad* 13.409). W. W. Merry compares ἤια at *Odyssey* 2.289, the word of the provisions for Telemachus' voyage (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.368; see [[provisions]]), and explains the heap: such heaps are left after winnowing, as the heaps of chaff that grow white on the threshing floor in a simile of the Iliad (*Iliad* 5.499–502; see [[winnowing]] and the translation's note on 5.368–369).

The tenor takes up the verb: ὣς τῆς δούρατα μακρὰ διεσκέδασʼ, “so it scattered the long beams of the raft” (*Odyssey* 5.370). The aorist διεσκέδασε is said of the wind in the simile and of the wave in the story. The beams are the long beams that Calypso told Odysseus to cut and fit together (*Odyssey* 5.162–163; see [[raft]]), and he felled twenty trees for them, hewed them and smoothed them “to the chalk line” (*Odyssey* 5.243–245). The point of likeness is the work of four days undone in a moment, as lightly as the wind undoes the winnowers' heap. Odysseus himself will tell Arete in the same verb that the storm scattered his raft (*Odyssey* 7.275, a plain rendering).

## In context

The simile is the last of the storm, and it fulfills Odysseus' own words. Ino has told him to leave the raft and swim (*Odyssey* 5.343–350; see [[like-a-diving-bird]]), and he has decided to stay on it: “as long as the beams hold together in their fastenings, / so long I will stay here and endure, suffering sorrows; / but when the wave shakes the raft apart for me, / I will swim” (*Odyssey* 5.361–364). While he is still pondering, Poseidon rouses a great wave, “dread and grievous, arching over him”, and it strikes him (*Odyssey* 5.365–367; see [[poseidon]]). τινάξῃ, “shakes”, the verb of the wind in the simile, is the verb of his fear, διὰ κῦμα τινάξῃ, “when the wave shakes the raft apart” (*Odyssey* 5.363). Then he rides a single beam and strips off Calypso's garments (*Odyssey* 5.371–372; see [[like-a-rider]]), stretches the veil under his chest and plunges into the sea, and Poseidon, seeing him, drives off to Aegae (*Odyssey* 5.373–381; see [[aegae]]).

The earlier simile of the storm likened the raft, still whole, to thistles that the North Wind carries over a plain, clinging together (*Odyssey* 5.328–332; see [[thistles-in-the-north-wind]]). Both likenesses are taken from dry stuff that the wind carries off the land; in the first the stalks hold together, in the second the heap flies apart.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The chaff of the threshing floor is a battle simile in the Iliad: the dust whitens the Achaeans as the wind carries the chaff across the sacred threshing floors (*Iliad* 5.499–505; see [[winnowing]]), and an arrow bounces off Menelaus' corselet as beans leap from a winnowing shovel (*Iliad* 13.588–592; see [[beans-on-the-threshing-floor]]).
