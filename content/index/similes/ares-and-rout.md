## The simile

{{quote:b29fb21f-20b8-5864-9759-45f51356f99e}}

οἷος δὲ βροτολοιγὸς Ἄρης πόλεμον δὲ μέτεισι, / τῷ δὲ Φόβος φίλος υἱὸς ἅμα κρατερὸς καὶ ἀταρβὴς / ἕσπετο, ὅς τʼ ἐφόβησε ταλάφρονά περ πολεμιστήν: “And as Ares, ruin of mortals, goes into war, and Rout, his dear son, strong and fearless, follows with him, who routs even a warrior of enduring heart” (13.298–300). Φόβος is the flight that panic brings, and the son is named for what he does: ἐφόβησε, “routs”, is his name made a verb (see [[rout]] and [[ruin-of-mortals]]). In the Iliad Rout goes with Ares and Terror (Δεῖμος) in the first battle (4.440), surrounds the Gorgon on Agamemnon's shield (11.37), is “set as a crown” all around the aegis (5.739), and yokes Ares' horses with Terror in Book 15 (15.119–120). Only here is he called Ares' son.

τὼ μὲν ἄρʼ ἐκ Θρῄκης Ἐφύρους μέτα θωρήσσεσθον, / ἠὲ μετὰ Φλεγύας μεγαλήτορας: “these two arm themselves to go out from Thrace to the Ephyri or to the great-hearted Phlegyes” (13.301–302). θωρήσσεσθον is a dual. Thrace is Ares' country: freed from Hephaestus' net, he goes off to Thrace (*Odyssey* 8.360–361; see [[thrace]]). Who the Ephyri and the Phlegyes were the poem does not say (see [[ephyri]], [[phlegyes]] and the translation's note on 13.298–303). The gods take a side: οὐδʼ ἄρα τώ γε / ἔκλυον ἀμφοτέρων, ἑτέροισι δὲ κῦδος ἔδωκαν, “and they do not listen to both sides, but give glory to one or the other” (13.302–303; see [[kydos]]).

The tenor is brief: τοῖοι Μηριόνης τε καὶ Ἰδομενεὺς ἀγοὶ ἀνδρῶν / ἤϊσαν ἐς πόλεμον κεκορυθμένοι αἴθοπι χαλκῷ, “like them, Meriones and Idomeneus, leaders of men, went into the war, armored in gleaming bronze” (13.304–305). The point of likeness is a pair of fighters who go out together and bring rout and victory with them. Father and son, an older and a younger, fit the pair: Idomeneus is “half gray” (13.361), and Meriones is his attendant (θεράπων, 13.246, 13.331), called in the same passage “the equal of swift Ares” (13.295, 13.328; see [[equal-of-ares]]). The gods who do not heed both sides look forward to the next scene. There the two sons of Cronus are divided, and each gives his help to one army (13.345–360; see [[the-rope-of-war]]).

## In context

Idomeneus has gone to his hut to arm, and come out “like the lightning” of Zeus (13.240–245; see [[like-lightning]]). Meriones, coming for a spear, meets him, and they speak of courage and of the ambush, where the brave man and the coward are shown for what they are (13.246–294; see [[ambush]]). Meriones takes a bronze spear from the hut and goes after Idomeneus, “much intent on war” (13.295–297), and the simile follows. Then Meriones asks where they should enter the throng, and Idomeneus chooses the left (13.306–329; see [[idomeneus]] and [[meriones]]). The Trojans see Idomeneus “like a flame in his courage” and all come at him (13.330–332; see [[like-a-flame]]), and the battle closes like a storm of dust (13.334–338; see [[the-dust-storm]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Warriors go to battle in the likeness of Ares. Ajax, armed for the duel, goes forward “as huge Ares goes forth when he goes to war among men” (7.208–209; see [[like-huge-ares]]), Agamemnon is like Ares in the waist (2.478–479; see [[like-zeus-ares-and-poseidon]]), and Hector rages like spear-wielding Ares, or like a deadly fire in the mountains (15.605–606). At the end of this book Hector leads the Trojans “the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals” (13.802).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes Rout and Terror the sons of Ares and Aphrodite: “terrible, who rout the close battalions of men in chilling war, with Ares, sacker of cities” (*Theogony* 933–936). On the Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles* Ares stands in his chariot with Terror and Rout beside him, eager to plunge into the war of men (*Shield* 191–196; see [[hesiod]]).

[[Strabo]] found the end of the simile a puzzle. The people of Gyrton in Thessaly were once called Phlegyae, after Phlegyas, the brother of Ixion, and the people of Crannon Ephyri, so that it is hard to say whom the poet means when he speaks of the Ephyri and the Phlegyes. He quotes the verses with θωρήσσοντο, a plural, for the dual (Strabo 9.5.21). The fragments of his seventh book report the same identification, with Crannon about a hundred stades from Gyrton (Strabo 7, fragments 14 and 16; see [[gyrtone]]).
