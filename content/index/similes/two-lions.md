## The simile

{{quote:92830825-5dee-55c8-b25f-69ee5e72a61a}}

οἵω τώ γε λέοντε δύω ὄρεος κορυφῇσιν / ἐτραφέτην ὑπὸ μητρὶ βαθείης τάρφεσιν ὕλης: “like two lions on the mountain peaks that have been reared by their mother in the thickets of the deep forest”. τάρφεα are thickets. The lions go down to the farms: τὼ μὲν ἄρʼ ἁρπάζοντε βόας καὶ ἴφια μῆλα / σταθμοὺς ἀνθρώπων κεραΐζετον, “these two seize cattle and fat sheep and ravage the farmsteads of men” (σταθμοί, the steadings and pens; κεραΐζω, “plunder, lay waste”). And they die: ὄφρα καὶ αὐτὼ / ἀνδρῶν ἐν παλάμῃσι κατέκταθεν ὀξέϊ χαλκῷ, “until they themselves too are killed at the hands of men with the sharp bronze” (5.557–558).

The simile is in the dual from beginning to end: λέοντε, ἐτραφέτην, ἁρπάζοντε, κεραΐζετον, αὐτώ. The tenor continues it, τοίω τὼ χείρεσσιν ὑπʼ Αἰνείαο δαμέντε / καππεσέτην, “such were these two, brought down at the hands of Aeneas, and they fell” (5.559–560). Greek has a grammatical number for pairs, and here it follows the twins (διδυμάονε, 5.548) from their birth to their fall.

The simile tells a life. The lions are born and reared together, grow strong, go out to raid, and are killed together. So the twins “grown to manhood” (ἡβήσαντε, 5.550) sailed together to Troy and died there together. The lions take what belongs to other men, and the twins had come to Troy “seeking satisfaction for the sons of Atreus” (5.552–553; see [[war-for-menelaus]]). The simile gives the young men the strength of predators, not the helplessness of prey, and it gives them a mother. Their genealogy has named only fathers, back to the river Alpheus (5.544–549). The end is the same for lions and men: the sharp bronze. With no pause the image changes, and they fall “like tall fir trees” (5.560; see [[fir-trees]]).

## In context

Aeneas, healed and sent back by Apollo, kills “two of the best men of the Danaans”, the twin sons of Diocles of Pherae (5.541–549; see [[crethon]], [[orsilochus-son-of-diocles]], [[diocles]], [[ortilochus]], [[alpheus]] and [[pherae-in-messenia]]). The obituary gives their line in four generations, from the river to the twins, and then their death: “and there the end of death covered them both” (5.553).

The pair of lions is answered by another pair. Menelaus pities them as they fall and goes through the champions against Aeneas; Antilochus, afraid for him, comes to stand at his side (5.561–570). Aeneas, “swift warrior though he was”, does not stand his ground “when he saw two men standing side by side” (5.571–572). The two Achaeans are also told in the dual, as they drag the twins' bodies back to their companions and turn to fight again (5.573–575). See [[menelaus]] and [[antilochus]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

Pairs of lions recur. Diomedes and Odysseus set out on the night raid “like two lions through the black night” (10.297). The two Ajaxes carry off the body of Imbrius like two lions carrying a goat they have snatched from the dogs (13.198–202). Hector and Patroclus fight over the body of Cebriones like two lions, both hungry, over a slain deer “on the mountain peaks” (ὄρεος κορυφῇσι, 16.756–758). On the shield of Achilles two lions seize a bull at the head of the herd (18.579–586).

The raider's death is told again of Patroclus, who leaps on Cebriones “with the rush of a lion that, ravaging the farmsteads (σταθμοὺς κεραΐζων), is struck in the chest, and its own courage destroys it” (16.752–753). The lion's young appear in two similes of the fight for Patroclus: Ajax stands over the body like a lion over its cubs (17.133–136), and Achilles groans over it like a bearded lion whose cubs a hunter has stolen (18.318–322).
