## The simile

αὐτὸς δʼ ἐν πρώτοισι μέγα φρονέων ἐβεβήκει, / ἐν δʼ ἔπεσʼ ὑσμίνῃ ὑπεραέϊ ἶσος ἀέλλῃ, / ἥ τε καθαλλομένη ἰοειδέα πόντον ὀρίνει: “and he himself strode among the foremost in high spirits, and fell on the battle like a blustering storm wind that swoops down and stirs up the violet sea” (11.296–298). ἄελλα is a storm wind or gust, and ὑπεραής, “blowing hard, blustering”, occurs only here. καθαλλομένη, “leaping down”, gives the squall's direction, down from the sky onto the water. ἰοειδής, “violet”, is the color of the sea here and twice in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 5.56, 11.107), and nowhere else in the Iliad. ὀρίνω is “stir up, rouse”, the verb of a troubled sea and of troubled hearts.

The point of likeness is a sudden, violent onset from above that throws everything below into turmoil. Hector does not fight a man at once. He “falls on the battle” as a whole, as the squall falls on the whole sea.

## In context

The storm comes straight after the hunter and his hounds (11.292–295; see [[the-hunter-and-his-hounds]]). The first simile shows Hector urging on others, the second Hector himself. Then come his nine victims, named in a single question and answer, “whom first, whom last did he slay?” (11.299–303; see [[whom-first-whom-last]]), and a second wind simile for the multitude he kills after them (11.305–309; see [[the-west-wind-and-the-clouds]]). In the morning of the same day Hector moved among the Trojans like a star that hides in the clouds (11.62–65; see [[the-baleful-star]]); now Zeus has given him the day, and he comes out as weather.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The half-verse returns at the start of the fight at the wall: Hector “fought as before, like a storm wind” (ἶσος ἀέλλῃ, 12.40). In Book 13 the Trojans come on behind him like a blast of grievous winds that falls on the plain under Zeus' thunder and mingles with the sea, heaping up wave on wave (13.795–800). Earlier similes set the winds on the sea to picture armies, the West Wind driving breakers onto the shore and two winds from Thrace stirring the fish-filled sea (4.422–428, 9.4–8; see [[waves-on-the-shore]] and [[two-winds-from-thrace]]). Here a storm stands for one man.
