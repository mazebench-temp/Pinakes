## The simile

{{quote:38d88024-8ca7-5f64-b736-9ccfed38d42f}}

οὔτε νόημα / γναμπτὸν ἐνὶ στήθεσσι, λέων δʼ ὣς ἄγρια οἶδεν, / ὅς τʼ ἐπεὶ ἂρ μεγάλῃ τε βίῃ καὶ ἀγήνορι θυμῷ / εἴξας εἶσʼ ἐπὶ μῆλα βροτῶν ἵνα δαῖτα λάβῃσιν: “nor any thought / in his breast that can be bent; he knows savage ways, like a lion / that gives way to his great strength and proud spirit / and goes among the flocks of men to take his feast” (24.40–43). γναμπτός, “bent”, is the word of what Achilles' friends asked of him in Book 9. Phoenix told him to give the Prayers the honor “that bends the minds of other men, brave as they are” (9.514), and that “even the gods themselves can be turned” (9.497); Walter Leaf compares also Iris' warning to Poseidon, “The hearts of good men can be turned” (15.203; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.41). ἄγρια οἶδεν, “knows savage things”, is the idiom by which Homer gives a man the character of what he knows, as Agamemnon might have “known kind things” toward Achilles (ἤπια εἰδείη, 16.73). ἄγριος is said of Achilles by others too: Ajax says that he “has made savage the proud heart in his breast” (9.629), and the river Scamander calls on his brother to help him “stop this savage man” (21.314).

The simile names no hunger, though the lions of the battle similes are often hungry (3.25, 12.299–300, 18.162). This lion goes for the flocks because it gives way, εἴξας, to its own great strength and its ἀγήνωρ θυμός, “proud spirit” (see [[proud]]). The same spirit bids the mountain lion of Sarpedon's simile “make an attempt on the sheep, and go even into the close-built fold” (12.300–301; see [[the-mountain-lion-and-the-fold]]), and in the Odyssey hunger bids the lion to which Odysseus is likened, in the same words (*Odyssey* 6.133–134). εἴξας with a dative is to yield to an impulse: Meleager went out to fight “yielding to his own heart” (9.598). The flocks are μῆλα βροτῶν, the sheep and goats of men (see [[sheep]]); Leaf finds βροτῶν strange, but possible in a god's mouth (on 24.42). δαίς, the word for the lion's “feast”, is everywhere else in Homer the meal of men or gods, and a few verses later Zeus uses it of Hector's offerings: “my altar never lacked an equal share of the feast” (24.69).

The tenor: ὣς Ἀχιλεὺς ἔλεον μὲν ἀπώλεσεν, οὐδέ οἱ αἰδὼς / γίγνεται, “so Achilles has lost all pity, and he has no shame” (24.44–45). The point of likeness is a nature that obeys nothing but its own force. The lion gives way to its strength, and Achilles has lost the two things that hold a man back, pity and shame (see [[pity]] and [[aidos]]). Apollo goes on with what other men do: a man may lose someone dearer still, a brother or a son, “yet when he has wept and mourned, he leaves off; / for the Fates have put an enduring heart in men” (24.48–49). Achilles does not leave off; he drags Hector around the mound of his companion, and in his rage he defiles “the senseless earth” (24.50–54; see [[the-senseless-earth]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector]]).

The verses are hard. Leaf finds the construction of 42–43 unnatural, two relatives, ὅς and ἐπεί, with a single main verb between them, so that ἄγρια οἶδεν must be supplied from the verse before; he thinks 42–45 probably all interpolated, 42–43 an imitation of the lion simile of Book 17, where the verb for ὅς comes after all (17.657–664; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]). The last verse, “which greatly harms men and helps them” (24.45), was athetized in antiquity as taken from Hesiod, where it is said of the shame that does not serve a man in need (*Works and Days* 317–318); Leaf agrees that it makes no sense here, since the shame that harms men can be no reproach to Achilles (on 24.45). If Aristarchus held, as Athenaeus says, that Homer uses δαίς only of men (see [[zenodotus-feast-for-birds]]), Leaf infers that he must have obelized the couplet of the lion's feast, though the scholia give no hint of it (on 24.42). The translation renders the verses as they stand (see the translation's note on 24.42–45).

## In context

Achilles has dragged Hector around the mound of Patroclus at every dawn, and Apollo has kept all defilement from the body (24.14–21). The gods pity Hector and urge the slayer of Argus to steal him away; Hera, Poseidon and Athena refuse (24.22–30). On the twelfth day Apollo speaks. He calls the gods “hard” and “destructive”, σχέτλιοι and δηλήμονες (24.33), the word with which Ajax called Achilles a “hard man” in Book 9 (σχέτλιος, 9.630), reminds them of Hector's sacrifices (24.33–34), and says that they choose to help “deadly Achilles” (24.39). The simile is the center of his speech. Hera answers in anger that Hector was a mortal, suckled at a woman's breast, and Achilles the son of a goddess whom she herself reared (24.55–63), and Zeus settles the quarrel: Thetis is to be called, and Achilles is to take gifts from Priam and give Hector back (24.64–76; see [[apollo]], [[hera]] and [[zeus]]).

Hecuba says the same later in the book, and the end of the book belies it. She calls Achilles ὠμηστής, “that eater of raw flesh”, who “will not pity you / or respect you at all” (24.207–208; see [[eating-raw-flesh]]), and wishes that she could eat his liver (24.212–213), as Achilles himself wished to carve Hector's flesh and eat it raw (22.346–347). But when Priam has begged him to respect the gods and pity him (24.503), Achilles raises the old man by the hand, “pitying his gray head and his gray beard” (24.515–516). Once more he is likened to a lion, when he springs out of the door of his hut to take the ransom (24.572; see [[like-a-lion]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The lion is Achilles' beast in the last books. He groans over Patroclus like a bearded lion whose cubs a hunter has stolen (18.318–322; see [[the-lion-and-its-cubs]]), rushes against Aeneas like a wounded lion that a whole people has gathered to kill (20.164–175; see [[wounded-lion]]), and tells Hector that there are no trusty oaths between lions and men (22.262; see [[no-oaths-between-lions-and-men]]). Here the likeness is given by a god, among the gods, and it is a likeness not of a moment in battle or in grief but of his nature. See [[lion]].
