## The simile

{{quote:6c37c7b0-a604-5a86-8648-6ec6bd0cd7e8}}

ὡς δὲ δράκων ἐπὶ χειῇ ὀρέστερος ἄνδρα μένῃσι / βεβρωκὼς κακὰ φάρμακʼ, ἔδυ δέ τέ μιν χόλος αἰνός: “As a serpent of the mountains waits for a man at its hole, / having fed on evil drugs, and dread anger has come into it” (22.93–94). δράκων is Homer's great snake, named, it seems, from δέρκομαι, “look, glare” (see [[serpent]]), and the simile gives it its glare: σμερδαλέον δὲ δέδορκεν, “and it glares terribly” (22.95). σμερδαλέος, “terrible”, was the serpent of the portent at Aulis, which Zeus sent from under the altar (2.308–309). χειή, the snake's hole, occurs only in this simile, twice (22.93, 22.95); Walter Leaf, on the cleft (χηραμός) into which the dove flies from the hawk, notes that χειή is used in the same sense (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.495; see [[the-dove-and-the-hawk]]). ὀρέστερος, “of the mountains”, is found once more in Homer, of the mountain wolves on Circe's island (*Odyssey* 10.212).

The snake's poison is something it has eaten. βεβρωκώς, “having fed”, is said once more in Homer, of the lion that has eaten an ox, to which Odysseus among the dead suitors is likened (*Odyssey* 22.402–403), and κακὰ φάρμακα, “evil drugs”, are what Circe gave those wolves and lions to bewitch them (*Odyssey* 10.213). Anger enters the snake as it enters a man: ἔδυ … χόλος, “anger entered”, is said of Meleager and of Achilles when he saw the new armor (9.553, 19.16; see [[cholos]]). ἑλισσόμενος περὶ χειῇ, “as it coils about its hole” (22.95), completes the picture: the snake has its den and will not leave it.

The tenor: ὣς Ἕκτωρ ἄσβεστον ἔχων μένος οὐχ ὑπεχώρει, / πύργῳ ἔπι προὔχοντι φαεινὴν ἀσπίδʼ ἐρείσας, “so Hector, with unquenchable might, did not give ground, / leaning his shining shield against a jutting tower” (22.96–97). ἄσβεστος, “unquenchable”, is a word for flame, for the shouting of battle and for the laughter of the gods (16.123, 11.50, 1.599); only here is it said of a man's might (see [[menos]]). The snake coils about its hole, and Hector leans on the wall of his city. The point of likeness is the waiting and the menace: the creature does not attack, but it holds its place, full of poison and anger, and glares at the man who comes.

## In context

Priam and Hecuba have begged Hector from the wall to come inside, the father tearing out his gray hair, the mother holding out the breast that nursed him (22.33–89; see [[priam]] and [[hecuba]]), “but they did not persuade Hector's heart, / but he waited for huge Achilles as he came nearer” (22.91–92). The simile gives the waiting its menace. Then Hector speaks to his own heart (22.98–130; see [[pondering-two-ways]] and [[hector]]). He cannot go in, for Polydamas would be the first to heap disgrace on him; he thinks of laying down his shield and helmet, going to Achilles unarmed, and offering him Helen and half of all the city holds; and he knows that Achilles would kill him naked, “just like a woman” (22.125; see [[like-a-boy-or-a-woman]]). Better to fight. But the serpent's resolve does not last. When Achilles comes near, his bronze shining like fire or the rising sun, “trembling seized him”, and he runs (22.131–137; see [[like-the-sun]] and [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The simile answers the serpent of *Iliad* 3, where Alexander, seeing Menelaus come out among the champions, shrinks back into the Trojan ranks: “As when a man sees a serpent and starts back / in the glades of a mountain, and trembling seizes his limbs” (3.33–34; see [[man-who-meets-a-serpent]]). There the Trojan was the man, and he recoiled. Here the Trojan is the serpent, and he waits. But in the end Hector does what the man of Book 3 did. “And when Hector saw him” (ὡς ἐνόησεν), the narrator says, “trembling seized him” (ἕλε τρόμος, 22.136), as Alexander drew back “when … caught sight of him” (ὡς οὖν ἐνόησεν, 3.30) and trembling seized the limbs of the man in the glades (3.34). The serpents of the portents are monsters: the blood-red snake of Aulis that ate the sparrow and her nestlings (2.308–319; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]), and the one the eagle dropped among the Trojans at the ditch, still alive and fighting (12.200–209; see [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]]).

## Outside Homer

[[virgil|Virgil]] gives the snake to Pyrrhus, the son of Achilles, at the door of Priam's palace on the night Troy falls. He exults in his weapons and the gleam of his bronze, “like a snake come out into the light, fed on evil herbs” (*qualis ubi in lucem coluber mala gramina pastus*), which cold winter had kept swollen under the earth; now, its old skin cast off, new and shining with youth, it rolls its slippery back, rears its breast high toward the sun, and flickers its three-forked tongue (*Aeneid* 2.469–475). Virgil's snake, fed on evil herbs, is Homer's, fed on evil drugs; but it comes out of its hole to attack, and the man it stands for is Achilles' son.
