## The simile

{{quote:43d4ff5d-4677-5512-89d7-ce8cda2b0f93}}

ἀλλʼ ὥς τε στάθμη δόρυ νήϊον ἐξιθύνει / τέκτονος ἐν παλάμῃσι δαήμονος, ὅς ῥά τε πάσης / εὖ εἰδῇ σοφίης ὑποθημοσύνῃσιν Ἀθήνης: “But as a chalk line makes a ship's timber straight in the hands of a skilled carpenter, who knows well all his craft by the promptings of Athena” (15.410–412).

στάθμη, found only here in the Iliad, is the carpenter's line, usually taken as a cord rubbed with chalk or ochre and snapped against the wood to mark a straight cut (see the translation's note on 15.410–412). In the Odyssey the carpenter's work is finished ἐπὶ στάθμην, “to the chalk line”: Odysseus makes the timbers of his raft straight by it (*Odyssey* 5.245; see [[the-building-of-the-raft]]), and so are made the cypress doorpost of his hall, the oak threshold of his storeroom and the post of his bed (*Odyssey* 17.341, 21.44, 23.197). δόρυ is here a beam of timber, its first sense, and νήϊον “for a ship”: Paris tells Hector that his heart is like the axe of a man who shapes a ship's timber with skill (3.60–63; see [[heart-like-an-axe]]), and builders fell oaks and pines in the mountains “to be ship-timber” (13.390–391; see [[the-felled-tree]]). ἐξιθύνω, “make straight”, is found only here. The τέκτων is a builder or carpenter, and he is δαήμων, “skilled”. σοφίη, his craft, is found only here in Homer, and it means skill in a trade, not wisdom. He has learned it ὑποθημοσύνῃσιν Ἀθήνης, “by the promptings of Athena”, words that the Odyssey uses of Odysseus' own coming to the swineherd's hut (*Odyssey* 16.233).

The tenor: ὣς μὲν τῶν ἐπὶ ἶσα μάχη τέτατο πτόλεμός τε, “so evenly was their battle and war stretched tight” (15.413). The verse is the one that ends the simile of the working woman's scales at the wall in *Iliad* 12 (12.436; see [[the-working-womans-scales]]). There the balance of an honest working woman, here the line of a skilled carpenter: both times the even battle is pictured by a craftsman's instrument of exactness. τέτατο, “was stretched”, is the verb of the rope of war that Zeus and Poseidon pull tight between the armies (13.358–360; see [[the-rope-of-war]]), and the chalk line, if it is the cord it is usually taken to be, is stretched taut before it is snapped.

The point of likeness is straightness under tension. The line does not sag to either side; the battle, though the Trojans are at the ships, bends neither way. The Achaeans cannot push the Trojans back from the ships, and the Trojans cannot break the battalions of the Danaans and force their way among the huts (15.405–409). The vehicle comes from the work that built the ships being fought over. The timbers of a ship were made straight by such a line, and the battle now runs along them.

## In context

Patroclus has left Eurypylus' hut to run to Achilles (15.390–405; see [[patroclus]]). After the simile the battle breaks up into fights around different ships, and Hector goes straight at Ajax. “The two were toiling over one ship, and could not, the one drive the other off and burn the ship with fire, nor the other thrust him back, since a god had brought him there” (15.414–418; see [[hector]] and [[ajax]]). The even battle of the simile becomes a duel over a single ship. Ajax kills Caletor as he brings fire to it, and Hector's spear kills Lycophron, Ajax's attendant (15.419–435; see [[caletor]] and [[lycophron]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Athena is the patron of craftsmen. The Trojan Phereclus, who built for Alexander the balanced ships that began the evil, “knew how to make with his hands every kind of intricate work; for Pallas Athena loved him above all others” (5.59–63; see [[phereclus]]). In the Odyssey the goldsmith who lays gold over silver is a man whom Hephaestus and Pallas Athena have taught every kind of craft (*Odyssey* 6.232–234 = 23.159–161), and Epeius made the wooden horse “with Athena” (*Odyssey* 8.493; see [[epeius]]). A builder's line of another kind, the σταφύλη, a plumb line or level, measures the backs of Eumelus' mares, “as level as a builder's line” (2.765; see [[swift-as-birds]]).
