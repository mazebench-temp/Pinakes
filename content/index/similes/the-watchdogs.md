## The simile

{{quote:014b4795-e0fe-5b19-afde-91b73482c078}}

ὡς δὲ κύνες περὶ μῆλα δυσωρήσωνται ἐν αὐλῇ: “as dogs keep a troubled watch around the sheep in a fold”. δυσωρέομαι occurs only here. It joins δυσ-, “hard, ill”, to the root of ὤρα, “care, heed”, which is also in θυραωροί, the dogs that “guard the door” of Priam's house (22.69). See the translation's note on 10.183. The αὐλή is the walled yard of a farmstead, where the flock is penned at night, the fold over whose wall the wounded lion of Book 5 leaps (5.138; see [[wounded-lion]]).

The dogs do not see the danger; they hear it. θηρὸς ἀκούσαντες κρατερόφρονος, “when they hear a fierce-hearted beast”, coming down through the woods among the mountains. The beast is not named. Then the men are awake too: πολὺς δʼ ὀρυμαγδὸς ἐπʼ αὐτῷ / ἀνδρῶν ἠδὲ κυνῶν, “a great din rises against it of men and dogs”. ὀρυμαγδός is the din of battle, the word of the formula for armies meeting (πολὺς δʼ ὀρυμαγδὸς ὀρώρει, 4.449, 8.63), and the farm makes a battle noise against the beast. The vehicle ends on sleep, ἀπό τέ σφισιν ὕπνος ὄλωλεν, “and sleep is lost to them”, and the tenor takes up the same verb: ὣς τῶν νήδυμος ὕπνος ἀπὸ βλεφάροιιν ὀλώλει, “so sweet sleep was lost from their eyelids”.

The sentries, like the dogs, listen. They keep watch “through the evil night”, always turned toward the plain, ὁππότʼ ἐπὶ Τρώων ἀΐοιεν ἰόντων, “to hear if the Trojans were coming on” (10.188–189). τετράφαθʼ, “they were turned”, is a pluperfect of τρέπω with the old ending -ατο, found only here. The dogs hear the beast, the sentries wait to hear the Trojans: the army is the flock, and the enemy on the plain is the beast in the woods.

## In context

Agamemnon feared that the sentries, “worn out by weariness and by sleep”, had lain down and forgotten their watch, with the enemy camped close by (10.97–101). When Nestor and the kings come among them, they find the leaders of the sentries not asleep, “but they were all sitting awake with their arms” (10.180–182). The old man is glad and cheers them: “Keep watch like this now, dear children, and let no one be taken / by sleep, so that we do not become a joy to our enemies” (10.192–193). The watch is the one Nestor had posted earlier that night, seven captains with a hundred young men each, between the ditch and the wall, among them his son Thrasymedes and Meriones (9.80–88; see [[night-watch]], [[thrasymedes]] and [[meriones]]).

The simile is about sleep, in a book that turns on sleep (see [[hypnos]]). The chiefs sleep and Agamemnon cannot (10.1–4); the sentries stay awake, and that is their virtue. The Trojans keep no such watch. Dolon tells his captors that there is “no picked guard”, only the men at their own fires, while the allies sleep and leave the watching to the Trojans (10.416–422), and the Thracians, the farthest out of all, are killed in their sleep (10.471–497). Diomedes falls on them as a lion falls on flocks that have no herdsman (10.485–486; see [[lion-among-the-flocks]]), the danger that the dogs of this simile keep off.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The guarded farm is the setting of many lion similes. Dogs and countrymen keep watch all night (πάννυχοι ἐγρήσσοντες) to keep a lion from the fat of the cattle, and it goes away at dawn without its prey (11.548–555, and again at 17.657–664); a hungry mountain lion goes for the sheep even when it finds herdsmen guarding the flock with dogs and spears (12.299–306). There the beast is the hero, and the watchers lose or win. Here the watchers are the heroes, and nothing comes. In the Odyssey four dogs “like wild beasts” sleep beside Eumaeus' pigsties and rush barking at the stranger (*Odyssey* 14.21–22, 14.29–32). See [[dogs]], [[sheep]] and [[lion]].
