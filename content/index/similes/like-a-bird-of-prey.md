## The simile

{{quote:7892c043-89e9-58aa-b6f5-ebd557a99a41}}

ἣ δʼ ἅρπῃ ἐϊκυῖα τανυπτέρυγι λιγυφώνῳ / οὐρανοῦ ἐκκατεπᾶλτο διʼ αἰθέρος: “and she, like a long-winged, shrill-voiced bird of prey, / leapt down from the sky through the upper air” (19.350–351). The ἅρπη is named only here in Homer, and what bird it was cannot be told (see [[harpe]] and the translation's note on 19.350). Its two epithets are of the wing and of the cry: τανυπτέρυξ, “with wings stretched out”, is said once more in the Iliad, of the birds of omen that Hector will not obey (12.237; see [[long-winged]]), and λιγύφωνος, “shrill-voiced”, occurs only here. ἐκκατεπᾶλτο, “leapt down”, is to be divided ἐκ-κατ-έπ-αλτο, from the verb of leaping, as Walter Leaf shows from κατεπάλμενος, “leaping down”, said of Oileus springing down from his chariot to face Agamemnon (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.351; 11.94); it is not from πάλλομαι, which means “shake”, not “leap”. The goddess comes out of the sky and down through the upper air (see [[sky]] and [[upper-air]]).

The likeness is of the stoop: a bird of prey dropping from the height, long-winged and crying. The poem does not say whether Athena takes the bird's shape or only goes like one; the question is the same for the gods who sit “like birds, like vultures” on the oak of Zeus (7.58–61; see [[like-vultures]] and the translation's note on 7.59).

## In context

The elders have failed to make Achilles eat. He will touch nothing until the sun goes down, and he laments for Patroclus, who used to set his meal before him in the hut (19.303–337; see [[fasting]]). Zeus sees them weeping and pities them, and speaks to Athena: “My child, you have utterly abandoned your brave man” (19.340–342). The others have gone to their meal, “while he is unfed and has tasted nothing”; she is to drip nectar and ambrosia into his breast, “so that hunger may not come upon him” (19.346–348). She was “eager already” (19.349). She does as she was told, “so that joyless hunger might not come upon his knees”, and goes back to the house of her father, while the Achaeans arm and pour out of the ships (19.352–356; see [[athena]], [[nectar]], [[ambrosia]] and [[snowflakes-from-zeus]]). At the beginning of the book Thetis dripped ambrosia and red nectar into the nostrils of Patroclus, to keep his body sound (19.38–39), with the same verb, στάζω, “drip” (στάξε, 19.39; στάξον, 19.348; στάξʼ, 19.354).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Gods come down from the heights like birds. Poseidon leaves the two Ajaxes as a swift-winged hawk rises from a sheer rock (13.62–65; see [[the-hawk-from-the-rock]]), Apollo goes down from Ida like a hawk, the dove-killer (15.237–238), and at the end of the last book Thetis “like a hawk leapt down from snowy Olympus” with the armor that Hephaestus made (18.616–617; see [[like-a-hawk]]). ἆλτο, “leapt”, the verb of Thetis' descent, is the verb that ἐκκατεπᾶλτο is made from: one goddess brings Achilles his arms, the other his food. Hera and Athena go to the battle “like timid doves in their step” (5.778; see [[timid-doves]]), and Athena's own descents are likened to a shooting star (4.75–78; see [[shooting-star]]) and to a purple rainbow (17.547–552; see [[like-rainbows]]). In the Odyssey she leaves Nestor and Telemachus at Pylos “in the likeness of a sea-eagle”, to the amazement of all who see it (φήνῃ εἰδομένη, *Odyssey* 3.371–372), and perches on the roof beam of Odysseus' hall “like a swallow” (*Odyssey* 22.239–240), and Hermes skims the waves like a gull (*Odyssey* 5.51; see [[the-gull]]). Sleep sits hidden in a fir on Ida like the shrill bird that the gods call chalcis and men cymindis (14.289–291; see [[chalcis-and-cymindis]]).
