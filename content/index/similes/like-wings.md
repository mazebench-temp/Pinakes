## The simile

{{quote:e55aa7af-3220-5739-bc8b-5635507da20b}}

τῷ δʼ εὖτε πτερὰ γίγνετʼ, ἄειρε δὲ ποιμένα λαῶν: “and it was like wings to him, and it lifted the shepherd of the people” (19.386). εὖτε is the particle of comparison, “as, like”, that opens the simile of the mist in Book 3 (εὖτʼ ὄρεος κορυφῇσι, 3.10). Walter Leaf reports that Aristarchus is said to have read αὖτε, “again”, on second thoughts; he answers that in epic language the line would then mean that the armor became real wings to Achilles again, which is not to be believed, and that ἄειρε, “lifted”, itself shows that a particle of comparison is needed, unless Achilles is to soar through the scenes that follow. With the particle before them, the wings cover the lifting: “like wings to lift him” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.386; see the translation's note on 19.386).

The trial is told in the verse before: εἰ οἷ ἐφαρμόσσειε καὶ ἐντρέχοι ἀγλαὰ γυῖα, “whether it fitted him and his splendid limbs moved freely in it” (19.385). Leaf takes the aorist ἐφαρμόσσειε in the transitive sense, “whether he had fitted the arms on well”; the English takes it with the dative, “whether it fitted him” (on 19.385; see the translation's note on 19.385). ἐντρέχοι is “ran in” it.

The likeness is of lightness. The armor is heavy: the shield is “great and heavy” and the helmet “heavy” (19.373, 19.381), and the spear that Achilles takes up next is “heavy, huge, and massive” (19.388). On him the armor weighs nothing, and it lifts him.

## In context

The trial comes between the helmet and the spear, and no other arming scene has it (see [[arming-scene]]). Before it the armor shines: the shield like the moon and like a fire seen by sailors from the sea, the helmet like a star (19.373–383; see [[the-fire-seen-by-sailors]] and [[like-a-star]]). After it Achilles draws his father's spear from its case, the Pelian ash that no other Achaean could wield (19.387–391; see [[the-pelian-ash]]).

Other men have worn armor that was not made for them. Paris goes out to his duel in the corselet of his brother Lycaon, “and fitted it to himself” (ἥρμοσε δʼ αὐτῷ, 3.333). When Hector puts on the armor he stripped from Patroclus, Zeus “fitted the armor to Hector's body, and Ares entered into him, / dread Enyalius, and his limbs within were filled / with courage and strength” (17.210–212; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]]). Leaf reports the scholia's remark that the divine armor seems to fit Peleus, Achilles, Patroclus and Hector equally well, and answers that it probably included no breastplate (on 17.210). The new armor was made for Achilles alone, and no god enters him here; the armor itself lifts him.

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the Odyssey wings are a measure for ships: oars “which are wings to ships” (*Odyssey* 11.125 = 23.272), and the ships of the Phaeacians, “swift as a wing or a thought” (*Odyssey* 7.36).
