## The simile

{{quote:f7714e69-14c6-5437-b3fc-b44b2abc24d9}}

ὡς δὲ πατὴρ οὗ παιδὸς ὀδύρεται ὀστέα καίων / νυμφίου, ὅς τε θανὼν δειλοὺς ἀκάχησε τοκῆας: “And as a father mourns as he burns the bones of his son, / newly married, whose death has grieved his wretched parents” (23.222–223). The son is a νυμφίος, a bridegroom. Walter Leaf concludes from the Odyssey, where Rhexenor is killed by Apollo while still νυμφίος in his hall, leaving only a daughter (*Odyssey* 7.64–66), that a man kept the name after his marriage until he had a son; that the father should lose his son just when he might expect grandsons would add to his grief (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.223). ὀστέα καίων, “as he burns the bones”, is the work of the dead man's kin at the pyre. The parents, τοκῆες, are the father and the mother: the death has grieved them both, and the father is the one who stands at the fire.

The tenor: ὣς Ἀχιλεὺς ἑτάροιο ὀδύρετο ὀστέα καίων, / ἑρπύζων παρὰ πυρκαϊὴν ἁδινὰ στεναχίζων, “so Achilles mourned as he burned the bones of his companion, / dragging himself beside the pyre, groaning without ceasing” (23.224–225). The verse of the tenor repeats the verse of the father: ὀδύρεται ὀστέα καίων, ὀδύρετο ὀστέα καίων. Only the mourner and the dead change, and Achilles does exactly what the father does. ἑρπύζων, “dragging himself”, is the weary movement of a broken-hearted man; Leaf notes that the verb is used again only of Odysseus, wandering on the shore of Ithaca and lamenting for his native land, and of the old Laertes creeping about his vineyard (*Odyssey* 13.219–220, 1.193; on 23.225). ἁδινὰ στεναχίζων, “groaning without ceasing”, ends the verse in which Laertes, told a false tale of his son, pours the black dust over his gray head (*Odyssey* 24.315–317), and when Thetis comes to Achilles in the last book she finds him groaning so still (ἁδινὰ στενάχοντα, 24.123).

The point of likeness is the grief that goes against nature: the old bury the young. Patroclus was the elder of the two (11.786–787), but the simile makes Achilles his father. It also looks at the fathers of the poem. Achilles has said that he could suffer nothing worse, not even the news of his father's death, while Peleus in Phthia weeps for lack of such a son (19.321–324; see [[peleus]]); Menoetius had sent his son with Achilles, who promised to bring him home to Opus (18.324–327; see [[menoetius]]). The father who burns his son's bones is what Peleus and Priam will be: Achilles tells Priam in the last book that Peleus had one son, doomed to an early death, and that he does not care for him in his old age (24.538–542), and Hector's brothers and companions gather his white bones at the end of the poem (24.793).

## In context

The pyre will not burn. Achilles prays to the North Wind and the West Wind and promises them fine sacrifices; Iris finds them feasting in the house of the West Wind, and they come over the sea with a wondrous din, fall on the pyre, and blow all night (23.192–217; see [[boreas]], [[zephyrus]] and [[iris]]). “And all night long swift Achilles, / taking a two-handled cup, drew wine from a golden mixing bowl / and poured it on the ground, and soaked the earth, / calling on the soul of poor Patroclus” (23.218–221; see [[libation]] and [[psyche]]). The simile follows. Then the morning star rises, the pyre dies down and the flame ceases, the winds go home over the Thracian sea, and Achilles turns away from the pyre, lies down worn out, and sweet sleep leaps upon him (23.226–232; see [[cremation]] and [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Iliad gives its grief for Patroclus to parents in its similes. Menelaus stands over his body as a cow stands over her first calf (17.4–6; see [[the-cow-and-her-calf]]), and Achilles groans over it like a bearded lion whose cubs a hunter has taken (18.318–323; see [[the-lion-and-its-cubs]]). Phoenix, received by Peleus, was loved by him “as a father loves his son” (9.481; see [[as-a-father-loves-his-son]]), and in the Odyssey the swineherd kisses Telemachus as a father welcomes his only son home after ten years (*Odyssey* 16.17–21). The newly married who die young are a theme of the battles: Protesilaus, whose wife was left in Phylace with her cheeks torn in mourning and his house half-finished (2.700–701); Iphidamas, who fell “far from his wife, whom he had wooed”, and had seen no joy of her (11.241–243); and Hyperenor, whose killer “widowed his wife in the inmost part of her new bridal chamber, / and brought accursed lamentation and grief upon his parents” (17.36–37).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus gives the same reversal to Croesus, a prisoner before Cyrus: no one is so foolish as to choose war rather than peace, for in peace sons bury their fathers, and in war fathers bury their sons (Herodotus 1.87.4).
