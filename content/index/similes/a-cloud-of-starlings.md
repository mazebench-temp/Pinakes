## The simile

{{quote:a9778259-cacb-56b4-b5d2-69a0c4da577a}}

The Trojans keep following the men who carry the body, “and two among them most of all, / Aeneas, son of Anchises, and shining Hector” (17.753–754). τῶν δʼ ὥς τε ψαρῶν νέφος ἔρχεται ἠὲ κολοιῶν / οὖλον κεκλήγοντες, ὅτε προΐδωσιν ἰόντα / κίρκον, ὅ τε σμικρῇσι φόνον φέρει ὀρνίθεσσιν, “And as a cloud of starlings or of jackdaws goes / screaming a cry of death, when they see coming / a falcon, which brings slaughter to small birds” (17.755–757). ψάρ is the starling and κολοιός the jackdaw (see [[starlings]] and [[jackdaws]]); Walter Leaf notes the older form ψαρῶν here beside the Ionic ψῆρας of Book 16 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.755). νέφος, “cloud”, is said of a mass of men as well as of birds: “a cloud of foot soldiers” follows the two Ajaxes into battle (4.274), and Achilles fears that “a dark cloud of Trojans” has settled about the ships (16.66). The κίρκος is a falcon, the bird to which Achilles is likened when he chases Hector around the walls of Troy (22.139–142; see [[falcon]]). σμικρός, “small”, is found only here in Homer, whose usual word for small is τυτθός (Leaf on 17.757).

οὖλον κεκλήγοντες, “screaming a cry of death”, is said of the birds and then of the men. κεκλήγων is the scream of birds and of men in an onset (12.125, 16.430). οὖλος is taken here as “deadly”, a death-shriek, as Leaf takes it after Buttmann, who distinguished three words of this form; others take it as “woolly”, a thick, confused cry, or as “whole”, unceasing (Leaf on 17.756; see the translation's note on 17.756, 17.759).

The tenor: ὣς ἄρʼ ὑπʼ Αἰνείᾳ τε καὶ Ἕκτορι κοῦροι Ἀχαιῶν / οὖλον κεκλήγοντες ἴσαν, λήθοντο δὲ χάρμης, “so before Aeneas and Hector the young men of the Achaeans / went screaming a cry of death, and forgot the joy of battle” (17.758–759; see [[charme]]). The two Trojans are one falcon, and the young Achaeans a flock. The point of likeness is the panic of many weak creatures before one that kills: they do not fight, they cry out and scatter. The book ends with what they leave behind: “And much fine armor fell around and about the ditch / as the Danaans fled; and there was no respite from the war” (17.760–761).

## In context

The body of Patroclus is on its way to the ships. Menelaus and Meriones carry it like mules dragging a beam down a mountain, and the two Ajaxes hold off the Trojans behind them like a wooded ridge that turns aside the floods (17.735–753; see [[mules-dragging-a-beam]] and [[the-wooded-ridge]]). The rest of the Achaeans break before Aeneas and Hector (see [[aeneas]] and [[hector]]), and the flight reaches the ditch of the Achaean wall (17.760; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). There the next book takes up the story: Antilochus brings the news to Achilles (18.1–21), and Achilles goes out to the ditch and shouts, and the Trojans fall back (18.215–231; see [[achilles]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same small birds fled in Book 16, when Patroclus went through the champions “like a hawk, / a swift one, which puts jackdaws and starlings to flight” (16.582–583; see [[like-a-hawk]]). There the Achaeans had the bird of prey and the Trojans were the starlings; at the end of Book 17 the likeness is turned around. Other birds flee a bird of prey: Artemis escapes Hera in tears like a dove that flies from a hawk into a hollow rock (21.493–495), and in the Odyssey the suitors are like the small birds that fly low over the plain while vultures with crooked talons swoop on them from the mountains (*Odyssey* 22.302–306; see [[like-vultures]]).
