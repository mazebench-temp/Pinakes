## The simile

Two comparisons come one after the other. οἳ δʼ ἄρʼ ἴσαν ὡς εἴ τε πυρὶ χθὼν πᾶσα νέμοιτο: “so they went on, as if the whole land were being devoured by fire”. νέμοιτο is literally “were being grazed”, a word for cattle at pasture, used here of fire eating up the land.

Then the earth: γαῖα δʼ ὑπεστενάχιζε Διὶ ὣς τερπικεραύνῳ / χωομένῳ, ὅτε τʼ ἀμφὶ Τυφωέϊ γαῖαν ἱμάσσῃ / εἰν Ἀρίμοις, ὅθι φασὶ Τυφωέος ἔμμεναι εὐνάς: “and the earth groaned beneath them, as beneath Zeus who delights in thunder in his anger, when he lashes the earth around Typhoeus among the Arimi, where they say Typhoeus has his bed”. ὑπεστενάχιζε is “groaned beneath”, χωομένῳ “in anger”, and ἱμάσσῃ “lashes”, as with a whip; the lash is presumably the thunderbolt ([[delighting-in-thunder]]). εὐναί is a bed or lair. The tenor comes back with the same verb: “so the earth groaned greatly beneath their feet as they came” (μέγα στεναχίζετο γαῖα, 2.784).

The point of likeness is weight and noise. The tread of the army shakes the ground as Zeus' blows shake it over the monster.

## In context

{{quote:07c77e5c-9453-5206-9ae0-9537e78b2b79}}

The Catalogue is done, and the best men and horses have been named (2.760–779). Now the march resumes that the similes before the Catalogue had begun. The fire takes up the first of them, the forest fire whose glare is seen far off ([[forest-fire]]), so that fire frames the Catalogue. The groaning earth recalls the assembly, where “the earth groaned as the people sat down” (2.95–96), and the earth that “rang terribly” under the feet of the men and their horses (2.465–466; [[birds-on-the-asian-meadow]]).

The narrator cites report: ὅθι φασί (“where they say”). Before the Catalogue he told the Muses that “we hear only the report, and know nothing” (2.486; [[we-hear-only-the-report]]). The bed of Typhoeus is left to what people say. See [[typhoeus]], [[arimi]] and [[zeus-and-typhoeus]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells the fight. Typhoeus, the youngest child of Earth, is struck down by Zeus' thunderbolts while the earth burns, and is hurled into Tartarus (*Theogony* 820–868). Hesiod's verbs are the simile's: as Zeus rises to fight, “the earth groaned” (ἐπεστενάχιζε δὲ γαῖα, 843), and when he has beaten Typhoeus down, “lashing him with blows” (πληγῇσιν ἱμάσσας), “the huge earth groaned” (στενάχιζε δὲ γαῖα πελώρη, 857–858). Pindar puts him under Etna and says that a Cilician cave once reared him (*Pythian* 1.15–28). Where the Arimi lived was disputed in antiquity.
