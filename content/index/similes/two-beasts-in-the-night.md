## The simile

{{quote:3794a65c-f222-5460-b307-2effee115495}}

οἳ δʼ ὥς τʼ ἠὲ βοῶν ἀγέλην ἢ πῶϋ μέγʼ οἰῶν / θῆρε δύω κλονέωσι μελαίνης νυκτὸς ἀμολγῷ / ἐλθόντʼ ἐξαπίνης σημάντορος οὐ παρεόντος: “And as a herd of cattle or a great flock of sheep is thrown into confusion by two wild beasts in the dead of black night, coming on them suddenly when the herdsman is not there” (15.323–325). πῶϋ μέγʼ οἰῶν, “a great flock of sheep”, is a formula (11.696; see [[sheep]]), and the herd and the flock are named together, as the wealth of a farm (see [[cattle]]). θῆρε δύω are two wild beasts, in the dual, the number for a pair; the poem does not say what kind. κλονέω is to drive in confusion, the verb of the rout that Zeus sees when he wakes on Ida (15.7). νυκτὸς ἀμολγῷ is of uncertain sense, usually taken as the dead of night (see the translation's note on 11.173). The beasts come ἐξαπίνης, “suddenly”, and σημάντορος οὐ παρεόντος, “when the herdsman is not there”: σημάντωρ is “one who gives the signal”, the word for the commanders of an army (4.431) and for a charioteer (8.127).

The tenor: ὣς ἐφόβηθεν Ἀχαιοὶ ἀνάλκιδες· ἐν γὰρ Ἀπόλλων / ἧκε φόβον, Τρωσὶν δὲ καὶ Ἕκτορι κῦδος ὄπαζεν, “so the Achaeans fled in rout, their courage gone; for Apollo sent rout among them, and granted glory to the Trojans and Hector” (15.326–327). The tenor names two, the god who sends the rout and the man to whom he gives glory, as the vehicle has two beasts (see [[apollo]] and [[hector]]).

The point of likeness is panic: a herd that scatters at once, in the dark, with no one to stand between it and the beasts. Nothing is said of a kill. The herdsman who is not there may be read in the story as the god who has just left the Achaeans: Poseidon, who led them in Book 14 with a dread sword in his hand, has gone down into the sea at Zeus' command (14.384–387, 15.218–219; see [[poseidon]]), and Zeus has said that he will let no other god defend the Danaans until Achilles' wish is fulfilled (15.72–77). The vehicle is set at night in the middle of a day of battle, as is the simile of the lion that routs a herd at night for Agamemnon's pursuit of the Trojans (11.172–176).

## In context

The Trojans drive forward in a body, led by Hector with long strides, and before them goes Apollo, his shoulders wrapped in cloud, holding the aegis “which the smith Hephaestus gave to Zeus to carry, to put men to rout” (15.306–311; see [[aegis]]). The Argives hold their ground at first, and missiles strike home on both sides, “as long as Phoebus Apollo held the aegis motionless in his hands” (15.312–319). “But when he looked the Danaans of the swift horses in the face and shook it, and himself shouted very loudly besides, then their hearts in their breasts he bewitched, and they forgot their furious courage” (15.320–322). θέλγω, “bewitch”, is the word for what a god does to men's minds; Poseidon bewitched Alcathous' eyes before Idomeneus killed him (13.434–435). Zeus had told Apollo to do exactly this, to shake the aegis over the Achaeans and put them to flight (15.229–230). After the simile the battle scatters, and man kills man: Hector, Aeneas, Polydamas, Polites, Agenor and Paris kill eight Achaeans between them (15.328–342), and the Achaeans flee into the ditch and back behind their wall (15.343–345).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The lion that routs a herd of cattle at night, breaks the neck of one and gulps down her blood and entrails, pictures Agamemnon pursuing the Trojans in Book 11 (11.172–178; see [[lion-among-cattle]]). Diomedes falls on the sleeping Thracians as a lion falls on flocks that have no herdsman (μήλοισιν ἀσημάντοισιν, 10.485–486; see [[lion-among-the-flocks]]), and in Book 16 the Danaans fall on the Trojans as wolves fall on lambs and kids that have strayed from the flock through a shepherd's carelessness (16.352–355). Two beasts together are a pair of fighters elsewhere: the twin sons of Diocles fall like two lions reared in the mountains, which raid men's farmsteads until they are killed (5.554–560; see [[two-lions]]).
