## The simile

{{quote:b8f9b437-e7b6-550a-bc0b-dc06f31db995}}

στρόμβον δʼ ὣς ἔσσευε βαλών, περὶ δʼ ἔδραμε πάντῃ: “and with the blow he sent him spinning like a top, and he whirled all around” (14.413). στρόμβος, a top, occurs only here in Homer. The comparison is a single noun with ὥς set after it, the short form, and the half-verse is built like the one in which Agamemnon sends the trunk of Hippolochus “rolling like a log through the throng” (ὅλμον δʼ ὣς ἔσσευε, 11.147; see [[like-a-log]]): a noun, ὥς, and ἔσσευε, “set going, sent off”. There a dead man rolls; here a living man spins. περὶ δʼ ἔδραμε πάντῃ, literally “he ran round on every side”, is found only here.

A top spins where it stands, and so does Hector under the blow. The point of likeness is the spin, the body turned round on itself: he was going away (ἀπιόντα, 14.409), drawing back among his companions, when the stone hit him. The likeness comes from play, like the ball to which the Locrian Ajax's throw turns Imbrius' severed head in the book before (13.202–205; see [[like-a-ball]]), and like the ball it is set among the hardest blows of the battle. The top gives the spin; the next simile gives the fall, an oak uprooted by the thunderbolt of Zeus (14.414–418; see [[the-oak-struck-by-the-thunderbolt]]).

## In context

Hector threw first, at Ajax, who had turned straight toward him, and his spear struck where two straps crossed on Ajax's chest, the strap of his shield and the strap of his silver-studded sword; they protected his tender flesh, and Hector, enraged that his spear had flown in vain, drew back among his companions (14.402–408; see [[hector]], [[ajax]] and [[shield]]). Ajax picked up one of the stones, “the props of the swift ships”, that rolled about at the fighters' feet (ἔχματα νηῶν, 14.410; see [[props]]), and struck him in the chest over the rim of his shield, near the neck (14.412).

Then the oak, and the fall: “so the might of Hector fell quickly to the ground in the dust” (14.418). The Achaeans run up with a great cry to drag him away, but the best of the Trojans stand over him, and his companions carry him out of the battle to his horses (14.421–432; see [[horses]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Ajax has felled Hector with a stone before. In their duel in Book 7 he lifted a far greater stone than Hector's, hurled it “whirling it round”, broke Hector's shield with it and stretched him on his back, “but Apollo at once set him on his feet” (7.268–272). This time no god raises him at once. He is carried to the ford of the Xanthus, vomits dark blood and sinks back with black night over his eyes (14.433–439), and Apollo comes to revive him only in the next book, sent by Zeus (15.236–262).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates imagines a clever joker who offers spinning tops, στρόβιλοι, as things that stand still and move at once, when they whirl with the point fixed in one place; they stand, Socrates answers, in respect of their upright axis, and move in respect of their circumference (*Republic* 4.436d–e; see [[plato]]). In Aristotle's zoology στρόμβοι are spiral shells, to which he compares the inner part of the ear, and the στρομβώδη are the spiral-shelled creatures, such as the purple-fish and the trumpet-shell (*History of Animals* 1.11, 4.4; see [[aristotle]]).
