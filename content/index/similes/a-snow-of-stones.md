## The simile

{{quote:62dcbe0d-79df-53c1-bf79-be58e569615b}}

οἳ δʼ ἄρα χερμαδίοισιν ἐϋδμήτων ἀπὸ πύργων / βάλλον: “And these, with stones, from the well-built towers kept shooting” (12.154–155), defending themselves, their huts and their ships. A χερμάδιον is a stone that fills the hand, the common missile of the wall (see [[stone-throwing]]). νιφάδες δʼ ὡς πῖπτον ἔραζε, / ἅς τʼ ἄνεμος ζαὴς νέφεα σκιόεντα δονήσας / ταρφειὰς κατέχευεν ἐπὶ χθονὶ πουλυβοτείρῃ: “and the stones fell to the ground like snowflakes, that a blustering wind, driving the shadowy clouds, pours down thick upon the bountiful earth” (12.156–158). A νιφάς is a flake of snow. ζαής, “blowing hard”, occurs in the Iliad only here; δονέω is to shake or drive about; ταρφειός, “thick, close”, is the word for things that come many at once.

The tenor widens the snow to both armies: ὣς τῶν ἐκ χειρῶν βέλεα ῥέον ἠμὲν Ἀχαιῶν / ἠδὲ καὶ ἐκ Τρώων, “so the missiles poured from their hands, from the Achaeans and from the Trojans too” (12.159–160). Then the sound: “and the helmets rang dry all around, and the bossed shields, as men were struck with stones like millstones” (12.160–161). αὖον ἀΰτευν is a dry, harsh ringing, and μυλάκεσσι, from μύλη, “mill”, occurs only here (see the translation's note on 12.160–161).

The point of likeness is number and density. The stones are as many as the flakes of a snowstorm, and they come as thick and as steadily.

## In context

The simile follows the stand of the two Lapiths in front of the gates, who fight “trusting in the men above them” (12.153; see [[wild-boars-in-the-mountains]]). The men above are the defenders on the towers, and the snow is their part in the defense. Asius' complaint follows at once: he groans and strikes his thighs, and calls Zeus a lover of lies (12.162–172; see [[wasps-at-the-roadside]] and [[the-fight-at-the-wall]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Book 12 has a second snow simile for the stones, longer and windless. When the two Ajaxes have roused the Achaeans along the towers, the stones fly from both sides as thick as the snow that Zeus pours down on a winter's day when he has lulled the winds, covering the mountains, the plains and the fields to the edge of the sea (12.278–289; see [[the-great-snowfall]]). Here a blustering wind drives the flakes; there “he lulls the winds” (12.281).

Snowflakes are a measure of number elsewhere too. When the army arms for Achilles' return, the helmets, shields, corselets and spears pour from the ships as thick as the snowflakes of Zeus under the North Wind (19.357–361), and Antenor remembers Odysseus' words “like the snowflakes of winter” (3.222; see [[snowflakes-of-winter]]). Iris flies down from Ida as swiftly as snow or hail from the clouds (15.170–172).
