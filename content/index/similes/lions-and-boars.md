## The simile

{{quote:93995579-5adc-598e-8a67-617c5c2b7dd3}}

ἕστασαν ἀμφὶ βίην Διομήδεος ἱπποδάμοιο / εἰλόμενοι λείουσιν ἐοικότες ὠμοφάγοισιν / ἢ συσὶ κάπροισιν, τῶν τε σθένος οὐκ ἀλαπαδνόν: “they were standing around mighty Diomedes, tamer of horses, crowded together, like lions that eat raw flesh or wild boars, whose strength is no weakling's”. εἰλόμενοι is “packed together” (εἴλω, “press, hem in”). ὠμοφάγος, “eating raw flesh” (ὠμός, “raw”, and φαγεῖν, “eat”), is said of lions, and also of wolves (16.157) and of jackals (11.479; see [[eating-raw-flesh]]). σῦς κάπρος is the wild boar (see [[boar]]). ἀλαπαδνός is “weak, feeble”, and “no weakling's” strength is great strength said by denial (see [[litotes]]). βίη Διομήδεος, “the might of Diomedes”, names the hero by his strength, as βίη Ἡρακληείη names Heracles (see [[might-of-heracles]]).

The point of likeness is ferocity in a crowd, and the two beasts give it two sides. The lion is the beast of attack, the boar the beast that stands at bay and turns on the hunters (see [[like-a-boar]]). The Argives, packed around their champion, are dangerous in both ways, although for the moment they are neither attacking nor holding: they have been giving ground before Hector and Ares (5.699–702).

## In context

Hera and Athena have come down to the Simoeis and go toward the battle on foot, “like timid doves in their step” (5.778; see [[timid-doves]]). The doves find lions. Then Hera, in the likeness of Stentor, shouts at the Argives: “Shame, Argives, wretched disgraces, admirable in looks!” While Achilles fought, the Trojans never came out before the Dardanian gates; now they are fighting by the ships (5.784–791; see [[stentor]]). The simile shows the Argives as fierce as beasts; the goddess calls them cowards. Both are true of an army that has the strength and is not using it. Athena then goes to Diomedes himself (5.793; see [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The couplet is used again in Book 7, when Hector and Ajax, their spears thrown, draw them back and “fell upon each other like lions that eat raw flesh or wild boars, whose strength is no weakling's” (7.255–257). The first half-line returns alone for the Trojans storming the ships “like lions that eat raw flesh” (15.592).

Lion and boar meet in other similes. Hector turns among his enemies like a boar or a lion among dogs and hunters (12.41–48), and he kills Patroclus as a lion overcomes a tireless boar in a fight over a little spring on the mountain peaks (16.823–826). See [[lion]].
