## The simile

{{quote:c30e3f14-70b0-5853-b61c-1440047f5fcb}}

ἐν δʼ ἔπεσʼ ὡς ὅτε κῦμα θοῇ ἐν νηῒ πέσῃσι / λάβρον ὑπαὶ νεφέων ἀνεμοτρεφές· ἣ δέ τε πᾶσα / ἄχνῃ ὑπεκρύφθη, ἀνέμοιο δὲ δεινὸς ἀήτη / ἱστίῳ ἐμβρέμεται, τρομέουσι δέ τε φρένα ναῦται / δειδιότες· τυτθὸν γὰρ ὑπʼ ἐκ θανάτοιο φέρονται: “and fell on them as when a wave falls on a swift ship, violent under the clouds, wind-nurtured; and the ship is all hidden under the foam, and the dread blast of the wind roars in the sail, and the sailors tremble at heart in fear; for they are carried out from under death by only a little” (15.624–628).

λάβρος is “violent, furious”. ἀνεμοτρεφής, “nurtured by the wind”, is the wave swollen by the gale; the same word describes a spear, its ash grown tough in the wind (11.256; see [[wind-nurtured]]). ἄχνη is the foam or spray of the sea, which the wave spits out when it breaks on the shore (4.426), and ὑπεκρύφθη, “was hidden under”, is found only here. ἀήτη is the blast of the wind, and ἐμβρέμεται, “roars in”, also found only here, is from βρέμω, the roar of the surf on the shore (2.210, 4.425) and of the wind in the oaks (14.399). τρομέουσι … φρένα, “tremble at heart”, and τυτθὸν … ὑπʼ ἐκ θανάτοιο φέρονται, “they are carried out from under death by only a little”: τυτθόν, “a little”, is the narrow margin, and ὑπέκ, “out from under”, the word for dragging a man out from under the missiles (4.465, 18.232).

The tenor: ὣς ἐδαΐζετο θυμὸς ἐνὶ στήθεσσιν Ἀχαιῶν, “so the heart in the breasts of the Achaeans was torn” (15.629). The verse is the one that closes the first simile of *Iliad* 9, where the North Wind and the West Wind come down from Thrace on the fish-filled sea (9.4–8; see [[two-winds-from-thrace]]). There the sea itself was the Achaeans' heart; here they are the crew of a ship in the storm.

The simile is told from inside the ship. The wave falls; then the vehicle turns to what the men on board see, the foam over everything, and hear, the wind in the sail, and feel, their fear, and it ends with what they know, that they are only just escaping death. The Achaeans are seafarers fighting beside their own ships, and the ship of the simile is one of the things they are fighting for. Earlier in the book the Trojans came over the broken wall like a wave over a ship's side (15.381–384; see [[the-wave-over-the-ships-side]]). Now the wave is one man, and it is the Achaeans' hearts that are swamped.

## In context

The Danaans had held like a rock by the gray sea, which withstands the winds and the swollen waves (15.618–622; see [[the-rock-by-the-sea]]). “But he, blazing all over with fire, leapt into the throng” (15.623): λαμπόμενος πυρὶ πάντοθεν, Hector shines like fire in his bronze, and in the next verse he is water. The sea of one simile passes into the next, and the rock becomes a ship. A third simile follows at once, the lion that falls on cattle in a marsh, and the Achaeans flee in wondrous rout before Hector and father Zeus (15.630–637; see [[lion-among-cattle]] and [[hector]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Sailors in the Iliad's similes are men at the mercy of the wind. Hector and Paris come out to the Trojans as welcome as the fair wind that a god sends to sailors worn out at the oars (7.4–7; see [[the-fair-wind]]), and the gleam of Achilles' shield is like the blaze of a fire burning high in a lonely farmstead in the mountains, seen from the sea by sailors whom the storm winds carry, against their will, far from their friends (19.374–378). In the Odyssey the storm at sea is lived through. Poseidon's storm breaks Odysseus' raft, and the great wave throws him from it (*Odyssey* 5.291–332); on the ship of his men the West Wind snaps the forestays and brings the mast down, Zeus strikes the ship with his thunderbolt, and the sea tears its sides from the keel (*Odyssey* 12.403–425).
