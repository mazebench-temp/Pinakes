## The simile

{{quote:8761b312-70fc-5a59-8f1f-6ecead2a3873}}

τοὺς δὲ διὰ μελεϊστὶ ταμὼν ὡπλίσσατο δόρπον· / ἤσθιε δʼ ὥς τε λέων ὀρεσίτροφος, οὐδʼ ἀπέλειπεν, / ἔγκατά τε σάρκας τε καὶ ὀστέα μυελόεντα: “Then he cut them limb from limb and made ready his supper; / and he ate like a mountain-bred lion, and left nothing, / entrails and flesh and marrowy bones” (*Odyssey* 9.291–293). διὰ … ταμὼν μελεϊστί, “cut them limb from limb”, are the words of Priam's fear that Achilles has already cut Hector limb from limb and set him before his dogs (*Iliad* 24.408–409; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.291). οὐδʼ ἀπέλειπεν stands as a parenthesis, “without leaving anything”, and ἔγκατα and the rest are the objects of ἤσθιε (Merry on 9.292). ὀρεσίτροφος is “mountain-bred” (see [[mountain-bred]] and [[lion]]).

The point of likeness is the eating. The lion of the Iliad's similes eats in the same way: it breaks the neck of a cow it has seized, “and then gulps down her blood and all her entrails” (*Iliad* 11.175–176 = 17.63–64), and in the second of the two similes the dogs and herdsmen shout from far off and dare not come against it (*Iliad* 17.65–67). Here the lion leaves nothing at all, and there is no one to drive him off; the companions can only weep and lift their hands to Zeus (*Odyssey* 9.294–295). The words ὥς τε λέων ὀρεσίτροφος begin the simile of Sarpedon at the Achaean wall, a lion that goes after the sheep of men (*Iliad* 12.299–306; see [[the-mountain-lion-and-the-fold]]), and the simile of Odysseus coming out of the thicket toward the girls (*Odyssey* 6.130–136; see [[the-hungry-lion]]). In Book 9 the shepherd himself is the lion, and the men are his flock.

## In context

The meal follows the killing told in the simile before it, the two men dashed to the ground “like puppies” (*Odyssey* 9.289–290; see [[like-puppies]]). Then the Cyclops fills his great belly, “eating human flesh and drinking unmixed milk on top of it”, and lies down in the cave among his flocks (*Odyssey* 9.296–298). Odysseus would stab him as he lies there, but a second thought holds him back: the men could not have pushed the door-stone away from the doorway (*Odyssey* 9.299–305; see [[door-stone]]). The meal is repeated at dawn and at evening, with the words ὡπλίσσατο δεῖπνον and δόρπον (*Odyssey* 9.311, 9.344; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]).

## Outside Homer

Virgil's Achaemenides tells the Trojans that the Cyclops “feeds on the entrails and the black blood of the wretched” (*visceribus miserorum et sanguine vescitur atro*, *Aeneid* 3.622), and that he saw him chew the limbs dripping with black gore, and the warm joints quivering under his teeth (*Aeneid* 3.626–627; see [[virgil]]). In Euripides' *Cyclops* the meal is cooked (*Cyclops* 396–404; see [[like-puppies]]).
