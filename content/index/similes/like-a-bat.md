## The simile

{{quote:d7a85426-58f3-587b-8298-59150f127c7b}}

αὐτὰρ ἐγὼ ποτὶ μακρὸν ἐρινεὸν ὑψόσʼ ἀερθείς, / τῷ προσφὺς ἐχόμην ὡς νυκτερίς: “but I, springing up on high toward the tall wild fig tree, / clung to it and held on like a bat” (*Odyssey* 12.432–433). νυκτερίς, the bat, is the creature of the night, νύξ; Homer names it only here and in the last book of the Odyssey. προσφύς, “clinging”, is the aorist participle of προσφύω, “grow onto”, and ἐχόμην, “held on”, is the verb of a man who clings for his life: on the coast of Scheria Odysseus seized a rock with both hands “and clung to it, groaning, until the great wave went past” (τῆς ἔχετο, *Odyssey* 5.428–429; see [[the-octopus]]). W. W. Merry renders the verses “but I, springing upwards towards a lofty fig-tree, held on, clinging to it as a bat (holds)” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.432). The tree is the one Circe named: “On it is a great wild fig tree, in full leaf; / and beneath it brilliant Charybdis sucks down the black water” (*Odyssey* 12.103–104; see [[the-fig-tree-of-charybdis]]).

The likeness is two words long and builds no scene. Merry counts it among the concentrated similes: ὄρνις ὥς, “like a bird”, said of Athena's departure from Telemachus, is “merely a simile such as Odysseus uses of himself” at the fig tree (on 1.320; see [[like-a-bird]]). What the bat does is told in the next verses. Odysseus cannot rest his weight anywhere: “And I could not anywhere / either plant my feet firmly or climb up it; / for its roots were far below, and its branches hung high out of reach, / long and great, and they overshadowed Charybdis” (*Odyssey* 12.433–436). He hangs by his hands under the boughs, his feet over the water, as a bat hangs from a branch or from the roof of a cave. στηρίξαι, “plant firmly”, is intransitive here, where the Iliad has the middle στηρίξασθαι (*Iliad* 21.242; Merry on 12.434). ἀπήωροι, “hung high out of reach”, found only here, has the long vowel of ἄωρτο, “hung”, and of αἰωρέω, “hang”; Bothe would write ἀπηόριοι, the form of an epigram of Antiphilus, κλῶνες ἀπηόριοι, “overhanging boughs” (Merry on 12.435, citing *Palatine Anthology* 9.71; see the translation's row νυκτερίς; ἀπήωρος).

## In context

After the thunderbolt Odysseus lashed the keel and the mast together with the oxhide backstay and rode them, borne by the winds (*Odyssey* 12.420–425; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). The West Wind dropped, “and the South Wind came quickly, bringing sorrows to my heart, / that I might measure out again the way to deadly Charybdis” (*Odyssey* 12.427–428; see [[notus]]), and at sunrise he came to Scylla's crag and to Charybdis, just as “She sucked down the salt water of the sea” (*Odyssey* 12.429–431; see [[scylla]] and [[charybdis]]). This was the moment Circe had warned him against: “May you not be there when she sucks it down; / for not even the earth-shaker could save you from the evil” (*Odyssey* 12.106–107). The fig tree saves him. “And I held on unceasingly, until she should vomit up again / the mast and keel; and as I yearned for them they came, / late” (*Odyssey* 12.437–439), at the hour that Odysseus tells by a judge going home to his supper (see [[the-judge-rising-for-supper]]). Then he lets himself drop beside the timbers, sits on them and rows with his hands, and the father of men and gods does not let Scylla see him (*Odyssey* 12.442–446). Nine days later the gods bring him to Ogygia (*Odyssey* 12.447–449; see [[ogygia]] and [[calypso]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same words tell another clinging in the tale. Under the Cyclops' ram Odysseus grasped the fleece and, “turning myself over, I held on unceasingly with enduring heart” (νωλεμέως στρεφθεὶς ἐχόμην, *Odyssey* 9.435; see [[ram]]). Merry joins νωλεμέως with ἐχόμην there, as in the fig tree's verse (*Odyssey* 12.437), and takes στρεφθείς to mean “turned round”, that is, hanging downward, just as Odysseus clings to the fig tree and holds on ὡς νυκτερίς (on 9.435). In the Iliad Achilles, swept by the river, could not keep his footing, and caught hold of an elm with his hands; it fell, torn out by the roots, and bridged the stream (*Iliad* 21.240–246; see [[elm]]). The bats themselves return in the last book, in a long simile: the souls of the suitors follow Hermes gibbering, as bats in the depths of a wondrous cave flit gibbering when one has fallen from the cluster on the rock, and they cling to one another (ἀνά τʼ ἀλλήλῃσιν ἔχονται, *Odyssey* 24.6–9, a plain rendering; see [[a-clamor-as-of-birds]]). The bats of the cave hang together from the rock; the bat of Book 12 hangs alone from its tree.

## Outside Homer

[[strabo|Strabo]] uses the verses against Circe. She said that Charybdis sends up the water three times a day and sucks it down three times, and that not even the earth-shaker could save him if he were there when she sucked it down; yet Odysseus was there and did not perish, but sprang up to the fig tree, clung to it like a bat, and was saved when he had waited for the wreckage. So Circe spoke falsely, Strabo says, to frighten him away, and her “three times”, for the tide's twice a day, is the same kind of exaggeration (*Geography* 1.2.36; for the rest of his argument see [[the-judge-rising-for-supper]]).
