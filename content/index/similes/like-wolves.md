## The simile

ὣς τὸν μὲν λίπε θυμός, ἐπʼ αὐτῷ δʼ ἔργον ἐτύχθη / ἀργαλέον Τρώων καὶ Ἀχαιῶν· οἳ δὲ λύκοι ὣς / ἀλλήλοις ἐπόρουσαν, ἀνὴρ δʼ ἄνδρʼ ἐδνοπάλιζεν: “So his spirit left him, and over him there was work, grievous work, of Trojans and Achaeans; and they like wolves leapt upon one another, and man hurled man about”. λύκοι are wolves ([[wolves]]), and ὥς placed after its noun is the short form of comparison, “like wolves”. ἐπόρουσαν is “rushed at, leapt upon”. ἐδνοπάλιζεν, “shook, hurled about”, is a rare verb; Homer has it once more, in the Odyssey (14.512). ἔργον, “work”, is the poem's plain word for fighting.

There is no prey in this simile. Homer's wolves hunt in packs and fall on sheep, goats and deer; here both sides are wolves, and they fall on each other. The point of likeness is savagery at close quarters, and the half-line that follows makes it single combat many times over: ἀνὴρ δʼ ἄνδρʼ, “man … man”.

## In context

{{quote:9f24ec24-2ed2-5a59-b07f-f96791810c84}}

Echepolus has just fallen “like a tower” (4.462; see [[like-a-tower]]). Elephenor, leader of the Abantes, took him by the feet and dragged him out from under the missiles, “in eager haste to strip off his armor”; but as he stooped, his side showed beside his shield, and Agenor stabbed him there and loosened his limbs (4.463–469; see [[elephenor]], [[agenor]] and [[loosened-his-limbs]]). The man who fell over the body becomes the body fought over. It is the first fight over a corpse in the poem, and it grows out of the first death. Such fights recur through the battle books, and the greatest of them, the fight for the body of Patroclus, fills Book 17.

The simile is one of the shortest in the book, and it comes between two long ones: the torrents that roar as the armies meet (4.452–456; see [[torrents-meeting]]) and the poplar to which Simoeisius is compared (4.482–487; see [[the-poplar]]). See [[the-first-battle]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same short simile returns in the battle at dawn in Book 11. Trojans and Achaeans leap on one another, the battle holds their heads level, and they rage “like wolves” (οἳ δὲ λύκοι ὣς / θῦνον), while Strife looks on and rejoices (11.70–73). In Book 4 too Strife has just gone through the throng (4.440–445; see [[strife]]). Wolves stand for bands of fighters. The Myrmidons arm like wolves that have killed a great antlered stag in the mountains and go in a pack to drink from a dark spring, their jaws red with blood (16.156–163). The Danaans fall on the Trojans like wolves on lambs or kids that a careless shepherd has let scatter in the mountains (16.352–355). When Hector asks for an agreement about the body of the loser, Achilles refuses: there are no trusty oaths between lions and men, “nor do wolves and lambs have a like-minded heart” (22.261–264).
