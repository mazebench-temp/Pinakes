## The simile

{{quote:8d703838-5a16-5c7c-bc46-442f44f0f49c}}

ἠΰτε πάρδαλις εἶσι βαθείης ἐκ ξυλόχοιο / ἀνδρὸς θηρητῆρος ἐναντίον: “As a leopard comes out of a deep thicket / to face a hunting man” (21.573–574). πάρδαλις is feminine, and the translation keeps her a she (see [[leopard]]). A ξύλοχος is a thicket, the lair of wild beasts, and the first verse ends as the verse of the boar in Odysseus' simile, which “comes out of a deep thicket” when the hounds and young men rush at it (εἶσι βαθείης ἐκ ξυλόχοιο, 11.415; see [[the-boar-at-bay]]). θηρητήρ is the hunter, as in the simile of Hector setting the Trojans on like hounds (11.292; see [[the-hunter-and-his-hounds]]). ἐναντίον, “to face”, is the beast's own move: she does not wait to be found.

οὐδέ τι θυμῷ / ταρβεῖ οὐδὲ φοβεῖται, ἐπεί κεν ὑλαγμὸν ἀκούσῃ: “and in her heart / neither fears nor flees, when she hears the baying of the hounds” (21.574–575). ταρβεῖ οὐδὲ φοβεῖται is said of the boar or lion at bay among hounds and hunters in Hector's simile at the ditch, whose heart “never fears or flees” (12.46; see [[the-boar-or-lion-at-bay]]); φοβέομαι in Homer is to flee in fear (see [[phobos]]). ὑλαγμός, “baying”, is found only here in Homer. Walter Leaf quotes Herodian's report that, according to Aristarchus, some wrote κυνυλαγμόν, “the baying of dogs”, and that Stesichorus seems to have read it, since he speaks of ἀπειρέσιοι κυνυλαγμοί, countless bayings of dogs (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.575; see the translation's note on 21.575).

εἴ περ γὰρ φθάμενός μιν ἢ οὐτάσῃ ἠὲ βάλῃσιν, / ἀλλά τε καὶ περὶ δουρὶ πεπαρμένη οὐκ ἀπολήγει / ἀλκῆς, πρίν γʼ ἠὲ ξυμβλήμεναι ἠὲ δαμῆναι: “for even if he is first and stabs her or shoots her, / yet even pierced through with the spear she does not give up / her courage, until she either closes with him or is brought down” (21.576–578). οὐτάω is to stab with a spear held in the hand, βάλλω to hit with a thrown one. περὶ δουρὶ πεπαρμένη, “pierced through with the spear”, has the shaft still in her. ἀλκή is the courage to fight back (see [[alke]]), and ξυμβλήμεναι, “to close with him”, is to come to grips.

The tenor: ὣς Ἀντήνορος υἱὸς ἀγαυοῦ δῖος Ἀγήνωρ / οὐκ ἔθελεν φεύγειν, πρὶν πειρήσαιτʼ Ἀχιλῆος, “so the son of noble Antenor, brilliant Agenor, / would not flee before he had made trial of Achilles” (21.579–580; see [[agenor]], [[antenor]] and [[noble]]). Leaf notes that πειρήσαιτο is the only optative after πρίν in Homer, apart from 9.489 (on 21.580). The point of likeness is the beast at bay that goes out to meet the hunter: no fear of the noise of the hunt, and no giving up, even wounded, until the fight is settled one way or the other.

## In context

Apollo has put daring into Agenor and stands beside him hidden in mist (21.544–549). Agenor has argued with his own heart whether to flee, and has chosen to go out to meet Achilles “in front of the city” (21.567), since Achilles too can be pierced by the sharp bronze and has one life (21.553–570; see [[pondering-two-ways]]). “So saying, he crouched and waited for Achilles, and his valiant heart / within him was eager to make war and to fight” (21.571–572). The simile shows the choice. κατεναντίον, “to meet him” in his words (21.567), is the leopard's ἐναντίον, “to face” (21.574).

The event follows the simile up to its end. Agenor holds his shield before him, takes aim and shouts his challenge, and throws; he hits Achilles on the shin below the knee, and the greave of newly wrought tin, the god's gift, throws back the bronze (21.581–594; see [[greaves]]). The leopard's alternatives, to close with the hunter or be brought down, are both taken away: when Achilles rushes at him, Apollo snatches Agenor out of the fight and takes his likeness to lead Achilles away (21.595–605; see [[divine-rescue]] and [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Only here is a man likened to a leopard. The beast is a hunter of deer in Poseidon's taunt that the Trojans used to be like deer quick to flee, the prey of jackals, leopards and wolves (13.101–104; see [[timid-deer]]), and Menelaus names it first among the beasts of greatest might, before the lion and the boar (17.20–23; see [[leopard]]). Its place in this simile is the place of the boar and the lion in the others of the beast at bay. Odysseus, left alone, is the boar that comes out of a deep thicket to meet the dogs and the young men (11.414–420; see [[the-boar-at-bay]]), after a debate with his own heart that Agenor's follows (11.403–410); Hector at the ditch is the boar or lion that never fears or flees, “and it is his pride that kills him” (12.41–48; see [[the-boar-or-lion-at-bay]]). In the next book Hector waits for Achilles at the gates like a snake by its hole, before his own debate (22.93–97).
