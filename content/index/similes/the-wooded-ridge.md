## The simile

{{quote:4d642e76-6bb1-5275-baa0-5392041de4df}}

αὐτὰρ ὄπισθεν / Αἴαντʼ ἰσχανέτην, ὥς τε πρὼν ἰσχάνει ὕδωρ / ὑλήεις πεδίοιο διαπρύσιον τετυχηκώς, “And behind them / the two Ajaxes held the enemy back, as a ridge holds back water, / a wooded ridge that stretches right across the plain” (17.746–748). The verb is said twice, of the men and of the ridge: ἰσχανέτην, ἰσχάνει, “held back, holds back”. πρών is a ridge or a headland; the high headlands, πρώονες ἄκροι, stand out when the sky clears in the simile of the watch fires (8.557 = 16.299). ὑλήεις is “wooded” (see [[wooded]]). διαπρύσιος is elsewhere a word of sound, “piercingly”, as when Menelaus shouts to the Danaans earlier in the book (17.247). Here, Walter Leaf explains, it means “in a continuous line”, without a break, and τετυχηκώς that the ridge is there by nature, without any work of men; he sees in the picture a natural barrier that protects a village or the tilled fields behind it in time of flood (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.748).

ὅς τε καὶ ἰφθίμων ποταμῶν ἀλεγεινὰ ῥέεθρα / ἴσχει, ἄφαρ δέ τε πᾶσι ῥόον πεδίον δὲ τίθησι / πλάζων· οὐδέ τί μιν σθένεϊ ῥηγνῦσι ῥέοντες, “which holds back even the harsh streams of mighty rivers, / and at once sets the course of all of them onto the plain, / turning them aside; nor can they break it with their strength as they flow” (17.749–751). πεδίον δέ is πεδίονδε, “onto the plain”, with the suffix of direction printed as a separate word (see the translation's note on 17.383, 17.457, 17.579, 17.750). πλάζω is to drive something out of its course (Leaf on 17.751). The ridge does not stop the rivers by meeting their force. It turns them aside, and they flow away over the plain.

The tenor: ὣς αἰεὶ Αἴαντε μάχην ἀνέεργον ὀπίσσω / Τρώων, “so the two Ajaxes kept holding back the battle / of the Trojans” (17.752–753). The point of likeness is a barrier that will not break: the Trojans come on like floodwater and are held and turned. But they keep coming, “and two among them most of all, / Aeneas, son of Anchises, and shining Hector” (17.753–754).

## In context

Menelaus and Meriones are carrying the body to the ships, laboring like mules that drag a beam down a mountain, and the war blazes around them like a fire in a city (17.735–746; see [[mules-dragging-a-beam]] and [[the-burning-city]]). The two Ajaxes fight behind them, as Ajax had planned: “we two will fight the Trojans and brilliant Hector, / with equal spirit, of one name” (17.719–720; see [[the-two-ajaxes]]). The ridge holds; the men in front do not. Before Aeneas and Hector the young men of the Achaeans flee like starlings and jackdaws before a falcon, and the book ends at the ditch with “no respite from the war” (17.753–761; see [[a-cloud-of-starlings]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The simile reverses the river in flood of *Iliad* 5, to which Diomedes was likened as he rushed across the plain: “the close-built dikes do not hold it back” (5.89), the same verb, ἰσχανόωσιν, said of barriers that fail (see [[river-in-flood]]). Here the barrier holds. Defenders stand like natural things that do not move: the two Lapiths at the gate of the wall like oaks in the mountains “that withstand the wind and the rain all their days” (12.132–136; see [[oaks-on-the-mountains]]), and the Danaans before Hector like a great rock by the sea “which withstands the swift paths of the shrill winds” and the swollen waves (15.618–622; see [[the-rock-by-the-sea]]). The verb of holding on is also the fly's, which Athena put in Menelaus' breast: however often it is driven off, it “keeps on trying to bite” (ἰσχανάᾳ, 17.570–572; see [[the-daring-of-a-fly]]).
