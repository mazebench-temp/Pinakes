## The simile

{{quote:13e93c13-681b-5c78-81f9-ae50137e6078}}

ὥς τε δύʼ αἶγα λέοντε κυνῶν ὕπο καρχαροδόντων / ἁρπάξαντε φέρητον ἀνὰ ῥωπήϊα πυκνὰ / ὑψοῦ ὑπὲρ γαίης μετὰ γαμφηλῇσιν ἔχοντε: “As two lions snatch a goat away from sharp-toothed dogs and carry it through the thick brushwood, high above the ground, holding it in their jaws” (13.198–200). The simile is in the dual from its first word to its last, λέοντε, ἁρπάξαντε, φέρητον, ἔχοντε, and so is the tenor, which takes up its words: ὥς ῥα τὸν ὑψοῦ ἔχοντε δύω Αἴαντε κορυστὰ / τεύχεα συλήτην, “so the two Ajaxes, helmeted men, held him high and stripped his armor” (13.201–202). Greek has a number for pairs, and the poem uses it for the two Ajaxes again and again (see [[the-two-ajaxes]]). καρχαρόδοντες, “sharp-toothed” (κάρχαρος, “jagged”, and ὀδούς, “tooth”), is said of the two hounds that chase a fawn or a hare in Book 10 (“two sharp-toothed hounds”, 10.360; see [[sharp-toothed]] and [[two-hounds]]). ῥωπήϊα are thickets of brushwood, and γαμφηλαί are jaws, as of the lion that kills a bull among the herd (16.489).

The point of likeness is a pair working together, and a prize carried off from under the guard of its keepers. The Trojans are the dogs, and the dead man is the goat, a light burden for two lions. The goat is held “high above the ground”, and the Ajaxes hold Imbrius ὑψοῦ, “high”, as they strip him.

The tenor then goes beyond the simile. κεφαλὴν δʼ ἁπαλῆς ἀπὸ δειρῆς / κόψεν Ὀϊλιάδης κεχολωμένος Ἀμφιμάχοιο: “and his head from his tender neck the son of Oileus cut off, in his anger for Amphimachus”, and sent it flying like a ball through the throng until it fell in the dust in front of Hector's feet (13.202–205; see [[like-a-ball]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). Lions eat their prey. The son of Oileus mutilates his, and sends the head to the feet of the man who killed Amphimachus.

## In context

Teucer has killed Imbrius, and Hector, throwing at Teucer, has killed Amphimachus, son of Cteatus. Ajax's spear drives Hector back from both bodies, “and the Achaeans dragged them away” (13.170–194; see [[the-felled-ash]]). Stichius and Menestheus, the leaders of the Athenians, carry Amphimachus back to the army, and the two Ajaxes, “eager with furious courage”, carry off Imbrius (13.195–197). Poseidon, angry for his grandson, goes along the huts to rouse the Danaans (13.206–209; see [[amphimachus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Pairs of lions recur: the twin sons of Diocles, killed by Aeneas, were like two lions reared on the mountain peaks (5.554–560; see [[two-lions]]), Diomedes and Odysseus set out on the night raid “like two lions through the black night” (10.297), Hector and Patroclus fight over the body of Cebriones like two lions over a slain deer (16.756–758), and on the shield of Achilles two lions seize a bull from the herd while the dogs and young men follow it (18.579–586). Lions take goats elsewhere: the lion that finds a horned stag or a wild goat (3.23–24; see [[lion-on-a-carcass]]), and the bleating goats that shudder at a lion in Paris' boast (11.383; see [[goats-before-a-lion]]). Later in this book the two Ajaxes are a yoke of oxen at the plow (13.701–708; see [[oxen-at-the-plow]]).
