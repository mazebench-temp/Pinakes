## The simile

{{quote:e97bdf35-a942-5632-bd1a-77a6f63350a4}}

ἠΰτε κίρκος ὄρεσφιν ἐλαφρότατος πετεηνῶν / ῥηϊδίως οἴμησε μετὰ τρήρωνα πέλειαν: “As a falcon in the mountains, the nimblest of winged birds, / swoops easily after a timid dove” (22.139–140). κίρκος is a bird of prey of the hawk kind, rendered “falcon” to keep it apart from the ἴρηξ, the hawk (see [[falcon]] and [[hawk]]). The half-verse ἐλαφρότατος πετεηνῶν, “the nimblest of winged birds”, belongs to the falcon in the Odyssey too, where not even a hawk, a falcon, could have kept pace with the Phaeacian ship (*Odyssey* 13.86–87); the hawk and the eagle are each, in the same place of the verse, the swiftest of birds (15.238, 21.253; see [[like-a-hawk]] and [[like-an-eagle]]). τρήρων, “timid”, the dove's standing epithet, is formed from τρέω, “tremble, flee in fear” (see [[timid]] and [[doves]]), and the same verb returns in the tenor for the man: τρέσε δʼ Ἕκτωρ, “and Hector fled” (22.143). οἴμησε, “swoops”, is a sudden rush. *Iliad* 22 gives the verb to Hector at the end, when, drawing his sword for his last charge, he swoops like an eagle flying high (22.308, 22.311; see [[the-eagle-flying-high]]): the dove of the chase dies a bird of prey.

ἣ δέ θʼ ὕπαιθα φοβεῖται, ὃ δʼ ἐγγύθεν ὀξὺ λεληκὼς / ταρφέʼ ἐπαΐσσει, ἑλέειν τέ ἑ θυμὸς ἀνώγει: “and she flees out from under him, but he, close behind, with shrill cries, / darts at her again and again, and his heart bids him seize her” (22.141–142). ὕπαιθα, “out from under”, is the word of Artemis' flight from Hera in the simile of the dove and the hawk a book before (21.493; see [[the-dove-and-the-hawk]]). ταρφέα, “again and again”, and the present tenses keep the bird in the air: the falcon strikes and misses and strikes again.

The tenor: ὣς ἄρʼ ὅ γʼ ἐμμεμαὼς ἰθὺς πέτετο, τρέσε δʼ Ἕκτωρ / τεῖχος ὕπο Τρώων, λαιψηρὰ δὲ γούνατʼ ἐνώμα, “so he flew straight on in his fury, and Hector fled / beneath the wall of the Trojans, and plied his nimble knees” (22.143–144). The man is given the bird's verb: Achilles “flew” (πέτετο). Hector flees under the wall, as the dove flees from under the falcon, and the half-verse of Achilles' run toward the city at the start of the book, “plied his nimble feet and knees” (22.24; see [[like-a-racehorse]]), is now the hunted man's. The point of likeness is speed and terror, and the nearness of the hunt: the bird of prey is always close behind, and the simile ends before it strikes home.

## In context

Hector has waited before the gates like a serpent at its hole, and has argued with himself that it is better to fight (22.92–130; see [[the-serpent-at-its-hole]]). Then Achilles comes near, “the equal of Enyalius, the warrior of the shaking helmet”, brandishing the Pelian ash over his right shoulder, and the bronze shines about him like the light of a blazing fire or of the rising sun (22.131–135; see [[the-pelian-ash]] and [[like-the-sun]]). “And when Hector saw him, trembling seized him; and he could no longer bear / to stay there, but left the gates behind him, and went off in flight; / and the son of Peleus sprang at him, trusting in his swift feet” (22.136–138). ποσὶ κραιπνοῖσι πεποιθώς, “trusting in his swift feet”, was said of Paris when he ran down to the war like the stallion (6.505; see [[the-stallion]]).

The falcon opens the chase. The two run past the lookout and the windy wild fig tree, along the wagon road, to the springs of the Scamander (22.145–156; see [[the-lookout-at-troy]], [[the-fig-tree]] and [[the-springs-of-the-scamander]]), and three more similes follow the race: prize-winning horses around the turning posts (22.162–166; see [[racehorses-at-the-turning-post]]), a dog that tracks a fawn (22.189–193; see [[the-dog-and-the-fawn]]), and a dream in which no one can catch or escape (22.199–201; see [[as-in-a-dream]] and [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The falcon is the terror of small birds. The Achaeans flee before Aeneas and Hector like a cloud of starlings or jackdaws that see a falcon coming, “which brings slaughter to small birds” (17.755–757; see [[a-cloud-of-starlings]]). In the Odyssey a falcon, “Apollo's swift messenger”, flies past Telemachus on the right with a dove in its talons, plucking her, and the feathers fall between the ship and Telemachus himself (*Odyssey* 15.525–528). The hawk hunts the dove as well. Apollo goes down from Ida like a hawk, “a swift dove-killer” (15.237–238; see [[like-a-hawk]]), and Artemis, beaten by Hera, flees in tears like a dove that flies from a hawk into a hollow rock, “nor was it her fate to be caught” (21.493–496; see [[the-dove-and-the-hawk]]). There the dove reaches the rock. Hector does not reach the gates: whenever he makes for them, Achilles is there before him and turns him back toward the plain (22.194–198; see [[dardanian-gates]]). At the games for Patroclus a timid dove is the archers' mark, tied by the foot to a ship's mast; Teucer's arrow cuts the cord, and Meriones shoots the bird as it flies free under the clouds (23.852–876).

## Outside Homer

[[virgil|Virgil]] gives the bird of prey to the warrior maiden Camilla. The son of Aunus, a Ligurian, dares her to fight on foot and then flees on his horse; she outruns the horse, seizes the reins, and takes vengeance from his blood, “as easily as the hawk, the sacred bird, from a high rock overtakes on its wings a dove high in a cloud, and holds her caught and tears her open with its hooked feet; then blood and plucked feathers fall from the sky” (*Aeneid* 11.699–724). Homer's falcon is always about to strike; Virgil's strikes, and the feathers of the Odyssey's sign fall with the blood.
