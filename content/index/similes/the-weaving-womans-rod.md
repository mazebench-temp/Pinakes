## The simile

{{quote:e1e2ed21-be90-56a8-94d1-88e1dc171b25}}

ἄγχι μάλʼ, ὡς ὅτε τίς τε γυναικὸς ἐϋζώνοιο / στήθεός ἐστι κανών, ὅν τʼ εὖ μάλα χερσὶ τανύσσῃ / πηνίον ἐξέλκουσα παρὲκ μίτον, ἀγχόθι δʼ ἴσχει / στήθεος: “very close, as close as to a fair-belted woman's / breast is the rod, when she pulls it very skillfully with her hands, / drawing the spool out past the warp, and holds it near / her breast” (23.760–763). The three words of the loom occur nowhere else in Homer in these senses. κανών, “rod”, is elsewhere one of the rods of a shield (8.193, 13.407); πηνίον, the spool that carries the thread of the weft, and μίτος, the warp, are found only here.

Walter Leaf explains the simile, after Blümner, from the upright loom of the Greeks. The threads of the warp hung down from a beam, and each was tied to one of two horizontal rods, the even threads to one and the odd to the other. When the weaver pulled one rod toward her, an opening was made between the two sets of threads, and she passed the spool with the weft through it; she had to stand close up to the warp and its rods to reach across the loom (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.760; see the translation's note on 23.760–763, and [[loom]] and [[weaving]]). The rod comes almost to her breast as she draws it, and that is the measure of the distance: εὖ μάλα, “very skillfully”, and ἀγχόθι, “near”, keep the gap as small as a practiced hand can make it.

The tenor: ὣς Ὀδυσεὺς θέεν ἐγγύθεν, αὐτὰρ ὄπισθεν / ἴχνια τύπτε πόδεσσι πάρος κόνιν ἀμφιχυθῆναι, “so close Odysseus ran, and behind / he trod in his footprints before the dust could settle around them” (23.763–764), “and brilliant Odysseus poured his breath on his head, / running swiftly all the while” (23.765–766). Leaf compares the horses of Tros in the chariot race, whose breath warmed the back and shoulders of Eumelus (on 23.764; 23.379–381; see [[horses]]). The point of likeness is nearness, and something more: the woman's skill. Odysseus, “who knew crafty ways” (23.709), runs as a weaver works, close and exact, and the simile gives the man of μῆτις a likeness from the craft of women, the craft with which Penelope deceived the suitors, weaving her great web by day and undoing it by night (*Odyssey* 2.94–105; see [[metis]]).

## In context

Achilles sets out the prizes for speed: a mixing bowl of silver, the most beautiful on earth, made by Sidonians and once given by Euneus as the price of Lycaon; an ox; and half a talent of gold (23.740–751; see [[mixing-bowl]]). The son of Oileus rises, then Odysseus, then Antilochus, the fastest of the young men (23.754–756). “For them the running was strained to the full from the post; and quickly then / the son of Oileus took the lead; and brilliant Odysseus rushed on, / very close” (23.758–760). The simile follows, and the Achaeans cheer Odysseus on (23.766–767). As they come to the finish he prays to Athena, she makes his limbs light, and Ajax slips in the dung of the oxen that Achilles had killed for Patroclus; Odysseus takes the mixing bowl (23.768–779; see [[athena]], [[ajax-son-of-oileus]], [[odysseus]] and [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Iliad's women weave in the house. Helen weaves into a great purple cloth the trials of the Trojans and Achaeans (3.125–128), and Andromache is weaving a purple cloth in the inner part of the house when she hears the wailing for Hector (22.440–447). Hector sends her back to “the loom and the distaff” (6.490–491). The other simile of a woman's work is the honest working woman who holds the scales, with the weight on one side and the wool on the other, until they are equal (12.433–435; see [[the-working-womans-scales]]); it measures a battle evenly balanced, as this one measures a race that is nearly level. In the Odyssey Calypso, singing in her cave, goes to and fro before her loom and weaves with a golden shuttle (*Odyssey* 5.61–62).
