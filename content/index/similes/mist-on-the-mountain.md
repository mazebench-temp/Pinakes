## The simile

εὖτʼ ὄρεος κορυφῇσι Νότος κατέχευεν ὀμίχλην, / ποιμέσιν οὔ τι φίλην, κλέπτῃ δέ τε νυκτὸς ἀμείνω, / τόσσόν τίς τʼ ἐπιλεύσσει ὅσον τʼ ἐπὶ λᾶαν ἵησιν: “as when the South Wind pours mist over the peaks of a mountain, no friend to shepherds, but better than night for a thief, and a man can see only as far as he can throw a stone”. εὖτε usually means “when”; here, as at 19.386, it means “as”. Νότος is the South Wind (see [[notus]]), the wet wind that brings cloud. ὀμίχλη is mist or fog. κλέπτης, “thief”, occurs nowhere else in Homer. ἐπιλεύσσει is “sees over, sees as far as”.

The tenor: ὣς ἄρα τῶν ὑπὸ ποσσὶ κονίσαλος ὄρνυτʼ ἀελλὴς / ἐρχομένων, “so a whirling cloud of dust rose from under their feet as they came”. κονίσαλος is a cloud of dust (κόνις, “dust”), and ἀελλής, “whirling” (from ἄελλα, “squall”), is found only here. The point of likeness is thickness: the dust shuts out the view as the mist does. The middle lines look at the mist from the ground. It is bad for shepherds, who cannot see their flocks, and good for a thief, who cannot be seen. They give a touch of stealth and menace to an army that comes on without a word.

## In context

{{quote:649db162-3327-5321-b5d0-a457bae99885}}

The simile answers the cranes (see [[cranes-and-pygmies]]). The Trojans get a picture of sound; the Achaeans, who come on “in silence” (σιγῇ, 3.8), get a picture of sight, or of sight taken away. Both similes come from the weather. The cranes flee the winter and its endless rain; the mist comes on the South Wind. The cranes are heard in the open sky, crying as they go; the Achaeans are hidden in their own dust, and “very quickly they crossed the plain” (3.14).

Mist returns at the end of the duel. When Menelaus is about to kill Alexander, Aphrodite snatches him away “very easily, as a god can, and hid him in thick mist” (ἐκάλυψε δʼ ἄρʼ ἠέρι πολλῇ, 3.381; see [[aphrodite-rescues-paris]]). The book opens with an army hidden in its dust, and its duel ends with a god hiding one man in a cloud.

## Elsewhere in Homer

Dust as mist returns in a battle simile of Book 13. Squalls rush under shrill winds on a day when the dust lies thickest on the roads, “and they raise together a great mist of dust” (κονίης μεγάλην … ὀμίχλην), and so the fighting came together at the ships (13.334–337). κονίσαλος is used twice more, of the dust thrown up by horses' hooves that whitens the Achaeans like chaff on a threshing floor (5.499–505), and of the dust that rises around Hector's body as Achilles drags it behind his chariot (22.401). Clouds on the peaks give a different picture when the Danaans stand firm against the Trojans “like clouds that the son of Cronus has set in windless weather on the tops of the mountains” (5.522–523), and a cloud coming over the sea makes a goatherd shudder and drive his flock into a cave (4.275–279). In Book 1 Thetis rises from the gray sea “like a mist” (1.359; see [[like-a-mist]]).
