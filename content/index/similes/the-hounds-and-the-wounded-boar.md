## The simile

{{quote:03d61e4f-165a-5d58-9931-05af7f6090ef}}

Menelaus and Meriones take the body up from the ground and lift it “very high, with a great effort”, and the Trojan army shouts behind them, “when they saw the Achaeans lifting the corpse” (17.722–724). Then: ἴθυσαν δὲ κύνεσσιν ἐοικότες, οἵ τʼ ἐπὶ κάπρῳ / βλημένῳ ἀΐξωσι πρὸ κούρων θηρητήρων, “And they charged like hounds that at a wild boar / that has been hit dart ahead of the young hunters” (17.725–726). The dogs run in front of the hunters to a beast that the hunters have already hit, as Antilochus leaps on the fallen Melanippus in Book 15 like a dog that darts at a wounded fawn (βλημένῳ ἀΐξῃ, 15.579–582; see [[the-dog-and-the-fawn]]). ἕως μὲν γάρ τε θέουσι διαρραῖσαι μεμαῶτες, / ἀλλʼ ὅτε δή ῥʼ ἐν τοῖσιν ἑλίξεται ἀλκὶ πεποιθώς, / ἄψ τʼ ἀνεχώρησαν διά τʼ ἔτρεσαν ἄλλυδις ἄλλος, “for a while they run, eager to tear it apart, / but when it wheels around among them, trusting in its courage, / they draw back and scatter in fear, this way and that” (17.727–729). ἑλίξεται is an aorist subjunctive, “whenever it turns” (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.728); the hound in Book 8 watches for the boar or the lion “as it wheels” (ἑλισσόμενον, 8.340; see [[the-hound-at-the-heels]]). ἀλκὶ πεποιθώς, “trusting in its courage”, is said of the beasts that hold their ground, the lion and the boar (5.299, 13.471, 17.61). The dogs scatter ἄλλυδις ἄλλος, “this way and that”, as the Trojans scattered from Odysseus in Book 11 when Ajax came up like the lion among the jackals (11.486; see [[the-jackals-and-the-stag]]).

The tenor: ὣς Τρῶες εἷος μὲν ὁμιλαδὸν αἰὲν ἕποντο / νύσσοντες ξίφεσίν τε καὶ ἔγχεσιν ἀμφιγύοισιν· / ἀλλʼ ὅτε δή ῥʼ Αἴαντε μεταστρεφθέντε κατʼ αὐτοὺς / σταίησαν, τῶν δὲ τράπετο χρώς, “so the Trojans for a while kept following in a mass, / stabbing at them with swords and double-pointed spears; / but whenever the two Ajaxes turned round / and stood against them, their color changed” (17.730–733). The first two verses of the tenor were said in Book 15 of the Danaans, who pursued the Trojans in a mass until Hector appeared among them like a bearded lion in the hunters' path (15.277–278; Leaf on 17.730; see [[the-hunters-and-the-lion]]). A change of color is the mark of fear: in an ambush, Idomeneus says, “the coward's color changes this way and that”, and the brave man's does not (13.279, 13.284). One boar stands for the two Ajaxes, who turn together (see [[the-two-ajaxes]]), and the hounds are the Trojans, of whom “no one dared / to dart forward and fight for the body” (17.733–734).

The simile turns round a threat of the book. Hector meant to give the body of Patroclus to “the Trojan dogs”, and Menelaus and Zeus feared it (17.127, 17.255, 17.272–273; see [[dogs]]). Now the Trojans are themselves the dogs, and they cannot come near the body.

## In context

Ajax has given the plan: Menelaus and Meriones are to lift the body and carry it out of the toil, while the two Ajaxes, “with equal spirit, of one name”, fight off the Trojans and Hector behind them (17.715–721; see [[ajax]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). The lifting and the Trojans' rush follow (17.722–734). Then four more similes carry the book to its end: the war around the bearers is like a fire that falls on a city (17.735–741; see [[the-burning-city]]); the bearers labor like mules dragging a beam down a mountain (17.742–746; see [[mules-dragging-a-beam]]); the Ajaxes hold back the Trojans like a wooded ridge that holds back the floods (17.746–753; see [[the-wooded-ridge]]); and the young Achaeans flee before Aeneas and Hector like starlings and jackdaws before a falcon (17.753–761; see [[a-cloud-of-starlings]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Earlier in the book Ajax went through the Trojans “like a boar in courage”, a wild boar that scatters the dogs and young men in the mountains (17.281–285; see [[like-a-boar]]). Other boars stand against dogs and hunters: Odysseus, hemmed in, is like a boar that comes out of a thicket whetting its tusk while dogs and young men close in (11.414–420; see [[the-boar-at-bay]]); Hector turns among his enemies like a boar or a lion among hounds and hunting men (12.41–48; see [[the-boar-or-lion-at-bay]]); and Idomeneus waits for Aeneas like a boar in the mountains with its back bristling (13.471–475; see [[the-bristling-boar]]). A hunter sets his hounds on a wild boar or a lion as Hector sets the Trojans on the Achaeans (11.292–295; see [[the-hunter-and-his-hounds]]). See [[boar]].
