## The simile

{{quote:5ab87532-8e58-5cf7-8c7f-0c772e5808c3}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτε τοῖχον ἀνὴρ ἀράρῃ πυκινοῖσι λίθοισι / δώματος ὑψηλοῖο βίας ἀνέμων ἀλεείνων: “And as when a man fits the wall together with close-set stones / of a high house, keeping out the force of the winds” (16.212–213). τοῖχος is the wall of a house or a hall (9.219, 18.374, 24.598), not the τεῖχος of a city or a camp. ἀράρῃ is from ἀραρίσκω, “fit together”, the verb of the joiner and the builder; πυκινοί, “close-set”, are stones laid so near one another that no gap is left. βίας is the plural of βίη, “force”: the house is high, and the winds strike it.

The tenor takes up the builder's verb: ὣς ἄραρον κόρυθές τε καὶ ἀσπίδες ὀμφαλόεσσαι, “so closely were the helmets and bossed shields fitted together” (16.214; see [[helmet]], [[shield]] and [[bossed]]). The verse before the simile has it too, στίχες ἄρθεν, “the ranks closed up” (16.211), so that the same verb is said three times in four verses, of the ranks, of the wall and of the armor. Walter Leaf notes that ἄραρον is intransitive here, like ἄρθεν, and that setting it beside the transitive ἀράρῃ of the simile, though perhaps harsh, “emphasizes the simile” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.214). The point of likeness is closeness. A wall of close-set stones lets nothing through, and the ranks are packed as tight as the stones.

Three verses follow that are not part of the simile but finish its picture: ἀσπὶς ἄρʼ ἀσπίδʼ ἔρειδε, κόρυς κόρυν, ἀνέρα δʼ ἀνήρ, “Shield pressed on shield, helmet on helmet, man on man”, and the helmets with horsehair crests touched with their bright ridges when the men nodded, so close did they stand to one another (16.215–217; see [[horsehair-crested]] and [[neuo]]). Each thing is set against its like (see [[polyptoton]]). The three verses are those of Book 13, where the best of the Achaeans stood so around the two Ajaxes to await Hector (13.130–133; see [[battle-order]]). There they come without a simile; here the builder's wall comes first and the close ranks are its stones.

## In context

Achilles has armed the Myrmidons and set them in order under their five leaders (16.155–199; see [[myrmidons]]). He reminds them of the threats they made all through his wrath, when they called him hard and pitiless and wanted to sail home: “now the great work of the din of battle has appeared, which you loved before” (16.200–209). The ranks close up at their king's voice, and the simile follows. In front of them all two men are arming, Patroclus and Automedon, “with one heart” (16.218–220; see [[patroclus]] and [[automedon]]).

The Myrmidons' going out is told in two similes. The first is of a wall built to stand against the winds; the second, when they reach the Trojans, is of wasps that pour out of their nest by the road and fly at any passerby who stirs them (16.259–267; see [[wasps-at-the-roadside]]). The one is the army drawn up, the other the army let loose.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The second verse of the simile returns in the funeral games, for Ajax and Odysseus gripping each other in the wrestling, like the crossing rafters that a famous builder fits together (ἤραρε) for a high house, keeping out the force of the winds (23.712–713). Armies are fitted together like joinery: the allies under Sarpedon locked themselves together with their oxhide shields and went straight at the Danaans (ἄραρον, 12.105–106), and the Danaans held against Hector “fitted together in close order like a tower” (πυργηδὸν ἀρηρότες, 15.618; see [[the-rock-by-the-sea]]). In the Odyssey Odysseus built his bedchamber around the olive trunk “with close-set stones” (πυκνῇσιν λιθάδεσσι, *Odyssey* 23.192–193).

## Outside Homer

Virgil gives the clash of the Trojan and Latin lines the close ranks of the verse that follows the simile (16.215 = 13.131): *haeret pede pes densusque viro vir*, “foot clings to foot, and man close to man” (*Aeneid* 10.361; see the Book 13 note on [[virgil]]).
