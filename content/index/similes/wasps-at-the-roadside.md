## The simile

{{quote:c73de222-c52e-5558-b6e6-df3c72f06b95}}

Asius speaks the simile, and it is his excuse. Zeus, he says, has become a lover of lies, for Asius did not think that the Achaeans would hold back the might of the Trojans (12.164–166; see [[lover-of-lies]]). Then: οἳ δʼ, ὥς τε σφῆκες μέσον αἰόλοι ἠὲ μέλισσαι / οἰκία ποιήσωνται ὁδῷ ἔπι παιπαλοέσσῃ, “But they, like wasps with nimble waists, or bees, that have made their homes beside a rocky road” (12.167–168). σφῆκες are wasps and μέλισσαι bees (see [[wasps]] and [[bees]]); the speaker offers both and does not choose, as the narrator offered a boar or a lion for Hector (12.42). μέσον αἰόλοι, “quick at the middle”, is the wasp's narrow, supple waist, or its banded body, since αἰόλος is both quick-moving and shifting in color (see the translation's note on 12.167). The same adjective is given forty lines later to the “writhing” snake of the portent (12.208). παιπαλόεις, “rugged, rocky”, is usually said of mountains and islands. οὐδʼ ἀπολείπουσιν κοῖλον δόμον, ἀλλὰ μένοντες / ἄνδρας θηρητῆρας ἀμύνονται περὶ τέκνων: “and do not leave their hollow house, but stand their ground and fight off the hunting men for the sake of their young” (12.169–170).

The tenor: “so these men, though they are only two, will not give way from the gates until they have either killed or been taken” (12.171–172). A swarm is many, and the Lapiths are two (δύʼ ἐόντε, a dual), so the likeness is not of number. It is of a home defended to the end. The nest of the simile is by a road, and the gate the two hold is the one by which the Achaeans came back from the plain with their horses and chariots (12.118–120).

## In context

Asius drove his chariot for the open gate, and the Lapiths met him like oaks rooted in the mountains (12.110–136; see [[asius]] and [[oaks-on-the-mountains]]). When they come out and fight like wild boars, and stones fall like snow from the towers, he groans, strikes both his thighs, and complains (12.141–163; see [[wild-boars-in-the-mountains]] and [[a-snow-of-stones]]). The narrator answers him at once: “he did not persuade the mind of Zeus by saying this; for his heart wished to hold out glory to Hector” (12.173–174). Asius is wrong about Zeus and right about the Lapiths. They hold the gate and bring down eight Trojans (12.182–194; see [[polypoetes]] and [[leonteus]]). The narrator foretold Asius' own death before he reached the gate (12.113–117).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Wasps return when the Myrmidons pour out to battle behind Patroclus, σφήκεσσιν ἐοικότες … εἰνοδίοις, like wasps by the road that boys keep teasing where they live beside the way, making them a common bane to many; if a traveler disturbs them unawares, they fly out with valiant hearts, every one, and fight for their young (16.259–267). The two similes share the road, the nest and the defense of the young. In Book 16 the swarm comes out and attacks; in Book 12 it stays at home and holds.

Bees are the image of number in Book 2, where the army streams to the assembly like swarms of bees pouring out of a hollow rock (2.87–93; see [[swarming-bees]]).
