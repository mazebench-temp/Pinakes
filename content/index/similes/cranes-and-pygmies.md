## The simile

Τρῶες μὲν κλαγγῇ τʼ ἐνοπῇ τʼ ἴσαν ὄρνιθες ὣς / ἠΰτε περ κλαγγὴ γεράνων πέλει οὐρανόθι πρό: “the Trojans came on with clamor and with cries, like birds, as the clamor of cranes rises under the face of heaven”. κλαγγή is a harsh, ringing cry, the cry of birds and also the clang of Apollo's bow (1.49); ἐνοπή is the shouting of men. οὐρανόθι πρό, “before the sky”, occurs only here. The cranes (γέρανοι; see [[cranes]]) have fled “the winter and its endless rain” (ἀθέσφατον ὄμβρον), and they fly with clamor to the streams of [[oceanus|Oceanus]] at the edge of the world, ἀνδράσι Πυγμαίοισι φόνον καὶ κῆρα φέρουσαι, “bringing slaughter and doom to the Pygmy men”. The [[pygmies|Pygmies]] take their name from πυγμή, the “fist”, which was also a measure, the length from the elbow to the knuckles: men of that height. ἠέριαι, rendered “at dawn”, can also be taken as “through the air”. Either way the birds “bring on their evil strife” (κακὴν ἔριδα προφέρονται; see [[eris]]).

κλαγγή is said three times in four lines (3.2, 3.3, 3.5). The tenor is the noise of the Trojan advance; the vehicle, a migrating flock whose cry fills the sky. Then the simile follows the birds to the end of the earth and to their war, so that the noise carries a threat: slaughter at the end of the flight. Big birds fall on small men, and the Trojans are the big birds.

## In context

{{quote:5676e478-163f-51ba-8f88-54e6329cc2fe}}

The armies meet for the first time in the poem. Book 2 marshaled them and named their leaders; now the companies set in order (κόσμηθεν, 3.1) advance across the plain. The simile of the Trojans is set against a plain statement of two lines: “But the Achaeans came on in silence, breathing fury, eager in their hearts to stand by one another” (3.8–9; see [[silence]] and [[breathing-fury]]). The contrast is of sound. The Trojans shout like birds, and the Achaeans say nothing. The next simile gives the Achaeans a picture of their own, the dust of their march like a mountain mist (see [[mist-on-the-mountain]]).

The contrast returns when the armies meet again after the truce is broken. The Danaan companies move to war with each leader giving orders and the rest going in silence, for fear of their commanders; the Trojans are like the sheep of a rich man bleating in his fold, for their cry is not one and their tongues are mixed, “the men were called from many lands” (4.427–438; see [[waves-on-the-shore]] and [[sheep-in-the-fold]]). Iris had told Hector that the allies of Troy speak many languages (2.803–804; see [[trojan-allies]]).

The cranes' war is told in the words of the Achaeans' own. “Bringing slaughter and doom” (φόνον καὶ κῆρα φέρουσαι) is how Nestor described the Argives sailing for Troy, “bringing slaughter and doom to the Trojans” (2.352), and the Odyssey uses the same words of the Achaeans in the Wooden Horse (Odyssey 4.273, 8.513). Here the phrase is given to the Trojans' side of the comparison.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Pygmies are named only here in Homer. Cranes appear in one other line, “of geese or cranes or long-necked swans”, used for the flocks of the Asian meadow and for the birds that an eagle swoops on by a river (2.460, 15.692; see [[birds-on-the-asian-meadow]]). The same word for the cry returns in the underworld, where the clamor of the dead around Heracles is “like birds” (κλαγγὴ νεκύων … οἰωνῶν ὥς, Odyssey 11.605).

## Outside Homer

Strabo defends the geography of the simile. Cranes fly south along the whole southern coast of Oceanus, he argues, so the Pygmies of the story must be imagined anywhere along it; if later writers placed them only near Egypt, that has nothing to do with the poet (*Geography* 1.2.28). Elsewhere he suggests that the Pygmies were imagined from the small stature of some Ethiopian peoples, “for no trustworthy man says he has seen them” (17.2.1). Aristotle took the war as fact: the cranes fly from Scythia to the marshes above Egypt where the Nile rises, and there the Pygmies live (*History of Animals* 8.12, 597a4–9; see [[aristotle-pygmies]]). The battle of the Pygmies and the cranes is painted on the foot of the François Vase, an Athenian vase of about 570 BCE.

Plato's Socrates approved the silent Achaeans of the other half of the contrast as a lesson in self-control (see [[plato-silent-achaeans]]).
