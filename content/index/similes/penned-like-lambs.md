## The simile

{{quote:97dcf488-4c4d-5e4d-b4ec-a19b95799549}}

καί νύ κε σήκασθεν κατὰ Ἴλιον ἠΰτε ἄρνες: “and they would have been penned up in Ilios like lambs”. σήκασθεν is the aorist passive of σηκάζω, “shut in a pen”, from σηκός, the pen or fold of a farmstead. The verb occurs only here in Homer. The noun names the folds on the shield of Achilles (18.589) and the pens of the Cyclops, crowded with lambs and kids, each kind kept apart by age (*Odyssey* 9.219–222). ἄρνες are lambs, the young of the flock (see [[lambs]]). ἠΰτε, “as, like”, makes a short comparison without a scene, as it does for Ajax's shield “like a tower” (7.219; see [[like-a-tower]]).

The likeness is carried as much by the verb as by the animal. A pen is where the weakest of a farm's creatures are shut in, safe and trapped at once, and a city under siege is the same: its people are safe for the moment behind the walls and cannot come out. The subject is the Trojans, who that morning poured out through all the gates of the city, foot soldiers and horsemen (8.58–59).

The line describes what did not happen. It is the second half of a sentence that the narrator turns back: “Then there would have been ruin, and deeds past mending would have been done, and they would have been penned up in Ilios like lambs, had not the father of men and gods been quick to see it” (see [[would-have-had-not]]). The god who turns it is Zeus. He thunders and sends a gleaming thunderbolt into the ground before Diomedes' horses; a flame of burning sulfur rises, the horses cower under the chariot, and the reins slip from Nestor's hands (8.133–137; see [[zeus]] and [[thunderbolt]]).

## In context

The simile marks the high point of the Achaean day. At midday the scales of Zeus have sunk against the Achaeans, his thunder has put their leaders to flight, and only Nestor is left on the field, his trace horse shot by Paris (8.68–86; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). Diomedes takes the old man into his own chariot, behind the horses he captured from Aeneas, and drives at Hector. His spear misses Hector and kills the charioteer Eniopeus, and Hector has to find another driver (8.99–129; see [[diomedes]], [[nestor]], [[eniopeus]] and [[archeptolemus]]). For a moment one man and one chariot are turning the battle.

To be penned up in Ilios was the Trojans' state while Achilles fought. Hera, in the likeness of Stentor, reminded the Achaeans in Book 5 that the Trojans never came out before the Dardanian gates while Achilles went to war (5.788–791), and Achilles himself says that Hector would not then rouse battle far from the wall (9.352–355). Diomedes almost brings that back without Achilles. Zeus, who has promised Thetis to give the Trojans the upper hand until the Achaeans honor her son, will not allow it (see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[the-second-day-of-battle]]). Nestor reads the thunderbolt at once: “Do you not see that no help from Zeus goes with you?” (8.140).

By evening the likeness has turned around. It is the Achaeans who are shut in: the space between their ditch and their wall is filled with horses and men “hemmed in” by Hector (εἰλομένων, 8.213–216), and at nightfall they are in their camp while the Trojans sit out all night on the plain around their fires (8.553–565; see [[stars-around-the-moon]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Lambs in Homer's similes are victims. Wolves fall on lambs or kids that have strayed from the flock through a shepherd's carelessness (16.352–355), and an eagle swoops through the clouds to the plain to seize a lamb or a hare (22.308–310; see [[eagle]]). Refusing Hector any pact, Achilles says that there are no trusty oaths between lions and men, “nor do wolves and lambs have hearts in accord” (22.262–263).
