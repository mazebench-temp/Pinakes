## The simile

{{quote:b4537eeb-5489-5604-87e0-fcf1fbe3ceba}}

Ἀντίλοχος δʼ ἐπόρουσε κύων ὥς, ὅς τʼ ἐπὶ νεβρῷ / βλημένῳ ἀΐξῃ, τόν τʼ ἐξ εὐνῆφι θορόντα / θηρητὴρ ἐτύχησε βαλών, ὑπέλυσε δὲ γυῖα: “And Antilochus sprang at him like a dog that darts at a wounded fawn, which, as it leapt from its lair, a hunter has hit with his shot, and loosened its limbs” (15.579–581). νεβρός is a fawn, the young of the deer (see [[deer]]), and βλημένος “hit, wounded”. εὐνή, a bed, is here an animal's lair, with the old ending -φι. The θηρητήρ is a hunter, and ὑπέλυσε δὲ γυῖα, “loosened its limbs”, is said of the beast in the words used of men struck down in battle (see [[loosened-his-limbs]]).

The tenor turns to the dying man: ὣς ἐπὶ σοὶ Μελάνιππε θόρʼ Ἀντίλοχος μενεχάρμης / τεύχεα συλήσων, “so at you, Melanippus, Antilochus, steadfast in battle, leapt to strip your armor” (15.582–583). The narrator speaks to Melanippus in the second person as he falls, as he spoke to Apollo when the wall came down (15.365; see [[apostrophe]]).

The simile divides one man's work between two figures. The hunter's shot has already brought the fawn down, and the dog runs in on the fallen beast; Antilochus threw the spear that struck Melanippus in the chest beside the nipple (15.573–578), and now he runs in to take the armor. The dog does not kill; it is quick and it seizes. The point is the speed of the rush on the body, the rush that strips the dead (see [[stripping-the-dead]]).

## In context

After Hector has rebuked Melanippus for holding back while their cousin Dolops lies dead (15.545–559), Menelaus urges Antilochus on: “no other of the Achaeans is younger than you, nor swifter of foot, nor as valiant as you in fighting” (15.568–571; see [[antilochus]] and [[menelaus]]). Antilochus leaps out from the champions, glancing around him, and the Trojans shrink back as he throws; the spear strikes Melanippus, son of Hicetaon, as he is going into the war (15.573–578; see [[melanippus-son-of-hicetaon]]). The simile follows. But Antilochus “did not escape brilliant Hector's notice, who came running through the combat to meet him” (15.583–584), and the dog of this simile becomes, three verses later, a beast that has killed a dog and runs before the men can gather (15.585–591; see [[the-beast-that-has-done-harm]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Dogs and fawns meet again in two chases. Diomedes and Odysseus press Dolon toward the ships as two sharp-toothed hounds press a fawn or a hare through wooded country (10.360–364; see [[two-hounds]]). Achilles pursues Hector around the walls of Troy as a dog in the mountains, having roused a fawn from its lair, chases it through glens and valleys and tracks it down when it hides under a thicket; so Hector does not escape the son of Peleus (ὣς Ἕκτωρ οὐ λῆθε, 22.189–193). Here it is the dog that does not escape Hector (οὐ λάθεν Ἕκτορα, 15.583). The fawn is the helpless young of the deer in the Iliad: the eagle of Zeus drops one by the altar (8.247–250), and Agamemnon asks his men why they stand dazed like fawns (4.243; see [[like-fawns]]).
