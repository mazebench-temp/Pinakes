## The simile

Two short comparisons, a hundred lines apart. The first is the robe that Hecuba takes from her storeroom for Athena:

{{quote:28c616d6-599e-5f33-90c4-6e5b3338e815}}

ἀστὴρ δʼ ὣς ἀπέλαμπεν: “and it shone like a star”. ὣς placed after its noun is the short form of comparison, as in αἴγειρος ὣς, “like a poplar” (4.482; see [[the-poplar]]). ἀπολάμπω is “shine out, gleam from afar”. The robe is the most beautiful of all in its embroidery (ποικίλμασιν) and the largest, and it “lay beneath all the others” (ἔκειτο δὲ νείατος ἄλλων), at the bottom of the store. The robes are the work of Sidonian women whom Alexander brought home from Sidon (6.289–292; see [[sidonians]]).

The second is Hector's son, carried by the handmaid who comes with Andromache to the Scaean gates:

{{quote:915a8bbc-80e3-5a16-9ff0-d4082a5abbf6}}

παῖδʼ ἐπὶ κόλπῳ ἔχουσʼ ἀταλάφρονα νήπιον αὔτως, / Ἑκτορίδην ἀγαπητὸν ἀλίγκιον ἀστέρι καλῷ: “holding the child at her breast, an infant of tender mind, quite small, the beloved son of Hector, like a lovely star”. ἀλίγκιος is “like” (also in the fuller form ἐναλίγκιος, as in the star of late summer, 5.5). ἀγαπητός, “beloved”, is the Odyssey's word for Telemachus, an only son (μοῦνος ἐὼν ἀγαπητός, *Odyssey* 2.365; 4.727, 4.817, 5.18). Ἑκτορίδης, “son of Hector”, a patronymic for a baby, is found only here, and so is ἀταλάφρων, “tender-minded” (see [[tender-hearted]]). The child is named in the next two lines, twice: Scamandrius to his father, Astyanax to everyone else (see [[astyanax]]).

The point of both comparisons is brightness and beauty. The robe is the finest treasure of the queen's store, and the child is the most precious thing in Hector's house.

## In context

Both are held out to the gods. The robe is carried to the temple of Athena on the citadel, laid on the goddess's knees by the priestess Theano, and offered with a prayer that Diomedes may fall before the Scaean gates; “but Pallas Athena shook her head” (6.297–311; see [[the-robe-for-athena]]). The child is kissed and lifted in his father's arms while Hector prays that he may rule Ilios and come back from war with bloody spoils, and that someone may say of him, “He is far better than his father” (6.474–481; see [[hector-and-andromache]]). The narrator reports Athena's refusal at once and says nothing of Hector's prayer. Andromache's lament in Book 24 says what will become of the child: some Achaean will take him by the arm and throw him from the tower (24.734–736).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The line of the robe is used again in the Odyssey. At Sparta, Helen goes to the chests where her robes lie, which she made herself, and takes out the most beautiful in its embroidery and the largest, which “shone like a star, and lay beneath all the others” (*Odyssey* 15.104–108 ~ 6.293–295). She gives it to Telemachus for his bride to wear on her wedding day, “a keepsake of Helen's hands” (*Odyssey* 15.125–128). There a woman's robe is a gift made and received with joy.

Elsewhere in the Iliad stars are the flash of armor and of dangerous men. Athena comes down like a shooting star (4.75–78; see [[shooting-star]]). A fire like the star of late summer blazes from Diomedes' helmet and shield (5.4–7; see [[the-autumn-star]]). Hector moves among the ranks like a baneful star that shines out of the clouds and hides again (11.62–65). Achilles' helmet “shone like a star” (ἀστὴρ ὣς ἀπέλαμπεν, 19.381); Priam sees Achilles coming across the plain like the star of late summer that men call Orion's Dog, bright and an evil sign (22.26–31); and the point of the spear with which Achilles kills Hector gleams like the evening star, “the most beautiful star that stands in the sky” (22.317–319). The two stars of Book 6 are a robe and a child. The last star of Hector's life is the gleam on the point of his enemy's spear.
