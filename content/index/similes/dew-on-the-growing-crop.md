## The simile

{{quote:1c6a3249-63f2-5fb1-b4ca-ff154dffcad0}}

τοῖο δὲ θυμὸς / ἰάνθη ὡς εἴ τε περὶ σταχύεσσιν ἐέρση / ληΐου ἀλδήσκοντος, ὅτε φρίσσουσιν ἄρουραι: “and his heart / was warmed, as when there is dew around the ears / of a growing crop, when the fields are bristling” (23.597–599). ἰαίνω is to warm, and of the heart to cheer: Hera, angry with Zeus, laughed with her lips, “but her forehead above her dark brows / was not warmed” (15.102–103), and Odysseus tells Achilles that Agamemnon will bring his gifts into the assembly for all to see, “and you may be warmed in your heart” (19.172–174). The σταχύες are the ears of grain, which the West Wind bowed down in the simile of the assembly in *Iliad* 2 (2.147–148). λήϊον is the standing crop, and ἀλδήσκω, “grow”, occurs only here in Homer. φρίσσω, “bristle”, is said of a field of ears standing up like spears, as a battle “bristled with the spears” (13.339) and the sitting ranks of both armies bristled with shields and helmets and spears like the sea under the ripple of the West Wind (7.61–62; see [[the-ripple-of-the-west-wind]]).

The case of ἐέρση, “dew”, is disputed. The text prints the nominative. Walter Leaf reads the dative with the manuscript T, and takes the sense to be “as the heart of the growing corn is gladdened with the dew upon the ears”; the nominative, he argues, gives no good sense, since it is the dew that comforts the corn and not the corn the dew. He rejects Heyne's view that ἰάνθη means “melted”, “as the dew on the corn melts”, since ἰαίνω means only “warm” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.598; see the translation's note on 23.598). The translation, “as when there is dew around the ears”, gives the picture without deciding the grammar.

The tenor turns to Menelaus himself: ὣς ἄρα σοὶ Μενέλαε μετὰ φρεσὶ θυμὸς ἰάνθη, “so, Menelaus, was the spirit in your heart warmed” (23.600). The narrator addresses him in the second person (see [[apostrophe]]), as he did in the tenors of two earlier similes for Menelaus: “so, Menelaus, your thighs were stained with blood” (4.146; see [[ivory-stained-with-purple]]), and “So then, Menelaus, nurtured by Zeus, your shining eyes / roved everywhere”, looking for Antilochus, as an eagle looks for a hare (17.679–681; see [[the-eagle-and-the-hare]]). In *Iliad* 17 he searched for the son of Nestor to send him to Achilles with the news; here the same young man has cheated him, and he forgives him. The point of likeness is relief. Menelaus stood up “grieving in his heart, / angry without end at Antilochus” (23.566–567), and the young man's yielding comes to his anger as dew to a crop that is still growing: the heart revives, and the field that bristles with its ears is the picture of a heart lifted up. It is a simile of farming and of peace, given to the most peaceable scene of the games.

## In context

Menelaus has accused Antilochus of cheating him with his worse horses and has asked him to swear by Poseidon that he did not hamper his chariot by a trick (23.566–585). Antilochus does not take the oath: “Bear with me now; for I am much younger / than you, lord Menelaus, and you are the elder and the better. / You know what the transgressions of a young man are like; / for his mind is quicker, and his counsel thin” (23.587–590). He gives up the mare and offers more from his own store rather than fall from Menelaus' favor, and he leads the mare and puts her in his hands (23.591–597). The simile follows, and then Menelaus' answer: “Antilochus, now I myself will give way to you, / for all my anger, since you were never flighty or light of mind / before” (23.602–604). He gives the mare back, so that the others may know “that my heart is never overbearing and harsh” (23.609–611), and takes the cauldron, the third prize (23.613; see [[menelaus]], [[antilochus]] and [[amends-and-reconciliation]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The fields of grain in the Iliad's similes belong to peace. The West Wind stirs a deep field, and it bows down with its ears, as the assembly stirs and rushes to the ships (2.147–150; see [[wind-in-the-wheat]]); the reapers cut their swaths through a rich man's wheat or barley as the armies cut each other down (11.67–71; see [[the-reapers]]). Dew falls twice from the sky: Zeus sends down dewdrops wet with blood before a day of slaughter (11.53–54), and glistening drops of dew fall from the golden cloud that covers him and Hera on Ida (14.350–351). In the Odyssey the heart of Menelaus is warmed again, though he is grieving, when Proteus tells him that he may yet find Aegisthus alive or come in time for his funeral (ἰάνθη, *Odyssey* 4.543–549).

## Outside Homer

Leaf notes that [[aeschylus|Aeschylus]] gives the idea of the passage almost exactly (on 23.598). Clytemnestra, standing over the husband she has killed, says that he struck her “with a dark shower of bloody dew” (ἐρεμνῇ ψακάδι φοινίας δρόσου), and that she rejoiced no less than the sown field rejoices in the gladness given by Zeus when the bud is born (χαίρουσαν οὐδὲν ἧσσον ἢ διοσδότῳ / γάνει σπορητὸς κάλυκος ἐν λοχεύμασιν, *Agamemnon* 1388–1392). The dew of the Iliad's reconciliation has become blood. Leaf also notes that [[apollonius|Apollonius of Rhodes]] seems to have read the nominative, and imitates the simile where the heart can melt (on 23.598): when Jason has praised her, Medea “was warmed in her heart within, melting, as the dew melts around roses, warmed by the morning light” (ἰαίνετο δὲ φρένας εἴσω / τηκομένη, οἷόν τε περὶ ῥοδέῃσιν ἐέρση / τήκεται ἠῴοισιν ἰαινομένη φαέεσσιν, *Argonautica* 3.1019–1021).
