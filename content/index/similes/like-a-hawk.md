## The simile

{{quote:63326c3b-a313-5e7e-8ee9-c4422ec03bbc}}

βῆ δὲ κατʼ Ἰδαίων ὀρέων ἴρηκι ἐοικὼς / ὠκέϊ φασσοφόνῳ, ὅς τʼ ὤκιστος πετεηνῶν: “and he went down from the mountains of Ida like a hawk, a swift dove-killer, which is the swiftest of winged birds” (15.237–238). ἴρηξ is the epic form of ἱέραξ, the hawk or falcon (see [[hawk]]). φασσοφόνος, “dove-killer”, from φάσσα, the wood pigeon, and φόνος, “slaughter”, is found only here. ὤκιστος πετεηνῶν, “the swiftest of winged birds”, is said also of the eagle, the strongest and swiftest of birds, whose swoop Achilles has when he springs away from the river (21.252–253), and the κίρκος, another hawk, is ἐλαφρότατος πετεηνῶν, the lightest of winged birds (22.139; *Odyssey* 13.87). The first half-verse is the one that took Iris down from Ida to Ilios (15.169).

The comparison gives two things. The first is speed: the hawk is the swiftest of birds, and the god has the whole distance from Ida to the plain to cover. The second is in the epithet. The dove is the bird that flees, and the hawk is its killer; Artemis flees from Hera like a dove into a hollow rock (21.493–495; see [[the-dove-and-the-hawk]]), and Achilles swoops on Hector like a falcon in the mountains on a dove (22.139–142; see [[the-falcon-and-the-dove]]). The god who goes down like a hawk goes down to put the Achaeans to flight. Zeus has told him to shake the tasseled aegis over them and drive them back to the ships and the Hellespont (15.229–233), and on the plain Apollo shakes it, and they flee like a herd scattered by two beasts in the night (15.320–327; see [[two-beasts-in-the-night]]).

In the Odyssey Apollo's bird is the falcon: as Telemachus lands on Ithaca a κίρκος flies by on the right with a dove in its talons, “Apollo's swift messenger” (κίρκος, Ἀπόλλωνος ταχὺς ἄγγελος, *Odyssey* 15.525–527; see [[falcon]]).

## In context

Poseidon has gone back into the sea, and Zeus sends Apollo to Hector, who has lain gasping on the plain since Ajax's stone struck him (15.220–235; 14.409–439; 15.9–11). Apollo is to take the aegis, rout the Achaeans and rouse great might in Hector “until the Achaeans in their flight reach the ships and the Hellespont” (15.232–233). He goes down like a hawk and finds Hector “sitting up, no longer lying down, and he was just gathering his spirit again”, since the mind of Zeus had roused him (15.239–242). Hector does not know him and asks who among the gods he is. Apollo names himself, “Phoebus Apollo of the golden sword” (15.256), and breathes great might into him, and Hector runs back to the battle like a stallion that has broken its halter (15.243–270; see [[apollo]], [[hector]] and [[the-stallion]]).

The simile is the last of three for the journeys of the gods in this part of the book. Hera flies to Olympus as swiftly as a traveler's thought (15.80–83; see [[the-travelers-thought]]), and Iris goes down to Poseidon like snow or hail before the North Wind (15.170–172; see [[snow-and-hail]]). Apollo goes like a hunting bird.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The hawk is the gods' likeness for speed. Poseidon leaves the two Ajaxes like a swift-winged hawk that rises from a sheer rock to chase another bird across the plain (13.62–65; see [[the-hawk-from-the-rock]]); Thetis leaps down from snowy Olympus like a hawk, carrying the armor Hephaestus has made (18.616–617); Athena springs down from the sky through the upper air like a long-winged, shrill-voiced bird of prey, the ἅρπη (19.350–351; see [[like-a-bird-of-prey]]). For mortals it is the speed of the charge. Patroclus darts through the champions like a swift hawk that scatters jackdaws and starlings, in the words of this simile (ἴρηκι ἐοικὼς / ὠκέϊ, 16.582–583). Ajax tells Hector that the time is near when he will pray for horses “swifter than hawks” (13.819). Gods who sit like birds are another matter (7.58–61; see [[like-vultures]]).
