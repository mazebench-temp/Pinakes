## The simile

{{quote:88366296-b5ac-5397-bcfb-ecbe9c82eab1}}

Most of the man's work is named in words found nowhere else in Homer. He is ὀχετηγός, “leading a channel”, from ὀχετός, a watercourse, and ἄγω, “lead”; he holds a μάκελλα, “a mattock”, and throws the ἔχματα, the “obstructions”, out of the ἀμάρη, the “channel”; the ψηφῖδες, “pebbles”, roll along beneath the water, and it runs down χώρῳ ἔνι προαλεῖ, “on a sloping place”, a word that, as Walter Leaf notes, does not recur before Apollonius (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.262). The water comes from a κρήνη μελάνυδρος, “a spring of dark water”, the spring of the wolves that lap the dark water (16.160) and of the weeping simile (9.14; see [[the-spring-of-dark-water]] and [[of-dark-water]]), and it runs κελαρύζει, “murmuring”, the verb of the dark blood that gushes from Eurypylus' wound (11.813). φθάνει δέ τε καὶ τὸν ἄγοντα, “and outruns even the man who leads it”, has the verb with which Ate outruns the Prayers over all the earth (9.506; see [[litai]]).

Leaf notes that the practice of irrigation is not alluded to elsewhere in Homer, unless at the garden of Alcinous in the *Odyssey*, where of two springs “one spreads through the whole garden” (*Odyssey* 7.129–130), and calls the simile particularly vivid and striking (on 21.257; see [[alcinous]]). The only other garden of the Iliad is the poppy's, heavy with its seed and the showers of spring (8.306–307; see [[the-poppy]]).

The comparison turns on the water's speed. The man leads the water and governs it, but on the slope it runs ahead of him; so the wave of the river keeps overtaking Achilles, “nimble though he was” (21.264). The picture is of the gentlest water a farmer knows, water he has made to serve him, set beside a river in flood that is itself a god; and the narrator draws the moral in his own voice, θεοὶ δέ τε φέρτεροι ἀνδρῶν, “for the gods are stronger than men” (21.264; see [[gods-and-mortals]]).

## In context

The river has risen against Achilles, and he has fled over the plain with the swoop of an eagle (21.233–256; see [[like-an-eagle]] and [[achilles-and-the-river]]). After the simile, as often as he turns to stand against it, the great wave beats on his shoulders and the current eats away the ground from under his feet, until he cries out to Zeus (21.265–283; see [[scamander]]). The farmer's water returns at the end of the fire, in the opposite simile: a newly watered orchard that the North Wind of late summer dries, to the joy of the man who tends it (21.346–349; see [[the-north-wind-dries-the-orchard]]).
