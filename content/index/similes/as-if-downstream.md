## The simile

{{quote:bd403d18-85f3-5832-8674-339f3c930339}}

ἐπλέομεν Βορέῃ ἀνέμῳ ἀκραέϊ καλῷ / ῥηϊδίως, ὡς εἴ τε κατὰ ῥόον: “we sailed with a fresh and fair North Wind, / easily, as if downstream” (*Odyssey* 14.253–254; see [[boreas]]). κατὰ ῥόον is “with the current”, “down the stream”. Elsewhere in Homer it is the stream itself: Achilles cuts the young men down “along the stream” of the Scamander (21.147), the great wave carries Odysseus' raft “with the current this way and that” (*Odyssey* 5.327) and carries Ino's veil back down the river of Scheria (*Odyssey* 5.461), and the oars of his frightened men splash down “in the current” as they come near the strait (*Odyssey* 12.204). Here there is no river: the open sea is likened to one, and the wind drives the ships as a stream carries a boat that has only to be steered. ἀκραής, “fresh”, seems to mean “blowing at its height”, with the notion of rightness or perfection, a metaphorical use of ἄκρος with no other example in Homer (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 14.253; the translation's row ἀκραής; see [[the-winds]]). The same wind, Βορέῃ ἀνέμῳ ἀκραέϊ καλῷ, carries the Phoenician's ship past Crete before Zeus wrecks it (*Odyssey* 14.299).

The likeness is of ease and of safety. The next verses spell it out: “and not one of my / ships came to harm, but unharmed and free of sickness / we sat, and the wind and the helmsmen steered the ships” (*Odyssey* 14.254–256). ἀσκηθέες καὶ ἄνουσοι, “unharmed and free of sickness”, is said only here, and the men “sat” as passengers; “the wind and the helmsmen steered” is the verse of Odysseus' own voyages, after the storm that followed the raid on the Cicones and twice from Circe's island (*Odyssey* 9.78, 11.10, 12.152). “On the fifth day we came to the fair-flowing Aegyptus” (*Odyssey* 14.257; see [[aegyptus-river]]).

## In context

The Cretan of the tale had stayed only a month at home after Troy, until “my heart bade me sail to Egypt”; he fitted out nine ships, and for six days gave his companions victims to sacrifice and feast on (*Odyssey* 14.243–251; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]] and [[sacrifice]]). The easy voyage is followed by disaster. In the river Aegyptus his men, “giving way to arrogance”, lay waste the fields of the Egyptians, the army comes out at dawn, Zeus sends panic among them, and the Cretan saves himself only by clasping the knees of the king (*Odyssey* 14.258–284; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]] and [[horses]]). The voyage out is the easiest of the tale, and the voyage away from Egypt, on the Phoenician's ship, ends in the wreck (*Odyssey* 14.299–315; see [[like-sea-crows]]). When the beggar tells the raid again to Antinous, the voyage has become “a long way”: Zeus sent him off with far-wandering pirates “to go to Egypt, a long way, so that I might be destroyed” (*Odyssey* 17.424–426, a plain rendering), and the verses of the raid follow as in Book 14 (*Odyssey* 17.427–441 = 14.258–272).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Fair voyages are short in Homer and need few words. Nestor sailed home from Troy to Pylos, “and the fair wind never / died down, once the god had first sent it forth to blow” (*Odyssey* 3.182–183), and Athena sent Telemachus “a following wind, / a fresh West Wind, sounding over the wine-dark sea” (*Odyssey* 2.420–421). The Phaeacian ship that takes Odysseus home runs like a team of stallions under the whip (*Odyssey* 13.81–88; see [[the-four-stallions]]). The likeness of this voyage to a river's current is found only in the [[the-tale-to-eumaeus|Cretan's tale]].

## Outside Homer

Strabo gives the sail from Samonium, the eastern cape of Crete, to Egypt as four days and nights, or three by other reckonings (*Geography* 10.4.5): the Cretan's five days with a fair North Wind are of the same order.
