## The simile

{{quote:a0ccc06e-c191-5412-a540-ebc43793c267}}

ὡς ὁπότε νέφεα Ζέφυρος στυφελίξῃ / ἀργεστᾶο Νότοιο βαθείῃ λαίλαπι τύπτων: “as when the West Wind batters the clouds of the brightening South Wind, striking with a deep squall”. στυφελίζω is “batter, buffet”. Two winds meet in the simile. The South Wind, Νότος, has brought the clouds, and the West Wind, Ζέφυρος, drives into them with a λαῖλαψ, a squall of wind and rain (see [[zephyrus]] and [[notus]]). ἀργεστής, said of the South Wind here and at 21.334, is taken as “clearing, brightening” or as “white”; Hesiod gives it to the West Wind (*Theogony* 379, 870; see the translation's note on 11.306). Then the sea: πολλὸν δὲ τρόφι κῦμα κυλίνδεται, “and many a swollen wave rolls on” (τρόφις, “well-fed, swollen”, only here), ὑψόσε δʼ ἄχνη / σκίδναται ἐξ ἀνέμοιο πολυπλάγκτοιο ἰωῆς, “and the spray is scattered high under the blast of the wandering wind” (11.307–308). πολύπλαγκτος, “much-wandering”, is used only here in the Iliad; in the Odyssey it describes men who wander.

The tenor gives the point: ὣς ἄρα πυκνὰ καρήαθʼ ὑφʼ Ἕκτορι δάμνατο λαῶν, “so thick were the heads of the people beaten down by Hector” (11.309). The clouds broken and the waves and spray thrown up are the men struck down, many and fast. Heads stand for men throughout the book: Zeus means to send “many mighty heads” to Hades (11.55), the heads of the Trojans fall under Agamemnon (11.158), and by the Scamander “the heads of men were falling” (11.500).

## In context

The simile is the second of two wind similes for Hector's charge. The first is a single gust that stirs up the sea (11.297–298; see [[the-storm-wind-on-the-sea]]); this one is a full storm. Between them comes the list of the nine Achaean leaders, “these were the leaders of the Danaans he killed”, and the simile is for what comes after them, πληθύν, “the multitude” (11.299–305; see [[whom-first-whom-last]]). The rout would have reached the ships, had Odysseus not called on Diomedes to stand with him (11.310–312; see [[would-have-had-not]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The West Wind is the wind of the Iliad's army similes: it bows a deep field of grain, drives a storm cloud over the sea, and drives breaker after breaker onto the shore (2.147–149, 4.275–279, 4.422–426; see [[wind-in-the-wheat]], [[storm-cloud]] and [[waves-on-the-shore]]). It meets another wind in Book 9, where the North Wind and the West Wind stir the fish-filled sea as grief tears the Achaeans' hearts (9.4–8; see [[two-winds-from-thrace]]). The same two winds as here are named together once more, when Hera promises to rouse from the sea a storm of the West Wind and the brightening South Wind, to carry Hephaestus' flame and burn the heads and the armor of the Trojans (21.334–337).
