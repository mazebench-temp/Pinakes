## The simile

{{quote:680071a3-4384-51e2-91d3-29f2b11ece41}}

φίλοι δʼ ἅμα πάντες ἕποντο / πόλλʼ ὀλοφυρόμενοι ὡς εἰ θάνατον δὲ κιόντα: “and all his dear ones followed with him, / lamenting much, as if he were going to his death” (24.327–328). ὀλοφύρομαι is to lament aloud. ὡς εἰ, “as if”, makes the comparison, as in the city's wailing when Hector was dragged before the walls, “as if all / beetling Ilios were smoldering with fire from top to bottom” (22.410–411; see [[as-if-ilios-were-smoldering]]). θάνατον δέ, “to death”, is θάνατόνδε, with the suffix of direction printed apart in the text, as elsewhere in this book (see the translation's note on 16.188, 16.445, 16.693, 16.779, 16.856). The Iliad uses the word twice more, each time of a man at the moment of his death: “when the gods called you to your death” (16.693), says the narrator to Patroclus, and Hector, seeing that Deiphobus is not beside him, says, “Ah, truly the gods have called me to my death” (22.297).

The point of likeness is the certainty of those who watch. They lament the old man as a dead man, since a journey alone to the ships of the man who killed his sons can end only one way. Hecuba has said so: “How can you wish to go alone to the ships of the Achaeans, / before the eyes of the man who has slain many and brave / sons of yours? Your heart is iron” (24.203–205; see [[hecuba]]), and Priam has answered that if it is his fate to die beside the ships, he would have it so, once he has held his son in his arms (24.224–227; see [[priam]]).

## In context

The eagle has appeared on the right, and Priam sets out in haste, driving his chariot out from the doorway and the colonnade, with the mules in front drawing the wagon and Idaeus driving them (24.322–326; see [[wings-as-wide-as-a-door]], [[horses]] and [[mules]]). His kin go with him through the city, lamenting. “And when they had come down from the city and reached the plain, / the others went back toward Ilios, / his sons and his sons-in-law” (24.329–331). Then Zeus sees the two old men alone on the plain, pities Priam, and sends Hermes to guide him (24.331–338; see [[hermes]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Hector was lamented in the same way in his own house. When he had parted from Andromache in Book 6 to go back to the battle, she went home and roused the lament among her handmaids: “So they lamented Hector in his own house while he was still alive; / for they thought he would never come back again from the war” (6.500–501; see [[hectors-visit-to-troy]] and [[lament]]). Hector did not come back; Priam does. He drives home at dawn with Hector's body on the wagon, and the city that lamented him at his going laments his son at his return (24.695–717). Hermes, waking him in Achilles' hut, tells him what he would have been worth to the Achaeans alive: his sons would give three times as much ransom for him (24.683–688).
