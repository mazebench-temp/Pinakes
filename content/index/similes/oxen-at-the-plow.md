## The simile

{{quote:fcdcb7b1-7293-5c32-8e03-a2981850ad37}}

ἀλλʼ ὥς τʼ ἐν νειῷ βόε οἴνοπε πηκτὸν ἄροτρον / ἶσον θυμὸν ἔχοντε τιταίνετον· ἀμφὶ δʼ ἄρά σφι / πρυμνοῖσιν κεράεσσι πολὺς ἀνακηκίει ἱδρώς· / τὼ μέν τε ζυγὸν οἶον ἐΰξοον ἀμφὶς ἐέργει / ἱεμένω κατὰ ὦλκα· τέμει δέ τε τέλσον ἀρούρης: “but as in a fallow field two wine-dark oxen pull the jointed plow with equal spirit, and around them, at the roots of their horns, much sweat bubbles up; and only the polished yoke keeps the two apart as they strain down the furrow, and the plow cuts to the end of the field” (13.703–707). The νειός is fallow land, plowed but not sown (10.353, 18.541). βόε is a dual, a yoke of two oxen, and οἶνοψ, “wine-dark”, the color of the sea, is given to oxen here and in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 13.32; see [[cattle]]). The πηκτὸν ἄροτρον, the “jointed plow”, is built of pieces fitted together (πήγνυμι, “fix”; see [[jointed]]), as in Book 10, where mules are better than oxen at dragging it through deep fallow (10.351–353). The τέλσον is the strip at the end of the field where the team turns (18.544, 18.547; see the translation's note on 13.703–707). ἀνακηκίω, “bubble up”, occurs only here.

The tenor: ὣς τὼ παρβεβαῶτε μάλʼ ἕστασαν ἀλλήλοιιν, “so the two stood close beside each other” (13.708). The oxen are in the dual throughout, as the two Ajaxes are so often in this book (13.46–47, 13.197, 13.201; see [[the-two-ajaxes]]). The point of likeness is two who work as one: a single will (ἶσον θυμόν, “equal spirit”), a single burden, a hard straining labor, and no space between them but the yoke, ζυγὸν … ἐΰξοον, “the polished yoke” (see [[polished]]). The sweat of the oxen is in the tenor too. Telamonian Ajax has many brave companions who take his shield “whenever weariness and sweat came over his knees” (13.709–711).

The simile also says what keeps the pair apart, and the narrative goes on to tell it. Telamonian Ajax is followed by companions who carry his shield; the Locrians do not follow the son of Oileus in close battle, since they have no helmets, round shields or spears, but bows and slings, and shoot from behind (13.712–722; see [[locrians]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). The two leaders stand together, and their men fight in different ways.

## In context

Hector does not know that his men are being cut down on the left (13.674–676). He holds on where he first broke in at the gate, by the ships of Ajax and Protesilaus, where the wall was built lowest (13.679–684), and there the Boeotians, Ionians, Locrians, Phthians and Epeians hold him off with difficulty (13.685–700). Then the narrative comes to the two Ajaxes: “but Ajax, the swift son of Oileus, no longer stood at all apart from Telamonian Ajax, not even a little” (13.701–702; see [[ajax]]). Hector's Trojans fall back in confusion, and they would have retreated to Ilios, had not Polydamas spoken to Hector (13.719–725; see [[polydamas]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The plow team is a picture of hard and patient work. On the shield of Achilles many plowmen drive their teams across a broad fallow field, and as they reach the headland a man gives them a cup of wine (18.541–547). In the Odyssey Odysseus, sitting among the Phaeacians, longs for the sun to set as a man longs for his supper whose two wine-dark oxen have dragged the jointed plow through the fallow all day long (*Odyssey* 13.31–35), and he challenges the suitor Eurymachus to plow a furrow with a matched pair of oxen (*Odyssey* 18.371–375). Earlier in this book the two Ajaxes were a pair of lions carrying off a goat (13.197–202; see [[two-lions-and-a-goat]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells the farmer to keep two male oxen nine years old, at the height of their strength, for these will not quarrel in the furrow and break the plow and leave the work undone (*Works and Days* 436–440; see [[hesiod]]).
