## The simile

{{quote:e0cd4018-c691-5544-ade6-ed67c6955d8c}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἂν ἄγραυλοι πόριες περὶ βοῦς ἀγελαίας, / ἐλθούσας ἐς κόπρον, ἐπὴν βοτάνης κορέσωνται, / πᾶσαι ἅμα σκαίρουσιν ἐναντίαι· οὐδʼ ἔτι σηκοὶ / ἴσχουσʼ, ἀλλʼ ἁδινὸν μυκώμεναι ἀμφιθέουσι / μητέρας: “And as when the calves of the fields, around the cows of the herd / as they come back to the dung-yard when they have had their fill of pasture, / all skip together to meet them, and the pens no longer / hold them, but lowing without ceasing they run around / their mothers” (*Odyssey* 10.410–414). πόρις, more commonly πόρτις, is a calf or young heifer (*Iliad* 5.162; see [[cattle]]); W. W. Merry connects it with the root of πορεῖν and of Latin *pario*, and perhaps with παρθένος (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.410). The words that go with the calves, πᾶσαι, ἐναντίαι, μυκώμεναι, are feminine. ἄγραυλοι, “of the fields” (see [[of-the-fields]]), are here the calves “in the fold-yard”, Merry says, the ἀγρός being the homestead in the country, as when Eumaeus complains that Telemachus does not often come out to the farm and the herdsmen, but stays in the town (on 10.410; *Odyssey* 16.27–28, a plain rendering).

The scene is a farm when the herd comes home. The cows have been out to pasture in a drove, βοῦς ἀγελαίας, “the cows of the herd”, and they come back to the κόπρος, the dung-yard, literally the mixen; the calves, who have stayed at the farm penned up, welcome them with frisking and lowing (Merry on 10.412). The herd of the Shield of Achilles goes the other way, “and lowing they hurried out of the dung-yard to the pasture” (*Iliad* 18.575; see [[the-herd-and-the-lions]]). The pens, σηκοί, “no longer hold them” (see [[fold]]). σκαίρουσιν, “skip”, is said elsewhere in Homer only of the girls and young men of the vintage on the Shield, who follow the boy with the lyre “skipping with their feet” (*Iliad* 18.567–572). It is an indicative after ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἄν, which elsewhere takes the subjunctive: a true anacoluthon, Merry says, for the parenthesis of the cows' homecoming breaks the sentence, and σκαίρουσιν begins as if it were a main clause; Bekker conjectured σκαίρωσιν (on 10.412; see the translation's note on 10.410–414). ἁδινόν goes with μυκώμεναι, “lowing without ceasing”, not with ἀμφιθέουσι, as ἁδινά and ἁδινόν go with verbs of weeping and groaning (Merry on 10.413), as when Penelope speaks “lamenting without ceasing” (*Odyssey* 4.721).

The tenor follows at once: ὣς ἔμʼ ἐκεῖνοι, ἐπεὶ ἴδον ὀφθαλμοῖσι, / δακρυόεντες ἔχυντο: “so those men, when they saw me with their eyes, / poured around me in tears” (*Odyssey* 10.414–415). Merry takes ἐμέ with ἔχυντο, which keeps something of the ἀμφί of ἀμφιθέουσι, as if ἀμφέχυντο, “they threw themselves upon me weeping”; ἐμέ stands where μητέρας stood, and the verb is changed with the scene (on 10.414). The men are the calves, and Odysseus, come back, is the mother. The likeness then passes into feeling: “and it seemed to their hearts / as if they had come to their native land and to the very city / of rugged Ithaca, where they were born and raised” (*Odyssey* 10.415–417), and they say it themselves: “At your return, you who are nurtured by Zeus, we are as glad / as if we had come to Ithaca, our native land” (*Odyssey* 10.419–420; see [[ithaca]] and [[nostos]]). δόκησε δʼ is “and their feeling seemed to be just as if” (Merry on 10.415). πόλιν αὐτήν, “the very city”, is Aristarchus' reading, where all the manuscripts but one have αὐτῶν (Merry's critical note on 10.416; see the translation's note on 10.416).

## In context

Circe has freed the companions she had turned into swine and sends Odysseus to the ship, to drag it ashore, store the possessions and the tackle in caves and bring back his trusty companions (*Odyssey* 10.400–405; see [[circe]] and [[possessions]]). He finds them at the ship “lamenting piteously, shedding big tears” (*Odyssey* 10.409): they had waited there since Eurylochus came back alone with the news of the others' fate, and had heard him say that Odysseus would not come back himself (*Odyssey* 10.244–269; see [[eurylochus]] and [[hetairos]]). After the welcome they ask him to tell them “of the destruction of the rest of our companions” (*Odyssey* 10.421), and he answers with gentle words, bidding them drag up the ship and come to see their companions in Circe's house, “drinking and eating; for they have unfailing plenty” (*Odyssey* 10.422–427). Only Eurylochus tries to hold them back (*Odyssey* 10.429–437).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the Iliad the cow and the lion are parents standing over their young: Menelaus bestrides the body of Patroclus as a cow stands over her first calf, and Ajax stands over it like a lion over its cubs (*Iliad* 17.4–6, 17.133–136; see [[the-cow-and-her-calf]] and [[the-lion-and-its-cubs]]). Here the young run to the parent, and the parent is the one who comes home. The Odyssey measures other joys by a recovery or a homecoming: the land is as welcome to Odysseus on the raft as a father's recovery from long sickness is to his children (*Odyssey* 5.394–398; see [[the-fathers-recovery]]), and Eumaeus greets Telemachus as a father greets his only son come home from a far land in the tenth year (*Odyssey* 16.17–19, a plain rendering; see [[as-a-father-loves-his-son]]). The cows of the herd, βόες ἀγελαῖαι, are also those through which the herdsman's throwing stick flies in the Iliad (*Iliad* 23.845–846; see [[the-herdsmans-throwing-stick]]).
