## The simile

{{quote:f88a4b8b-44e0-5ff3-9a4b-ab33ff537382}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτε καπνὸς ἰὼν εἰς οὐρανὸν εὐρὺν ἵκηται / ἄστεος αἰθομένοιο: “As when smoke goes up and reaches the wide sky / from a burning city” (21.522–523). The verse begins as the simile of the beacon fires, where the smoke goes up from an island town under siege and reaches the αἰθήρ, the upper air (ὡς δʼ ὅτε καπνὸς ἰὼν ἐξ ἄστεος αἰθέρʼ ἵκηται, 18.207; see [[the-beacon-fires]]). There the town is fighting for its life and lights its beacons to call for help; here the city is already on fire. θεῶν δέ ἑ μῆνις ἀνῆκε, “and the wrath of the gods has sent it up”: ἀνίημι is to send up or let loose (see the translation's table), and μῆνις is the anger of gods, the word of Achilles' own wrath in the first verse of the poem (1.1; see [[menis]]). πᾶσι δʼ ἔθηκε πόνον, πολλοῖσι δὲ κήδεʼ ἐφῆκεν, “and it brings toil on all, and sends sorrows on many” (21.524; see [[ponos]]). κήδεʼ ἐφῆκεν, “sent sorrows”, is said in Book 1 of Apollo, angry for his priest, who “has now sent grievous sorrows upon the Argives” (1.445). The tenor repeats the words of the simile: ὣς Ἀχιλεὺς Τρώεσσι πόνον καὶ κήδεʼ ἔθηκεν, “so Achilles brought toil and sorrows on the Trojans” (21.525; see [[achilles]] and [[trojans]]).

The likeness is loosely joined, and Walter Leaf finds it unsuccessful. He thinks the simile adapted from the beacon fires of *Iliad* 18: the disasters that Achilles causes seem to be compared to those of a burning city, but by the words they are compared to the rising smoke, which is pointless (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.522). θεῶν δέ ἑ μῆνις ἀνῆκε can be taken as a parenthesis, showing perhaps that the fire is an accident and not the work of an enemy; then the smoke is the subject of the verbs that follow, and the simile is held together, but only on the surface. To make the wrath their subject is almost as unsatisfactory, since the smoke is then lost sight of. Bothe left out 524, which, Leaf says, certainly improves the passage: it removes the sham contrast of “all” and “many” and the fourfold -ηκε (ἀνῆκε, ἔθηκε, ἐφῆκεν, ἔθηκεν), and Achilles' progress over the plain can then be likened to the slow, unceasing advance of a great column of smoke (on 21.523). Without 524, he adds, the variant πολύστονα, “grievous”, is better in 525 than πόνον καί (on 21.525; see the translation's note on 21.522–525).

## In context

The battle of the gods is over. Apollo has gone into Ilios, mindful of its wall, and the other gods have gone back to Olympus and sat down beside Zeus; “But Achilles / was killing the Trojans themselves and their single-hoofed horses alike” (21.515–521; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]] and [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]). The simile turns the narrative from the gods to the city toward which Achilles is driving the Trojans, and the next verses show that city from within: old Priam, on the divine tower, sees the Trojans routed before Achilles and has the gates opened for them (21.526–536; see [[priam]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

A city on fire is a picture of the poem's later books. The war around Patroclus' body is like a fire that falls on a city of men and blazes up in a moment (17.735–741; see [[the-burning-city]]), and the light of Achilles' head is like the smoke and the beacons of a besieged town (18.207–214; see [[the-beacon-fires]]). In this book the river swears never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans, “not even when all Troy blazes with ravening fire” (21.375), the oath that Hera and Athena have sworn (20.313–317; see [[troy]] and [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). When Hector is killed, the wailing in the city is as if all of Ilios were smoldering with fire from top to bottom (22.410–411; see [[as-if-ilios-were-smoldering]]).
