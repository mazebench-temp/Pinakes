## The simile

{{quote:3f7c8e35-e6b6-57c8-9988-f3be55c5c88f}}

καππεσέτην, ἐλάτῃσιν ἐοικότες ὑψηλῇσι: “and they fell like tall fir trees”. καππεσέτην is the dual aorist of καταπίπτω, “fall down”, shortened in the epic way (κατ- to καπ-). ἐλάτη is the fir, the tall, straight tree of the Greek mountains. Its wood made oars (7.5; Odyssey 12.172) and the beams of Achilles' hut (24.450). The tallest fir on Ida “reached through the mist to the upper air”, and Sleep hides in its branches (14.286–289), and Odysseus builds his raft of fir “reaching to the sky” (Odyssey 5.239). ὑψηλαί is “tall”.

The point of likeness is height and the fall of the tall. The twins were young, full grown (5.550) and “two of the best men of the Danaans” (5.541). They fall together, as two trees might be felled side by side. The comparison follows the two lions without a pause (5.554–559; see [[two-lions]]): the lions, killed by men with the bronze, become trees cut down, and the image passes from the beasts' violence to the stillness of their fall.

## In context

The next line turns to the living: “And Menelaus, dear to Ares, pitied them as they fell” (5.561). The half-line τὼ δὲ πεσόντʼ ἐλέησε returns fifty lines later, when Hector kills Menesthes and Anchialus, two men in one chariot, and “great Telamonian Ajax pitied them as they fell” (5.610). Both pairs fall to a Trojan, and both times an Achaean leader answers the deaths by going forward (see [[menelaus]] and [[ajax]]). See [[crethon]] and [[orsilochus-son-of-diocles]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

Warriors in the Iliad often fall like trees. Echepolus, the first man killed in the poem, falls like a tower (4.462; see [[like-a-tower]]) and Simoeisius like a poplar felled by a chariot-maker (4.482–487; see [[the-poplar]]). Imbrius falls like an ash on a mountain top cut down with the bronze (13.178–181). Asius falls “as an oak falls, or a white poplar, / or a tall pine, which on the mountains builders / cut down with newly sharpened axes to be ship-timber” (13.389–391), and the same three lines are used of Sarpedon's death (16.482–484). Hector goes down under Ajax's stone like an oak uprooted by the lightning of Zeus (14.414–417), and Euphorbus like a young olive tree torn up by a sudden gale (17.53–58).
