## The simile

{{quote:981a4b1d-0dc0-533d-9364-c9941ffdb746}}

Αἴας δʼ ἀμφὶ Μενοιτιάδῃ σάκος εὐρὺ καλύψας / ἑστήκει ὥς τίς τε λέων περὶ οἷσι τέκεσσιν: “And Ajax covered the son of Menoetius with his broad shield / and stood like a lion over his young” (17.132–133). The shield that came “like a tower” a few verses before now covers the body (17.128; see [[like-a-tower]] and [[the-shield-of-ajax]]). τέκεσσιν, “young”, is a word for children, and in the next verse the cubs are νήπια, “little ones”, the word for helpless children (see [[nepios]]). ᾧ ῥά τε νήπιʼ ἄγοντι συναντήσωνται ἐν ὕλῃ / ἄνδρες ἐπακτῆρες, “when men meet him in the forest as he leads his little ones, / huntsmen” (17.134–135). ἐπακτήρ, “huntsman”, is found once more in Homer, of the hunters who take the young Odysseus to the boar hunt on Parnassus (*Odyssey* 19.435). Walter Leaf reports the scholiast's explanation of the word, “from setting on the dogs” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.135).

ὃ δέ τε σθένεϊ βλεμεαίνει, / πᾶν δέ τʼ ἐπισκύνιον κάτω ἕλκεται ὄσσε καλύπτων, “and he exults in his strength, / and draws down all the skin of his brow, covering his eyes” (17.135–136). σθένεϊ βλεμεαίνει is said in this book of the spirit in a boar's breast (17.22), and of Hector as he goes among the foremost (8.337; see [[exulting-in-strength]]). ἐπισκύνιον, “the skin of the brow”, is found only here in Homer. The lion frowns until its brows cover its eyes. Leaf quotes a scholium that explains the gesture as a habit observed in life: a lion attacked by hunters while it has cubs looks down and covers its eyes, so that it will not see the weapons coming, take fright and abandon its young. He compares Pliny's account of the lioness that fights for her cubs with her eyes fixed on the ground, so as not to fear the hunting spears (Leaf on 17.136, citing Pliny, *Natural History* 8.16).

The tenor: ὣς Αἴας περὶ Πατρόκλῳ ἥρωϊ βεβήκει, “so Ajax stood over the hero Patroclus” (17.137). The lion does not charge. It stands its ground over what it guards, ready for the men who come at it. The body of Patroclus is the cub, and Ajax will not leave it.

Leaf reports that the three verses of the cubs and the hunters (17.134–136) were not in the text of Zenodotus or in the Chian edition. The objection made to them was that the lioness, not the lion, leads the cubs; Zenodotus, Leaf supposes, took λέων as a word for either sex, since Homer has no word for a lioness, and so had to remove the verses with masculine pronouns (on 17.134, comparing 18.318 and 21.483).

## In context

Hector has stripped Patroclus and is dragging the body away, “to cut the head from his shoulders with the sharp bronze, / and drag off the corpse and give it to the Trojan dogs” (17.126–127; see [[dogs]]). Menelaus, driven from the body, has found Ajax and brought him (17.114–124). Ajax comes near with his shield, and Hector gives ground, springs into his chariot and sends the armor to the city (17.128–131). Ajax stands over the body, and Menelaus on the other side stands “nursing great grief in his breast” (17.138–139). Glaucus then rebukes Hector for not daring to stand against Ajax (17.140–168; see [[glaucus]] and [[ajax]]).

The book has already pictured the other defender of the body as a parent: Menelaus stood over Patroclus as a cow stands over her first calf (17.4–6; see [[the-cow-and-her-calf]]). The two similes are the two guardians of the body, each over its young.

## Elsewhere in Homer

In Book 11 a lion is the destroyer of the young: it comes into the lair of a swift hind and crushes her helpless fawns in its strong teeth, while the mother, close by, cannot help them (11.113–119). Here the lion is the parent. Its young return in Book 18, when Achilles groans over Patroclus like a bearded lion whose cubs a hunter has snatched from the thick wood, and which follows the man's tracks through many glens (18.318–322). Animals defending their young appear in other similes: wasps by a road fight off the hunters for the sake of their young (12.167–172, 16.259–265; see [[wasps-at-the-roadside]]), and Achilles likens himself to a bird that brings every morsel to her unfledged chicks (9.323–324; see [[the-bird-and-her-chicks]]). Ajax was a lion in Book 11 too, but a lion driven from the cattle yard (11.548–557; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]). See [[lion]].
