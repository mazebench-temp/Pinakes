## The simile

{{quote:03c0ab68-9d6d-596e-a9de-fae4d6b33300}}

ἴσχον γὰρ πυργηδὸν ἀρηρότες, ἠΰτε πέτρη / ἠλίβατος μεγάλη πολιῆς ἁλὸς ἐγγὺς ἐοῦσα, / ἥ τε μένει λιγέων ἀνέμων λαιψηρὰ κέλευθα / κύματά τε τροφόεντα, τά τε προσερεύγεται αὐτήν: “for they held, fitted together in close order like a tower, like a rock, lofty and great, close to the gray sea, which withstands the swift paths of the shrill winds and the swollen waves that belch against it” (15.618–621).

Two likenesses stand side by side. πυργηδόν, “like a tower”, is a word of formation: the Achaeans stand so, in a solid block, when Hector boasts that they will not hold him back for long (13.152), and so do the hunters who face the boar or lion in a simile of Book 12 (12.43; see [[battle-order]] and [[the-boar-or-lion-at-bay]]). ἀρηρότες, “fitted together”, is the perfect of ἀραρίσκω, the verb of joinery. Then the rock: ἠλίβατος, always said of rocks and cliffs, is of uncertain derivation, and is taken as “sheer” or “high”. It stands πολιῆς ἁλὸς ἐγγύς, “close to the gray sea” (see [[gray-sea]]). μένει is “withstands, awaits”, the verb the tenor gives the Danaans (μένον). λιγέων ἀνέμων λαιψηρὰ κέλευθα, “the swift paths of the shrill winds”, are the words of Nestor's heaving sea in Book 14, which senses the winds before they come (14.17; see [[the-heaving-sea]]). τροφόεντα, “swollen”, from τρέφω, “nourish, thicken”, and προσερεύγεται, “belch against”, are found only here; ἐρεύγομαι is used of the sea that spews and roars at a river's mouth (17.265) and of the waves that belch against the rocks of Scheria (*Odyssey* 5.403, 5.438).

The tenor: ὣς Δαναοὶ Τρῶας μένον ἔμπεδον οὐδὲ φέβοντο, “so the Danaans awaited the Trojans steadfastly and did not flee” (15.622). The verse is the one that closes the simile of the clouds in Book 5, which the son of Cronus sets motionless on the mountain tops while the North Wind and the other winds sleep (5.522–527; see [[clouds-on-the-mountains]]). There the winds were asleep; here they blow and the waves break, and the rock does not move.

The point of likeness is passive strength. The rock does nothing; it is there, and it is great, and what comes against it falls back. The Danaans have been routed and driven back to their ships, and here, for a moment, they stand. Hector has tried them “where he saw the greatest throng and the best armor”, and could not break them, “though he raged greatly” (15.615–617).

## In context

Zeus is rousing Hector to bring fire to the ships, and Hector rages like Ares or a forest fire, with foam at his mouth and eyes blazing (15.592–614; see [[forest-fire]]). The rock stands for a moment only. In the next verse Hector, “blazing all over with fire, leapt into the throng”, and falls on the Danaans like a wave on a swift ship (15.623–629; see [[the-ship-in-the-storm]]). The sea of the rock simile, its winds and its swollen waves, passes straight into the next, and the rock becomes a ship. Then he falls on them like a lion among cattle, and they flee (15.630–637; see [[lion-among-cattle]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

A rock that the waves never leave alone is in the last sea simile of the assembly of Book 2, but there the wave is the Argives' shout, breaking on a jutting crag on a high shore (2.394–397; see [[wave-on-the-headland]]). The Lapiths at the gate stood like oaks in the mountains that withstand the wind and the rain all their days (12.131–136; see [[oaks-on-the-mountains]]). The two Ajaxes will hold back the Trojans in Book 17 as a wooded ridge holds back the floods of rivers (17.747–751). In the Odyssey, when Antinous strikes Odysseus with a footstool, he stands as firm as a rock and does not stagger (*Odyssey* 17.462–465).

## Outside Homer

Virgil gives the rock in the sea to single men: King Latinus, holding out against his people's clamor for war, and Mezentius, standing alone against the Trojans (*Aeneid* 7.586–590, 10.693–696; see the Book 15 note on [[virgil]]).
