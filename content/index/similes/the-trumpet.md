## The simile

{{quote:e82e12a4-f9b7-5467-995f-144f08aca534}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἀριζήλη φωνή, ὅτε τʼ ἴαχε σάλπιγξ / ἄστυ περιπλομένων δηΐων ὕπο θυμοραϊστέων: “And as a voice rings out clear, when a trumpet sounds / because enemies who destroy the spirit surround a city” (18.219–220). ἀρίζηλος is “clear, conspicuous”, plain to the senses; it is said of the portent of the serpent that a god made plain (2.318), of the gleams of Zeus' lightning (13.244), of Ares and Athena on the Shield, “plain for all to see from every side” (18.519), and later of the rays of the star that is Orion's dog (22.27; see [[orion]]). Here the clearness is a sound. σάλπιγξ is the trumpet. Walter Leaf notes that ἴαχε is an aorist, as the tense of a simile requires, that ὕπο with the genitive gives the cause, “by reason of” the enemies, and that περιπλομένων is used of men only here, elsewhere of the revolving year (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.219, 18.220; see the translation's note on 18.219–221). θυμοραϊστής, “who destroy the spirit”, is elsewhere the epithet of death in battle (13.544, 16.414, 16.580), and of enemies in a simile of Book 16 whose words Leaf compares for ὕπο (16.591; see [[that-destroys-the-spirit]]).

Leaf leaves open what the trumpet is doing: it may be the alarm with which the watchmen of a beleaguered town call the citizens to the walls, or the besiegers' signal for the assault (on 18.220). Either way the sound means a city surrounded, and the simile is the second in a row to picture a siege, after the island town that lights its beacons at sunset (18.207–214; see [[the-beacon-fires]]).

The tenor: ὣς τότʼ ἀριζήλη φωνὴ γένετʼ Αἰακίδαο, “so clear then rang out the voice of the grandson of Aeacus” (18.221; see [[grandson-of-aeacus]]). The next verse calls it ὄπα χάλκεον, “the bronze voice” (18.222). Leaf takes χάλκεον as “unwearied”, as in χάλκεον ἦτορ, “a heart of bronze” (2.490), perhaps with the ringing quality that the trumpet suggests; Stentor, whose likeness Hera takes in Book 5, is χαλκεόφωνος, “with the voice of bronze”, and could shout as loud as fifty other men (5.785–786; Leaf on 18.222; see the translation's note on 18.222).

## In context

Athena has set a golden cloud on Achilles' head and kindled a flame from it (18.203–214). He goes from the wall and stands at the ditch, without mingling with the Achaeans, “for he heeded his mother's shrewd command” (18.215–216). “There he stood and shouted, and apart from him Pallas Athena / cried out; and among the Trojans he roused untold tumult” (18.217–218); Leaf thinks it was probably Achilles who roused the tumult, with Athena's cry in parenthesis (on 18.218; see the translation's note on 18.218 and [[athena]]). The trumpet follows, and then the effect of the voice:

{{quote:904b9d35-3c21-5912-aacc-1169bc68300d}}

The horses foresee sorrows and turn the chariots back, the charioteers are struck with terror at the fire, and the shout is given three times, as Hector seized the body three times and three times was driven off (18.155–158). Twelve of the best Trojans perish in the confusion, “among their own chariots and spears”, and the Achaeans gladly draw the body of Patroclus out from under the missiles (18.230–233; see [[horses]], [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]]). Achilles has no armor; his voice is his weapon. Polydamas tells the Trojans that night that he is afraid of the son of Peleus and that they must go back to the city (18.254–283; see [[polydamas]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The trumpet is named again only in a metaphor, when the gods join battle in Book 21: the wide earth rang, and the great sky trumpeted around them (ἀμφὶ δὲ σάλπιγξεν μέγας οὐρανός, 21.388). Leaf reports the ancient remark, which he gives from Aristonicus, that the poet knows the trumpet himself but never brings his heroes on using it, and he sets it beside the like remarks on riding horseback (15.679) and on the boiling of meat (21.362), things the poet knew and kept to his similes (on 18.219). The poet, Leaf thinks, was conscious that such inventions were still recent and not to be ascribed to the time of which he sang (on 15.679; see [[the-rider-of-four-horses]]). The heroes give their own signals with their voices. The Iliad's other great shouts are Hera's in the likeness of Stentor (5.784–792), Strife's from beside the ship of Odysseus (11.3–14; see [[strife]]), and Poseidon's, as loud as nine or ten thousand men in war (14.147–152; see [[poseidon]]).
