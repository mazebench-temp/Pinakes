## The simile

{{quote:74dcae00-14bf-58e4-ae02-4fd12b8175f0}}

οὔτε γὰρ ἐξοπίσω φυγέειν δύνατʼ οὔτʼ ἀλέασθαι, / ἀλλʼ ὥς τε στήλην ἢ δένδρεον ὑψιπέτηλον / ἀτρέμας ἑσταότα στῆθος μέσον οὔτασε δουρὶ / ἥρως Ἰδομενεύς: “for he could neither escape backward nor swerve aside, but like a pillar or a tree with high foliage, as he stood motionless, he stabbed him in the middle of the chest with his spear, the hero Idomeneus” (13.436–439). The comparison is folded into the sentence of the killing: the man is like a pillar while the spear goes in.

A στήλη is a standing stone, in Homer above all the pillar set on a grave. Hera, and then Zeus, speak of the burial due to Sarpedon as “a mound and a pillar” (τύμβῳ τε στήλῃ τε, 16.457 = 16.675); Paris, leaning against the pillar on the mound of Ilus, shoots at Diomedes (11.369–372); and Achilles' horses, weeping for Patroclus, stand as still as a pillar that stands on the grave of a dead man or woman (17.434–436). The pillars of the Achaean wall are its “jutting buttresses” (στήλας, 12.259). δένδρεον ὑψιπέτηλον, “a tree with high foliage” (ὕψι, “on high”, and πέταλον, “leaf”), is a phrase of the Odyssey too, for one of the shapes of Proteus and for the fruit trees over Tantalus (*Odyssey* 4.458, 11.588). The point of likeness is stillness. Pillar and tree stand and cannot move, and ἀτρέμας ἑσταότα, “as he stood motionless”, gives the stillness of a man who should be fighting or running.

The stillness is a god's work. Poseidon beat him down at the hands of Idomeneus, θέλξας ὄσσε φαεινά, πέδησε δὲ φαίδιμα γυῖα, “bewitching his bright eyes and fettering his shining limbs” (13.434–435). θέλγω is the spell of a god, as Zeus bewitches the minds of the Achaeans with his wind from Ida (12.255), Apollo their hearts with the aegis (15.321–322), and Hermes the eyes of men with his wand (24.343). πεδάω, “fetter”, is what fate does to a man about to die: “fate fettered Diores” (4.517), and deadly fate binds Hector to stay outside the gates (22.5). Then the spear breaks the tunic of bronze, which “rang dry” as it tore, and the heart, still beating, shakes the butt of the spear, until “mighty Ares let its might go” (13.439–444; see the translation's notes on 13.409, 13.441 and 13.443–444).

## In context

Alcathous, son of Aesyetes, is the son-in-law of Anchises. He married Hippodameia, the eldest daughter, whom her parents loved with all their hearts, “for she surpassed all the girls of her age in beauty and handiwork and wits; and so the best man in wide Troy had married her” (13.427–433; see [[alcathous]], [[hippodameia-daughter-of-anchises]] and [[anchises]]). He is the third man Idomeneus kills in this part of the book, after Othryoneus and Asius (13.363–393), and Idomeneus boasts of three killed for one (13.445–454); Deiphobus fetches Aeneas, whom Alcathous reared in his house (13.455–467); and the fight goes on over his body (13.496–539; see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]] and [[idomeneus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Gods disable a man before he is killed. Apollo strikes Patroclus from behind with the flat of his hand, his eyes whirl, his armor falls from him, “and he stood dazed”, and Euphorbus and Hector kill him (16.791–806). In the Odyssey Athena “fettered” Amphinomus to be killed by Telemachus' spear (*Odyssey* 18.155–156). Trees stand firm in other similes for another reason: the two Lapiths at the gate wait for the Trojans like oaks on the mountains that withstand wind and rain (12.132–134; see [[oaks-on-the-mountains]]). Alcathous stands because he cannot move.
