## The simile

{{quote:bf0520f2-2304-55ad-b466-911f858d6f66}}

ἦμος δʼ ἐπὶ δόρπον ἀνὴρ ἀγορῆθεν ἀνέστη / κρίνων νείκεα πολλὰ δικαζομένων αἰζηῶν, / τῆμος δὴ τά γε δοῦρα Χαρύβδιος ἐξεφαάνθη: “at the hour when a man rises from the assembly for his supper, / one who judges the many quarrels of young men pleading their cases, / at that hour the timbers came to light out of Charybdis” (*Odyssey* 12.439–441). ἦμος … τῆμος, “at the hour when … at that hour”, is the pair with which the poems tell the time of day (see below). The man is a judge, κρίνων νείκεα, “judging quarrels” (see [[neikos]]), and the young men are the parties, δικαζόμενοι, “going to law”, the middle of δικάζω, “give judgment”. He sits in the ἀγορή, the place of assembly where cases were heard, as the elders sit to judge a blood-price on the shield of Achilles (*Iliad* 18.497–508; see [[assembly]] and [[the-trial-on-the-shield]]). δόρπον is the evening meal (see [[feast]]). W. W. Merry puts the hour “towards evening, when a judge may be supposed to have got through his cases, and when the market-place begins to empty”, and compares the phrase by which Herodotus tells a time of day, μέχρι οὗ ἀγορῆς διαλύσιος, “until the market breaks up” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.439–441; Herodotus 3.104). ἀνέστη is an aorist of custom; for the present κρίνων, where κρίνας might be expected, Merry compares ἐπαύσατο λαίλαπι θύων, “ceased raging with its storm” (*Odyssey* 12.400), and ἔγρετο … εὕδων, “woke … where he slept” (*Odyssey* 13.187–188).

The likeness is not of a picture but of a time. Odysseus measures his wait by a long day's business in a city. He came to Charybdis “with the rising sun” (*Odyssey* 12.429), and the timbers came back to him only “late” (ὄψʼ, *Odyssey* 12.439), at the end of a day of lawsuits. The judge is a man who sits and hears quarrel after quarrel until his supper; the shipwrecked man hangs from the tree all day, waiting for his timbers to come up.

The verses were doubted in antiquity “in many copies”, as the scholia report (Schol. H. Q.). The objection, as Merry gives it, is that they make Charybdis hold the timbers from sunrise to evening, against Circe's word that she sends the water up three times a day (*Odyssey* 12.105); Merry adds that ἦμος δέ elsewhere in Homer always stands at the beginning of a verse, as it does not here, and reports that Aristarchus read τάδε for τά γε in the last line (his critical note and note on 12.439–441; see the translation's note on 12.430–441).

## In context

Swept back to Charybdis on the keel and mast, Odysseus springs up to the fig tree as she sucks down the sea and hangs from it “like a bat”, unable to set his feet or to climb (*Odyssey* 12.426–436; see [[like-a-bat]] and [[the-fig-tree-of-charybdis]]). “And I held on unceasingly, until she should vomit up again / the mast and keel; and as I yearned for them they came, / late” (*Odyssey* 12.437–439). When they come he lets himself drop into the water beside them and rows with his hands, and Zeus does not let Scylla see him (*Odyssey* 12.442–446; see [[charybdis]] and [[scylla]]). It is the last likeness of Odysseus' tale. The tale ends a few verses later on Ogygia, and with a word about its own telling: “it is hateful to me / to tell over again a tale that has been plainly told” (*Odyssey* 12.452–453; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Homer tells the hours of the day by men's work. In the Iliad the Danaans break the Trojan battalions “at the hour when a woodcutter makes ready his meal / in the glens of a mountain, when his arms have had their fill / of cutting tall trees” (ἦμος δὲ δρυτόμος περ ἀνὴρ ὁπλίσσατο δεῖπνον, *Iliad* 11.86–90): the midday meal of a man who works in the mountains, as this is the supper of a man who works in the city. The afternoon is “the time for unyoking oxen”, βουλυτόνδε, when the plowman's day ends (*Iliad* 16.779; *Odyssey* 9.58; see [[cattle]]), and at sunset the plowman longs for his supper, as Odysseus longs for the night that will take him home (*Odyssey* 13.31–35; see [[the-plowman-and-his-supper]]). The judge himself has his figure in the Iliad's storm of late summer, which Zeus sends against men “who by force in the assembly judge crooked ordinances / and drive out justice” (*Iliad* 16.386–388; see [[the-storm-of-late-summer]] and [[themis]]), and in the land of the dead Minos sits with a golden scepter, “giving the law to the dead”, while they ask him for judgment around him (*Odyssey* 11.568–571; see [[minos]]).

## Outside Homer

[[strabo|Strabo]] reads the hour as evidence that Homer knew the true tides of the strait. Charybdis sends up her water twice in a day and a night, not three times as Circe says, and the long wait fits two turns of the tide better than three: the poet does not simply say “when the judge rises”, but when he rises after judging many quarrels, so that he stays the longer, and the evening is drawn out (*Geography* 1.2.36; see [[like-a-bat]] and [[polybius]]). Earlier in the same book Strabo reports Polybius' view that the “three times” of Circe's verse is an error, either of the text or of the information (*Geography* 1.2.16).
