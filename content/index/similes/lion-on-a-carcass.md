## The simile

ὥς τε λέων ἐχάρη μεγάλῳ ἐπὶ σώματι κύρσας / εὑρὼν ἢ ἔλαφον κεραὸν ἢ ἄγριον αἶγα / πεινάων: “as a lion rejoices when he comes upon a great carcass, finding an antlered stag or a wild goat, when he is hungry”. λέων is the [[lion]]. κύρσας means “lighting upon”. ἔλαφος κεραός is an antlered stag ([[deer]]) and ἄγριος αἴξ a wild goat ([[goats]]). πεινάων (“hungry”) is held back to the start of the next line. The lion “devours it greedily (μάλα … κατεσθίει), even though the swift dogs and the young men in their prime rush at him” (see [[dogs]]).

σῶμα in Homer always means a dead body. It is used seven times, always of the dead: of this carcass, of Hector's body (7.79, 22.342), of the carcass a lion will not leave (18.161), of the flayed victims on Patroclus' pyre (23.169), of the unburied Elpenor (Odyssey 11.53) and of drowned sailors (Odyssey 12.67). So the lion has found an animal already dead, most likely the hunters' kill, since their dogs and young men are close at hand. He takes their quarry from under their noses.

The tenor: ὣς ἐχάρη Μενέλαος Ἀλέξανδρον θεοειδέα / ὀφθαλμοῖσιν ἰδών, “so Menelaus rejoiced when he saw godlike Alexander with his eyes”. The reason follows: φάτο γὰρ τίσεσθαι ἀλείτην, “for he thought he would take vengeance on the wrongdoer”. ἀλείτης is an offender, a man who has sinned against gods or men. The point of likeness is the joy of a hungry beast at the sight of food. In Menelaus' eyes the man who wronged him is already a carcass, and nothing will keep him from it.

## In context

{{quote:a4e1ce0a-6707-58b9-985d-215c02ba8603}}

Alexander has stepped out as champion for the Trojans, with a leopard's skin on his shoulders, his bow, a sword and two spears, and challenged all the best of the Argives (3.15–20; see [[paris]] and [[leopard-skin]]). Menelaus sees him and at once leaps from his chariot to the ground in his armor (3.29). Then the scene turns round: when Alexander catches sight of Menelaus, he shrinks back into the ranks like a man who meets a serpent (3.30–37; see [[man-who-meets-a-serpent]]). The two similes begin from the same half-line, τὸν δʼ ὡς οὖν ἐνόησεν (“and when … caught sight of him”, 3.21 and 3.30), and both are set in the mountains. One man sees his enemy and rejoices like a beast; the other sees his and recoils like a man.

The simile promises more than the duel will give. Menelaus has Alexander by the helmet and is dragging him toward the Achaeans when Aphrodite breaks the strap and carries him off (3.369–382; see [[aphrodite-rescues-paris]]). The book ends with Menelaus still ranging the throng “like a wild beast”, looking for his prey (3.449; see [[like-a-wild-beast]]). The dogs and young men of the simile do not stop the lion. A goddess does.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The lion is the commonest beast of the Iliad's similes, and this is the first of them. Menelaus is a lion again in Book 17. Over the body of Patroclus he is like a mountain lion that seizes the best cow of a herd, while dogs and herdsmen shout from a distance and dare not come near (17.61–69); and twice he withdraws unwillingly, like a lion driven from a farmyard by dogs and men (17.108–113, 17.657–667). The hungry lion and the quarry return in other forms. Two lions fight over a slain deer on the mountain peaks, “both hungry”, as Hector and Patroclus fight over the body of Cebriones (16.756–758). Shepherds cannot drive a tawny lion, “very hungry”, from a carcass (σώματος), as the two Ajaxes cannot drive Hector from Patroclus' corpse (18.161–164). The line “an antlered stag or a wild goat” recurs when dogs and country men chase a quarry, and a bearded lion appears in their path (15.271–276).
