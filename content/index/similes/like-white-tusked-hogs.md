## The simile

{{quote:a61d5baf-8177-5966-9719-174ab41ce025}}

ὣς θάνον οἰκτίστῳ θανάτῳ· περὶ δʼ ἄλλοι ἑταῖροι / νωλεμέως κτείνοντο σύες ὣς ἀργιόδοντες, / οἵ ῥά τʼ ἐν ἀφνειοῦ ἀνδρὸς μέγα δυναμένοιο / ἢ γάμῳ ἢ ἐράνῳ ἢ εἰλαπίνῃ τεθαλυίῃ: “So I died a most pitiable death; and around me my other companions / were killed unceasingly, like white-tusked hogs / that are slaughtered in the house of a rich man of great power / for a wedding, or a shared feast, or a rich banquet” (*Odyssey* 11.412–415). ὥς follows its noun and takes the accent, as in ἰχθῦς δʼ ὥς, “like fish” (*Odyssey* 10.124; see [[like-fish]]). νωλεμέως, “unceasingly”, is the word of a grip that does not let go (*Odyssey* 9.435) and of fighting that does not stop (*Iliad* 13.3). The relative clause has no verb of its own, and the hearer supplies the slaughter: W. W. Merry compares the poppy of the Iliad, “a poppy in a garden / heavy with its seed and with the showers of spring”, and the fisherman on the jutting rock, where the verb is likewise left out, and takes ἐν ἀφνειοῦ ἀνδρός as “in the house of a rich man”, with οἴκῳ understood (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.414; *Iliad* 8.306–307, 16.406–408).

ἀργιόδους, “white-tusked”, is the epithet of swine killed for a feast (see [[white-tusked]]). Achilles' Myrmidons singe “many white-tusked hogs, rich with lard” for the funeral feast of Patroclus (*Iliad* 23.32), Alcinous sacrifices “eight white-tusked hogs” for the feast of the Phaeacians (*Odyssey* 8.60), and Odysseus sends Demodocus the chine “of a white-tusked boar” (*Odyssey* 8.476); it is said too of the wild boar that Artemis sent against Calydon (*Iliad* 9.539) and of hunting dogs (*Iliad* 11.292; see [[boar]]). The companions die as such beasts die in a rich man's house, many at once and for others to eat.

**Three feasts.** γάμος, ἔρανος, εἰλαπίνη: the wedding, the shared feast and the banquet. The same three are named together when Athena, in the likeness of Mentes, sees the suitors in Odysseus' hall: “What feast, what throng is this? … / A banquet, or a wedding? For this is surely no shared feast” (*Odyssey* 1.225–226; see [[feast]] and [[marriage]]). Merry explains the εἰλαπίνη as a feast given by a single host, and the ἔρανος as a feast held at the house of each partaker in turn, whether or not all contribute to it, and so the least splendid of the three; the word kept that character, he says, and gives point to Aristotle's vulgar man, who makes a tasteless show in small expenses, “entertaining his club-mates as if at a wedding”, ἐρανιστὰς γαμικῶς ἑστιῶν (on *Odyssey* 1.226; *Nicomachean Ethics* 1123a19–23). εἰλαπίνη τεθαλυῖα is “a rich banquet” (see the Odyssey translation's row εἰλαπίνη τεθαλυῖα).

## In context

Agamemnon has told Odysseus that Aegisthus, with his ruinous wife, killed him in the house to which he had called him, “and feasted me, as one kills an ox at the manger” (*Odyssey* 11.409–411; see [[the-ox-at-the-manger]], [[agamemnon]] and [[aegisthus]]). The king dies like the beast of the stall, his men like the beasts of the feast. He turns to Odysseus, who has seen the deaths of many men in single combat and in battle: “but you would have grieved most in your heart, had you seen that sight, / how around the mixing bowl and the laden tables / we lay in the hall, and the whole floor seethed with blood” (*Odyssey* 11.416–420; see [[mixing-bowl]], [[table]] and the Odyssey translation's row θύω (of blood)). In Proteus' account of the same day none of the companions of either man survived, “but they were killed in the halls” (*Odyssey* 4.536–537; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]] and [[hetairos]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Odyssey's own homecoming reverses the scene. The suitors are killed at their feast in Odysseus' hall, the first of them as he lifts his cup, kicking the table away so that the food is spilled on the floor (*Odyssey* 22.8–21, a plain rendering; see [[the-suitors]]), and the verse that ends Agamemnon's picture, “and the whole floor seethed with blood”, is said of the suitors' hall (*Odyssey* 22.309), and again in the land of the dead, when the soul of the suitor Amphimedon tells the soul of Agamemnon how they died (*Odyssey* 24.185). In the Iliad the beasts of the feast are slaughtered beside the dead: around the body of Patroclus oxen bellow as their throats are cut, with sheep, goats and hogs (*Iliad* 23.30–34; see [[feast]]).
