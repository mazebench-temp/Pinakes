## The simile

{{quote:173580e2-2a01-5926-b2c1-bbaeed86e5af}}

ἦρχε δʼ ἄρʼ Ἕκτωρ / ἀντικρὺ μεμαώς, ὀλοοίτροχος ὣς ἀπὸ πέτρης: “and Hector led them, rushing straight on, like a rolling boulder from a rock” (13.136–137). ὀλοοίτροχος, a round boulder that rolls, occurs only here in Homer (see the translation's note on 13.137–142). A ποταμὸς χειμάρροος, “a river in winter flood”, swollen by the winter rains, thrusts it down κατὰ στεφάνης, “from a crest”, the brow of the hill, ῥήξας ἀσπέτῳ ὄμβρῳ ἀναιδέος ἔχματα πέτρης, “breaking with its untold rain the props of the shameless rock”. The ἔχματα are whatever held the rock in place (ἔχω, “hold”). The rock is ἀναιδής, “shameless”, as if it had a will (see [[shameless-stone]]; on ἄσπετος, [[untold]]). So is the stone that crushes Diores' ankle (“The shameless stone”, 4.521), and so is the stone of Sisyphus, which rolls back down to the plain each time he has pushed it nearly to the top (*Odyssey* 11.593–598).

Then the boulder moves like a living thing. ὕψι δʼ ἀναθρῴσκων πέτεται, “it leaps up high and flies”, and the forest crashes beneath it; ὃ δʼ ἀσφαλέως θέει ἔμπεδον, “it runs on surely and unchecked”, with the words of a runner, εἷος ἵκηται / ἰσόπεδον, “until it reaches level ground” (ἰσόπεδον, only here), “and then it rolls no more, for all its haste” (13.140–142).

The tenor follows the vehicle point by point. Hector “for a while threatened that he would go through to the sea easily, through the huts and ships of the Achaeans, killing” (13.143–145). The boulder's ground runs out; Hector's way is blocked: “but when he came up against the close-packed battalions, he stood still, pressed hard against them”, and the Achaeans, stabbing with swords and double-pointed spears, pushed him away, “and he gave ground, reeling” (13.145–148; see the translation's note on 13.147). The point of likeness is force that nothing on the slope can stop and that the level ground stops at once. The close ranks are the level ground (see [[battle-order]]). The simile tells the end of the charge before the tenor reaches it.

Elsewhere the winter torrent is swollen by “the rain of Zeus” (5.91, 11.493). Here the rain is only “untold”, but Poseidon has just granted that the Olympian himself may be rousing Hector (13.58), and Hector believes it: the Achaeans will give ground, “if truly the best of the gods has roused me, the loud-thundering husband of Hera” (13.153–154). The boulder's haste, ἐσσύμενός περ, is the word of Poseidon's hope that the Ajaxes might hold Hector back “for all his haste” (ἐσσύμενόν περ, 13.57), and Idomeneus will say that they will give him his fill of it, “for all his eagerness for war” (13.315).

## In context

Poseidon has rallied the Achaeans, and the best of them have formed around the two Ajaxes, “shield pressed on shield, helmet on helmet, man on man” (13.126–135; see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]] and [[the-two-ajaxes]]). The Trojans drive forward in a body behind Hector (13.136). When he is checked, he shouts to the Trojans, Lycians and Dardanians to stand by him: the Achaeans will not hold him long, “though they have arrayed themselves in close order like a tower” (13.149–154; see [[hector]]). Deiphobus comes forward behind his shield, and the fighting breaks up into single combats (13.156–168).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Torrents in flood are the force of a charging hero: Diomedes and Ajax sweep the plain like rivers swollen by the rain of Zeus (5.85–94, 11.492–497; see [[river-in-flood]]), and when the armies first meet, their roar is the roar of two winter torrents rushing together (4.452–456; see [[torrents-meeting]]). The image is turned round in Book 15. Hector, testing the ranks, cannot break them, because they hold “like a tower” (πυργηδόν), like a great sheer rock beside the gray sea that withstands the winds and the swelling waves (15.615–622). In Book 14 Hector himself is struck by Ajax's stone and spun like a top (14.409–413).

## Outside Homer

The word passed into oracles. When Eetion went to Delphi to ask about children, the Pythia told him that his wife Labda was with child and would bear an ὀλοοίτροχος, a rolling boulder, which would fall on the men who ruled alone and bring justice to Corinth (Herodotus 5.92β.2; see [[herodotus]]). The child was Cypselus, who made himself tyrant of Corinth (5.92ε.1–2). In Herodotus' own prose the word is a weapon. The Athenians who held the Acropolis against Xerxes rolled boulders (ὀλοιτρόχους) down on the Persians as they came up to the gates (Herodotus 8.52.2).
