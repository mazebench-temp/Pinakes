## The simile

{{quote:6bb8944d-f29b-53eb-9d43-a3c5e5806fca}}

μή πως ὡς ἀψῖσι λίνου ἁλόντε πανάγρου / ἀνδράσι δυσμενέεσσιν ἕλωρ καὶ κύρμα γένησθε: “beware that you are not caught as if in the meshes of an all-catching net, and become the prey and spoil of hostile men”. ἀψῖδες are the meshes, the loops of a net (ἁψίς, from ἅπτω, “fasten”). λίνον is flax, and so anything made of it: linen, a fishing line (16.408), a net. πάναγρος, “catching all” (πᾶς “all” and ἄγρα “catch, prey”), occurs only here in Homer. ἁλόντε, “caught”, is dual, although Sarpedon is speaking to Hector and the verb that follows is plural; perhaps Hector and his people are thought of as two parties (see the translation note on 5.487).

ἕλωρ καὶ κύρμα, “prey and spoil”, are what a hunter seizes (ἕλωρ, from ἑλεῖν, “take”) and what one comes upon (κύρμα, from κυρέω, “light upon”). The pair is used of bodies left for birds and beasts. The point of likeness is capture in bulk. A net does not take one animal but everything inside it, and the Trojans, standing idle, will be taken all together. The next line says how: “and they will soon sack your well-peopled city” (5.489; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).

## In context

The net ends the reproach that began with the dogs (5.471–477; see [[dogs-around-a-lion]]). Hector's kinsmen cower, the allies fight, and Hector stands idle and does not even tell the rest of the people “to stand firm and defend their wives” (5.485–486). After the net, Sarpedon tells him what his duty is, night and day: to beg the leaders of the allies to hold on (5.490–492). Hector leaps from his chariot and rallies the Trojans (5.493–497). See [[sarpedon]] and [[hector]].

The words come back to the man who spoke them. Wounded by Tlepolemus' spear, Sarpedon begs Hector not to leave him “as prey for the Danaans” (ἕλωρ, 5.684–685; see [[tlepolemus-and-sarpedon]]). When he is dead, in Book 17, Glaucus reproaches Hector for leaving Sarpedon, his guest and companion, “to become the prey and spoil of the Argives” (ἕλωρ καὶ κύρμα, 17.150–153).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Nets are rare in the Iliad. Its one fisherman is in a simile of Book 16, where Patroclus hauls Thestor out of his chariot on his spear as a man sitting on a jutting rock draws a fish from the sea with line and bronze hook (16.406–410). In the Odyssey, the suitors lie heaped in the hall like fish that fishermen have drawn out of the sea onto the beach in a net of many meshes (δικτύῳ … πολυωπῷ, Odyssey 22.383–389), and the maids hang in a row like thrushes or doves caught in a snare set in a thicket (Odyssey 22.468–472; see [[doves]]). The pair ἕλωρ καὶ κύρμα is used of the bard whom Aegisthus left on a desert island for the birds (Odyssey 3.270–271), and of the fate Odysseus fears, washed up exhausted on the shore of Scheria, if he goes up into the woods: to become the prey and spoil of wild beasts (Odyssey 5.473).
