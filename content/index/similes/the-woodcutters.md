## The simile

{{quote:6a01dc0d-1f92-5e96-9d78-7cf9858359d4}}

τῶν δʼ ὥς τε δρυτόμων ἀνδρῶν ὀρυμαγδὸς ὀρώρει / οὔρεος ἐν βήσσῃς, ἕκαθεν δέ τε γίγνετʼ ἀκουή: “And as the din of woodcutters rises / in the glens of a mountain, and the sound is heard from far away” (16.633–634). δρυτόμος, “oak-cutter” (δρῦς, “oak”, and τέμνω, “cut”), is the Iliad's word for the woodcutter; it is used three times, here, in the woodcutter who makes his meal in a mountain glen when his arms are tired of felling tall trees (11.86–89), and in Nestor's saying that the woodcutter does better by skill than by strength (23.315). ὀρυμαγδὸς ὀρώρει, “the din rose”, ends the verses in which the Trojan army pours out of the city and the two armies meet (2.810, 4.449; see [[the-armies-clash]]); here it is given to the woodcutters. ἀκουή, “hearing”, is “the sound” as it reaches a listener, and it is heard ἕκαθεν, “from afar”.

The tenor: ὣς τῶν ὄρνυτο δοῦπος ἀπὸ χθονὸς εὐρυοδείης / χαλκοῦ τε ῥινοῦ τε βοῶν τʼ εὐποιητάων, / νυσσομένων ξίφεσίν τε καὶ ἔγχεσιν ἀμφιγύοισιν, “so the thudding rose from them, up from the wide-wayed earth, / of bronze and hide and well-made shields of oxhide, / as they stabbed at one another with swords and double-pointed spears” (16.635–637). δοῦπος is a heavy, dull sound: the roar of torrents heard by a shepherd (4.455; see [[torrents-meeting]]), and the thud of a man falling, δούπησεν δὲ πεσών, “he fell with a thud”, a verse that ends four deaths in this book (16.325, 16.401, 16.599, 16.822; see [[fell-with-a-thud]]). The bronze, the hide and the oxhides are the layers of the shields (βόες, “oxen”, are shields of oxhide; see [[shield]]). The last verse is the one that told the fighting Nestor saw when he came out of his hut (16.637 = 14.26).

The point of likeness is the sound: blow after blow of axes on oak, carried far through the glens, is the sound of swords and spears on shields. The simile is heard, not seen. The woodcutters' work is felling, and in this part of the book a man has just fallen like a felled tree: Sarpedon, over whose body they fight, went down as an oak or a poplar or a tall pine falls under the newly sharpened axes of the builders in the mountains (16.482–484; see [[the-felled-tree]]).

## In context

Glaucus has rallied the Lycians and the Trojans to fight for Sarpedon's body, and Patroclus the Achaeans; Zeus has spread deadly night over the battle, Hector has killed Epeigeus, and Meriones and Aeneas have traded spears and taunts (16.508–625; see [[sarpedon]] and [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]). Patroclus rebukes Meriones: “For the outcome of war lies in our hands, and the outcome of words in council; so there is no need to heap up words, but to fight” (16.630–631). The woodcutters' din follows at once, as if the poem turned from words to blows. Then the body is described as no one could know it now: “Nor could even a sharp-eyed man any longer have recognized brilliant Sarpedon, since with missiles and blood and dust he was wrapped from his head to the tips of his feet” (16.638–640). A second simile follows, of flies buzzing around the milk pails (16.641–644; see [[flies-around-the-milk-pails]]). Both are pictures of work in the country, the axes of the woodcutters and the dairy of a farm in spring, set around the dead man.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Iliad hears its battles in the sounds of nature and of work: the roar of torrents meeting in a ravine (4.452–456), the wave, the fire and the wind of Book 14 (14.394–401; see [[louder-than-sea-fire-and-wind]]), and, later in this book, the din of the East Wind and the South Wind breaking the branches of a forest (16.765–771; see [[the-winds-in-the-forest]]). The woodcutters' work comes into the story itself in the funeral of Patroclus, when the men sent to Ida with woodcutting axes and ropes cut down the high-leafed oaks with the long-edged bronze, “and they fell with a great crash” (23.114–120).
