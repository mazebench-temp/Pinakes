## The simile

ἔσταν δʼ ἐν λειμῶνι Σκαμανδρίῳ ἀνθεμόεντι / μυρίοι, ὅσσά τε φύλλα καὶ ἄνθεα γίγνεται ὥρῃ: “and they stood in the flowering meadow of the Scamander, numberless, as the leaves and flowers that grow in their season”. μυρίοι means “numberless, countless”. φύλλα are leaves, ἄνθεα flowers, and ὥρῃ is “in season”, that is, in spring.

The comparison is one of number: as many men as there are leaves and flowers in spring. It grows out of the scene itself. The army stands in a meadow that is ἀνθεμόεις (“flowering”), and the men are compared with the flowers around them.

## In context

{{quote:2522049d-cd1a-5c03-85ae-944d6fc34c5c}}

The men have poured onto the plain like the birds of the Asian meadow ([[birds-on-the-asian-meadow]]). Now they stand still, and the similes turn to their number. This line and the flies that follow ([[flies-around-the-milk-pails]]) both measure the army, and both are set in spring, like the bees that brought it to the assembly, flying “over the flowers of spring” (ἐπʼ ἄνθεσιν εἰαρινοῖσιν, 2.89; [[swarming-bees]]).

μυρίοι looks ahead to the poet's confession a few lines later: “the multitude I could not tell or name, not even if I had ten tongues and ten mouths” (2.488–489; [[ten-tongues]], [[invocation-of-the-muses]]). And Iris uses the same measure from the Trojan side. The Achaeans, she warns, are coming “like leaves or like the sands” (2.800; [[like-leaves-or-sand]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Nearly the same half-line counts the Cicones who come down on Odysseus' men in the morning, as many as the leaves and flowers that grow in their season (Odyssey 9.51). The Iliad's most famous leaves are those of Glaucus' answer to Diomedes: as are the generations of leaves, so are those of men (Iliad 6.146–149). There too the leaves grow when “the season of spring” comes (ἔαρος … ὥρη, 6.148), but they measure the shortness of a life, not the size of an army.
