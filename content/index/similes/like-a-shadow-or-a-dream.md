## The simile

{{quote:0f262f31-da89-52d4-975d-6f5eca7b87f4}}

τρὶς μὲν ἐφωρμήθην, ἑλέειν τέ με θυμὸς ἀνώγει, / τρὶς δέ μοι ἐκ χειρῶν σκιῇ εἴκελον ἢ καὶ ὀνείρῳ / ἔπτατʼ: “Three times I sprang toward her, and my heart bade me clasp her, / and three times she flew from my hands like a shadow or a dream” (*Odyssey* 11.206–208). σκιή is a shadow, the word the dead themselves are called by when Circe says that Teiresias alone has a mind, “but the others flit as shadows”, τοὶ δὲ σκιαὶ ἀίσσουσιν (*Odyssey* 10.495; see [[psyche]] and the Odyssey translation's row σκιή, ὄνειρος). ὄνειρος is a dream (see [[dream]]). εἴκελον, “like”, is neuter, though it describes the soul or the mother: W. W. Merry takes it as an adverb, as ἶσον is used a few hundred verses later, where the Achaeans grieve for Ajax “as much as” for Achilles (*Odyssey* 11.557), though Nitzsch called it almost a noun; otherwise it is a neuter adjective set in loose apposition to a noun of another gender (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.207). A scholion records a variant ἰκέλη, agreeing with ψυχή, and Bekker printed ἴκελον for the metre, since εἴκελον begins with a digamma (Merry's critical note on *Odyssey* 11.207). ἔπτατο, “flew”, is a bird's verb. The repetition is in the grief too: ἄχος ὀξὺ γενέσκετο, “sharp grief rose” (*Odyssey* 11.208), is an iterative, suited to the three tries, for each disappointed effort brought its sorrow (Merry on *Odyssey* 11.208).

The second comparison is Anticleia's answer:

{{quote:98e0fb00-f7c1-5abc-b938-9e1e932b8690}}

ψυχὴ δʼ ἠύτʼ ὄνειρος ἀποπταμένη πεπότηται: “and the soul, like a dream, flits away and hovers” (*Odyssey* 11.222). The dream that Zeus sent to Agamemnon left him in the same way, ᾤχετʼ ἀποπτάμενος, “and flew away and was gone” (*Iliad* 2.71), with the same participle. πεπότηται is a perfect of continuing state, “hovers to and fro”, as the bees of the Iliad's simile πεποτήαται, are on the wing here and there (*Iliad* 2.90); Nitzsch took it as “is gone” (Merry on *Odyssey* 11.222). The first comparison gives what Odysseus feels, the second what the soul is. The soul is no phantom sent to deceive him, as he fears (*Odyssey* 11.213–214); it is all that remains when the sinews no longer hold the flesh and the bones and the fire has overcome them, “as soon as the spirit has left the white bones” (*Odyssey* 11.219–221; see [[thymos]] and [[the-way-of-mortals]]). Both likenesses are of things seen and not held.

## In context

Anticleia has drunk the blood, known her son, asked how he came alive into the darkness, and told him of Penelope, Telemachus and Laertes, and of her own death: no sickness and no arrow of Artemis killed her, “but longing for you, and for your counsels, shining Odysseus, / and for your gentleness, robbed me of my honey-sweet life” (*Odyssey* 11.152–203; see [[anticleia]]). Odysseus wishes to clasp her (*Odyssey* 11.204–205), and the three embraces follow. He asks her why she does not stay for him, “so that even in the house of Hades, throwing our arms around each other, / we two might take our fill of chilling lament”, or whether noble Persephone has sent a phantom to make him mourn the more (*Odyssey* 11.210–214; see [[persephone]] and [[eidolon]]). She answers that Persephone does not deceive him, explains what the dead are, and sends him back: “But long for the light as quickly as you can” (*Odyssey* 11.216–224).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Iliad has the same scene with the same failure. When the soul of Patroclus comes to Achilles in his sleep, Achilles reaches out with his hands and cannot take hold of it, “and the soul went beneath the earth like smoke, / gibbering” (*Iliad* 23.99–101; see [[like-smoke]] and [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]]); there it was smoke, here a shadow and a dream. In the Iliad's pattern of three attempts a fourth follows, when a god stops the hero (see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]); Odysseus' three attempts have no fourth. The phantom that Athena sends to comfort Penelope in her sleep leaves her as such things leave the sleeper, slipping away past the bolt of the doorpost “into the blasts of the winds” (*Odyssey* 4.838–839; see [[eidolon]]). The only simile in Homer drawn from what happens within a dream is the Iliad's chase that cannot end (*Iliad* 22.199–201; see [[as-in-a-dream]]).

## Outside Homer

[[virgil|Virgil]] gives Aeneas the three embraces twice, with Homer's dream in them: when the ghost of Creusa has spoken and vanished into thin air, “three times there I tried to put my arms around her neck; / three times the image, clasped in vain, fled from my hands, / like the light winds and most like a winged dream”, *par levibus ventis volucrique simillima somno* (*Aeneid* 2.792–794), and the same three verses are said of the shade of Anchises in the underworld (*Aeneid* 6.700–702). Merry quotes the first passage on *Odyssey* 11.206.
