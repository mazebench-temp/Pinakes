## The simile

{{quote:b2154e6b-cb51-562d-9d88-97dc5dc5bf5c}}

ὅσσα δὲ μερμήριξε λέων ἀνδρῶν ἐν ὁμίλῳ / δείσας, ὁππότε μιν δόλιον περὶ κύκλον ἄγωσι: “And as much as a lion ponders in a throng of men / in fear, when they draw their crafty circle around him” (*Odyssey* 4.791–792). ὅσσα … τόσσα, “as much as … so much”, measure the likeness by the quantity of thought: the lion ponders as much as Penelope does (ὁρμαίνουσαν, *Odyssey* 4.793, takes up ὁρμαίνουσʼ of *Odyssey* 4.789). μερμηρίζω, “ponder”, is the verb of a man weighing what to do, of Menelaus in this book (*Odyssey* 4.117) and of Aegisthus planning the murder (*Odyssey* 4.533); here it is given to the beast. The hunters' circle is δόλιος, “crafty”, the word of Proteus' crafty art and of Aegisthus' crafty scheme earlier in the book (*Odyssey* 4.455, 4.529; see [[dolos]]).

W. W. Merry, after Eustathius, puts the point of the comparison in the fear: Penelope is likened not to a lion made bold but to a lion afraid, and pondering because it is afraid. The poet chose the lion, he adds, because it brings in the designs of the suitors, who like huntsmen draw a cordon of men around the beast and narrow the circle (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.791). The suitors have already launched their ship, and wait for evening to sail out and lie in wait for her son (*Odyssey* 4.778–786, 4.842–847; see [[ambush]]).

## In context

The herald Medon has told Penelope that the suitors mean to kill Telemachus on his way home (*Odyssey* 4.696–702; see [[medon-the-herald]]). She sits on the threshold of her chamber and laments among her women, would send old Dolius to Laertes, is told by Eurycleia that she knew of the voyage, and on her advice bathes, goes up to her upper room and prays to Athena with barley grains in a basket (*Odyssey* 4.716–767; see [[penelope]], [[eurycleia]] and [[prayer]]). The suitors launch their ship and wait for evening (*Odyssey* 4.768–786). Then the narrative turns back to her, lying without food or drink and pondering her son's fate (*Odyssey* 4.787–790), and the simile follows. When sleep comes, Athena sends her the phantom of her sister Iphthime to comfort her (*Odyssey* 4.795–841; see [[iphthime]] and [[eidolon]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the Iliad the lion among hunters fights. Hector turns among the Achaeans like a boar or a lion among hounds and hunting men, whose heart “never fears or flees, and it is his pride that kills him” (*Iliad* 12.41–50; see [[the-boar-or-lion-at-bay]]); Achilles goes against Aeneas like a lion that a whole people has gathered to kill (*Iliad* 20.164–175; see [[wounded-lion]]); and even the lion of Ajax's retreat, which dreads the blazing torches, goes off unwilling at dawn (*Iliad* 11.548–557; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]). The lion of the Odyssey's simile does not fight: it is the hunted, held by fear.

In the same book Menelaus has made Odysseus the lion who comes home to his lair (*Odyssey* 4.333–340; see [[the-hind-in-the-lions-lair]]), and Penelope twice calls her husband θυμολέων, “lion-hearted” (*Odyssey* 4.724, 4.814; see [[lion-hearted]] and [[lion]]).
