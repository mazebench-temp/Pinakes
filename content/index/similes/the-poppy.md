## The simile

{{quote:76b95180-d01e-503b-9122-23709b6957cd}}

μήκων δʼ ὡς ἑτέρωσε κάρη βάλεν, ἥ τʼ ἐνὶ κήπῳ / καρπῷ βριθομένη νοτίῃσί τε εἰαρινῇσιν, / ὣς ἑτέρωσʼ ἤμυσε κάρη πήληκι βαρυνθέν. μήκων, the poppy, occurs only here in Homer. It grows ἐνὶ κήπῳ, “in a garden”, and κῆπος is rare in the Iliad too: the only other garden is in a simile of irrigation, where a man leads water from a spring among his plants and garden plots (21.257–259). καρπῷ βριθομένη is “weighed down with its fruit”, the seed head, and νοτίῃσί τε εἰαρινῇσιν “and with the showers of spring” (νοτίαι, “rains”, from νότιος, “wet”). The poppy bows under two weights at once, its own ripeness and the rain. The man's head bows under one: πήληκι βαρυνθέν, “weighed down by his helmet” (see [[helmet]]). The verb ἠμύω, “bow, sink”, is used of a field of grain bowing under the West Wind (2.148; see [[wind-in-the-wheat]]) and of Achilles' horse Xanthus, who bows his head before he foretells his master's death (19.404–417).

The ὡς of the first line is printed without an accent, as the conjunction “as”, so that the poppy is the subject of βάλεν and the ὣς of the third line answers it: “as a poppy droops its head to one side …, so his head drooped”. Read with an accent, ὥς, “like a poppy”, the verb would be Gorgythion's, and he would let his head fall like a poppy. The sense is the same either way (see the translation's note on 8.306–308).

The comparison is exact in its point, the angle of a dead man's head on the neck, and it carries more than the point. The flower grows in a garden, where it is tended; it is in its season, full and ripe; and what bends it comes from outside. The helmet, which takes the place of the rain, is the one thing in the picture that belongs to war.

## In context

Teucer has shot eight men from behind Ajax's shield and been praised by Agamemnon (8.266–291; see [[like-a-child-under-its-mother]]). Now he aims at Hector, “and his heart was eager to hit him” (8.301), and misses. The arrow strikes another man, and the narrator stops to say who he was: a son of Priam, whose mother, a bride brought from Aesyme, was “lovely Castianeira, like the goddesses in form” (8.302–305; see [[gorgythion]], [[castianeira]], [[aesyme]], [[like-the-goddesses]] and [[sons-of-priam]]). A dead man's mother and her beauty are named, and then the flower. The next arrow, aimed at Hector again, is turned aside by Apollo and kills Hector's charioteer Archeptolemus (8.309–315; see [[archeptolemus]]). Hector then fells Teucer with a stone (8.320–329; see [[teucer]] and [[hector]]).

Gorgythion is the only man who dies in Book 8 with a simile, and he dies by mistake, in place of his brother. The Iliad gives such a likeness from the growing world to young men of whom it has just told something more than a name. Simoeisius, born on the banks of the Simoeis, falls like a poplar that a chariot-maker has cut (4.473–487; see [[the-poplar]]). Euphorbus, “the son of Panthous with the good ash spear”, falls like an olive sapling that a man has reared in a lonely place with plenty of water, white with blossom, until a sudden storm uproots it (17.53–60).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Iliad has one other poppy, and it is grim. Peneleos drives his spear into the eye of Ilioneus, strikes off his head with his sword, “the head, helmet and all” (αὐτῇ σὺν πήληκι κάρη), and holds it up on the spear “like a poppy head” (φὴ κώδειαν ἀνασχών), to show the Trojans as he boasts (14.493–500). The head and the helmet are the words of Gorgythion's line, and κώδεια is the seed head of the poppy, the καρπός of this simile.

## Outside Homer

In the *Geryoneis* of [[stesichorus|Stesichorus]], known from papyrus (P.Oxy. 2617; fr. S15), the dying Geryon bends his neck to one side like a poppy that spoils its tender form and sheds its petals. [[virgil|Virgil]] joined the poppy to a second flower for the death of Euryalus: he droops like a purple flower cut by the plough, or like poppies that bow their heads on weary necks when they are weighed down by rain (*Aeneid* 9.435–437).
