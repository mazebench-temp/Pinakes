## The simile

{{quote:1759684c-d9a1-594b-b0fe-6108aa5ad7eb}}

δακρυόεσσα δʼ ὕπαιθα θεὰ φύγεν ὥς τε πέλεια, / ἥ ῥά θʼ ὑπʼ ἴρηκος κοίλην εἰσέπτατο πέτρην, / χηραμόν: “And the goddess fled from under her in tears, like a dove / that flies from a hawk into a hollow rock, / a cleft” (21.493–495). πέλεια is the wild dove, most likely the rock dove, which nests in cliffs and hollows of the rock (see [[doves]]); ἴρηξ is the hawk, the swiftest of birds and the dove's killer (see [[hawk]] and [[like-a-hawk]]). εἰσέπτατο, “flies”, is the aorist of the simile, for what such a bird does. χηραμός, “a cleft”, is found only here in Homer; Walter Leaf notes that the word recurs in Aristotle and later writers, and compares χειή, the hole by which the mountain snake lies in wait for a man (22.93; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.495).

οὐδʼ ἄρα τῇ γε ἁλώμεναι αἴσιμον ἦεν, “nor was it her fate to be caught” (21.495): the clause is the bird's, and it suits the goddess, who escapes from under Hera's hands as the dove escapes into the rock (see [[aisa]]). The tenor follows: ὣς ἣ δακρυόεσσα φύγεν, λίπε δʼ αὐτόθι τόξα, “so she fled in tears, and left her bow there” (21.496). The archer leaves her weapon behind, and her mother picks it up (21.502–504; see [[leto]]).

## In context

Artemis has taunted her brother for yielding to Poseidon, and Hera, in anger, has called her “you shameless dog”, a lion only to women, who would do better to slay wild beasts on the mountains than to fight those stronger than she (21.470–488; see [[artemis]], [[a-lion-to-women]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). Then Hera seizes both her wrists in her left hand, takes the bow from her shoulders with her right, and beats her about the ears with it, smiling, while the arrows fall out (21.489–492; see [[hera]]). The simile turns the huntress into the hunted: the mistress of wild beasts flees like the bird that Homer's hawks hunt. She goes up to Olympus and sits weeping on her father's knees (21.505–513; see [[zeus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The dove is prey in Homer. Apollo goes down from Ida like a hawk, “a swift dove-killer” (15.237–238; see [[like-a-hawk]]); when Achilles chases Hector round the walls, he is like a falcon in the mountains swooping on a timid dove, which flees from under him (ἣ δέ θʼ ὕπαιθα φοβεῖται, 22.141), with the adverb of Artemis' flight from under Hera, ὕπαιθα (21.493; 22.139–142; see [[the-falcon-and-the-dove]] and [[falcon]]); and in the *Odyssey* a falcon, Apollo's messenger, plucks a dove in its talons over Telemachus' ship (*Odyssey* 15.525–528). Goddesses are like doves once more, when Hera and Athena go to the battle on foot “like timid doves in their step” (5.778; see [[timid-doves]]).
