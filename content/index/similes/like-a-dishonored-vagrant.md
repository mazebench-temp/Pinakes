## The comparison

{{quote:fb3ff388-449c-5976-94ef-61450f7f87e7}}

Ἀτρεΐδης ὡς εἴ τινʼ ἀτίμητον μετανάστην, “the son of Atreus, as if I were some dishonored vagrant”. μετανάστης is a man who has moved his dwelling (μετα-, “change”, and ναίω, “dwell”): a man who has left his own people and lives among strangers, with no kin to defend him and no one to claim a price for a wrong done to him. ἀτίμητος, “dishonored” (ἀ-, “not”, and τιμάω, “honor”), is a man without τιμή, without standing and without the right to satisfaction (see [[time]]). Homer has both words only in this line and in its repetition (16.59). ἀσύφηλον, “with contempt”, in the line before, is found elsewhere only in Helen's lament, where she says that she never heard from Hector a harsh or contemptuous word (24.767).

The comparison names the opposite of everything the envoys have promised. Agamemnon's cities will honor Achilles with gifts “like a god” (9.155, 9.297), the army will honor him like a god (9.302–303), and Phoenix says that the Achaeans will honor him “as equal to a god” if he comes while the gifts are offered (9.603). Achilles answers that Agamemnon has already shown what he thinks him worth: a man without a place or a price (see [[honor-and-dishonor]] and [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).

## In context

Ajax has spoken last and most bluntly, and Achilles grants that everything he said is “after my own heart” (9.645). But his heart swells with anger, οἰδάνεται κραδίη χόλῳ, whenever he remembers (9.646). Phoenix had used the same verb of the anger that came upon Meleager, “the anger that swells the mind in the breasts of others too, even of men who think shrewdly” (οἰδάνει, 9.553–554; see [[cholos]] and [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). The memory turns Achilles' last answer: he will not think of war until Hector reaches the huts and ships of the Myrmidons (9.649–655).

In Book 16 he says the line again to Patroclus, of the girl the Achaeans chose for him as his prize and he won with his spear when he sacked a city, and whom powerful Agamemnon took back from his hands “as if I were some dishonored vagrant” (16.56–59; see [[taking-of-briseis]]). There it introduces his decision to let the past go and send Patroclus out (16.60–63). In the Odyssey the dead Achilles chooses the lowest place among the living over his kingship among the dead: he would rather be bound to the soil as a hired man for another, a man without land of his own, than rule over all the dead (*Odyssey* 11.488–491).

## Outside Homer

[[Aristotle]] quotes the line twice. In the *Rhetoric* it shows how men who are slighted speak of the slight, beside Achilles' complaint that Agamemnon “has dishonored me” (*Rhetoric* 2.2, 1378b; see [[aristotle-anger]]). In the *Politics* it defines the citizen as the man who has a share in the honors, that is, in the offices of the city: “as Homer wrote, ‘as if I were some dishonored vagrant’; for the man who has no share in the honors is like a resident alien” (*Politics* 3.5, 1278a34–38).
