## The simile

{{quote:7f7e4b7d-deb6-5bf3-a419-66bdf66d0db8}}

αἳ δὲ βάτην τρήρωσι πελειάσιν ἴθμαθʼ ὁμοῖαι: “and the two went, like timid doves in their step”. βάτην, “the two went”, is dual. πέλεια is the wild dove, most likely the rock dove (see [[doves]]). τρήρων, “timid”, from the root of τρέω, “tremble, flee in fear”, is the dove's standing epithet (τρήρωνα πέλειαν, 22.140, 23.853; see [[timid]]), and it can stand alone for the bird (Odyssey 12.63). ἴθμα, “step, gait”, from εἶμι, “go”, occurs only here in Homer: ἴθματα, “in their steps”, limits the likeness to the way they move.

The goddesses are neither timid nor weak. One carries the aegis and a spear with which she overpowers ranks of heroes (5.738–747), and the other has come to see the war god driven from the field. The likeness has been taken in more than one way (see the translation note on 5.778). It may lie in their speed, the hurried going of birds, which the next line supports: they went “eager to bring help to the Argive men” (5.779). It may lie in the small, quick steps of doves walking on the ground, gods going on foot for once after their flight through the air. τρήρων may be only the fixed epithet of the bird and say nothing of the goddesses' mood.

## In context

The goddesses have crossed from Olympus in Hera's chariot, each leap of the horses as long as a man's view over the sea (5.768–772; see [[the-lookout]]). At the meeting of the Simoeis and the Scamander Hera unyokes the horses, pours a thick mist around them, and the river makes ambrosia spring up for them to graze (5.773–777; see [[simoeis]]). Then they go on foot. They find the best of the Argives crowded around Diomedes “like lions that eat raw flesh or wild boars” (5.782–783; see [[lions-and-boars]]): the goddesses come like doves to men like lions. Hera shouts in the likeness of Stentor, and Athena goes to Diomedes (5.784–834; see [[stentor]] and [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Goddesses are doves again in the battle of the gods. Hera boxes Artemis' ears with her own bow, and Artemis flees in tears “like a dove that flies from a hawk into a hollow rock” (21.493–496). There the dove is a defeated goddess in flight, and the image fits its usual place in Homer, where the dove is prey: Achilles swoops on Hector as a hawk on a timid dove (22.139–142). See [[doves]].

## Outside Homer

The same line, with ποσί (“with their feet”) added, is used in the Homeric *Hymn to Apollo* of Iris and Eileithyia as they go to Delos to help Leto give birth: βὰν δὲ ποσὶ τρήρωσι πελειάσιν ἴθμαθʼ ὁμοῖαι (*Hymn to Apollo* 114). There too two goddesses go on an errand of help, and there too the likeness lies in their going.
