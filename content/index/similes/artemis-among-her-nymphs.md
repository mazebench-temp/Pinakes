## The simile

{{quote:e782403c-e446-5763-80f0-de37e83064c1}}

οἵη δʼ Ἄρτεμις εἶσι κατʼ οὔρεα ἰοχέαιρα, “And as Artemis of the showering arrows goes over the mountains” (*Odyssey* 6.102; see [[artemis]] and [[of-the-showering-arrows]]). The text prints κατʼ οὔρεα, which a scholium calls the better reading; Merry's text has κατʼ οὔρεος, “down from the mountain”, and he pictures the goddess coming down from a peak and then ranging along the ridges, “along towering Taygetus or Erymanthus” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.102, and his critical note; see the translation's note on 6.102, [[taygetus]] and [[erymanthus]]). The two ranges are named only here in Homer, one in Laconia and one between Arcadia, Achaea and Elis, as Merry places them. Her quarry is the game of the mountains, τερπομένη κάπροισι καὶ ὠκείῃς ἐλάφοισι, “delighting in boars and swift deer” (*Odyssey* 6.104; see [[boar]] and [[deer]]).

Her company are the nymphs, “daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus, / who haunt the fields”, and they play (*Odyssey* 6.105–106; see [[nymphs]]). ἀγρονόμοι is accented on the penult, Merry explains, because νέμω and νέμομαι are used actively of haunting a place, as the nymphs of the Iliad “dwell in the lovely groves” (αἵ τʼ ἄλσεα καλὰ νέμονται, *Iliad* 20.8); some accented it on the antepenult, and Megaclides read a different verse, ἀγρόμεναι παίζουσιν ἀνὰ δρία παιπαλόεντα, “gathered, they play among the rugged thickets” (Merry on 6.106 and his critical note; see the translation's note on 6.106). παίζουσι, “play”, is the verb of the girls' game with the ball (ἔπαιζον, *Odyssey* 6.100), and Merry takes it here with the notion of hunting, what we call “sport”, comparing Sophocles, *Electra* 566, where the speaker's father, “sporting” in the grove of the goddess, starts a dappled stag (on 6.106). The game of the girls and the hunt of the goddess are one word.

γέγηθε δέ τε φρένα Λητώ, “and Leto is glad at heart” (*Odyssey* 6.106; see [[leto]]). The half-verse is the shepherd's in the Iliad's simile of the watch-fires, who sees all the stars shine out around the moon when the upper air is windless, “and the shepherd is glad at heart” (*Iliad* 8.559; see [[stars-around-the-moon]]): there a watcher rejoices at the brightness he sees, here a mother at her daughter. Then the point of likeness: “above them all she holds her head and brow, / and she is easy to know, though all are beautiful” (*Odyssey* 6.107–108). ὑπὲρ … κάρη ἔχει is most simply “lifts her head above all”, as the stallion of the Iliad “holds his head high” (ὑψοῦ δὲ κάρη ἔχει, *Iliad* 6.509); but Menelaus, who “rose above” Odysseus “by his broad shoulders”, and Ajax, “towering above the Argives by his head and broad shoulders” (*Iliad* 3.210, 3.227), rather support the sense “overtops them by head and shoulders”, and Merry weighs the two (on 6.107; see the translation's note on 6.107). ῥεῖά τʼ ἀριγνώτη, “easy to know”, is said again of the house of Alcinous: ῥεῖα δʼ ἀρίγνωτʼ ἐστί, “It is easy to know, and even a child could lead you” (*Odyssey* 6.300). The simile closes on the tenor: ὣς ἥ γʼ ἀμφιπόλοισι μετέπρεπε παρθένος ἀδμής, “so she stood out among her handmaids, a maiden unwed” (*Odyssey* 6.109; see [[handmaids]]).

The likeness is of a girl among girls. Nausicaa is not hunting, and her handmaids are not goddesses; what passes from the goddess to the princess is the height, the ease with which she is known among companions who are all beautiful, and the joy of the parent who watches. The last word of the simile, ἀδμής, “unwed”, is the epithet of the heifer and the mules that no one has yet broken to the yoke (*Iliad* 10.293; *Odyssey* 4.637; see [[unbroken]]), and Artemis is ἁγνή, “chaste” (*Odyssey* 5.123, 18.202, 20.71). The dream that sent Nausicaa to the river told her that she “will not be a maiden much longer” (*Odyssey* 6.33; see [[marriage]]).

## In context

When the clothes are washed and spread to dry on the shore, the girls bathe, anoint themselves and eat beside the river; then “they played with a ball, throwing off their veils; / and among them white-armed Nausicaa began the song and dance” (*Odyssey* 6.100–101; see [[nausicaa]], [[white-armed]] and [[dance]]). The simile follows at once. Then Athena turns the game to her purpose: Nausicaa throws the ball to one of her handmaids, misses her and throws it into a deep eddy, the girls shout aloud, and Odysseus wakes (*Odyssey* 6.110–117; see [[athena]] and [[odysseus]]).

The simile prepares his first words. Coming out of the bushes to the one girl who stands her ground, Odysseus asks whether she is a goddess or a mortal, and if a goddess, “to Artemis, the daughter of great Zeus, / I liken you most nearly, in looks and stature and build” (*Odyssey* 6.150–152; see [[divine-epiphany]]). The poet has made the likeness before the hero makes it, and the hero's guess is the one the poem has already given. Odysseus goes on to the parents and brothers whose hearts are “warmed with gladness” as they watch such a scion going into the dance (*Odyssey* 6.153–157; see [[three-times-blessed]]): the gladness of Leto in the simile is the gladness he imagines in Nausicaa's family.

## Elsewhere in Homer

Artemis is the likeness of a woman's beauty in the Odyssey. Helen comes out of her chamber “like Artemis of the golden distaff” (*Odyssey* 4.122), and Penelope twice comes out of hers “like Artemis or golden Aphrodite” (Ἀρτέμιδι ἰκέλη ἠὲ χρυσέῃ Ἀφροδίτῃ, *Odyssey* 17.37 = 19.54, a plain rendering; see [[helen]] and [[penelope]]). Among her gifts to women is stature: in Penelope's tale of the daughters of Pandareus, Hera gave them beauty and good sense, and “chaste Artemis gave them tallness” (μῆκος δʼ ἔπορʼ Ἄρτεμις ἁγνή, *Odyssey* 20.71, a plain rendering), the gift for which Nausicaa stands out here.

Her dance and her hunt are both in the Iliad. Hermes fell in love with Polymele when he saw her among the girls singing and dancing “in the dance of Artemis of the golden distaff, the clamorous” (*Iliad* 16.180–183; see [[polymele]]). Artemis taught Scamandrius “to shoot all the wild things that the forest nurtures in the mountains” (*Iliad* 5.51–52; see [[scamandrius]]), and Hera, mocking her in the battle of the gods, tells her that it is better “to slay wild beasts on the mountains / and wild deer” than to fight with those stronger (*Iliad* 21.485–486; see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]).

## Outside Homer

The short *Hymn to Artemis* gives the “revered maiden” (παρθένον αἰδοίην) the same two pursuits in turn. She ranges over the shadowy mountains and windy peaks “delighting in the hunt” (ἄγρῃ τερπομένη), and when she has had her pleasure she goes to her brother's house at Delphi and leads the dances of the Muses and the Graces, who sing of Leto and her children (*Homeric Hymn* 27.2–5, 27.11–20). The *Hymn to Aphrodite* names her among the three goddesses whom Aphrodite cannot subdue in love: she loves the bow and the slaying of beasts on the mountains, lyres, dances and piercing cries (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 16–20).

Virgil turned the simile to Dido, coming to the temple with a throng of young men about her: as Diana leads her dances on the banks of the Eurotas or along the ridges of Cynthus, with a thousand mountain nymphs gathering round her, carries her quiver on her shoulder and stands out above all the goddesses, while joy thrills Latona's silent heart, *Latonae tacitum pertemptant gaudia pectus*, “such was Dido” (*Aeneid* 1.496–504; see [[virgils-dido-as-diana]] and [[virgil]]). Valerius Probus, as Gellius reports, judged that Virgil had turned nothing from Homer so unhappily as these verses: Nausicaa at play among her friends in a lonely place is fitly likened to Diana hunting on the mountain ridges among the goddesses of the wild, but Dido walking through the middle of her city among the Tyrian lords, intent on her work and her kingdom to come, has nothing that answers to the play and the hunting of the goddess; Homer tells plainly of Diana's delight in the chase, while Virgil, saying nothing of her hunting, only gives her a quiver to carry like a burden; and Virgil followed only scantily the flower of the whole passage, the verse “she is easy to know, though all are beautiful”, when no greater praise of beauty could be spoken than that one should excel among all who are beautiful and be easily known among them all (Gellius, *Attic Nights* 9.9, as Merry quotes it on 6.102; see the translation's note on 6.102–109).

Merry adds that Erymanthus was the fabled haunt of the great boar slain by Heracles, so that the boars of *Odyssey* 6.104 are peculiarly fitting there (on 6.102).
