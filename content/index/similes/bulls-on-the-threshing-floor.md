## The simile

{{quote:68c4ea42-acff-57a1-a117-8e7d383edaad}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτε τις ζεύξῃ βόας ἄρσενας εὐρυμετώπους / τριβέμεναι κρῖ λευκὸν ἐϋκτιμένῃ ἐν ἀλωῇ, / ῥίμφά τε λέπτʼ ἐγένοντο βοῶν ὑπὸ πόσσʼ ἐριμύκων: “As when a man yokes broad-browed bulls / to tread out white barley on a well-built threshing floor, / and quickly it is husked beneath the feet of the loud-bellowing bulls” (20.495–497). βόες ἄρσενες are male cattle, rendered “bulls” (see [[bulls]] and [[cattle]]); they are εὐρυμέτωποι, “broad-browed”, and ἐρίμυκοι, “loud-bellowing” (see [[broad-browed]] and [[loud-bellowing]]). τρίβω, “rub”, is used of threshing only here in Homer: the beasts are driven round and round the floor, and their hooves break the ears and free the grain. κρῖ λευκόν, “white barley”, is elsewhere the fodder that horses munch beside their chariots (5.196, 8.564; see [[white]]). The ἀλωή is the threshing floor here, as in the similes of the winnowers and of the beans (5.499, 13.588); elsewhere it is an orchard or a vineyard (see the translation's table), and ἐϋκτιμένῃ ἐν ἀλωῇ, “on a well-built threshing floor”, are the words with which Lycaon, in the next book, recalls the orchard where Achilles once caught him (21.77; see [[well-built]]). λέπτʼ ἐγένοντο, “it is husked”: λεπτός keeps here, Walter Leaf thinks, its first sense as a verbal adjective of λέπω, “peel”, “shelled out from the husk” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.497; see the translation's note on 20.497).

The tenor: ὣς ὑπʼ Ἀχιλλῆος μεγαθύμου μώνυχες ἵπποι / στεῖβον ὁμοῦ νέκυάς τε καὶ ἀσπίδας, “so beneath great-hearted Achilles his single-hoofed horses / trampled corpses and shields alike” (20.498–499; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[single-hoofed]]). The point of likeness is the treading. The horses go over the dead as the bulls go over the grain, and as quickly, and what lies under their hooves is broken. The verses that follow are, with small changes, those of Hector's chariot driving into the battle in Book 11: the axle all spattered beneath with blood, and the rails that ran around the chariot, struck by the drops thrown up from the horses' hooves and from the tires (20.499–502, as 11.534–537; see [[chariot]]). The last verse of the book is the one that ended Agamemnon's pursuit of the Trojans: “the son of Peleus, and his irresistible hands were spattered with gore” (20.503, as 11.169 of the son of Atreus; see [[irresistible-hands]]).

Leaf reports that many editors (Heyne, Bekker, Düntzer and others) rejected 495–503, partly because 499–502 and 503 repeat verses of Book 11, but more because Achilles, who has fought on foot until now, is suddenly in his chariot. He answers that the borrowing is more likely to be in Book 11, where the verses stand among others of doubtful authenticity, that a hero mounts and dismounts as the moment needs without a word said about it, and that the simile of 495–497 is in the best epic style (on 20.495; see the translation's note on 20.495–503).

## In context

The simile is the second of a pair that ends the book and Achilles' first day of killing. Achilles has just killed Dryops, Demuchus, the two sons of Bias, Tros, son of Alastor, Mulius, Echeclus, Deucalion, Rhigmus and Areithous (20.455–489). Then he rushes everywhere with his spear, like a god, as a wondrous fire rages through the deep valleys of a dry mountain, and the black earth runs with blood (20.490–494; see [[forest-fire]]). The fire is the man on foot; the threshing is the man in his chariot, his horses driving over the dead. Earlier in the book a dying Trojan bellowed like a bull dragged to Poseidon's altar (20.403–406; see [[the-bull-of-the-heliconian-lord]]). The next book begins where the rout reaches the ford of the eddying Xanthus (21.1–4).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The threshing floor is the scene of two other similes. When the armies close again, the dust that the horses' hooves beat up whitens the Achaeans as the wind carries the chaff over the sacred threshing floors when men are winnowing (5.499–505; see [[winnowing]]); and Helenus' arrow glances off Menelaus' corselet as dark beans or chickpeas leap from a winnowing shovel across a great threshing floor (13.588–592; see [[beans-on-the-threshing-floor]]). Battle is a harvest in other places too: Trojans and Achaeans cut each other down like reapers driving their swaths through a rich man's field of wheat or barley (11.67–71; see [[the-reapers]]), and Odysseus calls battle a harvest in which the bronze strews the most straw on the ground but the harvest is smallest (19.221–224; see [[satiety-of-battle]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells the farmer to urge his slaves to thresh Demeter's sacred grain when the strength of Orion first appears, in an airy place on a well-rounded threshing floor (ἐυτροχάλῳ ἐν ἀλωῇ, *Works and Days* 597–599). Leaf reports that the variant ἐϋτροχάλῳ at 20.496 comes from that verse (on 20.496).
