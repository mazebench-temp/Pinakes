## The simile

οἷον δʼ ἀστέρα ἧκε Κρόνου πάϊς ἀγκυλομήτεω / ἢ ναύτῃσι τέρας ἠὲ στρατῷ εὐρέϊ λαῶν / λαμπρόν· τοῦ δέ τε πολλοὶ ἀπὸ σπινθῆρες ἵενται: “as the son of crooked-counseling Cronus sends a star as a portent for sailors or for a wide army of men, a bright one, and many sparks fly from it”. ἀστήρ is any star. One that Zeus “sends” (ἧκε) and that throws off sparks (σπινθῆρες, a word found only here in Homer) is a meteor, a shooting star. It is sent as a τέρας, a “portent” (see [[teras]]), and λαμπρόν, “bright”, is held back to the start of the third line. The sender is Zeus, the son of Cronus ([[son-of-cronus]], [[crooked-counseling]]).

The tenor: τῷ ἐϊκυῖʼ ἤϊξεν ἐπὶ χθόνα Παλλὰς Ἀθήνη, / κὰδ δʼ ἔθορʼ ἐς μέσσον, “in its likeness Pallas Athena darted down to the earth, and leapt into their midst”. ἐϊκυῖα is “like, resembling”. ἤϊξεν, “darted”, takes up ἀΐξασα of the line before, where she “went darting down from the peaks of Olympus” (4.74). The point of likeness is a swift, bright fall from the sky into the middle of the armies. The Greek does not say whether the goddess takes the star's form or only moves like one. The watchers see something: amazement holds them, and they take it for a sign.

A shooting star from Zeus is a message, and a message has to be read. The simile names the people who read such signs, sailors and armies, and says nothing of what the sign foretells. That is left to the men who see it.

## In context

{{quote:4e819775-d173-5e7c-a5cd-d2cac0a65191}}

On Olympus Hera and Zeus have agreed that Athena should go down and contrive that the Trojans be the first to do harm “in defiance of the oaths” (4.64–72; see [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]]). Zeus urges her on, “who was eager already” (4.73), and she goes.

The watchers are both armies, “the horse-taming Trojans and the well-greaved Achaeans” (4.80), and amazement (θάμβος) holds them, as it held them when Paris and Menelaus walked out to fight (3.342; see [[thambos]]). What they say is what “a man would say, looking at his neighbor” (see [[tis-speech]]), and it repeats the question that Zeus put to the gods a few lines before: whether evil war and the dread din of battle will come again, or friendship be set between the two sides (4.15–16, 4.82–83; see [[philotes]]). Zeus asked it as a choice for the gods. The men ask it as a question about Zeus, “who is the dispenser of war for men” (ταμίης πολέμοιο, 4.84); the line returns in Odysseus' mouth in Book 19 (19.224). They do not know that the question has been settled, or that the bright thing they saw was Athena on her way to settle it. The audience does. The sign that could mean war or peace is a goddess bringing war.

The star comes down “into their midst”, and there it disappears into a man. Athena slips into the Trojan throng in the likeness of Laodocus, son of Antenor, to find the archer Pandarus (4.86–88; see [[laodocus]] and [[pandarus-breaks-the-truce]]). See also [[divine-epiphany]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

The lines that send her down return when Zeus gives up Hector. There too Zeus has proposed to spare the Trojan side, Athena has objected in the words Hera uses here, “Do it; but not all of us other gods approve” (22.181 = 4.29), and Zeus gives way: “so saying he urged on Athena, who was eager already, and she went darting down from the peaks of Olympus” (22.186–187 = 4.73–74). The second line was used of her in Book 2 as well, when she went down to Odysseus to stop the flight to the ships (2.167).

The Iliad's other stars are seen in their seasons. Diomedes' helmet and shield blaze like the star of late summer (5.4–6); Hector moves among the front ranks like a baneful star that now shines and now hides in the clouds (11.62–65); Achilles runs across the plain like the star called Orion's Dog, “a sign of evil” (22.26–31), and the point of his spear shines like the evening star (22.317–320). This star is sent, and it means something.

Other descents of the gods are given images. Apollo comes down “like the night” (1.47; see [[like-night]]) and Thetis rises from the sea “like a mist” (1.359; see [[like-a-mist]]). Hera and Athena go like doves (5.778), Iris flies down like snow or hail (15.170–172), and Athena drops from the sky like a long-winged bird of prey (19.350–351). The nearest to this one is the rainbow. In Book 17 Athena comes down wrapped in a purple cloud, like the rainbow that Zeus stretches from heaven as a portent (τέρας) “of war or of cold storm” (17.547–552). Both times the goddess comes in the likeness of a sign, and both times the sign is of war.

## Outside Homer

Virgil gives the Trojans a real shooting star on the last night of Troy. When Anchises prays for a sign, a star falls from the sky with a long trail of light and sinks into the woods of Ida, and he agrees to leave the city (*Aeneid* 2.692–704).
