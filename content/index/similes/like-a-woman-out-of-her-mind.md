## The simile

{{quote:36927958-7b72-54e3-ad4c-ce943d817127}}

ἣ μὲν δὴ πρὸς τεῖχος ἐπειγομένη ἀφικάνει / μαινομένῃ ἐϊκυῖα: “she has gone hurrying to the wall like a woman out of her mind”. μαινομένη is the participle of μαίνομαι (“rage, be mad”), used as a noun: “a raving woman”. ἐϊκυῖα is the feminine of ἐοικώς, “like”. The comparison is with a kind of person, not with anything in nature, and it takes only two words. It is a simile of the shortest form, like θηρὶ ἐοικώς, “like a wild beast” (3.449; see [[like-a-wild-beast]]), and ἀΐδρεϊ φωτὶ ἐοικώς, “like a man who knows nothing” (3.219; see [[like-a-man-who-knows-nothing]]). Those two are the narrator's and Antenor's; this one is a servant's. The housekeeper saw her mistress go, and says how she looked.

The point of likeness is the haste and the distraction. She has left the house for the wall because she heard that the Trojans were hard pressed. The words that follow keep the household in the picture: the nurse goes with her, carrying the child.

## In context

Hector has come home to see his wife and son, “for I do not know if I will ever come back to them again” (6.365–368), and does not find her. He asks the slave women where she has gone: to his sisters, to his brothers' wives, or to the temple of Athena with the other Trojan women (6.374–380). The housekeeper answers each guess in turn, and then gives the fourth place, the great tower of Ilios (6.381–389; see [[handmaids]] and [[the-tower-of-ilios]]). Hector hurries back through the streets, and she meets him at the Scaean gates, “running” (θέουσα, 6.394). Her first words to him are a charge: “Strange one, your own might will destroy you” (6.407; see [[hector-and-andromache]]).

Madness runs through Book 6 in the same verb. Helenus says of Diomedes that he “rages beyond measure” (λίην μαίνεται, 6.100–101), and Diomedes tells of the nurses of “raving Dionysus” (μαινομένοιο Διωνύσοιο, 6.132; see [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]]). Anteia was “mad with longing” for Bellerophon (ἐπεμήνατο, 6.160). The warrior's madness is his fury in battle; Andromache's is fear for the man she will lose. Hector answers her with a comparison of his own: he would feel shame before the Trojans “if like a coward I shrink away from the war” (κακὸς ὣς, 6.443; see [[like-a-coward]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Book 22 repeats the scene with the end changed. Andromache is at her loom in the inner room, weaving a double purple cloth and telling her handmaids to heat water for Hector's bath when he comes home, when she hears the wailing from the tower (22.437–448). She runs from the house “like a maenad, her heart pounding” (μαινάδι ἴση παλλομένη κραδίην, 22.460–461), her handmaids go with her, and from the wall she sees Hector dragged behind Achilles' horses toward the ships (22.460–465). μαινάς, “maenad”, occurs only there in Homer; in later Greek it is the name of the women in the rites of Dionysus. In Book 6 she went to the tower on a report of losses and found her husband alive at the gate; in Book 22 she runs to it on the sound of mourning and sees him dead.

The *Homeric Hymn to Demeter* uses the same image for joy. When Hermes brings Persephone back from the underworld, Demeter sees her and rushes to her “like a maenad down a mountain thick with woods” (*Hymn to Demeter* 385–386).
