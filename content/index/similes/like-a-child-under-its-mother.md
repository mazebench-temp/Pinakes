## The simile

{{quote:aabfc98f-6cd2-5550-aa96-3210a7c52486}}

αὐτὰρ ὃ αὖτις ἰὼν πάϊς ὣς ὑπὸ μητέρα δύσκεν / εἰς Αἴανθʼ: “and he would go back again, like a child under its mother, and slip to Ajax”. πάϊς is a child, and ὑπὸ μητέρα with a verb of motion is “under its mother”, to her shelter, as a small child runs to her. The verbs are iterative. Ajax ὑπεξέφερεν, “would move his shield aside”; Teucer, “when he had shot someone” (βεβλήκοι, an optative of repeated action), δύσκεν, “would slip back”; Ajax κρύπτασκε, “would hide him”. The forms in -σκ- mark a routine, repeated as often as Teucer shoots. Its count follows at once: eight men, named in three lines (8.273–277; see [[teucer]]).

The likeness is one of shelter. An archer is exposed while he aims and draws, and Homer's archers shoot from cover. Pandarus' companions hold their shields in front of him while he strings his bow (4.112–115), and Paris shoots at Diomedes leaning against the gravestone of Ilus (11.369–372). Teucer's cover is his brother's shield of seven oxhides, the shield that Ajax carries “like a tower” (7.219–223), and Ajax himself is the “bulwark of the Achaeans” (see [[the-shield-of-ajax]], [[like-a-tower]] and [[bulwark-of-the-achaeans]]).

The comparison turns size and sex around. The biggest of the Achaeans is the mother, and the archer, a grown warrior, is a small child. The two are sons of Telamon by different mothers: Agamemnon, praising Teucer a few lines later, recalls that Telamon reared him in his own house, “bastard though you were” (8.283–284; see [[telamon]] and [[nothos]]). The narrator calls Ajax his κασίγνητος, “brother” (8.330), and in Book 12 Teucer goes with Ajax as “his brother by the same father” (κασίγνητος καὶ ὄπατρος, 12.371; see [[kasignetos]]). The elder brother shelters the younger as a mother shelters her child.

Other mothers in the Iliad's similes guard a child. Athena turns Pandarus' arrow from Menelaus as a mother brushes a fly from her sleeping child (4.130–131; see [[mother-and-fly]]); Menelaus stands over the body of Patroclus as a cow stands over her first calf (17.4–6), and Ajax over the same body as a lion over its cubs (17.133–136). Achilles turns the picture to mockery when Patroclus comes to him in tears: “like a little girl”, who runs beside her mother clutching her dress and asking to be picked up (16.7–11). Teucer's simile is neither mockery nor lament. It shows the trust on which the brothers' way of fighting depends.

## In context

When the eagle of Zeus has heartened the Achaeans, Diomedes is first across the ditch, and the other leaders follow him in order: the sons of Atreus, the two Ajaxes, Idomeneus and Meriones, Eurypylus; “and Teucer came ninth, stretching his back-bent bow” (8.253–266; see [[eagle]]). The shelter holds until he shoots at Hector. Two arrows meant for Hector kill Gorgythion and the charioteer Archeptolemus instead (8.300–315; see [[the-poppy]]). Then Hector leaps down with a jagged stone and strikes him where the collarbone parts the neck from the chest, as he draws the string back to his shoulder; the string breaks, his hand goes numb, and the bow falls (8.320–329). What the simile told as a routine is done once more in earnest: “But Ajax did not neglect his brother when he fell, but ran and stood over him, and covered him with his shield” (8.330–331). Two companions carry Teucer, groaning, to the ships (8.332–334; see [[ajax]] and [[the-second-day-of-battle]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Teucer fights beside Ajax again at the wall, where he wounds Glaucus with an arrow (12.370–372, 12.387–391), and at the ships. There Ajax calls him to bring his bow (15.436–444), and when he aims at Hector once more, Zeus himself, guarding Hector, snaps the new-twisted string; Ajax tells him to leave the bow, and Teucer takes up a shield of his own and a spear (15.458–483).
