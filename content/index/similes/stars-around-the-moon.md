## The simile

{{quote:653cd4d6-6c39-59e1-a2f8-ee8bc94293d0}}

ἄστρα φαεινὴν ἀμφὶ σελήνην / φαίνετʼ ἀριπρεπέα: the stars “around the shining moon” are ἀριπρεπέα, “conspicuous, clear to see”. The moon, σελήνη, is named in the Iliad only twice more: on the shield of Achilles, where Hephaestus makes “the moon at the full” with the sun and the stars (18.484), and in the darkness over the body of Patroclus, where “you would not have said that sun or moon was safe” (17.366–367). Elsewhere a gleam is “like the moon” (μήνη, 19.374, 23.455). νήνεμος αἰθήρ: the αἰθήρ is the bright upper air above the clouds, and it is “windless”. Then the land stands out beneath the sky: σκοπιαί, the peaks from which men keep watch, πρώονες, the jutting spurs of the hills, and νάπαι, the wooded glens. οὐρανόθεν δʼ ἄρʼ ὑπερράγη ἄσπετος αἰθήρ: “and from the sky the boundless upper air breaks open”, so that “all the stars are seen”. γέγηθε δέ τε φρένα ποιμήν, “and the shepherd is glad at heart”, gives the scene its watcher. τόσσα, “so many”, turns the picture to the plain: the likeness is of number and of light.

A bright moon dims the stars near it, and readers have asked how the stars can shine out clear “around the shining moon”. The usual answer is that the poet is not counting stars but painting the whole sky of a still, clear night, as the shepherd sees it: the moon, every star, and the hills standing out below. The point of comparison is the one that the next lines make plain, a great many lights spread over a dark expanse.

## The repeated lines

Two of the simile's verses are found again in Book 16 (8.557–558 = 16.299–300). There, when Patroclus has driven the Trojans from the burning ship, the Danaans breathe again as when Zeus moves a thick cloud from the top of a high mountain, and the peaks and headlands and glens stand out, and the boundless air breaks open from the sky (16.297–302). The breaking open of the sky suits the clearing of a cloud better than a night that is already windless (8.556), and the two verses are reported to have been rejected here in antiquity (see the translation's note on 8.557–558). In Book 8 they add the land to the sky, lit and visible to the shepherd on his watch.

## In context

The fires were Hector's order. At sunset he gathered the Trojans on the plain and told them to bring cattle, sheep, wine and bread from the city, and to burn many fires all night, “so that their glare may reach the sky”, in case the Achaeans should try to slip away over the sea in the dark (8.489–511; see [[hector]], [[night-watch]] and [[war-and-night]]). The Trojans now sit all night “in high spirits” (μέγα φρονέοντες, 8.553), along the bridges of war, and the narrator gives the numbers:

{{quote:571a0630-c99f-55ed-821c-0f311cd3e32c}}

A thousand fires with fifty men at each are fifty thousand men. In Book 2 Agamemnon said that the Achaeans outnumbered the Trojans who lived in the city by more than ten to one, but that the allies from many cities kept him from taking it (2.123–133; see [[trojan-allies]]). The horses waiting for the dawn are fed as Pandarus' horses were fed at home, “munching white barley and spelt” (5.195–196, 8.564), and the book ends where it began, with Dawn (8.1, 8.565; see [[eos]]).

The shepherd's gladness belongs to the Trojans' side of the plain. For the Achaeans the same fires are a terror. Book 9 opens on them in panic, their hearts torn as two winds from Thrace, the North and the West, stir up the dark sea (9.1–8): a stormy sea after the windless night. Nestor tells the council that the enemy burn many fires close to the ships, “who could rejoice at this?” (τίς ἂν τάδε γηθήσειε, 9.76–77), and Odysseus tells Achilles that the Trojans have camped close by the ships and the wall and lit many fires (9.232–235). In Book 10 Agamemnon, unable to sleep, looks out at the Trojan plain and marvels at “the many fires that burned before Ilios” (10.11–13), in words that echo the end of the simile (πυρὰ … Ἰλιόθι πρό, 8.561). Later that night Odysseus reads the time from the stars: they have moved on, and two parts of the night are gone (10.251–253).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Elsewhere in the Iliad's similes a star is a single light that stands out. The star of late summer blazes from Diomedes' helmet and shield (5.4–7; see [[the-autumn-star]]), and Athena darts down like a shooting star sent as a portent (4.75–79; see [[shooting-star]]). Hector shows now among the foremost, now among the hindmost, like a baneful star that shines out from the clouds and sinks into them again (11.62–65). Achilles' bronze, as he runs at Hector, is like the star called “the dog of Orion”, brightest of the many stars of the night and a sign of evil (22.25–32), and the point of his spear like Hesperus, the fairest star in the sky (22.317–319). Here no star stands out. The lights are many and alike, as the fires are, and the night is still.

## Outside Homer

The close of the book became a test piece for English translators of Homer. Pope's couplets adorned the simile with much that Homer does not have; Wordsworth and Matthew Arnold criticized them, and Tennyson rendered the passage in blank verse (see [[the-watch-fires-in-english]]).
