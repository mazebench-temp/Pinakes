## The simile

αἰεί τοι κραδίη πέλεκυς ὥς ἐστιν ἀτειρής, / ὅς τʼ εἶσιν διὰ δουρὸς ὑπʼ ἀνέρος ὅς ῥά τε τέχνῃ / νήϊον ἐκτάμνῃσιν, ὀφέλλει δʼ ἀνδρὸς ἐρωήν· / ὣς σοὶ ἐνὶ στήθεσσιν ἀτάρβητος νόος ἐστί: “your heart is always unyielding, like an axe that goes through a beam in the hand of a man who with skill shapes a ship's timber, and it adds to the man's force; so fearless is the mind in your breast”. πέλεκυς is an axe. δόρυ, here a beam of wood, is also the word for a spear. νήϊον is timber for a ship, and ἐκτάμνῃσιν “cuts out, shapes”. ἀτειρής, “unwearying, unyielding”, is most often said of bronze (5.292, 7.247). τέχνη is the man's skill, and ἐρωή the force of his stroke. ἀτάρβητος (“fearless”) gives the point of the comparison in the last line.

The tenor is Hector's heart (κραδίη), and then his mind (νόος). The vehicle is a tool at work: the axe does not tire or turn aside, and it multiplies the strength of the skilled man who swings it. Paris means it as praise, but there is an edge in it. An axe does one thing, and the lines that follow defend the gifts his brother lacks.

## In context

{{quote:43554c74-4651-53b4-b681-544a8cc7bd02}}

Hector has called his brother “Evil Paris, best in looks, mad for women, seducer”, and wished he had never been born (3.39–57; see [[paris]]). Paris does not dispute a word. He begins, “Hector, since you rebuke me fairly and not beyond what is fair” (κατʼ αἶσαν … οὐδʼ ὑπὲρ αἶσαν; see [[aisa]]), breaks off to describe his brother's heart, and then comes to his point: “do not throw in my face the lovely gifts of golden Aphrodite. The glorious gifts of the gods are not to be cast aside, whatever they themselves may give; no man could take them by his own will” (3.64–66; see [[gifts-of-the-gods]] and [[doron]]). Then, without a pause, he offers to fight Menelaus for Helen and all her possessions (3.67–75; see [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]]).

The simile sets two kinds of gift side by side. Hector's is a heart as hard as bronze. Paris' are the lyre, the hair and the looks that Hector has just called useless against Menelaus (3.54–55). Each brother has what the gods gave him, and Paris will not be blamed for his.

## Elsewhere in Homer

Paris answers a second rebuke from Hector with the same first line (6.333). The shipwright is a source of similes in the battle books: a warrior falls like an oak or a poplar or a tall pine that carpenters cut down in the mountains with new-whetted axes “to be a ship's timber” (νήϊον εἶναι, 13.389–391 = 16.482–484), and the battle is held level like a ship's timber straightened by the line in the hands of a skilled carpenter, who knows his craft by the teaching of Athena (15.410–413). Paris' own ships had a maker: the craftsman Phereclus, whom Athena loved, “built for Alexander the balanced ships that began the evil” (5.62–63). The axe is also a tool of sacrifice, which fells the heifer at Nestor's offering (Odyssey 3.442–449), and Calypso gives Odysseus a great bronze axe to build his raft (Odyssey 5.234–236).
