## The simile

{{quote:93a75901-ffcf-5360-a6e7-858d43d11309}}

ὡς δʼ ὄρνις ἀπτῆσι νεοσσοῖσι προφέρῃσι / μάστακʼ ἐπεί κε λάβῃσι, κακῶς δʼ ἄρα οἱ πέλει αὐτῇ: “As a bird brings to her unfledged chicks each morsel she takes, while it goes hard with her herself”. ὄρνις is the common word for any bird (see [[birds]]). ἀπτήν, “unfledged”, not yet able to fly (from ἀ-, “not”, and the root of πέτομαι, “fly”), occurs only here in Homer. νεοσσοί, “nestlings”, are elsewhere only the eight young of the sparrow at Aulis (2.311; see [[sparrow]]). μάσταξ is a mouthful, the morsel the bird carries; in the Odyssey the word is the mouth itself (*Odyssey* 4.287, 23.76). The subjunctives make the picture a habit, what such a bird always does, and the tenor answers with the imperfects of long labor: “so I too spent many sleepless nights, and passed through bloody days in battle, fighting with men for the sake of their wives” (9.325–327). Whose wives, the enemies' own or the Achaeans' (Helen above all), the Greek leaves open (see the translation's note on 9.327).

The likeness is the parent's labor for young who cannot feed themselves. The bird gives all she finds and goes hungry, and Achilles has fought night and day for others and has nothing laid up for himself (9.321–322). The image carries affection and contempt together. The chicks are helpless, the Achaeans who live on his fighting; the feeding goes on in plain words in the next lines, where Achilles brought all the treasures of twenty-three cities to Agamemnon, who stayed by the ships and kept most of them (9.328–333; see [[achilles-raids]]). And the bird is a mother. Achilles takes a mother's part for himself, as the narrator gives one to Athena, who turns the arrow from Menelaus as a mother brushes a fly from her sleeping child (4.130–131; see [[mother-and-fly]]), and to Ajax, under whose shield Teucer goes back “like a child under its mother” (8.271–272; see [[like-a-child-under-its-mother]]).

## In context

Odysseus has asked Achilles to pity the army (9.300–303). Achilles answers that there is no thanks for fighting: “The same share for the man who stays behind and for one who fights his hardest; the coward and the brave are held in a single honor” (9.318–319; see [[honor-and-dishonor]] and [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]). The simile is his picture of that unrewarded labor, and he chooses for it a creature that works for its young out of love, not for a prize. He is not asking for pity. He is saying that he has already given what the army now asks of him.

## Elsewhere in Homer

Homer's other parent birds are images of loss. At Aulis the mother sparrow flutters crying over her eight nestlings while the serpent eats them, and then is eaten herself (2.311–316; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]). In the Odyssey, Odysseus and Telemachus, reunited, weep more shrilly than sea eagles or vultures whose young the country people have taken from the nest before they could fly (*Odyssey* 16.216–219). Later in Book 9 Phoenix tells how Marpessa wept for herself as the halcyon laments, when Apollo carried her off (9.562–564; see [[halcyon]]). Achilles' bird loses nothing but her own strength.
