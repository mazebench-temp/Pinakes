## The simile

{{quote:380b2062-321f-52b2-9dc4-d71367e6656a}}

ὡς δʼ ὅθʼ ὑπὸ πληγῆς πατρὸς Διὸς ἐξερίπῃ δρῦς / πρόρριζος, δεινὴ δὲ θεείου γίγνεται ὀδμὴ / ἐξ αὐτῆς, τὸν δʼ οὔ περ ἔχει θράσος ὅς κεν ἴδηται / ἐγγὺς ἐών, χαλεπὸς δὲ Διὸς μεγάλοιο κεραυνός: “As when under the stroke of father Zeus an oak falls uprooted, and a dread smell of sulfur rises from it, and daring fails the man who sees it close by, for hard to bear is the thunderbolt of great Zeus” (14.414–417).

πληγή is a blow: the lash of the whip on a team (11.532), the shameful blows with which Odysseus threatens Thersites (2.264), the blow of the god that, with the spear, overcomes Patroclus (θεοῦ πληγῇ, 16.816). Here it is the thunderbolt, named only at the end of the vehicle (κεραυνός; see [[thunderbolt]]). ἐξερίπῃ, “falls”, is a compound of ἐρείπω, the verb of falling trees and falling men: Asius and Sarpedon fall, ἤριπε, as an oak falls, or a white poplar, or a pine (13.389, 16.482; see [[the-felled-tree]]). It is also the verb of the wall at the start of this book: ἐρέριπτο δὲ τεῖχος Ἀχαιῶν, “and the wall of the Achaeans was thrown down” (14.15), and Nestor tells the kings that it “has been thrown down” (κατερήριπεν, 14.55; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). πρόρριζος, “roots and all”, is said also of the thickets that a forest fire throws down (11.157; see [[forest-fire]]). θέειον is sulfur, whose smell the bolt leaves where it falls: a flame of burning sulfur rose where Zeus' bolt struck the ground before Diomedes' horses (8.135), and in the Odyssey the ship of Odysseus, struck at sea, is filled with it (*Odyssey* 12.415–417). θράσος, “daring”, is found only here in Homer, beside the usual θάρσος.

The tenor is one verse: ὣς ἔπεσʼ Ἕκτορος ὦκα χαμαὶ μένος ἐν κονίῃσι, “so the might of Hector fell quickly to the ground in the dust” (14.418). Not Hector but “the might of Hector” falls: the man is named by his strength (see [[menos]]). The point of likeness is the sudden fall of the strongest thing in its place, struck from above. The oak is the tallest and hardest of trees; it does not break but goes over whole, “roots and all”; and what strikes it is the weapon of the king of the gods. Ajax's stone is given the force of the thunderbolt.

The simile falls in the hour when Zeus is asleep. He lies on the peak of Gargarus with Hera in his arms, overcome by sleep and love (14.352–353), and Poseidon is leading the Achaeans (14.384–387). The thunderbolt of the vehicle belongs to the absent god, the one who had promised Hector the power to kill until he reached the ships (11.192–194). The onlooker in the vehicle loses his daring; the Achaeans who see Hector fall do the opposite, and run up with a great cry, hoping to drag him away (14.421–422).

## In context

Hector's spear was stopped by the two straps on Ajax's chest, and as he drew back among his companions, Ajax hit him in the chest with one of the stones that had propped the ships and sent him spinning like a top (14.402–413; see [[like-a-top]], [[ajax]] and [[props]]). After the fall: “and the spear dropped from his hand, and his shield fell upon him, and his helmet, and around him his armor rang, inlaid with bronze” (14.419–420). The Achaeans hurl their spears, but no one can stab or hit him, for Polydamas, Aeneas, Agenor, Sarpedon and Glaucus stand over him, and the others hold their shields before him (14.421–428). His companions carry him out of the battle to his horses and toward the city (14.428–432; see [[horses]]). At the ford of the Xanthus they lay him on the ground and pour water over him; he gets his breath back, kneels and vomits dark blood, then sinks back, and black night covers his eyes (14.433–439; see [[hector]] and [[scamander]]). With Hector gone, the Argives spring on the Trojans more fiercely (14.440–441).

When Zeus wakes on Ida, he sees Hector lying on the plain with his companions sitting around him, gasping and vomiting blood, since it was not the weakest of the Achaeans who had hit him (15.9–11). His first speech to Hera threatens her with blows, πληγῇσιν (15.14–17).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Warriors fall like trees in the Iliad: Simoeisius like a poplar felled by a chariot-maker (4.482–487; see [[the-poplar]]), Imbrius like an ash cut down on a mountain peak (13.178–181; see [[the-felled-ash]]), Asius and Sarpedon like an oak, a white poplar or a pine that builders fell for ship-timber (13.389–393 = 16.482–486; see [[the-felled-tree]]), and Euphorbus like an olive sapling uprooted by a sudden gale (17.53–58). Those trees are cut by men or torn up by the wind. Hector's alone is struck by Zeus, and Hector alone does not die. The Lapiths at the gate stood like oaks in the mountains that withstand wind and rain all their days, held by great roots (12.131–136; see [[oaks-on-the-mountains]]); Hector's oak goes over roots and all.

The thunderbolt is feared even by gods. Ares, hearing of his son's death, will go to avenge him even if it is his fate to be struck by the thunderbolt of Zeus (15.113–118), and Achilles says that even Oceanus, from whom all rivers and all the sea flow, fears “the thunderbolt of great Zeus” and his dread thunder (Διὸς μεγάλοιο κεραυνόν, 21.193–199). In the Odyssey the oak is Zeus' own tree: at Dodona men hear the will of Zeus from his high-leafed oak (*Odyssey* 14.327–328 = 19.296–297; see [[dodona]]).
