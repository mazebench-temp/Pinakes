## The simile

{{quote:e7b95430-c75d-5059-8e04-e2f12c68c9d7}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἂν ἔν τε κύνεσσι καὶ ἀνδράσι θηρευτῇσι / κάπριος ἠὲ λέων στρέφεται σθένεϊ βλεμεαίνων: “And as when among hounds and hunting men a boar or a lion turns this way and that, exulting in his strength”. κάπριος is the wild boar and λέων the lion (see [[boar]] and [[lion]]); the poet names both and does not choose. στρέφεται, “turns”, is the beast wheeling to face its attackers. σθένεϊ βλεμεαίνων, “exulting in his strength”, is said of Hector himself in Books 8 and 9 (8.337, 9.237; see [[exulting-in-strength]]). The hunters close up πυργηδόν, “in close order like a tower”, stand against the beast and hurl their spears thick and fast (12.43–45). τοῦ δʼ οὔ ποτε κυδάλιμον κῆρ / ταρβεῖ οὐδὲ φοβεῖται, ἀγηνορίη δέ μιν ἔκτα: “but his glorious heart never fears or flees, and it is his pride that kills him” (12.45–46). ἀγηνορίη is manliness carried past caution, from ἀγήνωρ, “manly, proud” (see [[proud]]). ἔκτα, “kills”, is an aorist of what always happens: the beast that will not run dies. Meanwhile it turns again and again, testing the ranks, and wherever it charges, the ranks give way (12.47–48).

The tenor: “so Hector went through the throng and begged his companions, urging them to cross the ditch” (12.49–50). ἐλλίσσεθʼ, “begged”, is from λίσσομαι, not from ἑλίσσω, “turn”, though the beast of the simile turns about (see the translation's note on 12.49). The point of likeness is courage among enemies who hold their ground. The beast attacks and the ranks give way, and yet the simile ends in its death. The narrator says in the simile what he does not say of Hector here: his courage will kill him.

## In context

Battle blazes around the wall, and the Argives, “beaten down by the lash of Zeus”, are penned in beside their ships “in fear of Hector, the mighty master of rout” (12.35–39; see [[master-of-rout]]). He fights “as before, like a storm wind” (12.40; see [[the-storm-wind-on-the-sea]]), and the simile follows. What Hector wants of his own men is that they cross the ditch. His horses balk at its edge, and Polydamas persuades him to go against the wall on foot (12.50–80; see [[horses]], [[polydamas]], [[hector]] and [[the-fight-at-the-wall]]).

The simile reverses the roles of Books 8 and 11. On the second day of battle Hector drove the Achaeans to their ditch like a hound that catches at a wild boar or a lion from behind (8.338–342; see [[the-hound-at-the-heels]]), and earlier on this day he set the Trojans on them as a hunter sets his hounds on a wild boar or a lion (11.292–295; see [[the-hunter-and-his-hounds]]). At the wall he is the boar or the lion, and the Achaeans are the hunters, drawn up behind their wall like a tower.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The hunters' formation returns in Hector's mouth: at the ships he tells the Trojans that the Achaeans will not hold him back for long, even drawn up πυργηδόν (13.150–154). In Book 15 he again goes “testing the ranks of men” (στίχας ἀνδρῶν πειρητίζων, 15.615, as at 12.47) and cannot break them, because they hold πυργηδόν, like a great sheer rock by the sea (15.615–620).

The end that the simile foretells is spoken by Andromache. When she hears the wailing from the tower, she fears that Achilles has put an end to the grievous ἀγηνορίη that held Hector, since he never stayed in the throng of men but ran far out in front (22.454–459). The same thought ends a lion simile for Patroclus: the lion that ravages the farmsteads is hit in the chest, and its own courage destroys it (ἑή τέ μιν ὤλεσεν ἀλκή, 16.752–753).

Odysseus, cut off in Book 11, is a boar at bay of another kind: there the dogs and young men close in around a beast that waits for them; here the beast charges and the ranks give way (11.414–420; see [[the-boar-at-bay]] and [[lions-and-boars]]).
