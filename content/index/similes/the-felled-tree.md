## The simile

{{quote:900bed96-9c61-5133-ad6c-6e0ea53ef583}}

ἤριπε δʼ ὡς ὅτε τις δρῦς ἤριπεν ἢ ἀχερωῒς / ἠὲ πίτυς βλωθρή, τήν τʼ οὔρεσι τέκτονες ἄνδρες / ἐξέταμον πελέκεσσι νεήκεσι νήϊον εἶναι: “And he fell as an oak falls, or a white poplar, or a tall pine, which on the mountains builders cut down with newly sharpened axes to be ship-timber” (13.389–391). Three trees are named and none is chosen: δρῦς, the oak, ἀχερωΐς, the white poplar, a word found only here and in the repetition (16.482), and πίτυς, the pine, βλωθρή, “tall”, as a pear tree is in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 24.234). The verb of the man is the verb of the tree, ἤριπε … ἤριπεν, “he fell … falls”, as when Echepolus “fell like a tower” (ἤριπε δʼ ὡς ὅτε πύργος, 4.462; see [[like-a-tower]]). The τέκτονες ἄνδρες are builders, here shipwrights, who cut the timber on the mountains with πελέκεσσι νεήκεσι, “newly sharpened axes” (νεηκής, only here and at 16.484; see [[axe]]). νήϊον is ship-timber, the wood that a shipwright's axe cuts in Hector's rebuke of Paris (3.61–62; see [[heart-like-an-axe]]) and that the carpenter's line makes straight (15.410–411).

The tenor: ὣς ὃ πρόσθʼ ἵππων καὶ δίφρου κεῖτο τανυσθεὶς / βεβρυχὼς κόνιος δεδραγμένος αἱματοέσσης, “so he lay stretched out in front of his horses and chariot, roaring, clutching at the bloody dust” (13.392–393). βεβρυχώς, “roaring”, is the perfect of a verb used elsewhere of the roar of a breaking wave (17.264; *Odyssey* 5.412). The point of likeness is size brought down: the tallest trees of the mountain, cut and laid at full length, as Asius lies stretched out before his horses.

## In context

The narrator foretold Asius' death at the wall. He would not leave his horses and chariot at the ditch, “the fool”, and so would never go back to windy Ilios “glorying in his horses and chariot”: before that, “fate of evil name enfolded him through the spear of Idomeneus” (12.110–117; see [[asius]] and the translation's note on 12.113–117). It comes now. Idomeneus has killed Othryoneus and is dragging him by the foot when Asius comes to the rescue, on foot in front of his horses, which his charioteer keeps so close that they breathe on his shoulders (13.383–386; see [[othryoneus]]). Idomeneus is quicker, and drives the spear through his throat under the chin (13.387–388). The charioteer, struck out of his wits, cannot turn the horses, and Antilochus kills him and drives them off to the Achaeans (13.394–401; see [[antilochus]] and [[horses]]). Asius' son Adamas dies later in the book (13.560–575; see [[the-roped-ox]]), and Deiphobus kills Hypsenor to avenge Asius and boasts that he has given him an escort to the house of Hades (13.402–416).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same five verses tell the death of Sarpedon, whom Patroclus strikes where the midriff closes around the heart, and they are followed there by a second simile, a lion killing a bull among the herd (16.480–491; see [[sarpedon]]). Other warriors fall like trees: Simoeisius like a poplar felled by a chariot-maker (4.482–487; see [[the-poplar]]), the twin sons of Diocles like fir trees (5.559–560; see [[fir-trees]]), Imbrius like an ash cut down with the bronze on a mountain peak (13.178–181; see [[the-felled-ash]]), Hector like an oak uprooted by the lightning of Zeus (14.414–417), and Euphorbus like a young olive tree torn up by a gale (17.53–58). The woodcutters of the similes work in the mountains: the woodcutter takes his meal in a mountain glen when his hands are weary of felling tall trees (11.86–89), and the men sent to Ida for Patroclus' pyre fell the high-leafed oaks with the bronze, and the trees fall with a great crash (23.114–120).
