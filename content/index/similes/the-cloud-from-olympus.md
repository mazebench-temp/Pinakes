## The simile

{{quote:b7b18798-4f76-5cab-9692-85e3f8f04a5b}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἀπʼ Οὐλύμπου νέφος ἔρχεται οὐρανὸν εἴσω / αἰθέρος ἐκ δίης, ὅτε τε Ζεὺς λαίλαπα τείνῃ: “And as when a cloud comes from Olympus into the sky / out of the shining upper air, when Zeus spreads a storm” (16.364–365). The mountain of the gods stands up into the αἰθήρ, the bright air above the clouds, and the cloud comes from it into the οὐρανός, the sky over the earth. δῖος, said of the upper air only here, is “bright, shining”. λαῖλαψ is a squall or storm, the storm that the goatherd sees coming over the sea in a cloud blacker than pitch (4.275–279; see [[storm-cloud]]), and τείνῃ, “stretches, spreads”, gives the storm the extent of a thing laid out across the sky.

The tenor: ὣς τῶν ἐκ νηῶν γένετο ἰαχή τε φόβος τε, “so from the ships came their shouting and rout” (16.366). The half-verse has been used twice before, both times of the Danaans: when the Trojans rushed on the wall, “among the Danaans there rose shouting and rout” (12.144), and the same words told what Patroclus saw from Eurypylus' hut before he ran to Achilles (15.395–396). Now it is the Trojans who are routed, and from the ships. The point of likeness is a movement outward, from the mountain into the sky and from the ships across the plain, and a darkening: what comes is a storm. The verse that follows says how the Trojans went: “and they did not cross back in good order” (οὐδὲ κατὰ μοῖραν πέραον πάλιν, 16.367). Walter Leaf found the point of comparison hard to see, since a crowd of men may be called a cloud but the likeness ends there, and took 16.364–371 for an addition made as a counterpart to the cloud simile of 16.297–302: the coming of the Myrmidons was the breaking of clouds, so the departure of the Trojans was made the coming of a cloud (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.364 and 16.365).

## In context

Ajax keeps trying to hit Hector with his spear. Hector, skilled in war, covers his broad shoulders with his shield of bull's hide and watches for the whizzing of arrows and the thudding of spears: “Truly he knew the victory that turns the battle; but even so he stood his ground, and saved his trusty companions” (16.358–363; see [[hector]]). The simile follows, and then the rout. Hector's horses carry him out, and he leaves the Trojan army, which the ditch holds back; many teams break their lords' chariots at the end of the pole; Patroclus follows, calling on the Danaans; the Trojans fill all the roads with shouting and rout, “and high up a storm wind of dust spread out under the clouds” (16.364–376; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[horses]]).

The storm of the simile passes into the story. The storm wind of dust (ἀέλλη, 16.374) rises over the routed Trojans as if Zeus' storm had broken, and ten verses later a second storm simile, the longest of the book, gives the Trojan mares running for the city the sound of torrents in the rains of Zeus (16.384–393; see [[the-storm-of-late-summer]]). Earlier in the battle Zeus moved a thick cloud away from a mountain peak, and the Danaans breathed again (16.297–302; see [[the-cloud-lifted-from-the-peak]]). Now the cloud is the Trojans' own rout.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The gates of the sky are held by the Hours, to whom great heaven and Olympus are entrusted, “both to throw open the thick cloud and to close it” (5.749–751). When Ares, wounded, goes up to the wide sky, Diomedes sees him go as a murky mist appears out of the clouds when after heat a stormy wind arises (5.864–867; see [[dark-mist-from-the-clouds]]). In the Odyssey Olympus itself is a place where no wind shakes and no rain or snow falls, but clear sky is spread out cloudless and a white radiance plays over it (*Odyssey* 6.42–46). In the simile a storm cloud comes from that clear height.

In *Iliad* 16 Zeus “stretches” more than storms. He stretches deadly night over the fight for Sarpedon's body, so that there may be deadly toil of battle around his dear son (16.567–568), and the Lycians flee when they see their king lying in the heap of the dead, for many had fallen on top of him “when the son of Cronus stretched tight the mighty strife” (16.660–662).
