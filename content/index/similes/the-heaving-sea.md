## The simile

{{quote:e57caf3d-d1bf-50a6-a51d-6767bf35e5a5}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτε πορφύρῃ πέλαγος μέγα κύματι κωφῷ / ὀσσόμενον λιγέων ἀνέμων λαιψηρὰ κέλευθα / αὔτως, οὐδʼ ἄρα τε προκυλίνδεται οὐδετέρωσε, / πρίν τινα κεκριμένον καταβήμεναι ἐκ Διὸς οὖρον: “As when the great open sea heaves with a soundless swell, sensing the swift paths of the shrill winds, aimlessly, and does not roll forward to either side until some settled wind comes down from Zeus” (14.16–19).

πέλαγος, the open sea, occurs only here in the Iliad. πορφύρω is “heave, surge”, and everywhere else in Homer it is a heart that heaves, turning things over: Agenor's as he waits for Achilles (21.551), Menelaus' and Odysseus' as they go to meet what lies ahead (*Odyssey* 4.427, 4.572, 10.309). Only here is it the sea (see the translation's note on 14.16). The adjective πορφύρεος describes the wave: πορφύρεον κῦμα, “the surging wave” (1.482; see [[purple]]). The swell is κωφόν, “dull, dumb”: a heavy sea that neither breaks nor roars. Diomedes calls a weakling's arrow κωφόν, “blunt” (11.390), and Apollo will say that Achilles outrages the κωφὴν γαῖαν, the senseless earth that Hector's body has become (24.54; see [[the-senseless-earth]]). ὀσσόμενον, “sensing”, gives the sea a mind. ὄσσομαι is to see ahead or within, as Telemachus, sitting among the suitors, sees his father in his mind (*Odyssey* 1.115). What it senses is λιγέων ἀνέμων λαιψηρὰ κέλευθα, “the swift paths of the shrill winds”, words that return in Book 15 for the sheer rock that withstands them (15.620).

The sea moves αὔτως, “aimlessly”, “to no purpose”, and does not roll forward οὐδετέρωσε, “to either side”; προκυλίνδεται and οὐδετέρωσε are found only here. It waits for κεκριμένον … οὖρον, “some settled wind”. κεκριμένος is the participle of κρίνω, “separate, decide”: a wind that has been decided, set in one quarter. It comes “from Zeus”, as a fair wind in the Odyssey is “the wind of Zeus” (Διὸς οὖρος, *Odyssey* 5.176, 15.297).

The tenor: ὣς ὃ γέρων ὅρμαινε δαϊζόμενος κατὰ θυμὸν / διχθάδιʼ, ἢ μεθʼ ὅμιλον ἴοι Δαναῶν ταχυπώλων, / ἦε μετʼ Ἀτρεΐδην Ἀγαμέμνονα ποιμένα λαῶν: “so the old man pondered, torn in his heart two ways: whether to go into the throng of the Danaans of the swift horses, or to the son of Atreus, Agamemnon, shepherd of the people” (14.20–22). δαΐζω is to cleave or rend, and it is the heart's verb in another sea simile: when two winds stir up the sea from Thrace, “so the heart in the breasts of the Achaeans was torn” (ἐδαΐζετο, 9.8; see [[two-winds-from-thrace]]). διχθάδια, “two ways”, is the word of Achilles' “two different fates” (διχθαδίας κῆρας, 9.411). ὁρμαίνω, “turn over, ponder”, is a verb of deliberation (see [[pondering-two-ways]]).

The point of likeness is motion without direction. The sea heaves where it lies because no wind has yet chosen its course, and Nestor's heart heaves between two courses because nothing has yet decided him. The vehicle carries its own ending: the settled wind comes down, the sea rolls one way, and the decision comes in the formula of choice, ὧδε δέ οἱ φρονέοντι δοάσσατο κέρδιον εἶναι / βῆναι ἐπʼ Ἀτρεΐδην, “And as he pondered, this seemed to him the better way: to go to the son of Atreus” (14.23–24; see [[decision-scene]]).

The swell is soundless, and everything around it is loud. The book begins with a shout that reaches Nestor over his wine, “the cry of the young men in their prime is louder now beside the ships” (14.4), and when the narrative turns back to the fighting, the unyielding bronze rings about the men's bodies (14.24–26). The old man's thought is the one silent thing in the scene.

## In context

Nestor has been sitting in his hut with the wounded Machaon since Book 11, where Hecamede mixed them a drink and they talked (11.618–643). The louder cry brings him out. He leaves Machaon to Hecamede's warm bath, takes the shield of his son Thrasymedes, since the son had his father's, and a spear (14.1–12; see [[nestor]], [[machaon]] and [[thrasymedes]]), and from outside the hut he sees “a shameful thing: the one side in turmoil, and the others driving them in confusion from behind, the high-hearted Trojans; and the wall of the Achaeans was thrown down” (14.13–15; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). The Achaeans are ὀρινομένους, “in turmoil”, with the verb of the two winds that stir up the sea in Book 9 (ὀρίνετον, 9.4). The simile follows at once.

He chooses the king over the throng. On his way he meets Agamemnon, Diomedes and Odysseus, all wounded, coming up from the ships to look at the battle, and their council debates whether to launch the ships and flee (14.27–134; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). The verse of what Nestor saw (14.14) returns when Zeus wakes on Ida at the start of the next book, with the sides reversed: now the Trojans are in turmoil and the Argives drive them from behind (15.6–8).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Another likeness of troubled hearts in the Iliad is also a sea. On the night after the Achaean defeat two winds, the North Wind and the West Wind, fall on the fish-filled sea at once and raise the dark wave, and so the hearts of the Achaeans are torn (9.4–8; see [[two-winds-from-thrace]]). There the winds have come, from two sides at once; here none has come yet. The swift paths of the shrill winds return in Book 15, where the Danaans stand against Hector like a great sheer rock by the gray sea, which withstands the winds and the swelling waves that break against it (15.618–622). In the Odyssey the sleepless Odysseus, pondering how to fall on the suitors alone, tosses like a paunch full of fat and blood that a man turns this way and that before a great blazing fire, eager to have it roasted (*Odyssey* 20.24–30).
