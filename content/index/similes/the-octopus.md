## The simile

{{quote:a1bca053-5c90-567c-bd46-8c8b1988a6b9}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτε πουλύποδος θαλάμης ἐξελκομένοιο / πρὸς κοτυληδονόφιν πυκιναὶ λάιγγες ἔχονται, / ὣς τοῦ πρὸς πέτρῃσι θρασειάων ἀπὸ χειρῶν / ῥινοὶ ἀπέδρυφθεν: “And as when an octopus is dragged out of its den, / many pebbles cling thick to its suckers, / so from his bold hands, against the rocks, / the skin was stripped away” (*Odyssey* 5.432–435). πουλύπους, “many-foot”, the octopus, is named only here in Homer (see [[octopus]]), and so are its θαλάμη, “den”, and its κοτυληδόνες, “suckers”, here in the case-form κοτυληδονόφιν. W. W. Merry explains the suckers as named from their cup-like shape, from κοτύλη, “cup”, and takes κοτυληδονόφιν as a dative answering πέτρῃσι, “against the rocks”, in the next verse (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.433). λάιγγες are the pebbles of the beach, which the sea washes clean on the shore where Nausicaa's maids spread the washing to dry (*Odyssey* 6.94–95). θρασειάων ἀπὸ χειρῶν, “from bold hands”, is a verse-end of the Iliad, where spears fly from bold hands (*Iliad* 11.553, 13.134, 15.314), and once the backs of Ajax and Odysseus creak under each other's bold hands in the wrestling (*Iliad* 23.714); here it is the skin that is torn from them.

Merry renders the whole: “And as when many a pebble sticks to the suckers of the polyp, as it is dragged forth from its hole, so the skin from his sturdy hands was stripped off against the rocks” (on 5.432). The resemblance, he says, is general: both cling with a tenacious grasp. But the simile is not to be pushed too far, for the stones stick to the suckers, while the skin of the man's hands is left sticking on the rock that he grasped (on 5.433; see the translation's note on 5.432–435). The octopus is torn from its hold and takes the stones with it; the man is torn from his hold and leaves his skin behind.

The simile shares its words with the first simile of the storm: πυκιναὶ … ἔχονται, “cling thick”, is said of the pebbles here and of the thistles there, as the North Wind carries them over the plain (*Odyssey* 5.329; see [[thistles-in-the-north-wind]]).

## In context

Odysseus has come near the coast of Scheria after two nights and two days in the sea, and found no landing: outside are sharp crags, the wave roars around them, and the rock runs up sheer (*Odyssey* 5.399–423; see [[shore]] and [[the-fathers-recovery]]). While he is pondering whether to swim on along the shore, “a great wave carried him onto the rugged shore. / There his skin would have been stripped off and his bones crushed together, / had not the gray-eyed goddess Athena put it in his mind” (*Odyssey* 5.425–427; see [[athena]]): he seized the rock with both hands and clung to it, groaning, until the great wave went past; but its backwash struck him again and flung him far out into the sea (*Odyssey* 5.428–431). The simile follows, and the skin that Athena saved at first is lost now: ἀπὸ ῥινοὺς δρύφθη, “his skin would have been stripped off” (*Odyssey* 5.426), comes true in ῥινοὶ ἀπέδρυφθεν, “the skin was stripped away” (*Odyssey* 5.435). “And the great wave covered him. / Then unhappy Odysseus would have perished against fate, / had not gray-eyed Athena given him presence of mind” (*Odyssey* 5.435–437): he comes out beyond the breakers and swims along the coast until he finds the mouth of a river (*Odyssey* 5.438–443; see [[the-river-of-scheria]]).

## Outside Homer

[[Aristotle]] describes the octopus that the simile pictures. The suckers, κοτυληδόνες, are on the arms of all the cuttlefish kind, and the octopus uses its arms both as feet and as hands (*History of Animals* 4.1). It lives in a den, a θαλάμη, Homer's word: it gathers everything into the den where it lives, throws out the shells of what it has eaten, and hunts fish by changing its color to match the stones it lies near; it is a stupid creature, he says, for it will even go toward a man's hand let down into the water (*History of Animals* 9.37).
