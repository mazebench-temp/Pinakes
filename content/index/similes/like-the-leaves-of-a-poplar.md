## The simile

{{quote:0d80de47-9227-5142-8589-510e0bdbe363}}

αἱ δʼ ἱστοὺς ὑφόωσι καὶ ἠλάκατα στρωφῶσιν / ἥμεναι, οἷά τε φύλλα μακεδνῆς αἰγείροιο: “and others weave at the looms and turn the yarn / as they sit, like the leaves of a tall poplar” (*Odyssey* 7.105–106; see [[loom]], [[weaving]] and [[distaff]]). Of the fifty slave women of the house, some grind the grain at the mills and others weave and spin (*Odyssey* 7.103–105; see [[handmaids]] and [[mill]]); the simile, half a verse long, is joined to ἥμεναι, “as they sit”. μακεδνός, “tall”, is found nowhere else in Homer.

What is compared? W. W. Merry takes the point to be the constant movement of the women at their task. He rejects Nitzsch's view that they sit as close together as leaves grow on the tree: leaves in Homer may stand for multitude, but they serve other likenesses as well; the leaves of the αἴγειρος, which he takes for the aspen, are remarkable not for their closeness but for their trembling; and Nitzsch's sense would want ὅσσα τε, “as many as”, where the verse has οἷά τε, “such as” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.106). The scholia give both explanations: the leaves either for the number, or for the quickness of the hands as they turn the weft, like the leaves of the poplar, which are set against one another and move easily even in a chance breeze; and they quote Sophocles' *Aegeus* for the tall poplar whose crown, if nothing else, some breeze will stir (fr. 24 Dindorf, as Merry gives the scholium, on 7.106). The translation's note on 7.106 follows Merry.

The women sit, and their hands do not rest. στρωφῶσιν, “turn”, is the verb of the queen herself, who sits at the hearth with her handmaids “turning the sea-purple yarn” (*Odyssey* 6.53, 6.306; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]), and of Penelope, who sits in her chair by the doorpost of the hall turning the fine yarn (*Odyssey* 17.96–97, a plain rendering; see [[penelope]]). ἠλάκατα are the wool spun from the distaff (see the translation's table of key words). The leaves that tremble on a living tree are the likeness for the work of a household at peace.

## In context

The women are the last part of the description of the house, after the bronze walls, the golden doors, the gold and silver dogs and the golden youths with their torches (*Odyssey* 7.84–102; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]]). The simile is followed by a verse on the linen they weave, “and from the close-woven linen the liquid olive oil drips off” (*Odyssey* 7.107; see the translation's note there), and by a second comparison, which sets the women's skill beside the men's: “As much as the Phaeacians are skilled beyond all men / in driving a swift ship on the sea, so are the women / skilled at the loom; for Athena has given them above all others / to be skilled in beautiful handiwork, and good wits” (*Odyssey* 7.108–111; see [[phaeacians]] and [[athena]]). ἱστῶν τεχνῆσσαι, “skilled at the loom”, is the reading of the text; the vulgate has ἱστὸν … τεχνῆσαι (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 7.110). Then the poem passes out of the house to the garden (*Odyssey* 7.112–132; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]]), where trees of another kind bear fruit in every season.

## Elsewhere in Homer

Leaves in Homer's similes are most often a measure of number: the Achaeans in the meadow of the Scamander are as many as the leaves and flowers that grow in their season, and Iris warns Priam that they come “like leaves or like the sands” (*Iliad* 2.468, 2.800; see [[leaves-and-flowers]] and [[like-leaves-or-sand]]); the Cicones come down on Odysseus' men as many as the leaves and flowers in their season (*Odyssey* 9.51, a plain rendering). They are also the likeness of mortality: “As is the generation of leaves, so is that of men” (*Iliad* 6.146; see [[the-generations-of-leaves]]), and Apollo will not fight Poseidon for the sake of mortals, “wretched creatures, who, like leaves, at one time / blaze with life, eating the fruit of the field, / and at another waste away lifeless” (*Iliad* 21.464–466). Merry cites both kinds, the leaves of number and the leaves of mortality, to show that leaves in Homer are not a likeness for multitude alone (on 7.106; *Iliad* 2.468, 2.800, 21.464). The leaves of the Phaeacian simile are neither many nor falling: they are moving.

The poplar itself is in the Iliad the likeness of a young man cut down, Simoeisius falling to Ajax' spear like a poplar grown in a marsh meadow, felled by a chariot-maker (*Iliad* 4.482–487; see [[the-poplar]]), and a white poplar falls with the oak and the pine in the verses for Asius and Sarpedon (*Iliad* 13.389–391, 16.482–484). In the Odyssey it grows on Calypso's island, around her cave and at the far edge where Odysseus fells his timber, and by water: in the grove of Athena on Scheria, by the spring on the island of goats, in Persephone's groves by Oceanus and around the fountain of Ithaca (*Odyssey* 5.64, 5.239, 6.291–292, 9.141, 10.509–510, 17.208; see [[trees]]).
