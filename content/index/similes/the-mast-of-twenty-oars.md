## The simile

{{quote:e777e95d-c96d-595a-80b0-401a5cebd791}}

τὸ μὲν ἄμμες ἐίσκομεν εἰσορόωντες / ὅσσον θʼ ἱστὸν νηὸς ἐεικοσόροιο μελαίνης, / φορτίδος εὐρείης, ἥ τʼ ἐκπεράᾳ μέγα λαῖτμα· / τόσσον ἔην μῆκος, τόσσον πάχος εἰσοράασθαι: “As we looked at it, we likened it / to the mast of a black ship of twenty oars, / a broad freight ship, which crosses the great gulf; / so great was it in length, so great in thickness to look upon” (*Odyssey* 9.321–324). The likeness is the men's own, made by eye, ἐίσκομεν εἰσορόωντες, and the last verse returns to the eye, εἰσοράασθαι. ὅσσον θʼ ἱστόν is a brachylogy for “as great as a mast is”, and μῆκος and πάχος are accusatives that qualify τόσσον, W. W. Merry explains; in ἐεικόσορος, “of twenty oars”, is the root of ἐρέσσω, “row” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.322, 9.324). The ship is a φορτίς, a broad-beamed merchant ship that crosses the open sea, λαῖτμα (see [[ships]], [[mast]] and the translation's table of key words), and its mast is the measure of a club that the Cyclops means to carry about as a staff.

The club, ῥόπαλον (see [[mace]]), was cut by the Cyclops from an olive and left to dry: “there lay beside the pen a great club of the Cyclops, / of green olive wood; he had cut it to carry with him / when it was dry” (*Odyssey* 9.319–321; see [[olive]]). Merry doubts whether any limb of an olive could be found long enough and straight enough for such a staff, and notes that the conjecture εἰλάτινον, “of fir”, which some proposed for that reason, would bring a new difficulty, since fresh pinewood blazes up in the fire instead of charring (on 9.322).

## In context

Left alone in the cave on the second day, Odysseus looks for a way to take vengeance, “And this plan seemed to me in my heart the best” (*Odyssey* 9.318). The plan begins with the club. He cuts off about a fathom's length, has his companions taper it, sharpens the point himself, hardens it in the fire and hides it under the dung (*Odyssey* 9.325–330; see [[stake]]). The size of the club is the size of its owner (see [[like-a-wooded-peak]]); the stake cut from it is lifted and driven by five men, the four on whom the lot falls and Odysseus (*Odyssey* 9.331–335, 9.382–388; see [[the-shipwrights-drill]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Odyssey measures by ships. Odysseus makes his raft “As wide as the floor of a ship that a man marks out, / a broad freight ship, a man well skilled in carpentry” (*Odyssey* 5.249–250; see [[the-building-of-the-raft]]), in the same words for the freight ship. The Iliad measures the wings of Priam's eagle by the door of a wealthy man's high-roofed chamber (*Iliad* 24.317–319; see [[wings-as-wide-as-a-door]]), and fells its tall pines on the mountains to be ship timber (*Iliad* 13.389–391; see [[the-felled-tree]]). In the next book the queen of the Laestrygonians is “as huge as a mountain peak” (*Odyssey* 10.112–113), which Merry sets beside this verse (on 9.322).

## Outside Homer

Virgil's blind Polyphemus comes down to the sea with “a lopped pine” in his hand to guide him and steady his steps (*trunca manum pinus regit et vestigia firmat*, *Aeneid* 3.659; see [[virgil]]). In Euripides' *Cyclops* Odysseus finds a branch of olive in the house for his brand (*Cyclops* 455), and Merry finds the Homeric picture spoiled by the branch in place of the club (on 9.326; see [[euripides]]). Merry also notes that Milton imitated the simile in his description of Satan's spear: “To which the tallest pine, / Hewn on Norwegian hills to be the mast / Of some great Ammiral, were but a wand” (as Merry quotes it, on 9.322; see [[milton]]).
