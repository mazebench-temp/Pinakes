## The simile

{{quote:e206b150-39e4-57e4-a2cd-42137887485c}}

οἳ δʼ ἴσαν ἀργαλέων ἀνέμων ἀτάλαντοι ἀέλλῃ, / ἥ ῥά θʼ ὑπὸ βροντῆς πατρὸς Διὸς εἶσι πέδον δέ, / θεσπεσίῳ δʼ ὁμάδῳ ἁλὶ μίσγεται: “And they went on like a blast of grievous winds, which comes down to the plain under the thunder of father Zeus, and with a wondrous din mingles with the sea” (13.795–797). ἄελλα is a squall, and ἀτάλαντος, “equal to, a match for”, is the word of the formula “the equal of swift Ares” (see [[equal-of-ares]]). The digitized text divides πέδονδε, “to the ground, to the plain” (see the translation's note on 13.796). The squall falls from the sky onto the sea, as Hector's storm wind did in Book 11, which “swoops down and stirs up the violet sea” (11.297–298; see [[the-storm-wind-on-the-sea]]).

Then the sea: ἐν δέ τε πολλὰ / κύματα παφλάζοντα πολυφλοίσβοιο θαλάσσης / κυρτὰ φαληριόωντα, πρὸ μέν τʼ ἄλλʼ, αὐτὰρ ἐπʼ ἄλλα, “and in it are many seething waves of the loud-roaring sea, arched and white-crested, some in front, and others behind” (13.797–799). παφλάζω, “seethe, bubble”, a word that imitates its sound, and φαληριάω, “be white-crested”, occur only here; κυρτός, “arched”, is the curve of the West Wind's wave in Book 4 (4.426). πολύφλοισβος is the sea's standing epithet (see [[loud-roaring-sea]]).

The tenor repeats the last words of the vehicle: ὣς Τρῶες πρὸ μὲν ἄλλοι ἀρηρότες, αὐτὰρ ἐπʼ ἄλλοι, / χαλκῷ μαρμαίροντες ἅμʼ ἡγεμόνεσσιν ἕποντο, “so the Trojans, some in front in close array, and others behind, gleaming with bronze, followed with their leaders” (13.800–801). The point of likeness is the advance in ranks, line behind line, with the force and the noise of a storm. The white of the crests becomes the gleam of the bronze. The wind comes “under the thunder of father Zeus”, and the narrator has just said that Zeus roused the newly arrived allies from Ascania to fight (13.794).

## In context

Persuaded by Paris, Hector goes where the battle is thickest, around Cebriones and Polydamas and the allies from Ascania who had come “as relief on the morning before” (13.788–794; see [[ascania]]). The Trojans follow him like the storm, and Hector leads “the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals”, holding his shield before him and testing the battalions to see where they will give way; but they do not give way (13.802–808). Ajax comes out to challenge him (13.809–820; see [[ajax]]), an eagle flies on the right, and both armies shout (13.821–837).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Waves are the Iliad's picture of armies on the move. The Achaeans advanced into the first battle rank upon rank, like waves that the West Wind drives onto a loud-echoing shore (4.422–428; see [[waves-on-the-shore]]), and in Book 2 the assembly heaved like the long waves of the Icarian sea (2.144–146; see [[waves-of-the-icarian-sea]]). Two winds from Thrace stirring up the fish-filled sea were the troubled hearts of the Achaeans (9.4–8; see [[two-winds-from-thrace]]). At the ships the image is the Trojans'. They go over the wall as a great wave goes over the side of a ship (15.381–384), Hector falls on the Achaeans as a wave falls on a swift ship (15.624–628), and the Danaans hold against him like a sheer rock that withstands the winds and the swelling waves (15.618–622).
