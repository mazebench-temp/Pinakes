## The simile

{{quote:f18350ad-f9f4-515f-881c-40577b65c8db}}

Thestor sits crouching in his chariot, “for he was struck out of his wits, and out of his hands / the reins had slipped” (16.403–404). Patroclus comes up and stabs him on the right jaw, and the spear goes through his teeth (διὰ δʼ αὐτοῦ πεῖρεν ὀδόντων, 16.405). Then: ἕλκε δὲ δουρὸς ἑλὼν ὑπὲρ ἄντυγος, ὡς ὅτε τις φὼς / πέτρῃ ἔπι προβλῆτι καθήμενος ἱερὸν ἰχθὺν / ἐκ πόντοιο θύραζε λίνῳ καὶ ἤνοπι χαλκῷ, “and he took hold of the spear and dragged him over the rail, as when a man / sitting on a jutting rock drags a sacred fish / out of the sea onto the shore with line and gleaming bronze” (16.406–408). προβλής, “jutting”, is said of a crag that the waves never leave (2.396) and of headlands in the Odyssey. θύραζε, “out of doors”, is “out” of the water, as when the angler of the Odyssey flings his catch ashore (*Odyssey* 12.254). λίνον, flax, is here the line (see [[the-net]]); ἤνοψ, rendered “gleaming”, is a word of uncertain sense said only of bronze (18.349; *Odyssey* 10.360), and the bronze is the hook, which the verse does not name. ἱερός, “sacred”, is given to the fish without a reason; the word also means “mighty, lively” in the Odyssey (ἱερὸν μένος Ἀλκινόοιο, *Odyssey* 7.167), and what is meant here is uncertain (see the translation's note on 16.407 and [[fish]]). Walter Leaf reports an ancient note that the sacred fish is not one kind, as some said the pompilus and others the kallichthys, but any fish left free and well fed, as a sacred ox is let loose from work; he himself took the epithet as a trace of the sanctity of fish, which Homer's men eat only when hunger forces them (*Odyssey* 4.368–369; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.407).

The tenor: ὣς ἕλκʼ ἐκ δίφροιο κεχηνότα δουρὶ φαεινῷ, / κὰδ δʼ ἄρʼ ἐπὶ στόμʼ ἔωσε· πεσόντα δέ μιν λίπε θυμός, “so he dragged him gaping from the chariot on his shining spear, / and threw him down on his face; and as he fell his spirit left him” (16.409–410). κεχηνότα, “gaping”, from χάσκω, is found only here in Homer. The details match one by one. The spear through the teeth is the hook in the fish's mouth; the man hauled over the rail of the chariot is the fish drawn out of the sea onto the shore; the gaping mouth is a hooked fish's. The fisherman sits (καθήμενος), as Thestor sat (ἧστο), but one sits at ease at his work, and the other sits crouching because he has lost his wits. The point of likeness is the ease of the kill: a man who has let the reins go is as helpless as a fish on a line.

## In context

Patroclus has turned the Trojans back from the city and kills them between the ships, the river and the high wall: Pronous first, in the chest bared beside his shield, then Thestor, son of Enops, then Erylaus with a stone, and then nine more, “all of them, one after another” (16.394–418; see [[thestor-son-of-enops]] and [[patroclus]]). The fisherman stands among a list of kills told briefly, and he gives one of them a picture. When Sarpedon sees his companions dying at Patroclus' hands, he leaps down from his chariot to meet him (16.419–427).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Fishing is a likeness for killing in the Odyssey too. The Laestrygonians spear Odysseus' men “like fish” and carry them off to a joyless feast (ἰχθῦς δʼ ὣς πείροντες, *Odyssey* 10.124), with the verb, πείρω, “pierce”, that drives Patroclus' spear through Thestor's teeth (πεῖρεν, 16.405). Scylla snatches six of Odysseus' companions as an angler on a headland, casting his bait with a long rod and the horn of an ox, flings a fish ashore gasping; so they gasped as they were lifted up to the rocks (*Odyssey* 12.251–255). The dead suitors lie heaped in the hall like fish that fishermen have drawn up on the beach in a net of many meshes, longing for the salt waves while the sun takes their life (*Odyssey* 22.383–388). In the Iliad the angler's tackle appears once more, when Iris plunges to the bottom of the sea like the lead weight on a lure of ox horn that goes down bringing death to the ravenous fish (24.80–82; see [[the-lead-weight]]).

Fish eat the dead in *Iliad* 21. Achilles throws Lycaon into the Scamander and tells him to lie there among the fish, which will lick the blood from his wound (21.122–127), and the eels and fish tear at Asteropaeus' body in the river (21.201–204). The Trojans hiding in the river from Achilles are like small fish fleeing a dolphin (21.22–26).
