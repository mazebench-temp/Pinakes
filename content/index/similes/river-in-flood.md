## The simile

{{quote:6631cd53-9934-5921-9da5-de844a5e284a}}

θῦνε γὰρ ἂμ πεδίον ποταμῷ πλήθοντι ἐοικὼς / χειμάρρῳ: “for he rushed across the plain like a river in flood, a winter torrent”. θύνω is to rush or dart. πλήθω is “be full”, of a river that has risen over its banks, and a χείμαρρος (χεῖμα “winter” and ῥέω “flow”) is a stream that runs full in the winter rains (see [[torrents-meeting]]).

The torrent “scatters the dikes” (ἐκέδασσε γεφύρας). Elsewhere in the Iliad the γέφυραι of war are the lanes or passages of the battlefield (4.371, 8.378, 11.160, 20.427; see [[obscure-gephyrai]]). Here they are the banks or embankments that hold a stream in its bed, and they are ἐεργμέναι, “close-built”, packed tight (from ἔργω, “shut in”). The ἕρκεα ἀλωάων ἐριθηλέων are the walls of flourishing orchards: ἀλωή is planted ground here, and a threshing floor in the winnowing simile later in the book (5.499; see [[winnowing]]). The flood comes “suddenly” (ἐξαπίνης), “when the rain of Zeus falls heavy” (ἐπιβρίσῃ Διὸς ὄμβρος), and “many fair works of young men fall in ruin before it” (5.92). ἔργα here are the works of the farm, the tilled and planted land. κατήριπε is from the verb (ἐρείπω, “throw down, fall”) that describes the fall of warriors in this same book: ἤριπε, “he fell” (5.47, 5.58, 5.68, 5.75).

The tenor: ὣς ὑπὸ Τυδεΐδῃ πυκιναὶ κλονέοντο φάλαγγες / Τρώων, “so before the son of Tydeus the close-packed battalions of the Trojans were thrown into confusion” (5.93–94). The battalions are πυκιναί, close-packed, as the dikes were close-built, and neither holds. The point of likeness is force that cannot be stopped, and the suddenness of the ruin: the flood undoes in an hour the work of years.

The lines before the simile prepare it: “you could not have told which side he was on, whether he was mingling with the Trojans or with the Achaeans” (5.85–86). A river in flood belongs to neither bank.

## In context

While the other Achaean leaders each kill one man (5.37–84; see [[androktasia]]), Diomedes is everywhere. The simile marks the turn from the single kills to his rampage, and the narrative takes up its words at once. Pandarus sees him θύνοντʼ ἂμ πεδίον πρὸ ἕθεν κλονέοντα φάλαγγας, “rushing across the plain, driving the battalions in confusion before him” (5.96), and shoots him in the shoulder (see [[pandarus]] and [[wounded-lion]]).

Later in the book the image is turned round. When Diomedes sees Ares beside Hector, he gives ground like a traveler who stops short at a swift river rushing to the sea and runs back (5.597–600; see [[man-at-the-river]]). The river of the first half of the book becomes the barrier of the second. See [[the-aristeia-of-diomedes]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

Rivers in flood are among the Iliad's great images of force. When the armies first meet, their roar is that of two winter torrents rushing together (4.452–456). Ajax drives the Trojans across the plain like a river swollen by the rain of Zeus, a winter torrent that carries down dry oaks and pines to the sea (11.492–497), and Hector charges like a boulder that a winter torrent tears from a cliff (13.137–142). In Book 16 Zeus pours down rain “on a day of late summer” in anger at men who give crooked judgments; the rivers run full (πλήθουσι), torrents cut away the hillsides, “and the works of men are diminished” (16.384–392).

Book 17 gives the reverse picture. The two Ajaxes hold back the Trojans as a wooded ridge that stretches across a plain holds back the floods of mighty rivers (17.746–751). The ridge “holds back” (ἰσχάνει) the water, where the dikes of Book 5 could not (ἰσχανόωσιν, 5.89). In Book 21 Achilles fights a real river, the Scamander, whose wave overtakes him again and again, “for the gods are stronger than men” (21.257–264).
