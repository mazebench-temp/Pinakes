## The simile

{{quote:34177725-3e34-5788-850a-c95c6f45690b}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἂν ἀμφὶ ἄνακτα κύνες δαίτηθεν ἰόντα / σαίνωσʼ, αἰεὶ γάρ τε φέρει μειλίγματα θυμοῦ, / ὣς τοὺς ἀμφὶ λύκοι κρατερώνυχες ἠδὲ λέοντες / σαῖνον: “And as when dogs fawn around their master as he comes from a feast, / for he always brings them tidbits to please their hearts, / so the strong-clawed wolves and lions fawned / around these men” (*Odyssey* 10.216–219). ἄναξ is here only the master of the house, as the blind Cyclops calls himself his ram's master (*Odyssey* 9.452; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.216; see [[anax]]). δαίτηθεν, “from a feast”, is found only here in Homer, and so is μείλιγμα, “a soothing thing”, from μειλίσσω, “soothe”. μειλίγματα θυμοῦ are “tit-bits to appease their appetite”, Merry explains, θυμός being the appetite, as in the verse of the Iliad's feasts, “and no one's heart lacked an equal share of the feast” (on 10.217; *Iliad* 1.468; see [[thymos]] and the translation's row μειλίγματα θυμοῦ). σαίνω is the dog's greeting with its tail, and κρατερῶνυξ, the Iliad's epithet of horses and mules, “strong-hoofed”, is “strong-clawed” when it is given to beasts of prey (see [[strong-hoofed]]).

The likeness is of manner: “Nor did these rush upon the men, but they / stood up on their hind legs, fawning on them with their long tails” (*Odyssey* 10.214–215): ἀνέσταν means not only that they rose from the ground but that they stood on their hind legs, as fawning dogs do (Merry on 10.215). A house dog greets the man who feeds it; these beasts greet strangers who bring them nothing, and the men, who see in them only “the dread monsters”, are afraid (*Odyssey* 10.219). The simile has a second edge, which the next verses bring out: in this house it is the strangers who will be fed, and the food will make them beasts penned in sties (*Odyssey* 10.233–243; see [[kykeon]] and [[sties]]).

The beasts are Circe's: “around her were mountain wolves and lions, / whom she herself had bewitched, since she had given them evil drugs” (*Odyssey* 10.212–213; see [[wolves]], [[lion]], [[thelgo]] and [[drugs]]). μιν, “her”, is Circe rather than the house, the beasts being in attendance upon her (Merry on 10.212). What the bewitching was is not said. The scholia, Merry reports, give two explanations of κατέθελξεν, that she made the beasts gentle and tame, or that she changed men into lions; Scaliger took the first, and set it against Virgil, whose Circe changes men into beasts. Merry prefers the second: θέλγω is the word of the sorcery that ends in the sty (*Odyssey* 10.291, 10.318–320); Eurylochus looks on the wolves and lions as Circe's creatures no less than the swine (*Odyssey* 10.432–433); and her drugs are “evil”, against the “good drug” that Hermes gives Odysseus (*Odyssey* 10.292; Merry on 10.213; see the translation's note on 10.212–213 and [[moly]]).

## In context

The lot falls on Eurylochus, and he sets out with twenty-two companions, weeping (*Odyssey* 10.203–209; see [[eurylochus]] and [[lots]]). “In the glens they found the house of Circe, built / of polished stones, in a place with a view all round” (*Odyssey* 10.210–211; see [[circe]] and [[in-a-place-with-a-view]]), with the beasts around it. Then they hear Circe singing within as she walks to and fro at her loom, Polites bids them call to her, and she opens the shining doors; they all go in “in their ignorance”, except Eurylochus, “for he suspected a trick” (*Odyssey* 10.220–232; see [[loom]], [[polites-the-companion]] and [[odysseus-and-circe]]). Odysseus was not with them. He tells the scene as he learned it afterwards, and Eurylochus' own report, which repeats the house, the loom and the song, says nothing of the beasts (*Odyssey* 10.251–260). They come back when Eurylochus tries to hold the crew from going up to the house: Circe “will make all of us / either swine or wolves or lions, / to guard her great house for her even under compulsion” (*Odyssey* 10.432–434; see [[shape-changing]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The dogs of the simile are table dogs, fed by the master's hand. Patroclus had nine “dogs that fed at his table”, τραπεζῆες κύνες, and Achilles cut the throats of two of them on his pyre (*Iliad* 23.173–174); Priam foresees that the dogs he reared in his halls “to share my table and guard my doors” will tear him at his own door (*Iliad* 22.66–71). σαίνω is found in Homer only in the Odyssey, and only of dogs and of these beasts. The herdsman's dogs, which ran baying at the disguised Odysseus (*Odyssey* 14.29–32; see [[dogs]]), fawn around Telemachus and do not bark, so that Odysseus knows that a friend is coming (*Odyssey* 16.4–10), and the old dog Argos, lying on the dung, wags his tail and drops his ears when he knows his master, but has no strength to come nearer (*Odyssey* 17.301–304); the passages of Books 16 and 17 are given in plain renderings.

## Outside Homer

Ovid keeps the fawning. In his telling the companions of Ulysses are met at Circe's house by “a thousand wolves, and bears and lionesses mixed with the wolves”, which frighten them, though none is to be feared and none will wound them: “rather they moved their coaxing tails through the air, and fawning they kept pace with our steps” (*mille lupi mixtaeque lupis ursaeque leaeque … quin etiam blandas movere per aera caudas / nostraque adulantes comitant vestigia*, *Metamorphoses* 14.255–259; see [[ovid]]). Merry quotes the first verses as laying more stress on the tameness of the beasts (on 10.213). Virgil's Trojans, sailing past Circe's shore by night, hear the groans and rage of lions, bristly swine and bears raging in their pens, and the howling shapes of great wolves, men “whom the cruel goddess with potent herbs” had clothed in the faces and backs of beasts (*Aeneid* 7.15–20; see [[virgil]]); this is the passage on which Scaliger wrote that Homer depicts wild beasts tamed with drugs, Virgil men changed into the form of beasts (Merry on 10.213). In Apollonius the beasts follow Circe as sheep follow their herdsman out of the fold (*Argonautica* 4.672 and following, as Merry reports it), and Merry compares the story of Beder and Giauhare in the *Arabian Nights*, where the horses, camels, mules, asses and cows that try to warn King Beder away from Queen Labe's shore had once been her lovers (on 10.212).
