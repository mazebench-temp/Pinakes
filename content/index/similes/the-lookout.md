## The simile

{{quote:837f0dd8-6ebf-59c0-8d6d-9d52d06dd509}}

ὅσσον δʼ ἠεροειδὲς ἀνὴρ ἴδεν ὀφθαλμοῖσιν / ἥμενος ἐν σκοπιῇ, λεύσσων ἐπὶ οἴνοπα πόντον, / τόσσον ἐπιθρῴσκουσι θεῶν ὑψηχέες ἵπποι: “as far as a man sees with his eyes into the haze as he sits on a lookout, gazing over the wine-dark sea, so far do the high-neighing horses of the gods leap at a bound”. ἠεροειδής is “misty, hazy” (ἀήρ, “mist”, and εἶδος, “look”), and ἠεροειδές here is the misty distance itself; the word is an epithet of the sea (23.744, and often in the Odyssey). A σκοπιή is a lookout, a height for watching, like the one from which a goatherd sees a storm cloud coming over the sea (4.275; see [[storm-cloud]]). λεύσσω is “gaze”, and οἶνοψ “wine-dark” (see [[wine-dark]]). ἐπιθρῴσκω is “leap upon, leap over”, and ὑψηχής, “high-neighing” (ὕψι, “high”, and ἠχή, “sound”), is said of horses (also 23.27; see [[high-neighing]]).

The point of likeness is distance. A leap is measured by a view: from a height over the sea, as far as the eye can reach into the haze, to the horizon. The horses fly “between earth and the starry sky” (5.769), and every bound covers the whole width of what a man can see. The man sits still and looks; the horses cross what he sees in one stride. The simile does not say how many leaps it takes to reach Troy. The next line has them there.

## In context

Hera and Athena have armed (5.711–747; see [[arming-scene]]), the gates of heaven kept by the Hours have groaned open of themselves (5.749–752; see [[hours]]), and Zeus has allowed Hera to drive Ares from the battle (5.753–766). “She whipped the horses; and the pair flew on not unwilling” (5.768), the line used of Ares' horses when Iris drove Aphrodite to Olympus (5.366). Then the goddesses reach the plain where the Simoeis and the Scamander meet, and Hera unyokes the horses and hides them in mist (5.773–777; see [[timid-doves]] and [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]).

The horses of the gods have their earthly counterpart in the book. Aeneas' horses are of the breed that Zeus gave Tros, “the best of all horses under the dawn and the sun” (5.265–267; see [[ganymede-and-the-horses-of-tros]] and [[horses]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Gods travel by the measure of the world. Poseidon comes down from the peak of Samothrace, and the mountains and the forest tremble under his feet: three strides he takes, and with the fourth he reaches Aegae, where his house is in the depths of the sea (13.17–21).

## Outside Homer

The author of the treatise *On the Sublime* quotes these lines as an example of Homer's greatness of conception. Homer measures the horses' leap by a cosmic distance, he says, and anyone might exclaim at the greatness of it that if the horses of the gods take two such leaps in a row they will find no room left in the world ([Longinus], *On the Sublime* 9.5; see [[longinus]]).
