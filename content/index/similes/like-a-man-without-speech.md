## The simile

{{quote:3239be21-9621-54bb-bc8e-2065eb5ea5ef}}

τίφθʼ οὕτως, Ὀδυσεῦ, κατʼ ἄρʼ ἕζεαι ἶσος ἀναύδῳ, / θυμὸν ἔδων, βρώμης δʼ οὐχ ἅπτεαι οὐδὲ ποτῆτος; “Why do you sit like this, Odysseus, like a man without speech, / eating your heart, and do not touch food or drink?” (*Odyssey* 10.378–379). ἄναυδος, “without speech”, is said once more in Homer, of Odysseus himself, when he comes out of the sea on Scheria “breathless and speechless” (*Odyssey* 5.456). ἶσος with the dative is a short likeness, as ἶσος Ἄρηι, “the equal of Ares”, is given to warriors (*Iliad* 11.295, 11.604; *Odyssey* 8.115; see [[equal-of-ares]]). The word began with a digamma, and W. W. Merry would scan ἕζεαι before it as two syllables; he reports that Buttmann and Ahrens, who deny a present ἕζομαι, read the aorist ἕζεο (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.378; see the translation's note on 10.374–378). θυμὸν ἔδων, “eating your heart”, is the grief in which the companions had lain two days on the shore of Aeaea, “eating our hearts out with weariness and sorrows together” (*Odyssey* 10.143, as 9.75), and in which Bellerophon wandered alone over the Aleian plain, “eating his heart out, avoiding the paths of men” (*Iliad* 6.202).

The likeness is Circe's, and it gives what she sees: a man who sits silent and will not put out his hands to the food. The narrator has already said what she cannot see: “but I sat with my thoughts elsewhere, and my heart foreboded evil” (*Odyssey* 10.374). ἀλλοφρονέων is “with my thoughts elsewhere”, Merry explains, comparing the boxer Euryalus led away from the ring, “his wits wandering” (on 10.374; *Iliad* 23.698; see the translation's row ἀλλοφρονέων; ἶσος ἀναύδῳ). Circe guesses wrong, “Do you suspect some other trick?”, and reminds him of her oath (*Odyssey* 10.380–381; see [[oath]]). His answer explains the silence: “Circe, what man who was fair-minded / would bring himself to taste food and drink / before he had set his companions free and seen them with his eyes?” (*Odyssey* 10.383–385). He will not eat in the house where his men are swine.

## In context

When Circe has sworn her oath and Odysseus has gone up into her bed, her four handmaids make ready the chairs, the tables and the wine, and heat the water for his bath; he is bathed, anointed and dressed, and seated on a silver-studded chair, and the housekeeper sets bread and many dainties before him (*Odyssey* 10.345–372; see [[handmaids]], [[bathing]] and [[circe]]). It is the welcome of a guest, and he refuses its meal (see [[xenia]] and [[fasting]]). His refusal frees the companions: Circe goes out with her wand, opens the doors of the sty and drives them out, and makes them men again (*Odyssey* 10.388–396; see [[boar]] and [[odysseus-and-circe]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Antenor remembers that Odysseus, before he spoke, would stand with his eyes fixed on the ground and his scepter held stiff, “like a man who knows nothing”, until the voice came (*Iliad* 3.216–224; see [[like-a-man-who-knows-nothing]]); among the Phaeacians he draws his mantle over his head and weeps at the singer's song (*Odyssey* 8.83–92; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]). Achilles, too, will take no food or drink while his companion lies dead and unavenged (*Iliad* 19.205–214, 19.303–308; see [[fasting]]).
