## The simile

{{quote:d01f9030-9be3-513a-ba12-b22781349d6b}}

ὡς δʼ ὅθʼ ὑπὸ φρικὸς Βορέω ἀναπάλλεται ἰχθὺς / θίνʼ ἐν φυκιόεντι, μέλαν δέ ἑ κῦμα κάλυψεν: “And as when under the ripple of the North Wind a fish leaps up / on a shore full of seaweed, and a dark wave hides it” (23.692–693). φρίξ, “ripple”, is the dark ruffling that a wind spreads over the face of the sea. The armies sat like the sea under the ripple of the West Wind when Hector made his challenge (7.63; see [[the-ripple-of-the-west-wind]]), and Achilles told the dead Lycaon that a fish, “Leaping along the wave, one will dart up under the dark ripple”, would eat his white fat (21.126–127; see [[fish]]). This fish leaps up under the North Wind's ripple, one of the two winds that fanned the pyre of Patroclus earlier in the book (23.192–230; see [[boreas]]). ἀναπάλλεται, “leaps up”, occurs only here, and so does φυκιόεις, “full of seaweed”, from the φῦκος that the two winds from Thrace cast up along the shore (9.7; see [[two-winds-from-thrace]]). Walter Leaf takes θίς here to be the sand where it lies under shallow water (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.692). μέλαν δέ ἑ κῦμα κάλυψεν, “and a dark wave hides it”, ends the verse in which the goddess Ino, in the likeness of a diving sea bird, an αἴθυια, goes down again into the sea (*Odyssey* 5.352–353).

The tenor: ὣς πληγεὶς ἀνέπαλτʼ, “so he leapt up when he was struck” (23.694). ἀνέπαλτο is the start of a body that is hit: Nestor's trace horse reared in agony when Paris' arrow went into its brain (8.85), and Achilles leapt up when he saw Hector (20.424). Leaf sees a difficulty: the narrator has just said that Euryalus' limbs gave way “there”, on the spot (23.691), and now he leaps. Leaf's solution is that the blow lifts him clean off his feet, so that he leaps with his body arched backward and falls like a leaping fish (on 23.692). The point of likeness is that sudden, helpless leap and the fall that follows it: the fish flashes up and the dark wave covers it, and the boxer is lifted by the blow and goes down. The North Wind is the force that throws the fish, as the fist of Epeius throws the man.

## In context

Epeius, the son of Panopeus, has claimed the mule before anyone else rises: he is no fighter in battle, he says, but no one will beat him with his fists, and he will tear his opponent's flesh and crush his bones (23.664–675; see [[epeius]]). Only Euryalus stands up, the son of Mecisteus, whose father once beat all the Cadmeians at the funeral games of Oedipus; Diomedes girds him, gives him the straps, and wishes him victory (23.676–684; see [[euryalus]] and [[diomedes]]). The two square up, their heavy hands mingle, and there is a terrible grinding of jaws; then Epeius rushes in and strikes him on the cheek as he peers about (23.685–691). The simile follows. Then “great-hearted Epeius / took him in his hands and set him upright” (23.694–695), and his companions lead him through the gathering with trailing feet, “spitting thick blood, his head lolling to one side”, and set him down among them with his wits wandering; they themselves fetch the two-handled cup, the loser's prize (23.695–699; see [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Fish appear in the Iliad's similes as prey. Patroclus drags Thestor over the rail of his chariot on his spear as a man sitting on a jutting rock drags a fish out of the sea with line and bronze hook (16.406–409; see [[the-fisherman]]), and the Trojans in the river cower under the banks as small fish flee a dolphin (21.22–24; see [[the-dolphin-and-the-fishes]]). In the Odyssey the beggar Irus, struck under the ear by Odysseus, falls in the dust with blood coming up into his mouth, gnashing his teeth and kicking the ground, and Odysseus drags him out by the foot (*Odyssey* 18.96–101).

## Outside Homer

Leaf points to [[virgil|Virgil]]'s boxing match, where the beaten Dares is led to the ships by his friends, dragging his weak knees, his head lolling from side to side, spitting thick blood and teeth mixed with blood (*ast illum fidi aequales genua aegra trahentem / iactantemque utroque caput crassumque cruorem / ore eiectantem mixtosque in sanguine dentes / ducunt ad navis*, *Aeneid* 5.468–471); Leaf finds in it the Roman taste for outdoing the Homeric verse in gory detail (on 23.697).
