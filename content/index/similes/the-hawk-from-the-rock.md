## The simile

{{quote:ec907289-460a-59da-ab3c-461b3406b456}}

αὐτὸς δʼ ὥς τʼ ἴρηξ ὠκύπτερος ὦρτο πέτεσθαι, / ὅς ῥά τʼ ἀπʼ αἰγίλιπος πέτρης περιμήκεος ἀρθεὶς / ὁρμήσῃ πεδίοιο διώκειν ὄρνεον ἄλλο: “And he himself sped off as a swift-winged hawk flies, that rises from a sheer and towering rock, and darts across the plain to chase some other bird” (13.62–64). ἴρηξ is the epic form of ἱέραξ, the hawk or falcon (see [[hawk]]). ὠκύπτερος, “swift-winged” (ὠκύς, “swift”, and πτερόν, “wing”), and ὄρνεον, “bird”, occur only here. αἰγίλιψ is “sheer”, a rock that ancient readers explained as one left even by goats (αἴξ, λείπω); the spring of dark water in the similes for Agamemnon's and Patroclus' tears runs down such a rock (9.15 = 16.4; see [[the-spring-of-dark-water]]). περιμήκης is “towering”, and πεδίοιο, “across the plain”, is the genitive of the ground the bird covers. The tenor is a single verse: ὣς ἀπὸ τῶν ἤϊξε Ποσειδάων ἐνοσίχθων, “so from them darted Poseidon the earth-shaker” (13.65).

The point of likeness is the sudden start and the speed. The hawk leaves its rock in pursuit of something else, and the god goes on from the Ajaxes to the weary men in the rear (13.83–90). The height is his too: he watched the battle from the topmost peak of Samothrace before he went down to his house in the sea (13.10–38; see [[poseidon]]).

## In context

Poseidon comes up out of the sea “in the likeness of Calchas in form and in tireless voice” (13.45). He tells the two Ajaxes that the Achaeans will hold the Trojans elsewhere, but not where Hector leads them “like a flame”, and strikes them both with his staff, making their limbs light, “their feet and their hands above” (13.46–61; see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]] and [[the-two-ajaxes]]). Then the hawk. The son of Oileus is the first to know him:

{{quote:838178da-bd0d-5175-996d-65f28e89b9cd}}

The god is known by his going, “the movements of his feet and legs from behind” (see the translation's note on 13.71 and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). Ajax knows the god by his feet and legs, not by a bird's flight, so the hawk is a likeness of manner, as in the other passages where a god goes like a bird (see [[like-vultures]]). What the god put into the Ajaxes they now feel: Telamonian Ajax answers that his own hands quiver around his spear and his feet are rushing on (13.77–80).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Gods go down like hawks. Apollo descends from the mountains of Ida “like a swift hawk, the dove-killer” (ἴρηκι ἐοικὼς ὠκέϊ φασσοφόνῳ, 15.237–238), Thetis leaps down from snowy Olympus “like a hawk” with Achilles' new armor (ἴρηξ ὥς, 18.616), and Athena darts from the sky like a long-winged, clear-voiced bird of prey (ἅρπῃ, 19.350–351). Men attack like hawks: Patroclus charges the Lycians like a swift hawk that scatters jackdaws and starlings (16.582–584), and Achilles swoops on Hector as a mountain hawk (κίρκος) swoops on a dove (22.139–142). The hawk is the measure of speed: Ajax tells Hector at the end of this book that he will soon pray for horses “swifter than hawks” (13.819), and in the Odyssey not even a hawk could keep pace with the Phaeacian ship (*Odyssey* 13.86–87).
