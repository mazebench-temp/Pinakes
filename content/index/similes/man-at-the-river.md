## The simile

{{quote:46396d8b-5de3-5bf9-b721-fc1ab86c70f5}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἀνὴρ ἀπάλαμνος ἰὼν πολέος πεδίοιο / στήῃ ἐπʼ ὠκυρόῳ ποταμῷ ἅλα δὲ προρέοντι / ἀφρῷ μορμύροντα ἰδών, ἀνά τʼ ἔδραμʼ ὀπίσσω: “as a helpless man, going across a wide plain, stops short at a swift-flowing river rushing on to the sea, and seeing it seething with foam, runs back”. ἀπάλαμνος (from ἀ-, “without”, and παλάμη, “palm, hand”, and so “means, resource”) is a man with no way of doing anything, and the word occurs only here in Homer. ὠκύροος, “swift-flowing”, is also said of the river Celadon (7.133; see [[swift-flowing]]). μορμύρω is the churning roar of rushing water: of Oceanus flowing round Hephaestus' cave (18.403), and of the Scamander in flood against Achilles, “seething with foam and blood and corpses” (21.325).

The tenor: ὣς τότε Τυδεΐδης ἀνεχάζετο, “so then the son of Tydeus gave ground” (5.600). ἀναχάζομαι is the verb of Diomedes' other retreats in the book. When Apollo warned him off Aeneas, he “drew back a little way” (5.443), and when Athena reproaches him he says, “that is why I am giving ground now myself” (5.822).

The point of likeness is a man stopped by something he cannot cross, and the sudden turn back. The river is Ares, who “ranged now in front of Hector, now behind him” (5.595). The simile turns an earlier one round. In the first battle of the book, Diomedes was the river in flood that no dike could hold (5.87–92; see [[river-in-flood]]). Now he is the traveler at the bank.

## In context

Hector charges with the Trojan battalions, and Ares and Enyo lead them, Enyo bringing Tumult with her and Ares wielding a huge spear (5.590–595; see [[ares]], [[enyo]] and [[tumult]]). “Seeing him, Diomedes, good at the war cry, shuddered” (5.596). He can see the god because Athena took the mist from his eyes so that he could tell god from man (5.127–128), and he can name him: “and now Ares is beside him there, in the likeness of a mortal man” (5.604). The verb of his fear, ῥίγησε, is the verb of the goatherd who sees a storm cloud coming over the sea and drives his flock into a cave (4.279; see [[storm-cloud]]).

His speech turns the retreat into an order. Hector always has a god at his side who keeps ruin away, and now it is Ares; so the Achaeans are to give ground “always backward”, with their faces turned toward the Trojans, and not be eager to fight in force against gods (5.601–606). It is a withdrawal, not a flight. Athena will taunt him for it (5.800–813), and he will answer with her own order not to fight the gods (5.815–824; see [[diomedes-wounds-ares]] and [[the-aristeia-of-diomedes]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

A real river stops a greater hero. In Book 21 the Scamander rises against Achilles, and its wave overtakes him again and again as water overtakes a man who leads it through a garden, “for the gods are stronger than men” (21.257–264). The recoil of a man who meets something too strong for him is the subject of another simile: Alexander starts back from Menelaus like a man who sees a serpent in a mountain glade (3.33–37; see [[man-who-meets-a-serpent]]).
