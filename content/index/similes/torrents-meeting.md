## The simile

ὡς δʼ ὅτε χείμαρροι ποταμοὶ κατʼ ὄρεσφι ῥέοντες / ἐς μισγάγκειαν συμβάλλετον ὄβριμον ὕδωρ / κρουνῶν ἐκ μεγάλων κοίλης ἔντοσθε χαράδρης, / τῶν δέ τε τηλόσε δοῦπον ἐν οὔρεσιν ἔκλυε ποιμήν: “as when winter torrents, flowing down the mountains, throw their heavy water together where two valleys meet, from great springs, inside a hollow ravine, and far off in the mountains a shepherd hears their roar”.

χείμαρροι (χεῖμα, “winter”, and ῥέω, “flow”) are streams that run full in the winter rains. μισγάγκεια, “the meeting of glens” (μίσγω, “mingle”, and ἄγκος, “glen”), occurs only here in Homer. συμβάλλετον is a dual, “the two of them throw together”: two torrents, for two armies. ὄβριμον ὕδωρ is “heavy, mighty water”, κρουνοί are springs, and a χαράδρη is a ravine cut by running water. δοῦπος is a heavy, dull sound, the roar of water and also the thud of a falling man (see [[fell-with-a-thud]]).

The tenor: ὣς τῶν μισγομένων γένετο ἰαχή τε πόνος τε, “so from their mingling came the shouting and the toil”. μισγομένων, “as they mingled”, answers μισγάγκεια, the place where the waters mingle. The point of likeness is sound: two masses rush together and make one roar. The shepherd hears it “far off” (τηλόσε), and from far off the two torrents are a single noise. Like the storm cloud, which needed a goatherd to see it (4.275–279; see [[storm-cloud]]), the simile has a herdsman in it to take in what happens.

## In context

{{quote:29c22b36-2608-5add-8471-8725d80d3606}}

The book has been moving toward this moment since the truce was broken. The two approaches were told apart: the Danaans silent like waves, the Trojans loud like ewes in a fold (4.422–438; see [[waves-on-the-shore]] and [[sheep-in-the-fold]]), with Ares rousing one side and Athena the other, and Terror, Rout and Strife among them (4.439–445; see [[strife]], [[terror]] and [[rout]]). Now they come “together into one place” (ἐς χῶρον ἕνα, 4.446), and the lines of the clash are made of joining words. σύν … σύν, “together”, dashes the shields, the spears and the fury of men together, and the bossed shields press against one another (4.447–449). Then the sounds: “cries of pain and cries of triumph together from men killing and being killed, and the earth ran with blood” (4.450–451). ὀλλύντων τε καὶ ὀλλυμένων, “killing and being killed”, sets the active and passive of one verb side by side (see [[polyptoton]] and [[the-armies-clash]]). The simile gathers all this into one sound.

After it the poem turns to single men. “Antilochus was the first to kill a helmeted man of the Trojans” (4.457; see [[the-first-battle]] and [[like-a-tower]]). The six lines of the clash (4.446–451) are repeated word for word when the armies meet again in Book 8 (8.60–65).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Rivers in flood are among the Iliad's great images of force. Diomedes storms over the plain like a river in winter flood that sweeps away the dikes (5.87–92). Ajax drives the Trojans like a river swollen by the rain of Zeus, a winter torrent coming down the mountains (χειμάρρους κατʼ ὄρεσφιν) to the plain and carrying dry oaks and pines (11.492–497). Hector rushes on like a boulder that a winter torrent tears from a cliff (13.137–142). In Book 16 the autumn rains that Zeus pours down on men who give crooked judgments fill every river, and the torrents cut away the hillsides as they roar down to the sea (16.384–393).

The listening shepherd has companions in other similes. The Trojan watch fires burn on the plain like the stars around the moon on a windless night, when every peak stands clear and the shepherd rejoices in his heart (8.555–559). When the Trojans follow Aeneas as sheep follow the ram to water, the shepherd is glad at heart (13.491–494).
