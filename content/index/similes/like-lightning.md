## The simile

{{quote:dfe10bab-beb0-55ff-8fbb-1a97c9b49806}}

ἔγχεα δέ σφιν / ὄρθʼ ἐπὶ σαυρωτῆρος ἐλήλατο, τῆλε δὲ χαλκὸς / λάμφʼ ὥς τε στεροπὴ πατρὸς Διός: “and their spears were driven upright on their butt-spikes, and far off the bronze shone like the lightning of father Zeus”. The companions of Diomedes sleep with their shields under their heads and their spears driven into the ground beside them, butt down and points up, ready to hand (σαυρωτήρ, the butt-spike, occurs only here; see the translation's note on 10.153–154 and [[spear]]). The comparison is a half-line. τῆλε, “far off”, gives its point: the points of bronze catch whatever light there is and flash out into the dark, as lightning does. στεροπή is the flash itself; the thunderbolt is κεραυνός (see [[thunderbolt]]).

The half-line is the only light in a scene of sleep. Diomedes lies on the hide of an ox of the fields with a shining rug under his head, and the old man wakes him with his heel (10.155–161; see [[diomedes]] and [[nestor]]).

## In context

Nestor goes through the camp waking the chiefs: Odysseus first, by calling out, and then Diomedes, whom he finds outside his hut among his sleeping companions (10.137–161). The book began with another lightning, the flashes of Zeus to which Agamemnon's groans are likened (10.5–10; see [[lightning-before-the-storm]]). There the lightning foretold a storm; here it is the gleam of the weapons with which the Achaeans will meet it. Aristotle took the custom of the planted spears as a problem: perhaps it was not the better way, he answers, but it was the way of the time, “as the Illyrians still do” (*Poetics* 25, 1461a; see [[aristotle-upright-spears]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same half-line describes Hector at the start of the next day's battle. He is now among the foremost, now among the hindmost, like a baleful star that shines out of the clouds and sinks into them again, and, “all in bronze, he shone like the lightning of father Zeus who bears the aegis” (λάμφʼ ὥς τε στεροπὴ πατρὸς Διὸς αἰγιόχοιο, 11.62–66; see [[hector]]). Idomeneus, armed in his hut, goes out “like the lightning which the son of Cronus takes in his hand and shakes from shining Olympus, showing a sign to mortals”, and the bronze shines about his chest as he runs (13.242–245; see [[idomeneus]]). The flash of bronze is itself called στεροπή: Zeus, sitting apart from the other gods, looks on “the flash of bronze, the killers and the killed” (χαλκοῦ τε στεροπήν, 11.80–83), and the whole earth laughs “under the flash of bronze” when the Achaeans pour out from the ships to arm with Achilles (19.356–364). In the Odyssey Telemachus marvels at “the flash of bronze” in the palace of Menelaus (*Odyssey* 4.71–72). See [[bronze]] and [[like-a-star]].
