## The simile

ἠΰτε πῦρ ἀΐδηλον ἐπιφλέγει ἄσπετον ὕλην / οὔρεος ἐν κορυφῇς, ἕκαθεν δέ τε φαίνεται αὐγή: “as a destroying fire sets a vast forest ablaze on the peaks of a mountain, and the glare is seen far off”. ἀΐδηλος is usually explained as “making unseen”, that is, “destroying” (ἀ- “not” and ἰδεῖν “see”); some take it as “unseen, dark”. ἄσπετος (“unspeakable”) means immense. αὐγή is the glare of the fire, seen ἕκαθεν (“from afar”).

The tenor: ὣς τῶν ἐρχομένων ἀπὸ χαλκοῦ θεσπεσίοιο / αἴγλη παμφανόωσα διʼ αἰθέρος οὐρανὸν ἷκε, “so, as they marched, from their marvelous bronze the dazzling gleam went up through the upper air to the sky”. αἴγλη is radiance and παμφανόωσα “shining all over”; αἰθήρ is the bright upper air, οὐρανός the sky. The point of likeness is light seen from far away: the [[bronze]] of an army on the move flashes like a fire on a distant mountain. The fire is also a destroyer, and its epithet keeps that in view.

## In context

{{quote:e83a7d61-3db0-5e2e-9361-c5f842bcf139}}

The heralds have summoned the army, the kings rush about marshaling the men, and Athena speeds “flashing” (παιφάσσουσα) through the ranks with the [[aegis]], until “war became sweeter to them than to go home” (2.441–454).

This is the first of a famous run of similes that carries the army from the camp to the plain (2.455–483). Each takes up a different aspect. The fire gives sight, the gleam of bronze. The birds give sound and motion as the men pour onto the plain ([[birds-on-the-asian-meadow]]). The leaves and flowers and the flies give number and eagerness ([[leaves-and-flowers]], [[flies-around-the-milk-pails]]). The goatherds give the leaders sorting out the men ([[goatherds-sorting-their-flocks]]), and last Agamemnon stands out, like three gods and like a bull ([[like-zeus-ares-and-poseidon]], [[bull-above-the-herd]]). Then the poet calls on the Muses to name the leaders ([[invocation-of-the-muses]]).

Fire returns when the Catalogue is over and the army sets off across the plain, “as if the whole land were being devoured by fire” (2.780; [[the-earth-groans-as-for-typhoeus]]). The Catalogue is framed by fire.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same words, πῦρ ἀΐδηλον (“destroying fire”), open the forest-fire simile of Agamemnon's great day of killing: the wind drives the flames everywhere, the thickets fall uprooted, and the heads of the fleeing Trojans fall before him (Iliad 11.155–159). When the army pours out for Achilles' return to battle, “the gleam reached the sky” (αἴγλη δʼ οὐρανὸν ἷκε), and all the earth laughed under the flash of bronze (19.362–363).
