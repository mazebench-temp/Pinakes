## The simile

{{quote:2930d636-c74e-5068-9322-3a90ff084757}}

ἀλλʼ ἔμενʼ ὡς ὅτε τις σῦς οὔρεσιν ἀλκὶ πεποιθώς, / ὅς τε μένει κολοσυρτὸν ἐπερχόμενον πολὺν ἀνδρῶν / χώρῳ ἐν οἰοπόλῳ, φρίσσει δέ τε νῶτον ὕπερθεν· / ὀφθαλμὼ δʼ ἄρα οἱ πυρὶ λάμπετον· αὐτὰρ ὀδόντας / θήγει, ἀλέξασθαι μεμαὼς κύνας ἠδὲ καὶ ἄνδρας: “but he stood his ground like a boar in the mountains, trusting in its courage, which awaits a great din of men coming on in a lonely place, and bristles up along its back; and its two eyes blaze with fire; and its tusks it whets, eager to ward off dogs and men” (13.471–475). σῦς is the boar (see [[boar]]). ἀλκὶ πεποιθώς, “trusting in its courage”, is said of lions and boars and of men in their strength: Aeneas standing over Pandarus “like a lion” (5.299; see [[like-a-lion]]), a mountain lion that seizes a cow from the herd and a boar that turns on the dogs in Book 17 (17.61, 17.728), Hector standing firm by Patroclus' body (18.158), and the mountain lion to which Odysseus is likened when he comes out of the thicket in Phaeacia (*Odyssey* 6.130; see [[alke]]). κολοσυρτός, the din of a hunting party, occurs only here and in the boar simile of the Lapiths (12.147; see [[wild-boars-in-the-mountains]]). οἰοπόλος is “lonely”, though some derived it from ὄϊς, “sheep”, a sheep-grazed place. The boar's eyes blaze with fire (πυρὶ λάμπετον, a dual; see [[eyes-like-fire]]), and it whets its tusks, as Odysseus' boar at bay whets its white tusk (11.416; see [[the-boar-at-bay]]).

The tenor: ὣς μένεν Ἰδομενεὺς δουρικλυτός, οὐδʼ ὑπεχώρει, / Αἰνείαν ἐπιόντα βοηθόον, “so Idomeneus, famed for his spear, stood his ground, and did not give way as Aeneas came on to help” (13.476–477). μένω, “stand, await”, runs through the passage: ἔμενε, μένει, μένεν. The point of likeness is courage at bay, one beast against many hunters in a lonely place. The verse before the simile sets it against another likeness: “flight did not seize Idomeneus as if he were a darling child” (13.470; see [[darling]]).

The boar shows no fear; the man admits it. Idomeneus calls his companions and tells them plainly: “I am alone, and terribly afraid of Aeneas, swift of foot, coming on”, since Aeneas is strong and “has the flower of youth, which is the greatest power”; if they were of an age, one of them would soon carry off the mastery (13.481–486; see [[kratos]]). The simile gives his stand, and the speech gives what it costs an older man.

## In context

Deiphobus, after Idomeneus' boast over Alcathous, has gone to fetch Aeneas, whom Alcathous reared, and Aeneas comes “much intent on war” (13.455–469). Idomeneus waits for him like the boar and calls on Ascalaphus, Aphareus, Deipyrus, Meriones and Antilochus, who stand close beside him with their shields leaning on their shoulders (13.477–488). Aeneas calls on Deiphobus, Paris and Agenor, and the army follows him as sheep follow the ram (13.489–495; see [[sheep-following-the-ram]]). The two are “the equals of Ares” (13.500), and Aeneas' spear misses (13.502–505).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The boar is Idomeneus' animal. In the review of the army he stands among the champions “like a boar in courage” (συῒ εἴκελος ἀλκήν, 4.253; see [[like-a-boar]]). Other defenders are boars at bay: Odysseus, cut off by the Trojans (11.414–420), the two Lapiths at the gate (12.146–150), Hector among the hunters, a boar or a lion (12.41–48; see [[the-boar-or-lion-at-bay]]), and Telamonian Ajax, who scatters the Trojans like a boar that scatters dogs and young men in the mountains (17.281–285). In the Odyssey the boar of Parnassus comes out of its thicket against Odysseus and the sons of Autolycus with its mane bristling and fire in its eyes (φρίξας εὖ λοφιήν, πῦρ δʼ ὀφθαλμοῖσι δεδορκώς, *Odyssey* 19.445–446).

## Outside Homer

In the Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles*, Heracles leaps down from his chariot like a tusked boar in a mountain glen that means to fight the hunters, whetting its white tusk, with foam around its mouth, its eyes like blazing fire and the bristles standing up on its mane and neck (*Shield* 386–392; see [[hesiod]]).
