## The simile

{{quote:48afa135-d98d-560b-a6dd-4fcd340c529b}}

ὡς εἴ τε δαφοινοὶ θῶες ὄρεσφιν / ἀμφʼ ἔλαφον κεραὸν βεβλημένον: “as blood-red jackals in the mountains press around a horned stag that is wounded”. θῶες are usually taken to be jackals (see [[jackal]]). δαφοινός, “blood-red”, is the color of a serpent's back and of a lion's hide (2.308, 10.23); whether these jackals are tawny or red with blood is left open (see the translation's note on 11.474). ἔλαφος κεραός is the horned stag (see [[deer]]). A man has hit it ἰῷ ἀπὸ νευρῆς, “with an arrow from the string”, and it has got away from him on its feet, ὄφρʼ αἷμα λιαρὸν καὶ γούνατʼ ὀρώρῃ, “as long as the blood is warm and its knees have strength to move” (11.476–477). When it is beaten down, “the jackals that eat raw flesh devour it in the mountains in a shady glade” (ὠμοφάγοι, “eating raw flesh”; δαρδάπτω, “devour”). Then a god (δαίμων) brings a lion, σίντης, “a ravager”: θῶες μέν τε διέτρεσαν, αὐτὰρ ὃ δάπτει, “and the jackals scatter in fear, and he devours it” (11.478–481).

The tenor: “so then around wise-hearted Odysseus of subtle wiles the Trojans pressed, many and valiant; but the hero, darting with his spear, kept off the pitiless day” (11.482–484). Odysseus is the wounded stag, the Trojans are the jackals, and the lion is Ajax, who comes up with his shield like a tower: “and the Trojans scattered in fear, this way and that” (11.485–486; see [[like-a-tower]] and [[ajax]]). The verb of the jackals' flight, διέτρεσαν, is the verb of the Trojans'.

The comparison goes past the story. The lion of the simile eats the stag; Ajax saves Odysseus, and Menelaus leads him out of the throng by the hand (11.487–488). What the two share is the scattering of the pack when the stronger beast arrives.

## In context

Socus' spear has torn the skin from Odysseus' ribs, Odysseus has killed him, and when the Trojans see his blood they all come at him (11.434–460). He gives ground and shouts three times. Menelaus hears and brings Ajax (11.461–472; see [[odysseus]] and [[menelaus]]). The simile marks Odysseus' change from the boar at bay of sixty lines before to a wounded animal that can only hold off the end (11.414–420; see [[the-boar-at-bay]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The scene of the simile recurs with Hector as the lion. When Apollo has restored him, the Achaeans give way as dogs and country men give up the chase of a horned stag or a wild goat when a bearded lion appears in the path and turns them all back (15.271–276). Wolves kill a great horned stag in the simile of the Myrmidons arming (16.156–163). Jackals are named once more, when Poseidon reminds the Achaeans that the Trojans used to be like timid deer, the food of jackals, leopards and wolves (13.101–104).
