## The simile

{{quote:1ae27d8f-d405-55b9-a689-20e4ddbca100}}

αἰθυίῃ δʼ ἐικυῖα ποτῇ ἀνεδύσετο λίμνης, “and like a diving bird on the wing she rose up from the sea” (*Odyssey* 5.337). The bird is the αἴθυια, named only in these two half-verses in Homer. ποτῇ, “on the wing”, “in flight”, occurs only here; a scholium reports a reading ποτήν and prefers the dative (Merry's critical note on 5.337). λίμνη, elsewhere a lake or a pool, is here the sea, as at Aegae, where Poseidon's glorious house is built “in the depths of the sea” (βένθεσι λίμνης, *Iliad* 13.21; see [[aegae]]); the sun rises out of the λίμνη of Ocean (*Odyssey* 3.1).

The goddess goes back in the same words: αὐτὴ δʼ ἂψ ἐς πόντον ἐδύσετο κυμαίνοντα / αἰθυίῃ ἐικυῖα· μέλαν δέ ἑ κῦμα κάλυψεν, “and she herself plunged back into the billowing sea / like a diving bird; and a dark wave hid her” (*Odyssey* 5.352–353). The first half of 5.352 is the verse with which Eidothea and Proteus go down into the sea (ὑπὸ πόντον ἐδύσετο κυμαίνοντα, *Odyssey* 4.425, 4.570; see [[billowing]]), and the second half of 5.353 ends the Iliad's simile of the fish that leaps up under the North Wind and is hidden by a dark wave (*Iliad* 23.693; see [[the-leaping-fish]]).

**The point of likeness.** W. W. Merry holds that the αἴθυια “must be a bird that dives, perhaps the *mergus*”, and keeps it apart from the λάρος, the gull of Hermes' flight earlier in the book, which does not dive deep (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.51; see [[gull]] and [[the-gull]]). The likeness, he says, is “not like a sea-bird in shape, but in the sudden plunge with which she dives into the sea”, and he reports Nitzsch's comparison of the Nereids in Apollonius, who go down into the depths like diving birds (*Argonautica* 4.966; Merry on 5.353). As with Hermes and the gull, it is the movement that is compared, out of the water onto the raft and down into the water again.

**The first verse.** The verse of her rising was wanting in most copies; Aristarchus hesitated over rejecting it, and wrote ὑπεδύσατο for ἀνεδύσετο (the scholia, in Merry's critical note on 5.337). Merry's text brackets it (see the translation's note on 5.337).

## In context

Poseidon's storm has broken the mast and thrown Odysseus from his raft; he has climbed back onto it, and the winds toss it over the open sea (*Odyssey* 5.313–332; see [[thistles-in-the-north-wind]]). Then the daughter of Cadmus sees him, “Ino of the lovely ankles, / Leucothea, who was once a mortal with human voice, / but now in the open sea has a share of honor from the gods” (*Odyssey* 5.333–335; see [[leucothea]] and [[cadmus]]). She pities him, rises like the bird, sits on the raft, and tells him to strip, leave the raft and swim for the land of the Phaeacians with her immortal veil stretched under his chest (*Odyssey* 5.336–350; see [[veil]]). When she has given him the veil, she goes back like the bird (*Odyssey* 5.351–353). Odysseus suspects a trap and stays on the raft until the wave breaks it (*Odyssey* 5.354–375). In the end he unties the veil at the mouth of the river and lets it go, and a great wave carries it back down the stream, “and at once Ino / took it in her own hands” (*Odyssey* 5.459–462).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Odyssey likens its gods to birds of the sea and the shore. Hermes skims the waves to Calypso's island like a gull in the same book (*Odyssey* 5.51–54; see [[the-gull]]), and Athena leaves Pylos in the likeness of a sea eagle (*Odyssey* 3.372; see [[phene]]). The Iliad's comparison of a plunge is the diver, ἀρνευτήρ, the man who falls headfirst from a height to his death (12.385, 16.742; see [[like-a-diver]]); and Iris goes down to Thetis in the depths “like a lead weight” (24.80; see [[the-lead-weight]]).

## Outside Homer

[[Aristotle]] counts the αἴθυια among the winged creatures that take their food and spend their time in the water but breathe air and breed out of it, with the κολυμβίς, another bird of the water (*History of Animals* 1.1). It lays two or three eggs in the rocks by the sea at the beginning of spring, where the gull lays in summer (*History of Animals* 5.9; see [[gull]]), and it is among the birds that live by the sea (*History of Animals* 8.3).
