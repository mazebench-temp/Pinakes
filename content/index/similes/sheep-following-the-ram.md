## The simile

{{quote:e1e0504a-85b5-5f36-b9e3-0dbb87b81c11}}

αὐτὰρ ἔπειτα / λαοὶ ἕπονθʼ, ὡς εἴ τε μετὰ κτίλον ἕσπετο μῆλα / πιόμενʼ ἐκ βοτάνης· γάνυται δʼ ἄρα τε φρένα ποιμήν: “and then the army followed, as sheep follow the ram from the pasture to drink, and the shepherd is glad at heart” (13.491–493). κτίλος, “tame”, is the ram that leads the flock, the bellwether, as in Priam's likeness of Odysseus (3.196; see [[ram]] and [[ram-among-the-sheep]]). μῆλα are the flock (see [[sheep]]), and βοτάνη is the pasture they come from. The shepherd's gladness is a verse-end of the similes: “and the shepherd is glad at heart” when every star shows in a clear night sky (8.559; see [[stars-around-the-moon]]).

The tenor: ὣς Αἰνείᾳ θυμὸς ἐνὶ στήθεσσι γεγήθει / ὡς ἴδε λαῶν ἔθνος ἐπισπόμενον ἑοῖ αὐτῷ, “so the heart of Aeneas rejoiced in his breast when he saw the crowd of the army following him” (13.494–495). The simile holds two figures, and Aeneas is both. He is the ram the flock follows, and his joy is the shepherd's. The point of likeness is the readiness of the following, and the leader's pleasure in it. The verb of following frames the passage, ἕπονθʼ, ἕσπετο, ἐπισπόμενον.

The joy has a cause in the story. A few verses before, Deiphobus found Aeneas standing at the back of the throng, “for he was always nursing wrath against brilliant Priam, because, brave as he was among men, Priam did not honor him at all” (13.459–461; see the translation's note on 13.459–461 and [[aeneas]]). Priam withholds honor from Aeneas; the army follows him.

## In context

Aeneas has come to defend the body of Alcathous, who reared him, and Idomeneus waits for him like a boar and calls his companions (13.455–488; see [[the-bristling-boar]]). Aeneas calls on his own companions, “looking to Deiphobus and Paris and brilliant Agenor, who were leaders of the Trojans with him” (13.489–491), and the army follows. The two sides clash around Alcathous with long spears, and Aeneas and Idomeneus, “the equals of Ares”, are eager to cut each other's flesh (13.496–501; see [[alcathous]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Priam likens Odysseus, walking among the seated ranks, to a thick-fleeced ram that goes through a great flock of white sheep (3.196–198; see [[ram-among-the-sheep]]). In the Odyssey the Cyclops speaks to his best ram, which has always been the first to go to the pasture and the first to reach the river streams, and is now the last to leave the cave, because Odysseus clings beneath it (*Odyssey* 9.447–452). Elsewhere in the Iliad's similes sheep are prey, or the noisy flock of the Trojan army (4.433–438; see [[sheep-in-the-fold]]). The figure of the leader as shepherd is in the title “shepherd of the people” (see [[shepherd-of-the-people]]).
