## The simile

Ἀργεῖοι δὲ μέγʼ ἴαχον ὡς ὅτε κῦμα / ἀκτῇ ἐφʼ ὑψηλῇ, ὅτε κινήσῃ Νότος ἐλθών, / προβλῆτι σκοπέλῳ: “and the Argives shouted aloud, like a wave against a high shore, when the South Wind comes and stirs it, against a jutting crag”. ἴαχον is “shouted”. ἀκτή is a shore or headland, here a high one (ὑψηλῇ, “high”). σκόπελος is a crag, and προβλής “jutting forward”. The waves never leave it alone, “from winds of every kind, whenever they rise from this side or that” (παντοίων ἀνέμων, ὅτʼ ἂν ἔνθʼ ἢ ἔνθα γένωνται).

The tenor is the shout; the vehicle, a wave crashing on a promontory. The point of likeness is the noise. The last lines follow the picture beyond the comparison, as Homer's similes often do: the crag is struck by waves from every wind, on one side or the other.

## In context

{{quote:de247400-f545-5cc1-a3a2-7b0f2978d266}}

Agamemnon has taken Nestor's advice, owned that he began the quarrel with Achilles, and sent the army to its meal before battle. He ends with a threat: any man he sees hanging back by the ships will have “no hope then of escaping the dogs and the birds” (2.370–393). The shout answers him in the half-line that answered Odysseus: ὣς ἔφατʼ, Ἀργεῖοι δὲ μέγʼ ἴαχον (“so he spoke, and the Argives shouted aloud”, 2.333 and 2.394).

The simile closes the series of sea similes that framed the assembly. First the waves rose on the open sea under the East and South Winds, as the assembly broke up in flight ([[waves-of-the-icarian-sea]]); then a wave roared on a long beach as the men came back ([[surf-on-the-beach]]); now a wave strikes a headland. The South Wind returns ([[notus]]), in the clause used of the West Wind in the field of grain, ὡς δʼ ὅτε κινήσῃ Ζέφυρος … ἐλθών (“as when the West Wind comes and stirs”, 2.147; [[wind-in-the-wheat]]). The men scatter among the ships again, but this time to make fires and take their meal (2.398–399).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In Book 15 the Danaans hold against Hector like a great sheer rock by the gray sea, which withstands the swift paths of the shrill winds and the swelling waves that break against it (Iliad 15.618–622). There the rock is the army; here the army is the wave.
