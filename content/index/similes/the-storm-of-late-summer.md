## The simile

{{quote:2ddeb2ab-2665-5e4b-8dd0-f461d74d696f}}

ὡς δʼ ὑπὸ λαίλαπι πᾶσα κελαινὴ βέβριθε χθὼν / ἤματʼ ὀπωρινῷ, ὅτε λαβρότατον χέει ὕδωρ / Ζεύς: “And as under a storm the whole black earth is weighed down / on a day of late summer, when he pours down the most violent rain, / Zeus” (16.384–386). λαῖλαψ is a squall or storm (see [[the-cloud-from-olympus]]). βέβριθε, “is weighed down”, is from βρίθω, “be heavy”, whose compound tells the rain of Zeus that “falls heavy” in two earlier similes (ἐπιβρίσῃ Διὸς ὄμβρος, 5.91, 12.286; see [[river-in-flood]] and [[the-great-snowfall]]). ὀπωρινός is “of ὀπώρη”, the season when the fruit ripens at the end of summer, as in “the star of late summer” (5.5; see [[the-autumn-star]]). Walter Leaf reports a variant χειμερίῳ, “of winter”, and keeps ὀπωρινῷ, since in Greece the heavy rains come in autumn and the winter is comparatively fine (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.385). λάβρος is “furious, violent”. The subject, Zeus, is held back to the head of the next verse.

The simile then gives the cause: ὅτε δή ῥʼ ἄνδρεσσι κοτεσσάμενος χαλεπήνῃ, / οἳ βίῃ εἰν ἀγορῇ σκολιὰς κρίνωσι θέμιστας, / ἐκ δὲ δίκην ἐλάσωσι θεῶν ὄπιν οὐκ ἀλέγοντες, “when in resentment he grows angry with men / who by force in the assembly judge crooked ordinances / and drive out justice, caring nothing for the regard of the gods” (16.386–388). κοτεσσάμενος is from κότος, a lasting grudge (see [[kotos]]). The ἀγορή is the place of assembly, where judgment is given (see [[assembly]]); the θέμιστες are the ordinances that kings and judges declare (see [[themis]]); σκολιός, “crooked”, occurs only here in Homer. δίκη, “justice”, is named here for the first time in the Iliad (see the translation's note on 16.384–392). ὄπις is the gods' watchful notice of what men do, and the vengeance that follows it: the suitors of the Odyssey have “no regard” (ὄπις) in their minds, and Heracles, who killed his guest Iphitus in his own house, “had no respect for the regard of the gods” (*Odyssey* 14.82, 21.26–29).

The storm falls on the land of those men: τῶν δέ τε πάντες μὲν ποταμοὶ πλήθουσι ῥέοντες, “and all their rivers run in flood” (16.389); torrents (χαράδραι; at 4.454 the word is the ravine that a torrent cuts) carve away many hillsides, κλιτῦς, and “groan loudly as they run into the purple sea / headlong from the mountains” (ἐπικάρ, “headlong”, only here), “and the works of men are diminished” (16.390–392). ἔργα here are the tilled fields and orchards, as when the river in flood of *Iliad* 5 throws down “many fair works of young men” (5.92). The end of the vehicle is a sound and a ruin: the rivers roar down to the sea, and what men have made dwindles.

The tenor takes up the sound: ὣς ἵπποι Τρῳαὶ μεγάλα στενάχοντο θέουσαι, “so the Trojan mares groaned loudly as they ran” (16.393). The verse repeats the torrents' words, μεγάλα στενάχουσι ῥέουσαι, “they groan loudly as they run” (16.391), and only the running changes, from water flowing to mares galloping. Τρῳαί is the feminine adjective: these are mares (see the translation's note on 16.393 and [[horses]]). The point of likeness is the roar and the headlong rush of a whole land's waters running down to the sea. Nothing in the tenor answers the unjust judges of the vehicle; the moral is the poet's account of the storm, and the likeness lies in its water. Walter Leaf thought the simile fine and fitting, the Trojan horses galloping in confusion like torrents in a rain so excessive that it can only be a divine judgment, but took the couplet of the judges (16.387–388) for a later addition: it spoils the balance of the simile, κρίνειν θέμιστας is not Homeric, ὄπις is otherwise a word of the Odyssey, and the couplet is made up from the *Works and Days*: “The excellent opportunity for a sententious interpolation was evidently too much for a poet of the Hesiodean school” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.384 and 16.387–388).

## In context

Patroclus has driven the Trojans from the ships. Their rout goes out like a cloud from Olympus, they fill all the roads, and a storm wind of dust rises under the clouds as their horses strain back toward the city; many chariots break at the end of the pole in the ditch, which Patroclus' immortal horses leap, but Hector's swift horses carry him away (16.364–383; see [[the-cloud-from-olympus]]). The storm simile closes the rout. After it Patroclus cuts off the foremost battalions and hems them back toward the ships, and will not let them reach the city: between the ships, the river and the high wall he rushes among them and kills them, “and exacted the penalty for many” (πολέων δʼ ἀπετίνυτο ποινήν, 16.394–398; see [[poine]]). The killing that follows is told in the words of retribution, as the storm was.

δίκη is named once more in the book, when Glaucus reproaches Hector for abandoning Sarpedon, the Lycian king “who guarded Lycia with his judgments and his strength” (δίκῃσί τε καὶ σθένεϊ ᾧ, 16.541–542).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Rivers in flood are the Iliad's image of a force that cannot be stopped: the roar of two winter torrents meeting in a ravine (4.452–456; see [[torrents-meeting]]), Diomedes like a river in winter flood that scatters the dikes (5.87–92), Ajax like a torrent swollen by the rain of Zeus that carries down dry oaks and pines (11.492–497). In no other simile is the rain sent in anger, and against men. The Iliad's other picture of justice done in the assembly is on the shield of Achilles, where two men dispute over the blood-price of a man killed, the elders give judgment in turn, and two talents of gold lie in the middle for the one who speaks the straightest judgment (δίκην ἰθύντατα, 18.497–508). In the Odyssey the gods “do not love cruel deeds, but honor justice and the righteous works of men” (*Odyssey* 14.83–84), and go about the cities in the likeness of strangers, “watching over the outrage and the good order of men” (*Odyssey* 17.485–487). The half-verse of the rivers, ποταμοὶ πλήθουσι ῥέοντες, “the rivers run in flood”, returns in the Odyssey for the snow that the East Wind melts on the high mountains, as Penelope's cheeks melt in tears (*Odyssey* 19.205–208).

## Outside Homer

The thought and much of the wording are in Hesiod. In the *Works and Days* Justice is dragged away wherever bribe-eating men “judge ordinances with crooked judgments” (σκολιῇς δὲ δίκῃς κρίνωσι θέμιστας, 221), and follows weeping through the city, bringing evil to those “who drive her out” (οἵ τε μιν ἐξελάσωσι, 224). On the city of men who care for outrage and cruel deeds Zeus brings from the sky famine and plague together; the people waste away, women do not bear, and households dwindle (238–245). The immortals are near and take note of those who grind one another down with crooked judgments, “caring nothing for the regard of the gods” (θεῶν ὄπιν οὐκ ἀλέγοντες, 250–251), the same words as the Iliad's (16.388).
