## The simile

ἀλλʼ ὅτε δὴ ὄπα τε μεγάλην ἐκ στήθεος εἵη / καὶ ἔπεα νιφάδεσσιν ἐοικότα χειμερίῃσιν, / οὐκ ἂν ἔπειτʼ Ὀδυσῆΐ γʼ ἐρίσσειε βροτὸς ἄλλος: “but when he let the great voice go from his chest, and words like the snowflakes of winter, then no other mortal could rival Odysseus”. ὄψ is the voice, and μεγάλη “great, loud”. ἔπεα are words ([[epos]]), νιφάδες snowflakes, and χειμέριαι “of winter”. εἵη is from ἵημι, “let go, send out”, and governs both the voice and the words. The sentence runs from a posture to a voice to a verdict.

The comparison is short, and its point is left to the hearer. In Homer's other snow similes the flakes fall thick and fast and cover everything (see below), so the likeness is usually taken to be abundance and force: the words come on without pause, one after another, until nothing else is left in view. The next line gives only the result: no one could rival him.

## In context

{{quote:1400c24c-8075-5e4a-b7ee-66655be59e11}}

Antenor has been asked nothing. Helen has just named Odysseus for Priam, and Antenor confirms her words from his own memory. Menelaus and Odysseus once came to Troy as envoys about Helen, and he entertained them in his house (3.203–208; see [[the-embassy-to-troy]]). He describes the two men as speakers. Menelaus “spoke fluently, few words but very clear, since he was not a man of many words nor one who missed the point” (3.213–215). Odysseus rose, stood looking at the ground, held the scepter still, and seemed a surly fellow and a fool (3.216–220; see [[like-a-man-who-knows-nothing]]). Then came the voice.

The last line turns back to looks: “then we did not so much marvel at Odysseus' looks” (οὐ τότε γʼ ὧδʼ Ὀδυσῆος ἀγασσάμεθʼ εἶδος ἰδόντες, 3.224). At the start of the book Hector told Paris that the Achaeans laugh at a champion chosen for his handsome face (3.43–45; see [[eidos]]). Odysseus is the reverse case: nothing to look at, and a voice that overpowers.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The snow similes of the battle books are all of multitude. The stones thrown from the Achaean wall fall like snowflakes that a wind drives thick onto the earth (12.154–158). On a winter's day, when Zeus snows, the flakes cover the mountain peaks and the headlands, the fields and the harbors and the shores, and only the sea's wave keeps it off; so thick fly the stones from both sides (12.278–289). When the army arms for Achilles' return, helmets, shields, corselets and spears pour from the ships as thick as the snowflakes of Zeus under the North Wind (19.357–361). Words, in Book 3, take the place of stones and weapons.

Nestor, the third great speaker of the Achaeans, is described in Book 1 as “the clear-voiced orator of the Pylians, from whose tongue the voice flowed sweeter than honey” (1.248–249; see [[sweeter-than-honey]]).

## Outside Homer

Teachers of rhetoric found the three styles of oratory in Homer's speakers: the plain style in Menelaus, the middle style in Nestor, and the grand style in Odysseus, whose speech Quintilian calls equal to winter snows in abundance and force (Quintilian, *Institutio Oratoria* 12.10.64; Aulus Gellius, *Attic Nights* 6.14.7; see [[three-styles-of-speech]]).
