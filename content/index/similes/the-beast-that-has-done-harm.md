## The simile

{{quote:31288f9f-4d1e-5f72-b8c7-16c3b541f417}}

ἀλλʼ ὅ γʼ ἄρʼ ἔτρεσε θηρὶ κακὸν ῥέξαντι ἐοικώς, / ὅς τε κύνα κτείνας ἢ βουκόλον ἀμφὶ βόεσσι / φεύγει πρίν περ ὅμιλον ἀολλισθήμεναι ἀνδρῶν: “but fled in fear, like a wild beast that has done some harm, that has killed a dog or a herdsman beside his cattle, and flees before a throng of men can gather” (15.586–588). θήρ is a wild beast, and the poem does not say which; κακὸν ῥέξαντι is “that has done harm”, a wrong that men will avenge if they can catch it. The βουκόλος is a cowherd, and ἀμφὶ βόεσσι “around the cattle”, defending them. ἀολλίζω is to bring together, and the throng of men is the farm roused to the hunt.

The tenor: ὣς τρέσε Νεστορίδης, ἐπὶ δὲ Τρῶές τε καὶ Ἕκτωρ / ἠχῇ θεσπεσίῃ βέλεα στονόεντα χέοντο, “so the son of Nestor fled in fear, and the Trojans and Hector with a wondrous din poured their groaning missiles on him” (15.589–590). The beast's prudence is Antilochus': he “did not stand his ground, swift warrior though he was” (15.585), and he runs before the Trojans can close on him. The narrator does not blame him. The beast is bold enough to kill and wise enough not to wait.

The simile follows hard on another. Three verses before, Antilochus leapt on the fallen Melanippus like a dog on a wounded fawn (15.579–581; see [[the-dog-and-the-fawn]]); now he is the beast that has killed a dog. The harm is named too: the beast has killed “a herdsman beside his cattle”, and Melanippus, when the narrator first named him, was a herdsman: “For a time he grazed shambling cattle in Percote, while the enemy were still far away” (15.547–548; see [[percote]]). The likeness puts the dead man's past into the killer's flight.

## In context

Antilochus has struck Melanippus, son of Hicetaon, in the chest and leapt on him to strip his armor, but Hector comes running through the combat to meet him (15.573–584; see [[antilochus]], [[melanippus-son-of-hicetaon]] and [[hector]]). Antilochus runs, and under the Trojans' missiles “he turned and stood, when he reached the crowd of his companions” (15.591). The verse is the one that ends Ajax's slow retreat in *Iliad* 11 (11.595), where Ajax too falls back “like a wild beast” and then like a lion driven from the farmyard (11.544–557; see [[like-a-wild-beast]] and [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]). The Trojans then rush on the ships like lions that eat raw flesh (15.592; see [[lions-and-boars]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the Iliad's other similes the men whom a beast of prey has wronged kill it, or drive it off, or stand off and shout. Two lions reared in the mountains carry off cattle and sheep and raid men's farmsteads until they are killed (5.554–558; see [[two-lions]]); a lion is kept from the fattest of the cattle all night by dogs and country men and goes off at dawn with grieving heart (11.548–555); a mountain lion seizes the best cow of a herd while the dogs and herdsmen shout from a distance and dare not come near (17.61–67). This beast does not wait to learn which it will be. It does its harm and is gone before the throng can gather.
