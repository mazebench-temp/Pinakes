## The simile

{{quote:824228e3-c914-5fc0-abfe-4fb950636b04}}

ὁ δʼ ἐν κονίῃσι χαμαὶ πέσεν αἴγειρος ὣς / ἥ ῥά τʼ ἐν εἱαμενῇ ἕλεος μεγάλοιο πεφύκει: “and he fell to the ground in the dust like a poplar that has grown in the low wet ground of a great marsh”. αἴγειρος is the black poplar, a tall tree of wet ground. εἱαμενή is a water meadow and ἕλος a marsh. λείη, “smooth”, is the straight trunk, bare of branches until the top (ὄζοι, “branches”, ἐπʼ ἀκροτάτῃ, “at the very top”). An ἁρματοπηγὸς ἀνήρ is “a chariot-building man” (ἅρμα, “chariot”, and πήγνυμι, “fix, build”), a word found only here in Homer. He cuts the tree with iron, which in Homer is mostly the metal of tools ([[iron]]), to make an ἴτυς, the rim of a wheel, which is made by bending (κάμψῃ) green wood. ἀζομένη is “drying, withering”.

The tenor: τοῖον ἄρʼ Ἀνθεμίδην Σιμοείσιον ἐξενάριξεν / Αἴας διογενής, “so was Simoeisius, son of Anthemion, stripped of his armor by Ajax, sprung from Zeus” (4.488–489). The point of likeness is the fall of something young, tall and straight. The details carry more than the fall. The tree grew (πεφύκει, πεφύασι) as the young man grew. His father's name, Anthemion, is from ἄνθεμον, “flower”. The smooth trunk suits a young body. The tree is cut down to be made into part of a chariot, a thing of war, and it is left lying by a river. ἐξενάριξεν, “stripped of his armor”, ends the comparison with the victor taking the dead man's arms, as the woodman has taken the tree.

## In context

{{quote:6912aca5-7766-5232-a212-c4e195edfdf6}}

Simoeisius is the second Trojan to die in the poem, and the first whose life is told before his death (see [[simoeisius]]). His mother bore him beside the banks of the Simoeis, coming down from Ida where she had gone with her parents to watch over the flocks, so they named him for the river (see [[simoeis]] and [[ida]]). “But he did not repay his dear parents for his rearing, and short was his life” (4.477–478). θρέπτρα is what a child gives back to the parents who raised him, and he will never give it (see [[threptra]]).

The simile tells the same life again. Born beside a river, he grows tall and smooth; he is cut down; he lies by a river. “Beside the banks of the Simoeis” (παρʼ ὄχθῃσιν Σιμόεντος, 4.475) is answered by “by the banks of the river” (ποταμοῖο παρʼ ὄχθας, 4.487). Between the two rivers the life is told twice in fifteen lines, once as a story and once as a tree. See [[the-first-battle]] and [[death-in-battle]].

The fighting goes on at once over his body. Antiphus, son of Priam, throws at Ajax and misses, and hits Leucus, Odysseus' companion, who is dragging the corpse away (4.489–493; see [[antiphus-son-of-priam]] and [[leucus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Many warriors in the Iliad fall like trees. Imbrius falls like an ash cut down on a mountain top (13.178–181). Asius and Sarpedon fall in the same lines, “as an oak falls, or a white poplar, / or a tall pine, which on the mountains builders / cut down with newly sharpened axes to be ship-timber” (13.389–391, 16.482–484). Hector goes down under Ajax's stone like an oak struck by the lightning of Zeus (14.414–417). Euphorbus falls like a young olive tree that a man has raised in a lonely, well-watered place, uprooted by a sudden gale (17.53–60). The words of Simoeisius' short life return for Hippothous, another young man killed by Ajax: he “did not repay his dear parents for his rearing, and short was his life, brought down by the spear of great-hearted Ajax” (17.301–303). And the young wood cut for a chariot returns in Book 21: Lycaon, a son of Priam, was cutting young shoots of a wild fig tree to make the rails of a chariot in his father's orchard when Achilles came on him by night and carried him off (21.34–38).
