## The simile

{{quote:95bd192d-34c9-5983-ab25-241dc0934cb6}}

οἴμησεν δὲ ἀλεὶς ὥς τʼ αἰετὸς ὑψιπετήεις, / ὅς τʼ εἶσιν πεδίον δὲ διὰ νεφέων ἐρεβεννῶν / ἁρπάξων ἢ ἄρνʼ ἀμαλὴν ἤ πτῶκα λαγωόν: “and crouching, he swooped like an eagle flying high / that goes down to the plain through the murky clouds / to snatch a tender lamb or a cowering hare” (22.308–310). ἀλείς, “crouching”, is a man gathering himself for a spring or to meet a charge: Agenor, deciding to stand, “crouched and waited for Achilles” (ἀλεὶς μένεν, 21.571). ὑψιπετήεις, “flying high”, is the longer form of ὑψιπέτης, the epithet of the eagles of omen (12.201, 13.822; see [[high-flying]] and [[eagle]]). πεδίον δέ, “down to the plain”, is πεδίονδε with the suffix of direction printed apart (see the translation's note on 22.309, 22.362, 22.456). ἐρεβεννός, “murky”, is the darkness of Erebus, the word for the night that covers the eyes of the dying (5.659, 13.580), and in this book of the night that covers Andromache's eyes when she sees Hector dragged (22.466; see [[murky]]).

ἀμαλός, “tender”, occurs only here in the Iliad; in the Odyssey it is said once more, of the tender puppies over which a bitch stands growling (*Odyssey* 20.14). πτῶκα λαγωόν, “a cowering hare”: πτώξ, “the cowerer”, is the hare's other name, and only here does Homer join the two (see [[hare]]). The lamb and the hare are the weakest creatures of the similes, the prey of wolves and dogs and birds (see [[lambs]]).

The tenor: ὣς Ἕκτωρ οἴμησε τινάσσων φάσγανον ὀξύ, “so Hector swooped, brandishing his sharp sword” (22.311). The verb frames the simile, οἴμησεν at its start and οἴμησε in the tenor. It was Achilles' verb in the first simile of the chase, when he swooped like a falcon after the timid dove (22.140; see [[the-falcon-and-the-dove]]); now the hunted man swoops in his turn. The point of likeness is the sudden fall of a hunter from on high on a creature that cannot resist. The likeness belongs to Hector's courage, not to the event. His quarry is no lamb but Achilles, armored by Hephaestus and holding the spear that Athena has given back to him, and Hector has only a sword (22.276–277, 22.312–316).

## In context

Hector's spear has glanced off Achilles' shield, and when he calls to Deiphobus for another, “he was nowhere near him” (22.289–295; see [[deiphobus]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). He understands that the gods have called him to his death, and asks only this: “Let me not die without a struggle and without glory, / but having done some great thing, even for men to come to hear of” (22.304–305; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). He draws the sharp sword “that hung beneath his flank, great and heavy” (22.306–307; see [[sword]]), and swoops. Achilles rushes to meet him behind his shield, the plumes of gold waving about his helmet, and the point of his spear gleams like the evening star as he looks for the place where the flesh will give way (22.312–321; see [[the-evening-star]] and [[the-death-of-hector]]). It is the last simile of Hector's life.

## Elsewhere in Homer

Hector has been the eagle before. In *Iliad* 15 he made straight for a dark-prowed ship as a tawny eagle swoops on a flock of geese or cranes or long-necked swans feeding beside a river, and Zeus pushed him on from behind with his very great hand (15.688–695; see [[the-eagle-and-the-birds]]). In Book 22 Zeus has weighed his fate, and Apollo has left him (22.209–213; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). The eagle is the hunter of the similes. Menelaus looks about him like the eagle that sees even from on high the hare crouching under a leafy bush, and swoops on it (17.674–678; see [[the-eagle-and-the-hare]]), and Achilles springs away from the river “with the swoop of an eagle, the black hunter” (21.252; see [[like-an-eagle]]). The eagle that Zeus sends as an omen in Book 8 carries a fawn in its talons and drops it by his altar (8.247–250).

The Odyssey gives the first verse, whole, to Odysseus at the end of the poem. When the kinsmen of the dead suitors turn and flee toward the city, he shouts terribly, οἴμησεν δὲ ἀλεὶς ὥς τʼ αἰετὸς ὑψιπετήεις (*Odyssey* 24.537–538). That swoop is cut short too: Zeus throws his smoking thunderbolt down before Athena, and she tells Odysseus to hold back and end the strife of war (*Odyssey* 24.539–544).

## Outside Homer

[[virgil|Virgil]] gives the swoop to Turnus. Lycus, far swifter of foot than his enemies, runs to the Trojan wall and reaches up for his comrades' hands; Turnus, following him with his spear, seizes him as he hangs there and tears him down with a great piece of the wall, as the armor-bearer of Jupiter, making for the heights, carries off a hare or a white-bodied swan in its hooked feet, or the wolf of Mars snatches from the folds a lamb whose mother seeks it with much bleating (*Aeneid* 9.556–566). Virgil keeps the eagle and the hare, and gives the lamb to the wolf (see [[wolves]]).
