## The simile

{{quote:0103caf6-c062-562a-b146-9bb65af93207}}

The narrator first says what the race is not: οὐχ ἱερήϊον οὐδὲ βοείην / ἀρνύσθην, ἅ τε ποσσὶν ἀέθλια γίγνεται ἀνδρῶν, “for it was no beast for sacrifice or ox-hide / they strove to win, such as are prizes for the feet of men” (22.159–160). ἱερήϊον, “a beast for sacrifice”, occurs only here in the Iliad. ἀρνύσθην, “they strove to win”, is a dual of ἄρνυμαι, “win, carry off”, the verb of Agamemnon's racehorses, “which have won prizes with their feet” (οἳ ἀέθλια ποσσὶν ἄροντο, 9.124). ποσσὶν ἀέθλια are the prizes of a footrace, and an animal or a hide is the ordinary stake. Then the stake: ἀλλὰ περὶ ψυχῆς θέον Ἕκτορος ἱπποδάμοιο, “but they ran for the life of Hector, tamer of horses” (22.161). ψυχή is the life that will fly from Hector's limbs to Hades (22.362; see [[psyche]]), and the line that names it ends on his epithet of horses, ἱππόδαμος (see [[tamer-of-horses]]), just before a simile of horses.

{{quote:4f39f886-6c77-5eb5-a754-1de6d09cb6ae}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἀεθλοφόροι περὶ τέρματα μώνυχες ἵπποι / ῥίμφα μάλα τρωχῶσι· τὸ δὲ μέγα κεῖται ἄεθλον / ἢ τρίπος ἠὲ γυνὴ ἀνδρὸς κατατεθνηῶτος: “And as when prize-winning single-hoofed horses round the turning posts / run very swiftly, and a great prize is set out, / a tripod or a woman, in honor of a man who has died” (22.162–164). ἀεθλοφόροι, “prize-winning”, was the epithet of the horse to which Achilles was likened at the start of the book (22.22; see [[like-a-racehorse]] and [[prize-winning]]), and μώνυχες, “single-hoofed”, is the horses' own (see [[single-hoofed]]). τέρματα are the turning posts round which the chariots wheel in a race: in *Iliad* 23 Nestor tells his son that he knows well how to wheel around them, and Achilles marks them far off on the level plain (23.309, 23.358–359). τρωχῶσι, “run”, is said once more in Homer, of Nausicaa's mules (*Odyssey* 6.318). The prize is set out ἀνδρὸς κατατεθνηῶτος, “in honor of a man who has died”: these are funeral games, such as the Epeians held for their king Amarynceus at Buprasium, where Nestor won every contest but the chariot race, and the Cadmeians for Oedipus at Thebes, where Mecisteus, the father of Euryalus, beat them all (23.630–642, 23.677–680; see [[contests]] and [[amarynceus]]).

The tenor: ὣς τὼ τρὶς Πριάμοιο πόλιν πέρι δινηθήτην / καρπαλίμοισι πόδεσσι, “so the two whirled three times around the city of Priam / on swift feet” (22.165–166). δινέω is to whirl or turn round. The city is the turning post, and the two men go round it as the chariots go round the mark. The likeness is the race, its speed and the prize that waits at the end, and the simile turns on the difference the narrator has just stated. At the games the prize goes to the winner and the dead man is honored; in this race the prize is one runner's life, and the dead man will be the one who loses it.

θεοὶ δʼ ἐς πάντες ὁρῶντο, “and all the gods were watching” (22.166). The gods are the spectators of the race, as the Argives, seated in the place of contest, will watch the horses in *Iliad* 23 (23.448–449), and Zeus at once asks them whether to save Hector or let him fall (22.167–176; see [[watching-the-war]] and [[zeus]]).

## In context

The pursuit has begun with the falcon and the dove (22.136–144; see [[the-falcon-and-the-dove]]). The two run past the lookout and the fig tree, along the wagon road under the wall, to the two springs of the Scamander and the stone troughs where the Trojan women used to wash their clothes “in former days, in time of peace, before the sons of the Achaeans came” (22.145–156; see [[the-springs-of-the-scamander]]). “There they ran past, one fleeing, the other pursuing behind; / in front a brave man fled, but a far better one pursued him” (22.157–158). They go round three times, and when they come to the springs for the fourth time Zeus lifts his golden scales, and Hector's day sinks (22.208–213; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]], [[the-scales-of-zeus]] and [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The prizes of the simile are set out in the next book. When the mound of Patroclus has been heaped, Achilles keeps the army there and brings out prizes from the ships, cauldrons and tripods, horses, mules and oxen, women and gray iron (23.257–261), and for the first in the chariot race he sets out a woman skilled in fine work and a tripod with handles that holds twenty-two measures (23.262–264): the tripod and the woman of the simile, at a dead man's games. The turning post of that race is a dry stump with a white stone on either side, and Nestor cannot say whether it is the marker of some man who died long ago or a turning post set up by men of an earlier time (23.326–333; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). In the Odyssey the shade of Agamemnon tells Achilles of the games held at his own burial, where Thetis set out in the middle of the place of contest the beautiful prizes she had asked of the gods (*Odyssey* 24.85–92). Racehorses are among the richest of gifts and spoils: twelve “strong prize-winners” stand in the list of Agamemnon's gifts to Achilles (9.123–124), and Nestor's father had four prize-winning horses that went with their chariot to race for a tripod in Elis and were kept by Augeas (11.698–702).

## Outside Homer

[[virgil|Virgil]] all but translates the narrator's words when Aeneas chases Turnus at the end of the *Aeneid*. Five circles they run, and as many back, this way and that, “for it is no light prize or prize of sport that they seek; they contend for the life and blood of Turnus” (*neque enim levia aut ludicra petuntur / praemia, sed Turni de vita et sanguine certant*, *Aeneid* 12.763–765; see [[virgil-the-death-of-turnus]]).
