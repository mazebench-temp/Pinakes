## The simile

{{quote:d0f6a8ca-6ce0-54df-a4b2-1a6cd9521ef1}}

ὡς δʼ ὁπότʼ ἐν ξυλόχῳ ἔλαφος κρατεροῖο λέοντος / νεβροὺς κοιμήσασα νεηγενέας γαλαθηνούς: “As when in the thicket of a strong lion a hind / has lulled her newborn suckling fawns to sleep” (*Odyssey* 4.335–336). ξύλοχος is the thicket, the lair in deep wood, as in the Iliad's similes of the cattle, the boar and the leopard (5.162, 11.415, 21.573); W. W. Merry takes it, like δρύοχος, to mean “having wood” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.335). νεηγενέας, “newborn”, and γαλαθηνούς, “suckling”, are found only here and in the repetition in *Odyssey* 17. The hind goes to graze on the mountain slopes and in the grassy valleys, and the lion comes back to his own lair, ἑὴν … εὐνήν, and kills both (*Odyssey* 4.337–339; see [[deer]] and [[lion]]). The aorist of the simile, ἀεικέα πότμον ἐφῆκεν, “brings a shameful doom”, becomes a future in the tenor, ἐφήσει, “will bring” (*Odyssey* 4.339–340): the simile is a prophecy. The same words, in the aorist of a past deed, tell what Tydeus did to the fifty Cadmeians who lay in ambush for him (*Iliad* 4.391–398).

The frame gives the simile its point. Menelaus begins, “Ah, truly it was in the bed of a fierce-hearted man / that they wished to lie, weaklings as they are” (*Odyssey* 4.333–334): the bed, ἐν εὐνῇ … εὐνηθῆναι, is Odysseus' marriage bed, which the suitors would take by marrying his wife, and it becomes the εὐνή of the lion, the lair to which he will come home. Merry draws out the parts of the likeness: the thicket is the house of Odysseus, the fawns, and perhaps the presumptuous hind, are the suitors, the lion is Odysseus, and the lion's absence and return carry the likeness further (on 4.335).

**Both of them.** Whom does ἀμφοτέροισι, “on both of them”, mean (*Odyssey* 4.339)? The scholia report that Aristophanes took it of the hind and her young (Merry's critical note). Merry prefers a remark of Eustathius, that the poet supposes a hind with twins so as not to liken so many suitors to one fawn: the slaughter takes place while the mother is away, and so “both” are the two fawns (on 4.339; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 4.339). He cites [[Aristotle]], who gives one or at most two fawns at a birth. Aristotle's account says more than that: the hind bears one as a rule, and only a few have been seen with two, and she gives birth beside the roads for fear of wild beasts (*History of Animals* 6.29). Homer's hind has put her young in the one place a beast of prey will surely come back to.

## In context

At dawn Menelaus asks Telemachus what business has brought him to Lacedaemon, and Telemachus tells him: his house is being eaten up, and the suitors are always slaughtering his flocks and his cattle (*Odyssey* 4.312–321; see [[the-suitors]] and [[telemachus]]). Menelaus is deeply troubled, and the simile is his first answer. He goes on to wish that Odysseus might come among the suitors as he was when he threw Philomeleides in Lesbos, “they would all be swift-fated, and bitter would their wedding be” (*Odyssey* 4.341–346; see [[odysseus-wrestles-philomeleides]] and [[swift-fated]]), and only then begins the tale of Proteus, in which he will give the news that Odysseus is alive on Calypso's island (*Odyssey* 4.351–586, 4.555–560; see [[menelaus]] and [[proteus]]).

When Telemachus comes home, he gives his mother Menelaus' words, the simile, the wish and the promise to tell what the Old Man of the Sea said, in the same verses (*Odyssey* 17.124–141 = 4.333–350). By then the lion has come home: Odysseus is back in Ithaca, disguised as a beggar.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Iliad has the scene the other way about. Agamemnon kills two sons of Priam as a lion comes into the lair of a swift hind and crushes her helpless young, while the mother, close by, can only flee (*Iliad* 11.113–121; see [[the-lion-and-the-fawns]]). There the lion goes into the hind's lair; here the hind has put her young in the lion's. The deer of the Iliad is the coward's beast (1.225, 4.243, 13.102–104; see [[like-fawns]]).

Odysseus is a lion in two more similes of the Odyssey. He comes out of the bushes toward Nausicaa and her maids like a mountain lion driven by hunger to go after cattle or sheep or wild deer (*Odyssey* 6.130–134), and when the suitors lie dead Eurycleia finds him spattered with blood and gore like a lion that has eaten an ox (*Odyssey* 22.401–406). In this book Penelope, in fear for her son, is a lion herself (*Odyssey* 4.791–793; see [[the-lion-encircled]]).
