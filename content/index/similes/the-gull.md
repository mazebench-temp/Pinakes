## The simile

{{quote:36339b0f-f3e0-5a83-bf3a-afc52ac8ff19}}

σεύατʼ ἔπειτʼ ἐπὶ κῦμα λάρῳ ὄρνιθι ἐοικώς, / ὅς τε κατὰ δεινοὺς κόλπους ἁλὸς ἀτρυγέτοιο / ἰχθῦς ἀγρώσσων πυκινὰ πτερὰ δεύεται ἅλμῃ: “then he hurried over the wave like a bird, a gull, / which through the dread bosom of the barren sea, / hunting fish, wets its thick plumage in the brine” (*Odyssey* 5.51–53). λάρος is named only here in Homer (see [[gull]]). The general word comes first and the kind after it, ὄρνιθι, “a bird”, then λάρῳ, as the gods on the oak of Zeus sit ὄρνισιν ἐοικότες αἰγυπιοῖσι, “like birds, like vultures” (*Iliad* 7.59; see [[like-vultures]] and [[birds]]). The κόλποι are the bosom of the sea: W. W. Merry takes the word properly of water lying enclosed between headlands, though it is used of the whole sea, as in θαλάσσης εὐρέα κόλπον, “the broad bosom of the sea” (*Iliad* 18.140; *Odyssey* 4.435), and he reports Curtius' guess that it is akin to κλέπτω, “hide”, as of something that covers (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.52; see the translation's table of key words). ἀτρύγετος, “barren”, is a standing epithet of the sea (see [[barren-sea]]). ἀγρώσσων, “hunting”, occurs only here in Homer, and so does ὀχήσατο, “rode”. Thick plumage, πυκινὰ πτερά, “is characteristic of sea-birds” (Merry on 5.53).

The tenor follows and names the likeness again: τῷ ἴκελος πολέεσσιν ὀχήσατο κύμασιν Ἑρμῆς, “like it, Hermes rode over the many waves” (*Odyssey* 5.54). Merry renders “rode on the thronging waves”, as if Hermes made the waves his carriage, his ὄχημα (on 5.54).

**The fifth verse.** The scholia say that someone added 5.54 without need, and Eustathius agrees (Merry's critical note on 5.54; see the translation's note on 5.54). After ἐοικώς, “like”, the verse does seem to repeat the likeness. Merry defends it by the same repetition in an Iliad simile, where Diomedes rushes over the plain “like a river in flood” and the tenor takes up the comparison again at its end, “so before the son of Tydeus the close-packed battalions / of the Trojans were thrown into confusion” (*Iliad* 5.87–94; see [[river-in-flood]]). A scholium on ἐοικώς gives the point of the likeness: it is in the swoop, not in the form, as Iris goes down into the sea “like a lead weight” (*Iliad* 24.80; see [[the-lead-weight]]). The words mean only, Merry concludes, that Hermes swooped down and skimmed the water like a sea bird (on 5.54).

## In context

Zeus has sent Hermes to tell Calypso that Odysseus must go home on a raft (*Odyssey* 5.28–42; see [[hermes]] and [[the-council-of-the-gods]]). The god binds on his golden sandals and takes his wand, in the verses of his errand to Priam (*Odyssey* 5.43–49 = *Iliad* 24.339–345; see [[sandals]] and [[wand]]), and goes down: “Stepping on Pieria, he fell from the upper air upon the sea” (*Odyssey* 5.50; see [[pieria]]). Hera took the same road from Olympus, “stepping on Pieria and lovely Emathia”, and stepped from Athos onto the sea (*Iliad* 14.225–229); Merry takes Πιερίην ἐπιβάς of Hermes' passage over the peaks of the Pierian range, north-west of Olympus (on 5.50). Then the long crossing is told in one likeness, and the island is reached in the next verse: “But when he came to the island, which lay far away” (*Odyssey* 5.55; see [[ogygia]] and [[calypsos-cave]]). The god of the wand and the sandals, who crosses the earth and the sea “as fast as the blasts of the wind” (*Odyssey* 5.46), crosses this sea at the height of a bird that fishes. Hermes himself will complain to Calypso of the distance: “who of his own will would speed across so much salt water, / boundless?” (*Odyssey* 5.100–101).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Odyssey's gods move like birds too. In this book Ino rises from the sea and goes back into it “like a diving bird” (*Odyssey* 5.337, 5.353; see [[like-a-diving-bird]]). Athena leaves Telemachus “like a bird” (*Odyssey* 1.320; see [[like-a-bird]]) and Nestor's company in the likeness of a sea eagle (*Odyssey* 3.372; see [[phene]]). In the Iliad the gods go down like hawks and like a bird of prey (13.62–65, 15.237–238, 18.616, 19.350–351; see [[like-a-hawk]] and [[like-a-bird-of-prey]]), and Thetis rises from the sea “like a mist” (1.359; see [[like-a-mist]]).

## Outside Homer

[[Virgil]] gives Mercury's flight to Carthage the same bird, unnamed: the god stops on Atlas, then throws himself headlong toward the waves, *avi similis, quae circum litora, circum / piscosos scopulos humilis volat aequora iuxta*, “like a bird that flies low near the water, around the shores, around the fish-filled rocks” (*Aeneid* 4.253–255; see [[virgils-mercury]]).

Proclus takes the likeness as a change of form. When one and the same god proceeds through the orders below it, the myths say that it changes into the form of the order it has reached, “for they say that Athena is likened to Mentor, Hermes to the gull and Apollo to the hawk” (*On the Republic* I 113 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). The scholium on ἐοικώς takes it, as Merry does, of the swoop alone.
