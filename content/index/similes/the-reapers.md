## The simile

{{quote:4dfb79f6-0da5-5d68-8e10-7ef6bbb1bbfb}}

οἳ δʼ, ὥς τʼ ἀμητῆρες ἐναντίοι ἀλλήλοισιν / ὄγμον ἐλαύνωσιν ἀνδρὸς μάκαρος κατʼ ἄρουραν / πυρῶν ἢ κριθῶν: “and as reapers facing one another drive their swaths through a rich man's field of wheat or barley”. ἀμητήρ, “reaper”, is from ἀμάω, “reap”, and occurs only here. ἐναντίοι ἀλλήλοισιν, “facing one another”, sets two bands of reapers working from the two ends of the field toward each other. ὄγμος is the swath, the strip that a reaper cuts as he moves along the field, and ἐλαύνω, “drive”, is the verb of pushing it forward. μάκαρ, “blessed”, is here “rich”: the field is large, and the crop belongs to a man of property. τὰ δὲ δράγματα ταρφέα πίπτει: “and the handfuls fall thick”. A δράγμα is as much as the reaper grasps in one hand and cuts, and ταρφέα is “thick, one after another”.

The tenor turns the reaping into killing: “so the Trojans and Achaeans, leaping at one another, / cut each other down, and neither side thought of deadly rout” (11.70–71). The verb δῄουν, “cut down, slay”, is the word for slaughter in battle. The points of likeness are the steady advance from both sides, the falling of many, and the evenness of the work. Neither band of reapers gives way, and the next verse says the same of the armies: “the battle held their heads level” (11.72; see the translation's note on 11.72). The handfuls of grain are men, and the reapers are men too.

## In context

The simile follows the baleful star of Hector (11.62–66; see [[the-baleful-star]]) and opens the fighting of the third day of battle. The next comparison is harsher, as both sides rage “like wolves” (11.72–73; see [[like-wolves]]), and the only god present is Strife, who rejoices to look on (11.73–74; see [[strife]]). The work goes on all morning, “as long as it was morning and the sacred day was growing”, until the hour of the woodcutter's meal, when the Danaans break through (11.84–91). The woodcutter is not a simile but a way of telling the time, the hour when a man who has felled trees all morning makes ready his meal (see the translation's note on 11.84–90 and [[the-third-day-of-battle]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The reaping of the simile is shown in peace on the shield of Achilles. On a king's estate hired laborers reap with sharp sickles; handfuls fall thick along the swath, binders tie them into sheaves, and the king stands among them in silence, glad at heart, while a meal is made ready under an oak (18.550–560). ὄγμος and δράγματα are the words of both scenes. Odysseus later turns the harvest into a figure for war: men quickly have their fill of battle, in which the bronze spills the most straw on the ground and the harvest is smallest, once Zeus tips his scales (19.221–224). The deep field of grain appears again in this book, eaten by the donkey of Ajax's simile (11.558–562; see [[the-donkey-in-the-field]]).
