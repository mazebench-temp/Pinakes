## The simile

{{quote:3d0c0980-7ca6-517a-9b68-91da2cc4af43}}

ὣς οἵ γʼ ἐμμεμαῶτε νέκυν φέρον ἐκ πολέμοιο / νῆας ἔπι γλαφυράς· ἐπὶ δὲ πτόλεμος τέτατό σφιν / ἄγριος ἠΰτε πῦρ, “So the two eagerly carried the corpse out of the war / toward the hollow ships; and the war was stretched tight over them, / savage as fire” (17.735–737). τέτατο, “was stretched tight”, is said earlier in the book of the mighty battle over Patroclus (ἐπὶ Πατρόκλῳ τέτατο κρατερὴ ὑσμίνη, 17.543), as ἐτάνυσσε, “stretched tight”, is said of the evil toil that Zeus stretched over him (17.400–401; see [[the-stretched-hide]] and [[the-rope-of-war]]). Walter Leaf explains it as the ropes that govern the course of the battle, pulled tight so that the fight rages fiercely (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.736; see [[the-rope-of-war]]).

τό τʼ ἐπεσσύμενον πόλιν ἀνδρῶν / ὄρμενον ἐξαίφνης φλεγέθει, μινύθουσι δὲ οἶκοι / ἐν σέλαϊ μεγάλῳ· τὸ δʼ ἐπιβρέμει ἲς ἀνέμοιο, “which, rushing on a city of men, / springs up suddenly and blazes, and the houses dwindle / in the great blaze; and the force of the wind roars over it” (17.737–739). ὄρμενον ἐξαίφνης, “springing up suddenly”, is said again of the fire that drives the locusts into the river in Book 21 (21.12–14; Leaf on 17.738). μινύθω is “grow less, dwindle”: the houses waste away as the fire eats them. σέλας is a blaze of light. ἐπιβρέμει is “roars upon”, or perhaps “makes it roar” (Leaf on 17.739); βρέμω is the roar of the sea in the surf (2.210, 4.425). ἲς ἀνέμοιο, “the force of the wind”, drives the fire as ἲς ἀνέμου drives the wave over a ship's side (15.383; see [[the-wave-over-the-ships-side]]).

The tenor: ὣς μὲν τοῖς ἵππων τε καὶ ἀνδρῶν αἰχμητάων / ἀζηχὴς ὀρυμαγδὸς ἐπήϊεν ἐρχομένοισιν, “So the din of horses and of spearmen, / ceaseless, came upon them as they went” (17.740–741). The point of likeness is the onset and the noise. The war falls on the men carrying the body as a fire falls on a city, suddenly, with the roar of the flames and of the wind that drives them, and it does not stop: ἀζηχής, “ceaseless”, is said of Zeus' unending pain for Heracles (15.25), and as an adverb of the ewes that bleat without ceasing (4.435) and of men calling to one another (15.658).

## In context

Ajax has told Menelaus and Meriones to lift the body and carry it out of the toil, while the two Ajaxes hold off the Trojans behind them (17.715–721). The Trojans rush at them like hounds at a wounded boar and fall back whenever the Ajaxes turn (17.722–734; see [[the-hounds-and-the-wounded-boar]]). The fire is the first of three likenesses for the carrying of the body: the war around the bearers is a burning city, the bearers are mules straining to drag a beam down a mountain path (17.742–746; see [[mules-dragging-a-beam]]), and the two Ajaxes behind them are a wooded ridge that holds back the floods (17.746–753; see [[the-wooded-ridge]]). See [[menelaus]] and [[meriones]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

Fire is the Iliad's common likeness for men fighting: they fight “like blazing fire” (11.596, 13.673, 17.366; see [[like-blazing-fire]]), Hector leads the Trojans “like a flame” (13.53) and comes for the body of Patroclus “like the flame of Hephaestus” (17.88; see [[like-a-flame]]), and an army's bronze flashes like a fire in a mountain forest (2.455–458; see [[forest-fire]]). Menelaus has just said of Hector that he “has the dread might of fire” (17.565). A city burning is a picture that the poem saves for its later books. The light from Achilles' head shines like the smoke and the beacons of an island city under siege (18.207–214; see [[the-beacon-fires]]); the toil Achilles brings on the Trojans is like the smoke that goes up to the sky from a burning city, sent by the wrath of the gods (21.522–525); and when Hector is killed, the lament in Troy is as if all of Ilios were smoldering with fire from top to bottom (22.410–411; see [[as-if-ilios-were-smoldering]]).
