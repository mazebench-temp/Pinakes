## The simile

{{quote:1ab74fab-dee4-52c5-be58-964386fb0f51}}

ὡς δʼ αἴθωνα λέοντα βοῶν ἀπὸ μεσσαύλοιο / ἐσσεύαντο κύνες τε καὶ ἀνέρες ἀγροιῶται: “as from the farmyard of the cattle a tawny lion is driven off by dogs and country men”. αἴθων, “tawny” or “blazing”, is the color of the lion's hide that Agamemnon and Diomedes wore in the night (10.24, 10.178). μέσσαυλος is the inner yard of a farm, where the cattle are penned. The men will not let the lion take “the fattest of the cattle” (βοῶν ἐκ πῖαρ), πάννυχοι ἐγρήσσοντες, “keeping watch all night long”. ὃ δὲ κρειῶν ἐρατίζων / ἰθύει, ἀλλʼ οὔ τι πρήσσει: “and he, craving meat, charges straight on, but gains nothing”, for thick javelins dart at him from bold hands, “and blazing torches, which he dreads, eager though he is” (δεταί, “torches”; τρεῖ, “dreads”). ἠῶθεν δʼ ἀπὸ νόσφιν ἔβη τετιηότι θυμῷ: “and at dawn he goes off far away, with grieving heart” (11.548–555).

The tenor takes up the lion's last words: “so then Ajax, grieving at heart (τετιημένος ἦτορ), went back from the Trojans very unwillingly; for he feared greatly for the ships of the Achaeans” (11.556–557). The lion has the hunger and the courage, and loses. What the simile gives is the retreat of a fighter who wants to attack, πόλλʼ ἀέκων, “very unwillingly”. The men who keep the lion off are the Trojans, whose spears come at Ajax “from bold hands”, in the formula of the simile (11.553, 11.571).

## In context

Hector has avoided Ajax (11.540–542; a verse quoted by Aristotle adds that Zeus was indignant with him whenever he fought a better man, 11.543). Then Zeus himself rouses Ajax to flight. He stands amazed, swings his shield of seven oxhides behind his back, and falls back toward the throng “glancing around like a wild beast”, turning again and again (11.544–547; see [[like-a-wild-beast]] and [[the-shield-of-ajax]]). The lion follows, and then a second simile for the same retreat, the sluggish donkey that the boys cannot drive out of the field (11.558–565; see [[the-donkey-in-the-field]]). The lion gives Ajax's unwillingness; the donkey gives his slowness and the uselessness of the blows. Ajax turns now to fight and now to go, and keeps the Trojans from the ships until Eurypylus and the others come to stand beside him (11.566–595; see [[ajax]] and [[retreat]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Six of the verses return in Book 17, when Menelaus leaves the body of Patroclus to send Antilochus to Achilles (17.657–664; 17.659–664 = 11.550–555). His first retreat from the body in the same book is told in a shorter version of the scene: he goes back, “turning again and again” (ἐντροπαλιζόμενος, as Ajax at 11.547), like a bearded lion that dogs and men drive from the steading with spears and shouting, and goes unwillingly from the farmyard (17.108–112). The lion that is kept from the fold or the herd is an old image of the similes. The sentries of Book 10 watch like dogs that hear a beast coming down through the woods (10.183–189; see [[the-watchdogs]]), and Sarpedon goes at the Achaean wall like a mountain lion that will not be driven from the fold untried, even if it finds herdsmen keeping watch with dogs and spears (12.299–306).
