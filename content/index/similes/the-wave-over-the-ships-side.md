## The simile

{{quote:e12b55b5-1430-5f07-8b95-3e0dc750b4b1}}

οἳ δʼ ὥς τε μέγα κῦμα θαλάσσης εὐρυπόροιο / νηὸς ὑπὲρ τοίχων καταβήσεται, ὁππότʼ ἐπείγῃ / ἲς ἀνέμου· ἣ γάρ τε μάλιστά γε κύματʼ ὀφέλλει: “And as a great wave of the wide-wayed sea comes down over the sides of a ship, when it is driven on by the force of the wind; for that above all swells the waves” (15.381–383). εὐρύπορος, “wide-wayed”, is an epithet of the sea, used again in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 4.432, 12.2). τοῖχος is a wall, the wall of a house or of a room (9.219, 18.374), and of a ship its sides, the planking that keeps the sea out; the sea breaks the sides of Odysseus' ship from its keel in the storm of Odyssey 12 (*Odyssey* 12.420). ἲς ἀνέμου is “the force of the wind”, and ὀφέλλω “swell, increase”: the last clause explains, in the manner of a man who knows the sea, that the wind above all makes the waves big.

The tenor: ὣς Τρῶες μεγάλῃ ἰαχῇ κατὰ τεῖχος ἔβαινον, “so the Trojans with a great shout went down over the wall” (15.384). The wave comes down ὑπὲρ τοίχων, over the ship's sides; the Trojans come down κατὰ τεῖχος, over the wall that Apollo has thrown down (15.361–366). τοῖχος and τεῖχος, the ship's side and the fortification, differ by one vowel. The likeness makes the Achaean camp a ship and its wall the planking that stood between the crew and the sea, and the wave is over it.

The vehicle carries its own cause. The wave is driven by the force of the wind, and the Trojans are driven by a sign: they have just heard Zeus thunder, and they spring on the Argives “more fiercely, and remembered the joy of battle” (15.379–380). The thunder was Zeus' answer to Nestor's prayer, not to them (15.370–378; see [[nestor]] and [[prayer]]).

## In context

Apollo has thrown down the wall like a boy scattering the sand (15.360–366; see [[the-boy-and-the-sand]]). The Achaeans halt beside the ships and pray with their hands lifted to all the gods, and Nestor, “the guardian of the Achaeans”, stretches out his hands to the starry sky: if ever anyone in Argos burned the fat thigh pieces of an ox or a sheep to Zeus and prayed for his return, let Zeus remember it and not let the Achaeans be beaten down by the Trojans (15.367–376). “So he spoke in prayer, and Zeus the counselor thundered loudly, hearing the prayers of the old son of Neleus” (15.377–378). The Trojans hear the thunder and take heart.

After the simile the battle is fought at the ships themselves, and it becomes a sea fight on land (see [[the-battle-at-the-ships]]). The Trojans drive their horses in and fight at the sterns from their chariots, and the Achaeans climb up onto the black ships and fight from above with the long spears kept on board “for fighting at sea, jointed, clad in bronze at the tip” (15.385–389; see [[ships]] and [[spear]]). In the second half of the book the sea returns twice: the Danaans stand like a rock by the gray sea, and Hector falls on them like a wave on a swift ship (15.618–629; see [[the-rock-by-the-sea]] and [[the-ship-in-the-storm]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Trojan advance is often a storm at sea. Hector falls on the battle like a storm wind that swoops down and stirs up the violet sea (11.297–298; see [[the-storm-wind-on-the-sea]]), and the Trojans come on rank behind rank like the seething waves raised by a blast of wind under the thunder of Zeus (13.795–800; see [[the-storm-and-the-waves]]). The waves of the earlier similes break on a shore (2.209–210, 4.422–426, 14.394–395); here, for the first time, the wave falls on a ship.
