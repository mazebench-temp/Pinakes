## The simile

{{quote:928a112c-1109-58ec-9ba6-c65344bd885a}}

στατὸς ἵππος is a horse kept standing in a stall, fed at the manger (φάτνη). ἀκοστήσας is found only in this simile and its repeat. It was explained in antiquity from ἀκοστή, said to be a dialect word for barley, and the translation renders it “fed full of barley” (see the translation's note on 6.506). δεσμὸν ἀπορρήξας is “breaking his tether”, and θείῃ πεδίοιο κροαίνων “he gallops over the plain, stamping”; κροαίνω, “strike with the hoof”, also occurs only here and in the repeat. He is εἰωθὼς λούεσθαι ἐϋρρεῖος ποταμοῖο, “used to bathing in the fair-flowing river”, and κυδιόων, “exulting”, the word for Agamemnon standing out among the heroes (2.579). ὑψοῦ δὲ κάρη ἔχει: “he holds his head high”, and his mane (χαῖται) streams about his shoulders. ἀγλαΐηφι πεποιθώς, “trusting in his splendor”: ἀγλαΐη is brightness and beauty, and -φι is an old case ending. His knees carry him lightly (ῥίμφα) μετά τʼ ἤθεα καὶ νομὸν ἵππων, “to the haunts and pasture of the horses”.

The comparison is of speed, freedom and display. A fine animal that has stood idle and well fed breaks loose and runs where it wants to be, head up, mane flying, sure of its beauty. Every detail answers something in Paris. ἀγλαΐηφι πεποιθώς, “trusting in his splendor”, repeats the words of the line before the simile, ποσὶ κραιπνοῖσι πεποιθώς, “trusting in his swift feet” (6.505). The horse's knees carry him lightly; Paris' “swift feet carried him” (6.514). The horse exults (κυδιόων); Paris comes down “laughing aloud” (καγχαλόων, 6.514), the verb Hector used in Book 3 for the Achaeans laughing at him (3.43). The horse has stood at the manger, and Paris has sat in his chamber, handling his armor, while others fought (6.321–322, 6.336). The simile flatters him and judges him at once.

## In context

Hector has found Paris at home and rebuked him, and Paris has promised to follow (6.325–341). Helen has told Hector that her husband's mind “is not steadfast now, nor will it ever be” (6.352–353). Hector goes on to Andromache. When he has left her, the poem turns back to Paris, who “did not linger long in his lofty house”, puts on “his glorious armor, inlaid with bronze”, and runs through the city (6.503–505). The simile follows, and the next line gives him the gleam of the sun (6.513; see [[like-the-sun]]).

He catches up with Hector “just as he was about to turn from the place where he had talked intimately with his wife” (6.515–516). The two scenes meet at the gate: the husband who has sent his wife home in tears to go to the war, and the brother who comes running from his wife's chamber, laughing. Paris apologizes for keeping him waiting (6.518–519), and Hector's answer measures him exactly:

## Elsewhere in Homer

The six lines return in Book 15 (15.263–268 = 6.506–511). Ajax's stone has struck Hector down, and he lies on the plain gasping and spitting blood (14.409–439, 15.9–11). Apollo comes to him at Zeus' command and breathes great might into him (15.220–262), and the simile follows: “so Hector plied his feet and knees swiftly, urging on the charioteers, when he heard the god's voice” (15.269–270). In Book 6 the tethered horse is Paris, idle while others fight; in Book 15 it is Hector, released from his wound. The picture of strength set free fits both; its pride and its pasture suit Paris more closely.

The line that ends the Book 15 simile, “so Hector plied his feet and knees swiftly”, is used again of Achilles, who runs toward Troy “like a prize-winning horse with its chariot, which runs easily at full stretch over the plain” (22.22–24). Achilles and Hector then run around the walls like prize-winning horses racing around the turning post, with a great prize set out, “a tripod or a woman” (22.162–164); but the prize of this race is Hector's life (22.159–161). In these similes the horse is a runner, and the last race is run for a life.

## Outside Homer

Ennius put the simile into Latin in his *Annals*, and [[virgil|Virgil]] gave it to Turnus. Turnus runs down from the high citadel, gleaming gold in his armor, “as when a horse breaks his tether and flees the stalls, free at last, master of the open plain, and makes for the pastures and the herds of mares, or, used to bathing in the familiar river, darts out and neighs, his neck high, and his mane plays over his neck and shoulders” (*Aeneid* 11.490–497). Virgil keeps the citadel and the gleam of Paris' descent (6.512–513), and his herds of mares say what Homer's “pasture of the horses” leaves unsaid. Macrobius set Homer's lines, Ennius' and Virgil's side by side (*Saturnalia* 6.3; see [[virgils-stallion]]).
