## The simile

{{quote:41c45a50-456a-56e4-a9c8-3fa86088b492}}

κὰδ δʼ ἄρʼ Ἀθηναίη τε καὶ ἀργυρότοξος Ἀπόλλων / ἑζέσθην ὄρνισιν ἐοικότες αἰγυπιοῖσι / φηγῷ ἐφʼ ὑψηλῇ πατρὸς Διὸς αἰγιόχοιο / ἀνδράσι τερπόμενοι. ἑζέσθην, “the two sat down”, is dual. ὄρνις is the common word for a bird, and αἰγυπιός names the kind, a large bird of prey that the translation calls a vulture (see [[vultures]]). ἐοικότες is “resembling, like”. The φηγός is the oak of Zeus by the Scaean gates, where the two gods met a few lines before (7.22; see [[the-oak-of-zeus]]). τερπόμενοι is “taking pleasure”: the gods enjoy the sight of the men.

The αἰγυπιός of Homer's similes is a hunter and a fighter. With crooked talons and curved beak it fights other vultures on a high rock (16.428–430), swoops on geese (17.460), comes down from the mountains on smaller birds in the plain (*Odyssey* 22.302–306), and cries for its young when countrymen have taken them from the nest (*Odyssey* 16.216–218). It watches from a height. The vultures of Book 16 fight “on a high rock” (πέτρῃ ἐφʼ ὑψηλῇ), and the gods here sit “on the high oak” (φηγῷ ἐφʼ ὑψηλῇ).

## Simile or transformation?

The case for a transformation is real. The gods sit on a tree, a bird's perch, and the poet names the kind of bird. The Odyssey shows that Homer could imagine a god in a bird's shape. Athena leaves Nestor's feast “in the likeness of a sea eagle” (φήνῃ εἰδομένη), amazement seizes all who see it, and Nestor knows that the guest was the goddess (*Odyssey* 3.371–379). In the slaughter of the suitors she perches on the roof beam, “like a swallow to look upon” (χελιδόνι εἰκέλη ἄντην, *Odyssey* 22.239–240). Sleep hides in a fir tree on Ida “in the likeness of a clear-voiced bird” (14.286–291).

The index reads the line as a simile, for four reasons. First, its words make a comparison, and in the Iliad the other gods compared with birds are compared for their manner. Apollo goes down from Ida “like a swift hawk, the dove-killer” (15.237–238), and Athena leaps from the sky like a long-winged bird of prey (19.350–351). Poseidon leaves the two Ajaxes as a hawk takes flight (13.62–65), yet the lesser Ajax knows him as a god by his feet and legs as he goes (13.70–72). Hera and Athena go “like timid doves in their step” (5.778; see [[timid-doves]]). Second, naming the kind of bird proves little. The phrase that sends Hermes low over the waves, λάρῳ ὄρνιθι ἐοικώς, “like a bird, a gull” (*Odyssey* 5.51), is built the same way, and it is usually taken to describe his flight. Third, no one in the scene sees the gods, and nothing depends on their shape. Fourth, gods watch battles from high places in their own form: Zeus from the peaks of Ida (8.51–52), Poseidon from the top of Samothrace (13.10–14), the gods of both parties from the wall of Heracles and from Callicolone (20.144–152).

What the likeness adds is manner. Two gods who favor opposite sides (7.17–27) settle side by side above the battle they have just agreed to stop, high, still and watchful, as birds of prey keep watch from a perch. The bird chosen is a killer, and the gods have arranged a fight. A transformation cannot be ruled out, and scholars remain divided. The translation, “like birds, like vultures”, leaves both readings open (see the translation's note on 7.59).

## In context

The scene is one of sitting. Hector makes the Trojans sit (7.55–56), Agamemnon the Achaeans (7.57), and the gods sit on the oak (7.58–60). Then the ranks sit “close-packed, bristling with shields and helmets and spears” (7.61–62), and a second simile turns them into the sea darkening under the West Wind (7.63–66; see [[the-ripple-of-the-west-wind]]). The two images divide the audience of the duel: gods above, perched like birds, and men below, spread out like the sea. The gods' pleasure in the sight is the pleasure of spectators (see [[watching-the-war]]). No one dies in the fight they watch, and the next day the dead of both armies are gathered and burned, not left to the birds (7.421–432; see [[the-truce-for-the-dead]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The other αἰγυπιοί of the Iliad are warriors: Meriones leaps “like a vulture” (13.531), Sarpedon and Patroclus rush at each other like two vultures (16.428–430), and Automedon drives among the Trojans like a vulture among geese (17.460). In the Odyssey Odysseus and his son weep aloud, more ceaselessly than sea eagles or vultures robbed of their young (*Odyssey* 16.216–219), and Odysseus and his men fall on the suitors like vultures on smaller birds (*Odyssey* 22.302–306).
