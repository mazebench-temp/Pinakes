## The simile

{{quote:df270364-d518-5c60-975f-8deaf5468be2}}

The comparison is made three times over, and each time denied: “not so loud … nor so loud … nor … so loud … as loud as was the voice”. Each likeness is set up to be outdone, and the tenor, the shout of two armies, is louder than all three.

οὔτε θαλάσσης κῦμα τόσον βοάᾳ ποτὶ χέρσον, / ποντόθεν ὀρνύμενον πνοιῇ Βορέω ἀλεγεινῇ: “Not so loud does the wave of the sea roar against the dry land, roused from the deep by the grievous blast of the North Wind” (14.394–395; see [[boreas]]). βοάω is to shout, and it is a verb of men: of heralds shouting to hold back the army (2.97), of Ajax shouting his orders along the ships (15.687, 15.732). Here the wave shouts, as the headlands of the shore do in a later simile (ἠϊόνες βοόωσιν, 17.265). ποντόθεν, “from the deep”, occurs only here.

οὔτε πυρὸς τόσσός γε ποτὶ βρόμος αἰθομένοιο / οὔρεος ἐν βήσσῃς, ὅτε τʼ ὤρετο καιέμεν ὕλην: “nor so loud is the roar of blazing fire in the glens of a mountain, when it rushes up to burn the forest” (14.396–397). βρόμος, the roar of the flames, occurs only here in Homer. The forest fire of the other similes is seen: its glare far off is the gleam of an army's bronze, and the thickets that fall before it are the men who fall before Agamemnon (2.455–458, 11.155–159). This one is heard (see [[forest-fire]]).

οὔτʼ ἄνεμος τόσσόν γε περὶ δρυσὶν ὑψικόμοισι / ἠπύει, ὅς τε μάλιστα μέγα βρέμεται χαλεπαίνων: “nor does the wind cry so loud around the high-leafed oaks, the wind that roars the loudest in its rage” (14.398–399). ἠπύω, “call aloud”, is a word of voices: the blinded Cyclops shouts to his neighbors with it, and a herdsman calls to another (*Odyssey* 9.399, 10.83). χαλεπαίνω, “be angry”, is a word of men's anger, as when Agamemnon owns that he was the first to grow angry with Achilles (2.378). The wind is given both. βρέμεται, “roars”, is the noise of the wave breaking on the shore (2.210, 4.425). ὑψίκομος, “with lofty foliage”, is said also of the oaks felled for the pyre of Patroclus (23.118) and of the oak of Zeus at Dodona (*Odyssey* 14.328 = 19.297).

The tenor: ὅσση ἄρα Τρώων καὶ Ἀχαιῶν ἔπλετο φωνὴ / δεινὸν ἀϋσάντων, ὅτʼ ἐπʼ ἀλλήλοισιν ὄρουσαν: “as loud as was the voice of the Trojans and the Achaeans as they shouted terribly, when they rushed upon one another” (14.400–401). The point of likeness is loudness alone. The simile measures it by the loudest things in nature, each at its height: the wave driven by the North Wind, the fire racing up the glen, the wind “that roars the loudest”. Menelaus uses the same denying form in scorn, when he says that neither the leopard nor the lion nor the boar has such might as the sons of Panthous think is theirs (17.19–23).

## In context

Sleep has told Poseidon that Zeus is asleep in Hera's arms and that he may help the Danaans while it lasts (14.354–360; see [[sleep]]). Poseidon leaps out among the foremost and calls on the Argives not to yield the victory to Hector, who boasts because Achilles stays by his ships, but to take the best shields, the all-gleaming helmets and the longest spears and follow him (14.361–377). The wounded kings see that the armor is exchanged (14.379–382; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]), and Poseidon leads them holding a dread long-edged sword “like lightning” (14.384–387; see [[like-lightning]]). On the other side Hector marshals the Trojans. “Then they stretched tight the most dread strife of war, dark-haired Poseidon and shining Hector” (14.389–390; see [[the-rope-of-war]] and [[poseidon]]). “And the sea surged up toward the huts and ships of the Argives; and they came together with a great shout” (14.392–393). ἐκλύσθη δὲ θάλασσα, “the sea surged up”, is said in the Odyssey of the sea thrown up by the rocks that the Cyclops hurls (*Odyssey* 9.484, 9.541). Here it surges as its god goes into battle. The simile follows, and after it the first blow, Hector's spear at Ajax (14.402).

Earlier in the book one voice was measured by armies: Poseidon shouted “as loud as nine thousand or ten thousand men shout” (14.148; see [[ten-thousand-men]]). Now the armies' own voice is measured by the sea, the fire and the wind.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Iliad hears its battles in the voices of nature. When the armies first met, the noise was the roar of two winter torrents throwing their water together where two valleys meet, heard by a shepherd far off in the mountains (4.452–456; see [[torrents-meeting]]). In Book 16 the clash of Trojans and Achaeans is the din of the East Wind and the South Wind contending in the glens of a mountain to shake a deep forest of oak, ash and cornel, whose long boughs dash together with a wondrous noise and crack as they break (16.765–771), and the thudding of bronze and hide rises like the din of woodcutters in the glens of a mountain, heard from far off (16.633–637). In Book 17 the Trojans come on with a shout like the great wave that roars against the current at a river's mouth, while the headlands of the shore cry out all around (17.263–266). In the assembly of Book 2 the army's voice was the surf on a long beach and a wave on a headland (2.209–210, 2.394–397; see [[surf-on-the-beach]] and [[wave-on-the-headland]]).
