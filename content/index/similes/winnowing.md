## The simile

{{quote:1c74f7ad-86c0-5607-ba4d-dc7527333a4a}}

ὡς δʼ ἄνεμος ἄχνας φορέει ἱερὰς κατʼ ἀλωὰς / ἀνδρῶν λικμώντων: “as the wind carries chaff across the sacred threshing floors when men are winnowing”. ἄχνη is anything fine that is blown off: the foam of the sea (4.426; Odyssey 5.403), and here the chaff. The ἀλωή is the threshing floor, a round, level, hard-beaten floor where the grain was trodden out by oxen (20.495–497) and then winnowed. The same word also means planted ground, an orchard or vineyard, as it does in the river simile earlier in the book (5.90; see [[river-in-flood]]). The floors are “sacred”, ἱεραί, perhaps because they belong to Demeter. λικμάω is “winnow”: the winnower (λικμητήρ, 13.590) throws the threshed grain into the wind, so that the light chaff blows away and the heavy grain falls back.

The goddess is in the picture, ξανθή, “fair-haired”, perhaps with the color of ripe grain (see [[fair-haired]]): ὅτε τε ξανθὴ Δημήτηρ / κρίνῃ ἐπειγομένων ἀνέμων καρπόν τε καὶ ἄχνας, “when fair-haired Demeter separates the grain from the chaff as the winds drive on” (5.500–501). Demeter, the goddess of grain, is named five times in the Iliad, and only here does she act. Elsewhere her name marks a precinct (2.696), a love of Zeus (14.326), and the grain itself, “Demeter's meal” (Δημήτερος ἀκτή, 13.322, 21.76; see [[demeter]]). The men winnow, the wind blows, and the work is hers. αἳ δʼ ὑπολευκαίνονται ἀχυρμιαί: the heaps of chaff (ἀχυρμιαί, a word found only here, from ἄχυρα, “chaff”) “grow white underneath” as the pale husks settle on them.

The tenor: ὣς τότʼ Ἀχαιοὶ / λευκοὶ ὕπερθε γένοντο κονισάλῳ, “so then the Achaeans grew white on top with the dust that through their midst the horses' hooves beat up into the brazen sky, as they closed again in battle; and the charioteers wheeled around” (5.502–505). κονίσαλος is the cloud of dust (see [[mist-on-the-mountain]]), and οὐρανὸς πολύχαλκος, “the brazen sky”, is the sky imagined as bronze (see [[brazen]]).

The point of likeness is whiteness. The dust settles on the men from above (ὕπερθε) as the chaff whitens the heaps from below (ὑπο-). The Achaeans are the heaps: they stand still while the wind of battle blows over them. The line before the simile says so, “the Argives held their ground in a body and were not put to flight” (5.498). A scene of peaceful work under a goddess is set beside the dust of war, and the only thing the two share is a color.

## In context

Stung by Sarpedon's rebuke, Hector leaps from his chariot and rallies the Trojans, who wheel round and stand facing the Achaeans (5.493–497; see [[dogs-around-a-lion]] and [[the-net]]). The simile holds the moment when the two sides close again. Then Ares shrouds the battle in night to help the Trojans, and Apollo sends Aeneas back from his temple, healed (5.506–518; see [[the-phantom-of-aeneas]]). A second image of Achaeans standing firm follows at once: clouds that the son of Cronus sets motionless on the mountain tops (5.522–527; see [[clouds-on-the-mountains]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The threshing floor returns in two battle similes. An arrow bounces off Menelaus' corselet as black beans or chickpeas leap from a broad winnowing shovel on a great threshing floor, under the shrill wind and the swing of the winnower (13.588–592). Achilles' horses trample the dead and their shields as oxen yoked on a well-built threshing floor tread out white barley, and the axle and the rails of his chariot are spattered with blood (20.495–503).

In the Odyssey, Teiresias tells Odysseus to walk inland with an oar on his shoulder until a stranger calls it a “chaff-destroyer” (ἀθηρηλοιγός), a winnowing shovel; there he is to plant it in the ground and sacrifice to Poseidon (Odyssey 11.126–131).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells the farmer to set his slaves to thresh “Demeter's holy grain” when Orion first appears, in a windy place, on a well-rounded threshing floor (*Works and Days* 597–599). Theocritus ends his seventh *Idyll* at a harvest feast beside the altar of Demeter of the threshing floor, with a wish to plant the great winnowing shovel on her heap again, while the goddess smiles with sheaves and poppies in her hands (*Idyll* 7.155–157).
