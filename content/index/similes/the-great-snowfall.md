## The simile

{{quote:411ac278-dd63-525a-abe1-d55443e95f54}}

τῶν δʼ, ὥς τε νιφάδες χιόνος πίπτωσι θαμειαὶ / ἤματι χειμερίῳ: “And as the flakes of snow fall thick on a winter's day” (12.278–279). The snow is sent: ὅτε τʼ ὤρετο μητίετα Ζεὺς / νιφέμεν ἀνθρώποισι πιφαυσκόμενος τὰ ἃ κῆλα, “when Zeus the counselor has set out to snow, showing men these arrows of his” (12.279–280). κῆλα are the arrows of Apollo's plague (1.53, 1.383), and Hesiod calls thunder, lightning and the thunderbolt κῆλα Διὸς μεγάλοιο, “the shafts of great Zeus” (*Theogony* 707–708); here Zeus' shafts are the flakes, or his weather (see the translation's note on 12.280, 12.286). κοιμήσας δʼ ἀνέμους χέει ἔμπεδον: “and he lulls the winds and pours it down steadily” (12.281). Then what the snow covers, from the heights down: the peaks of the high mountains and the high headlands, πεδία λωτοῦντα, “the lotus plains” (only here), and the rich tilled fields of men, and it is poured over the harbors and shores of the gray sea (12.281–284). κῦμα δέ μιν προσπλάζον ἐρύκεται: “but the wave that washes up against it holds it off”, and all else “is wrapped over from above, when the rain of Zeus falls heavy” (12.285–286), the half-line of the river in flood in Book 5 (5.91).

The tenor: “so from both sides the stones flew thick, some at the Trojans, and some from the Trojans at the Achaeans, as they pelted one another; and over the whole wall a thudding rose” (12.287–289). θαμειαί, “thick”, is the word of the flakes (12.278) and of the stones (12.287).

The point of likeness is a multitude that falls without pause and covers everything. The simile takes its time over the covering, field after field, while the battle it illustrates is all noise. The snow buries the land and stops only at the sea's edge, where the wave keeps it off.

## In context

The Trojans have torn at the copings, thrown down the battlements and pried out the buttresses of the towers, and the Danaans have fenced the battlements with oxhides and shoot from behind them (12.258–264; see [[battlements]]). The two Ajaxes range along the towers, rousing the Achaeans with soothing words and harsh: “men are by no means all alike in war, now there is work for all” (12.265–277; see [[the-two-ajaxes]]). Then the snow. After it, Zeus rouses his son Sarpedon against the wall (12.290–293; see [[the-mountain-lion-and-the-fold]] and [[the-fight-at-the-wall]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The first snow simile of the book is short and windy: there “a blustering wind” drives the flakes (12.157; see [[a-snow-of-stones]]); here Zeus lulls the winds. Windless weather made by Zeus is also the scene of the clouds to which the Danaans are likened in Book 5, set “in windless weather on the tops of the mountains, motionless, while the might of the North Wind sleeps” (5.522–527). The high headlands that the snow covers here stand out on a clear night “when the upper air is windless”, in the simile of the stars around the moon (8.555–559; see [[stars-around-the-moon]]).

Snow is Zeus' weather in the lightning simile of Book 10, when he makes ready “a heavy, endless rain, or hail, or snow, when the snowflakes sprinkle the fields” (10.5–7), and in the armor that pours from the ships as thick as the snowflakes of Zeus under the North Wind (19.357–361). Antenor's “snowflakes of winter” are Odysseus' words (3.222; see [[snowflakes-of-winter]]).
