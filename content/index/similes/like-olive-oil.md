## The simile

οὐδʼ ὅ γε Πηνειῷ συμμίσγεται ἀργυροδίνῃ, / ἀλλά τέ μιν καθύπερθεν ἐπιρρέει ἠΰτʼ ἔλαιον: “yet it does not mingle with the silver-eddying Peneius, but flows on top of it like olive oil”. συμμίσγεται is “mingles with”, καθύπερθεν “above, on top”, and ἐπιρρέει “flows over”. ἔλαιον is olive oil, which does not mix with water and floats on it.

The tenor is one river lying on another; the vehicle, oil on water. It is a plain, physical comparison, and the reason that follows is not physical at all: ὅρκου γὰρ δεινοῦ Στυγὸς ὕδατός ἐστιν ἀπορρώξ, “for it is a branch of the water of Styx, the dread oath”. ἀπορρώξ is “a piece broken off”, here an offshoot or branch of a river. The water of the gods' oath keeps itself apart from common water.

## In context

{{quote:0e56549d-e799-5d97-a5b1-dd703513a223}}

The lines belong to the entry of Gouneus. With him came the Enienes and the Perrhaebians, who lived around wintry Dodona, and the men who worked the fields around the lovely [[titaressus|Titaressus]] (2.748–751; see [[catalogue-of-ships]]). The river runs into the [[peneius|Peneius]], the great river of Thessaly, and yet keeps apart from it. The reason is the [[styx|Styx]], the water of the underworld by which the gods swear their greatest [[oath]].

This is the only simile in the Catalogue proper (2.494–759), whose lists of leaders, towns and ships leave little room for comparisons. The similes return when the Catalogue is done, for the mares of Eumelus ([[swift-as-birds]]) and for the army on the march ([[the-earth-groans-as-for-typhoeus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same half-line, Στυγὸς ὕδατός ἐστιν ἀπορρώξ (“is a branch of the water of Styx”), is said of Cocytus, a river of the underworld, in Circe's directions to Odysseus (Odyssey 10.513–514). Hera swears by the water of Styx, the greatest and most dread oath of the blessed gods (Iliad 15.36–38), and Calypso swears in the same words (Odyssey 5.184–186).

## Outside Homer

Lucan tells the same story in his description of Thessaly. The Titaressus keeps its own waters and glides over the Peneius as if over dry fields; report says that it flows from the Stygian marshes and, mindful of its source, will not suffer contact with a common river (*Pharsalia* 6.375–380).
