## The simile

{{quote:766295b2-ca7a-530b-9ef5-eeec864c6e03}}

ὡς δὲ λέων μήλοισιν ἀσημάντοισιν ἐπελθὼν / αἴγεσιν ἢ ὀΐεσσι κακὰ φρονέων ἐνορούσῃ: “as a lion, coming on flocks that have no herdsman, on goats or sheep, leaps on them with evil intent”. μῆλα are the small livestock, sheep and goats together, and the next verse names both (see [[sheep]] and [[goats]]). ἀσήμαντος, “without a herdsman”, occurs only here. It is the negative of σημάντωρ, the one who gives the signs: a herdsman (15.325), a charioteer (8.127), a commander (4.431). κακὰ φρονέων, “with evil intent”, is a phrase for men, and in Book 16 it is used with the same verb of Patroclus, who “leapt on the Trojans with evil intent” (16.783).

The tenor gives the count: ὣς μὲν Θρήϊκας ἄνδρας ἐπῴχετο Τυδέος υἱὸς / ὄφρα δυώδεκʼ ἔπεφνεν, “so the son of Tydeus went at the Thracian men, until he had killed twelve”. ἐπῴχετο, “went at, went among”, is the verb of Apollo's plague, which went first among the mules and dogs (1.50), and of Diomedes going after Aphrodite with the pitiless bronze (5.330).

The point of the likeness is the missing herdsman. The lion meets no dog and no spear; the flock cannot fight. The Thracians are all asleep, “worn out by weariness”, with their armor laid beside them on the ground (10.471–473). The lion's courage is not tested here, only its hunger, and the simile does not pretend otherwise.

## In context

Odysseus points out Rhesus and his horses and tells Diomedes to untie the horses or kill the men (10.476–481). Athena breathes might into him, and the verses before the simile give the killing its sound and color: “he killed, turning this way and that; and an ugly groaning rose / from the men struck by the sword, and the earth grew red with blood” (10.483–484). The same two verses, with water for earth, tell how Achilles kills the Trojans in the river (21.20–21), and the Odyssey uses the first of them for the killing of the suitors (*Odyssey* 22.308). After the simile Odysseus drags each dead man aside by the foot, so that the horses, not yet used to the dead, will not shy at treading on them (10.488–493), and the thirteenth to die is the king (10.494–497; see [[rhesus]] and [[the-doloneia]]).

The lion is the last of the book's beasts. Diomedes went out from the camp in a lion's skin (10.177–178), and he and Odysseus set out together “like two lions through the black night” (10.297; see [[two-lions]]). At the start of the night the Achaean sentries were like dogs keeping watch over the sheep in a fold, listening for the beast in the woods (10.183–189; see [[the-watchdogs]]). The Trojans keep no such watch; Dolon has told the two that the allies sleep and leave the watching to the Trojans (10.420–421), and the Thracians lie apart from all the rest (10.434). The flock has no herdsman.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The lion that falls on a flock or herd is the commonest picture of the Iliad's similes (see [[lion]]). Diomedes was given two such lions in Book 5, one wounded by a shepherd as it leapt into the fold, one breaking the neck of a heifer (5.136–142, 5.161–164; see [[wounded-lion]] and [[lion-among-cattle]]), and in Book 12 Sarpedon goes at the Achaean wall like a lion that will go for the sheep even when it finds herdsmen guarding them with dogs and spears (12.299–306). The absent herdsman returns in Book 15. When Apollo shakes the aegis, the Achaeans flee like a herd of cattle or a great flock of sheep that two beasts drive in confusion in the dead of black night, “when the herdsman is not there” (σημάντορος οὐ παρεόντος, 15.323–326), and Hector falls on them like a lion on cattle whose herdsman does not yet know how to fight a beast (15.630–636). In the Odyssey Odysseus among the dead suitors is like a lion that has eaten an ox, spattered with blood (*Odyssey* 22.402–406).
