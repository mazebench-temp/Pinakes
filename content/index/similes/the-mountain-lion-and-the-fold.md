## The simile

{{quote:9454edff-ccdf-5e42-b379-657e141cdfc7}}

βῆ ῥʼ ἴμεν ὥς τε λέων ὀρεσίτροφος, ὅς τʼ ἐπιδευὴς / δηρὸν ἔῃ κρειῶν: “he went on like a mountain-bred lion, which has long gone without meat” (12.299–300). ὀρεσίτροφος, “reared in the mountains”, is a lion's word (also 17.61; see [[lion]]). κέλεται δέ ἑ θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ / μήλων πειρήσοντα καὶ ἐς πυκινὸν δόμον ἐλθεῖν: “and his proud spirit bids him make an attempt on the sheep, and go even into the close-built fold” (12.300–301; θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ, see [[proud]]). Even if he finds herdsmen (βώτορας ἄνδρας) keeping watch with dogs and spears, οὔ ῥά τʼ ἀπείρητος μέμονε σταθμοῖο δίεσθαι, “he has no mind to be driven from the steading without a try” (12.302–304). ἀπείρητος, “untried”, and πειρήσοντα, “to make an attempt”, are from the same root: the lion must put it to the test. ἀλλʼ ὅ γʼ ἄρʼ ἢ ἥρπαξε μετάλμενος, ἠὲ καὶ αὐτὸς / ἔβλητʼ ἐν πρώτοισι θοῆς ἀπὸ χειρὸς ἄκοντι: “but either he springs on one and seizes it, or he himself is hit among the foremost by a javelin from a swift hand” (12.305–306).

The tenor takes up the lion's spirit: “so then his spirit sent godlike Sarpedon to rush at the wall and break through the battlements” (12.307–308). The fold is the Achaean camp behind its wall, and the herdsmen and dogs are its defenders. The point of likeness is hunger joined to daring. The lion goes in where the guards are, and accepts either outcome.

## In context

Not even then, the narrator says, would the Trojans and Hector have broken the gates of the wall, “had not Zeus the counselor roused his own son, Sarpedon, against the Argives, like a lion against cattle with curving horns” (12.290–293; see [[lion-among-cattle]]). Sarpedon holds before him his shield of beaten bronze, lined with oxhides stitched with golden wires, and shakes two spears (12.294–298; see [[sarpedon]]). The simile follows, and then his speech to Glaucus (12.309–328; see [[sarpedons-speech]]).

The simile and the speech answer each other. The lion's choice, to seize a sheep or to be hit himself, is the choice with which the speech ends: “let us go, and either hold out triumph to another, or another to us” (12.328). The lion is hit ἐν πρώτοισι, “among the foremost” (12.306), and Sarpedon tells Glaucus that the kings of Lycia must take their stand “among the foremost Lycians” (12.315, 12.321). The lion has gone long without meat; the kings are honored at home “with a seat of honor, with meat, and with full cups” (12.311).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The simile is used again in the Odyssey. Odysseus, naked and salt-stained, comes out of the thicket toward Nausicaa and her maids like a mountain-bred lion, rained on and wind-beaten, its eyes blazing, that goes after cattle or sheep or wild deer, and “his belly” (γαστήρ) bids him make an attempt on the sheep and go even into the close-built fold (*Odyssey* 6.130–134; 6.134 = 12.301). In the Iliad it is the lion's proud spirit that drives it, in the Odyssey its hunger.

Other lions of the Iliad are kept from the fold or the herd: a lion that a shepherd wounds as it leaps into the fold (5.136–142; see [[wounded-lion]]), and a lion that dogs and country men keep from the cattle all night and drive off at dawn (11.548–555; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]). In the Thracian camp the flock has no herdsman at all (10.485–488; see [[lion-among-the-flocks]]). Sarpedon's lion goes in among the guards. When he dies in Book 16 the image is turned round: Patroclus kills him as a lion kills a tawny bull among the cattle (16.487–491).
