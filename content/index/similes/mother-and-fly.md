## The simile

ἣ δὲ τόσον μὲν ἔεργεν ἀπὸ χροὸς ὡς ὅτε μήτηρ / παιδὸς ἐέργῃ μυῖαν ὅθʼ ἡδέϊ λέξεται ὕπνῳ: “she brushed it away from his flesh, as a mother brushes a fly from her child, when he lies in sweet sleep”. ἔεργεν and ἐέργῃ are the same verb, ἐέργω, “keep off, shut out”: once for the goddess, once for the mother. μυῖα is the fly ([[flies]]), and ἡδὺς ὕπνος, “sweet sleep”, is a common phrase. λέξεται is “lies down to sleep”.

τόσον μέν, “so far”, is answered in the next line: αὐτὴ δʼ αὖτʼ ἴθυνεν, “and she herself guided it” (4.132). The goddess turns the arrow only so far from his body, and then she steers it, to where the golden clasps of the belt held it together and the corselet met it in a double layer (4.132–133). She does not stop it. It goes through the belt, the corselet and the band he wore beneath, and grazes the outermost flesh (4.134–139; see [[belt]], [[corselet]] and [[band]]).

The vehicle is a household scene: a mother, a sleeping child, a fly. The tenor is a goddess and an arrow “never shot before, a source of black pains” (4.117). The point of likeness is the ease of the gesture. What could kill a king is, to a goddess, a fly. The mother's care suits the goddess whom Zeus has just called Menelaus' helper (4.7–8), and the sleeping child suits Menelaus, who sees neither the arrow nor the hand that turns it.

## In context

{{quote:90ce77e5-b5e5-516b-bda1-7e6075564a18}}

Athena, in the likeness of Laodocus, has persuaded Pandarus to shoot, and the arrow has leapt from the string (4.86–126; see [[pandarus-breaks-the-truce]]). Then the narrator turns to Menelaus and speaks to him: “But you, Menelaus, the blessed gods did not forget” (4.127). Homer's narrator seldom addresses a character in the second person (see [[apostrophe]]). Menelaus is addressed so seven times in the Iliad (4.127, 4.146, 7.104, 13.603, 17.679, 17.702, 23.600), and only Patroclus more often. The address returns at the end of the passage, in the simile of the stained ivory (4.146; see [[ivory-stained-with-purple]]).

The goddess who turns the arrow is the goddess who set it flying. Zeus and Hera sent her to make the Trojans break the oaths (4.64–72), and she chose the archer and told him what to pray and what to aim at (4.93–103). Her two acts serve one end. The oaths are broken by the shot, and a wound is all the shot needs to do. If Menelaus died, Agamemnon says, the Achaeans would remember their native land and sail home, leaving Argive Helen to the Trojans (4.169–175; see [[war-for-menelaus]]). The war would end with him. So the arrow must strike and must not kill, and the mother's hand makes sure of both.

Zeus' taunt at the start of the book is answered. Menelaus has two goddesses as helpers, Zeus said, Hera and Athena, but “they sit apart and look on”, while laughter-loving Aphrodite “is always at his side and wards off doom from him” (4.7–11). Aphrodite's care is for Paris (see [[aphrodite-rescues-paris]]). Now Athena stands in front of Menelaus and “warded off the piercing shaft” (ἄμυνεν, 4.129), the verb Zeus used of Aphrodite (ἀμύνει, 4.11).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The fly comes back to the same two figures in Book 17. Over the body of Patroclus, Athena puts into Menelaus' breast “the daring of a fly”, which keeps trying to bite, though it is driven from a man's skin again and again, “and human blood is sweet to it” (17.570–572). The words of Book 4 return: there the fly is “kept off” (ἐργομένη) from a man's skin (χροός), as here Athena “kept” the arrow “from his flesh” (ἔεργεν ἀπὸ χροός). The fly that was the arrow has become the image of Menelaus himself. The flies of Book 2 are a swarm, the measure of an army (2.469–473; see [[flies-around-the-milk-pails]]); this one is alone and harmless.

Other similes take their picture from a mother and her young, and in each the point is care, a child's need or a parent's protection. Teucer, shooting from behind Ajax's shield, goes back to him after every shot, “like a child to its mother”, and Ajax hides him with his shining shield (8.271–272). Achilles likens Patroclus in tears to a little girl who runs beside her mother, clutching her dress and begging to be picked up (16.7–10), and himself to a bird that brings its unfledged chicks whatever it finds, and fares badly itself (9.323–324).
