## The simile

αὐτὸς δὲ κτίλος ὣς ἐπιπωλεῖται στίχας ἀνδρῶν· / ἀρνειῷ μιν ἔγωγε ἐΐσκω πηγεσιμάλλῳ, / ὅς τʼ οἰῶν μέγα πῶϋ διέρχεται ἀργεννάων: “but he himself ranges like a ram through the ranks of men; I liken him to a thick-fleeced ram that goes through a great flock of white sheep”. Two words for the ram come one after the other. κτίλος, “tame”, is used as a noun for the ram that leads the flock, the bellwether; ἀρνειός is the ordinary word for a ram (see [[ram]]). πηγεσίμαλλος, “thick-fleeced” (πήγνυμι, “make firm, compact”, and μαλλός, “fleece”), occurs only here. A πῶϋ is a flock of [[sheep]], and ἀργεννός is “white, shining”. ἐπιπωλεῖται, “goes about, visits in turn”, is the verb of a leader's review of his troops.

The tenor is Odysseus, walking along the lines of seated men while the truce holds. The vehicle is the leader of a flock moving through it. The point of likeness is the way he goes: at his own pace, among men who stay where they are, as the one who leads them. The ram is also broad and solid, and Priam has just described him as “shorter by a head than Agamemnon, son of Atreus, but broader in the shoulders and chest to look at” (3.193–194). Priam marks the comparison as his own, with an emphatic “I” (ἔγωγε).

## In context

{{quote:d88e7e5f-a27c-50ee-a5fb-e25ead828669}}

Priam asks Helen to name the Achaeans he sees from the tower (see [[the-view-from-the-wall]]). Each question describes a man before he is named. Agamemnon is huge and handsome and stately, “for he looks like a king” (βασιλῆϊ γὰρ ἀνδρὶ ἔοικε, 3.166–170); Odysseus is a ram among the sheep; Ajax towers above the Argives “by his head and broad shoulders” (3.226–227). In Book 2 the poet made Agamemnon a bull standing out above the herd (2.480–483; see [[bull-above-the-herd]]). Now the second of the Achaean leaders gets a ram, a smaller animal and a leader of the flock, not a king over the herd.

Helen names him: “This is the son of Laertes, Odysseus of many wiles, who was raised in the land of Ithaca, craggy though it is, and knows all kinds of tricks and shrewd counsels” (3.200–202). Antenor then remembers how Odysseus looked when he came to Troy on an embassy, standing with his eyes on the ground and speaking words like the snowflakes of winter (see [[like-a-man-who-knows-nothing]], [[snowflakes-of-winter]] and [[the-embassy-to-troy]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The bellwether leads the Trojans in Book 13. Aeneas calls on Deiphobus, Paris and Agenor, and the people follow, “as sheep follow the ram (κτίλον) from the pasture to drink, and the shepherd is glad at heart”; so Aeneas rejoices to see them come (13.489–495). The verb ἐπιπωλέομαι describes Agamemnon's review of his army in Book 4, going through the ranks of men on foot (ἐπεπωλεῖτο στίχας ἀνδρῶν, 4.231, 4.250). In the Odyssey a ram saves Odysseus: he clings under the belly of the finest ram of the Cyclops' flock, and so escapes from the cave (Odyssey 9.431–463).
