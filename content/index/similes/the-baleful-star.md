## The simile

{{quote:04a43c5b-c561-55f9-b041-352db5522f91}}

οἷος δʼ ἐκ νεφέων ἀναφαίνεται οὔλιος ἀστήρ / παμφαίνων, τοτὲ δʼ αὖτις ἔδυ νέφεα σκιόεντα: “and as a baleful star shows itself from out of the clouds, shining bright, and then again sinks into the shadowy clouds”. οὔλιος, “baleful, destructive”, occurs only here; it belongs with οὖλος, the epithet of the Dream that Zeus sends to deceive Agamemnon and of Ares (2.6, 5.461). παμφαίνων is “shining all over”. ἔδυ, “sinks”, is the verb of a star or the sun going down, and a gnomic aorist, as the similes use it.

The tenor answers each half: “so Hector now would appear (φάνεσκεν) among the foremost, / and now among the hindmost, giving orders” (11.64–65). φάνεσκε takes up ἀναφαίνεται, and the iterative ending -σκε says that he did it again and again. The point of likeness is the coming and going: a bright, threatening thing that is seen and lost and seen again. Hector is not yet fighting. He is the commander going along the line, and the Achaeans see him now at the front, now at the back.

A second comparison follows at once: “and all of him in bronze / shone like the lightning of aegis-bearing father Zeus” (11.65–66; see [[like-lightning]]).

**Which star.** The poem does not name it. It is usually taken to be the Dog Star, Sirius, the star of late summer that is bright and an evil sign, and brings fever to wretched mortals (22.26–31; see the translation's note on 11.62). The star of Diomedes' armor in Book 5 is the same star of late summer, and there it is a likeness for fire, not for harm (5.4–6; see [[the-autumn-star]]).

## In context

The Trojans have drawn up on the rise of the plain around Hector, Polydamas, Aeneas and the three sons of Antenor (11.56–60; see [[polydamas]]). The simile of the reapers follows, and the battle begins (11.67–71; see [[the-reapers]]). The star that hides and shines out again also fits Hector's part in the book. Zeus draws him out of the fighting while Agamemnon rages (11.163–164) and has Iris tell him to hold back (11.186–209); when Agamemnon is wounded, Hector comes on again and falls on the battle like a storm wind (11.284–298; see [[the-storm-wind-on-the-sea]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Stars in the similes are bright and dangerous. Athena comes down from Olympus like a shooting star that Zeus sends as a portent (4.75–78; see [[shooting-star]]); fire blazes from Diomedes' helmet and shield like the star of late summer (5.4–7); Achilles' helmet shines like a star (19.381); Priam sees Achilles coming across the plain like the star that men call Orion's Dog (22.26–31); and the point of Achilles' spear, as he aims at Hector's throat, gleams like the evening star (22.317–319). Two stars of Book 6 are of another kind, a robe and a child (6.295, 6.401; see [[like-a-star]]). Hector's is the only star in the similes that hides in the clouds.
