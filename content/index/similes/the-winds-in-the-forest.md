## The simile

{{quote:1d0d820a-7ce6-535d-ab66-664981dd7530}}

ὡς δʼ Εὖρός τε Νότος τʼ ἐριδαίνετον ἀλλήλοιιν / οὔρεος ἐν βήσσῃς βαθέην πελεμιζέμεν ὕλην: “And as the East Wind and the South Wind strive with each other / in the glens of a mountain to shake a deep forest” (16.765–766; see [[eurus]] and [[notus]]). The verb is dual, ἐριδαίνετον, and so is ἀλλήλοιιν, “with each other”: two winds, as two armies. ἐριδαίνω is to strive or quarrel, the verb of Hephaestus' complaint at the feast of the gods, when Zeus and Hera fall out, “if you two quarrel like this for the sake of mortals” (1.574), and its noun is ἔρις, “strife” (see [[eris]]). πελεμίζω, “shake”, is said earlier in the book of Ajax, whom the Trojans could not shake from where he stood, however they pressed him with their missiles (16.108).

The forest is named tree by tree: φηγόν τε μελίην τε τανύφλοιόν τε κράνειαν, “oak and ash and the smooth-barked cornel” (16.767). The φηγός is the oak, the tree that stands as the oak of Zeus by the Scaean gates (see [[the-oak-of-zeus]]); the μελίη is the ash from which spears are made, as the Pelian ash of Achilles (16.143); the κράνεια is the cornel, whose fruit Circe throws to Odysseus' men when they are swine (*Odyssey* 10.242). τανύφλοιος, “with long, smooth bark”, is found only here. Then: αἵ τε πρὸς ἀλλήλας ἔβαλον τανυήκεας ὄζους / ἠχῇ θεσπεσίῃ, πάταγος δέ τε ἀγνυμενάων, “which dash their long-pointed branches against one another / with a wondrous din, and there is a crashing as they break” (16.768–769). τανυήκης, “long-pointed”, a word used elsewhere of long-edged swords (14.385, 16.473), is said here of branches. ἠχῇ θεσπεσίῃ, “with a wondrous din”, is the din of battle (12.252, 15.355); πάταγος, a crash or clatter, is otherwise only the chattering of a coward's teeth (13.283).

The tenor: ὣς Τρῶες καὶ Ἀχαιοὶ ἐπʼ ἀλλήλοισι θορόντες / δῄουν, οὐδʼ ἕτεροι μνώοντʼ ὀλοοῖο φόβοιο, “so the Trojans and Achaeans, leaping at one another, / cut each other down, and neither side thought of deadly rout” (16.770–771). The two verses are those that end the simile of the reapers on the morning of this same day of battle (11.70–71; see [[the-reapers]]). The point of likeness is an even fight: two winds of equal strength, and trees that strike one another and break, so that no side gives way and everything is broken. The vehicle is all sound, and the trees, like the men, are hurled against one another.

## In context

Hector and Patroclus have leapt down from their chariots and fight over the body of Cebriones like two hungry lions over a slain deer (16.755–761; see [[two-lions]] and [[cebriones]]). “Hector, once he had taken hold of his head, would not let go; and Patroclus on the other side held his foot; and the others, Trojans and Danaans, joined in fierce battle” (16.762–764). The simile follows. After it the poem shows the body in the middle: many sharp spears fixed around Cebriones, feathered arrows, and great stones battering the shields of the men who fought around him, “and he in a whirl of dust / lay, great over a great space, forgetful of his horsemanship” (16.772–776; see [[great-over-a-great-space]]). As long as the sun stood in the middle of the sky, the missiles of both sides struck home and men fell; when it moved on toward the time for unyoking oxen, the Achaeans prevailed beyond their portion (16.777–780). The even fight of the simile lasts until noon is past.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same pair of winds raised the long waves of the Icarian sea to which the assembly of Book 2 was likened (2.144–146; see [[waves-of-the-icarian-sea]]). Two winds from Thrace, the North Wind and the West Wind, stir up the sea in the simile of the Achaeans' grief (9.4–8; see [[two-winds-from-thrace]]), and the West Wind strikes the clouds of the South Wind in a simile for Hector's onset (11.305–308; see [[the-west-wind-and-the-clouds]]). In Book 14 the shout of the armies was louder than the wind crying in the high-leafed oaks (14.398–399; see [[louder-than-sea-fire-and-wind]]), and in this book the din of the fight over Sarpedon was the din of woodcutters in the glens of a mountain (16.633–637; see [[the-woodcutters]]). The trees that the Lapiths were likened to stand against the wind and the rain all their days (12.132–134; see [[oaks-on-the-mountains]]); these crack and break.

## Outside Homer

Virgil gives the same picture to the even battle of Trojans and Latins: as winds at odds in the great sky raise battle with equal spirit and strength, and neither they nor the clouds nor the sea give way, and the fight is long in doubt, so the lines meet, foot against foot and man against man (*Aeneid* 10.356–361). In the night of Troy's capture the Greeks fall on Aeneas and his companions from every side like winds that clash when a whirlwind bursts, the West, the South and the East: “the forests shriek” (*stridunt silvae*), and Nereus stirs the sea from its depths (*Aeneid* 2.413–419).
