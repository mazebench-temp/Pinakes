## The simile

{{quote:fdbf2d94-5382-55d5-bfcb-835ec9315033}}

ἵστατο δάκρυ χέων ὥς τε κρήνη μελάνυδρος / ἥ τε κατʼ αἰγίλιπος πέτρης δνοφερὸν χέει ὕδωρ: “stood up, shedding tears like a spring of dark water that pours its murky water down a sheer rock”. δάκρυ χέων, “shedding tears”, is the formula of a weeping man (see [[shedding-tears]]), and the simile spells it out: χέων, “shedding”, is answered by χέει, “pours”. μελάνυδρος, “of dark water” (μέλας, “dark”, and ὕδωρ, “water”), is the standing epithet of springs (16.160, 21.257; *Odyssey* 20.158), the shaded water of a deep source (see [[of-dark-water]]). αἰγίλιψ, of the rock, is taken as “sheer”, a rock that even goats leave (αἴξ, “goat”, and λείπω, “leave”; see [[sheer]] and the translation's note on 9.14–15). The Iliad uses it of the rock from which a hawk launches itself (13.62–63), and it is the name of a town in Odysseus' kingdom, “rugged Aegilips” (2.633). δνοφερός, “murky”, is used in the Iliad only of this water; in the Odyssey it is a word for night (*Odyssey* 13.269, 15.50; see [[dnopheros]]).

The likeness is first of all the flow: tears run down the king's face without stopping, as water runs down a rock face. The darkness of the water fits the hour and the grief. Agamemnon has had the heralds call each man to the assembly by name, without shouting, and the men sit “grieving” (τετιηότες, 9.13). He speaks “groaning heavily” (βαρὺ στενάχων, 9.16), and what he says is that Zeus has deceived him and they must flee (9.17–28; see [[the-test-of-the-army]]).

## In context

The weeping king is the book's second simile, after the two winds that tear the army's heart (9.4–8; see [[two-winds-from-thrace]]). The Iliad's heroes weep without shame (see [[tears]]), and Agamemnon has wept before. On the evening of the defeat he prayed with tears that the army might at least escape, and Zeus “pitied him as he shed tears” (δάκρυ χέοντα, 8.245). In Book 2 he proposed flight standing with the scepter of his fathers, to test the army (2.100–141). Now he proposes it in the same words, weeping, and the assembly answers him with silence (9.29–31).

The second spring comes at the next turn of the story. While the Trojans fight at the ships, Patroclus comes to Achilles “shedding hot tears like a spring of dark water” (δάκρυα θερμὰ χέων, 16.3), and the verse of the rock follows unchanged (16.4 = 9.15). Achilles pities him and asks why he is crying like a little girl who runs beside her mother, clutching her dress until she is picked up (16.5–11). Agamemnon's tears begin the night that sends the envoys to Achilles; Patroclus' tears win from Achilles what the envoys could not, a man in his armor sent out to save the ships (16.21–100; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).
