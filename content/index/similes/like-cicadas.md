## The simile

γήραϊ δὴ πολέμοιο πεπαυμένοι, ἀλλʼ ἀγορηταὶ / ἐσθλοί, τεττίγεσσιν ἐοικότες οἵ τε καθʼ ὕλην / δενδρέῳ ἐφεζόμενοι ὄπα λειριόεσσαν ἱεῖσι: “done with war now because of their age, but excellent speakers, like the cicadas that in the woods sit on a tree and send out their lily-soft voice”. τέττιξ is the cicada (see [[cicadas]]), named only here in Homer. ἀγορηταί are speakers in the assembly, and ὄψ is a voice. λειριόεις is literally “lily-like”, from λείριον (“lily”). Homer uses it once more, of skin, when Hector threatens that his spear will tear Ajax's “lily-white” flesh (χρόα λειριόεντα) and that Ajax will glut the dogs and birds of Troy (13.829–832). Of a voice it probably means delicate, clear or sweet; the exact point is disputed, and the translation renders it “lily-soft”.

The tenor: τοῖοι ἄρα Τρώων ἡγήτορες ἧντʼ ἐπὶ πύργῳ, “so the leaders of the Trojans were sitting on the tower”. The likeness is double. The old men sit, as the cicadas sit on their tree, and they talk on without end, as the cicadas sing. Their bodies are past fighting, and what is left of them is voice. The Greeks heard the cicada's song as music, a sound of high summer, so the comparison honors the elders as speakers even while it marks their frailty.

## In context

{{quote:e30dc29d-5f34-5f4c-8991-910bd39202ce}}

Helen, called by Iris to watch the duel, comes to the Scaean gates (3.121–145). The narrator names the old men who sit there with Priam, one by one: Panthous and Thymoetes, Lampus, Clytius and Hicetaon, Ucalegon and Antenor (see [[trojan-elders]]). Three of them are Priam's brothers. The whole of 3.147 returns in the genealogy of the kings of Troy, where Lampus, Clytius and Hicetaon are sons of Laomedon with Priam and Tithonus (20.237–238; see [[laomedon]]).

Then the cicadas speak. When the old men see Helen coming to the tower, “softly they spoke winged words to one another” (ἦκα, 3.155): “There is no blame that Trojans and well-greaved Achaeans should suffer sorrows for so long for such a woman; she is terribly like the immortal goddesses to look on. But even so, beautiful as she is, let her go home in the ships” (3.156–160; see [[no-blame]] and [[nemesis]]). Their words have the qualities the simile promised: soft voices, good sense, and no power to act. The scene goes on with Priam and Helen (see [[the-view-from-the-wall]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod describes the cicada in the words of this simile. In the heat of summer “the chirping cicada, sitting on a tree (δενδρέῳ ἐφεζόμενος), pours down its clear song” (*Works and Days* 582–584), and in the *Shield* it sits on a green branch and sings all day, feeding on dew (393–397). He calls the voice of the Muses “lily-like” (ὀπὶ λειριοέσσῃ, *Theogony* 41).

The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* tells the fate of Tithonus, the brother of Lampus, Clytius and Hicetaon. Dawn won immortality for him but forgot to ask for youth, and when he could no longer move his limbs she shut him in a chamber, where “his voice flows on without end” (ῥέει ἄσπετος, 218–238). The story that he shriveled into a cicada is later. Plato's Socrates tells another: the cicadas were once men so delighted by the song of the Muses that they forgot to eat and drink and died singing, and the Muses gave them the gift of needing no food (*Phaedrus* 258e–259d).
