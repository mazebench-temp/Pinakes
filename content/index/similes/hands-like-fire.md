## The simile

{{quote:e12e5541-34a0-52bd-beb0-f15e253d7dc1}}

τοῦ δʼ ἐγὼ ἀντίος εἶμι καὶ εἰ πυρὶ χεῖρας ἔοικεν, / εἰ πυρὶ χεῖρας ἔοικε, μένος δʼ αἴθωνι σιδήρῳ: “I will go against him, even if his hands are like fire, / if his hands are like fire, and his might like gleaming iron” (20.371–372). ἔοικα with the dative is “be like”, and χεῖρας and μένος are accusatives of respect: like fire in his hands, like gleaming iron in his might (see [[menos]]). Hector says the half-verse twice. Walter Leaf notes that such a repetition of a whole phrase is found again only at 22.128 and 23.642 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.371): in Hector's thought of a girl and a young man chatting (παρθένος ἠΐθεός τε, / παρθένος ἠΐθεός τʼ, 22.127–128), and in Nestor's memory of the twin charioteers who beat him at the games for Amarynceus (ἔμπεδον ἡνιόχευεν, / ἔμπεδον ἡνιόχευʼ, 23.641–642; see [[epanalepsis]]). The repetition holds the picture in front of the Trojans while Hector says that he will face it.

The fire is Achilles' own. Athena kindled a flame from his head at the ditch (18.205–227; see [[the-beacon-fires]]), his eyes shone like the blaze of fire when he armed (19.365–366; see [[eyes-like-fire]]), and before the book ends the narrator likens him to a wondrous fire raging through a dry mountain forest (20.490–494; see [[forest-fire]]). The hands that Hector calls fire are spattered with gore in the book's last verse (20.503; see [[irresistible-hands]]). The iron is αἴθων, “gleaming”, as the iron of the chariot-maker's tool and of the payment for Lemnian wine (4.485, 7.473; see [[tawny]]); and iron is the Iliad's image of what does not yield, as when Apollo tells the Trojans that the Argives' flesh is not stone or iron (4.510–511; see [[iron]]).

## In context

Achilles has called on the Achaeans to go man against man, saying that he will go right through the Trojan ranks, and that no Trojan who comes near his spear will rejoice (20.353–363). Hector answers with a call of his own (20.364–372; see [[exhortation]] and [[hector]]): “High-hearted Trojans, do not fear the son of Peleus. / I too could fight even with the immortals in words, / but with the spear it is hard, since they are far stronger than we are. / Nor will Achilles put fulfillment to all his words, / but one he will fulfill, and another he will cut short halfway” (20.366–370). The speech sets words against the spear, and the simile is part of it: Hector grants Achilles hands of fire and promises to meet them. The Trojans raise their spears, and the war cry rises (20.373–374). But Phoebus Apollo stands beside Hector and tells him not to step out as a champion against Achilles, and Hector goes back into the crowd of men, “afraid, when he heard the voice of the god as he spoke” (20.375–380; see [[apollo]]). Only when Achilles kills his brother Polydorus does he come against him, “like a flame” (20.419–423; see [[like-a-flame]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Fire is a common likeness for a fighter's fury, but nowhere else for a man's hands. Hector himself is “like a flame” in *Iliad* 13, 17 and 18 (see [[like-a-flame]]), the armies fight “like blazing fire” (see [[like-blazing-fire]]), and Agamemnon falls on the Trojans like a fire in uncut woodland (11.155–159; see [[forest-fire]]). When Achilles comes on against Hector in Book 22, the bronze about him shines like the gleam of a blazing fire or of the rising sun (22.134–135), and the dying Hector tells him that the spirit in his breast is iron (σιδήρεος ἐν φρεσὶ θυμός, 22.357).
