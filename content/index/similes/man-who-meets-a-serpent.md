## The simile

ὡς δʼ ὅτε τίς τε δράκοντα ἰδὼν παλίνορσος ἀπέστη / οὔρεος ἐν βήσσῃς, ὑπό τε τρόμος ἔλλαβε γυῖα, / ἂψ δʼ ἀνεχώρησεν, ὦχρός τέ μιν εἷλε παρειάς: “as when a man sees a serpent and starts back in the glades of a mountain, and trembling seizes his limbs, and he backs away, and pallor takes his cheeks”. δράκων is Homer's great snake (see [[serpent]]). παλίνορσος, “starting back” (πάλιν, “back”, and ὄρνυμαι, “rush”), occurs nowhere else in Homer, and neither does ὦχρος, “pallor”. βῆσσαι are mountain glens. “Trembling seized his limbs” (τρόμος ἔλλαβε γυῖα) is a formula of sudden fear.

The tenor: ὣς αὖτις καθʼ ὅμιλον ἔδυ Τρώων ἀγερώχων / δείσας Ἀτρέος υἱὸν Ἀλέξανδρος θεοειδής, “so back into the crowd of the proud Trojans he slipped, godlike Alexander, in fear of the son of Atreus”. The point of likeness is recoil: a sudden sight, the body's fear, a step back. The simile says nothing of the serpent except that it is seen. The danger lies in the man's reaction, in his trembling limbs and pale cheeks, and three verbs of withdrawal (ἀπέστη, ἀνεχώρησεν, ἔδυ) carry him back.

## In context

{{quote:c6707bf8-4db7-5da5-9c28-ecc33e6edbd2}}

The two similes of the meeting form a pair (see [[lion-on-a-carcass]]). They open with the same half-line, τὸν δʼ ὡς οὖν ἐνόησεν (“and when … caught sight of him”, 3.21 and 3.30), and both are set in the mountains. Menelaus sees his enemy and rejoices like a hungry lion; Alexander sees his and recoils like a man before a serpent. The injured husband is given the beast's part, and the man who wronged his host is given the man's.

Alexander had come out as a champion, striding in front of the throng and challenging all the best of the Argives (3.16–22). He goes back in the words of a formula of retreat, “into the crowd of his companions, avoiding doom” (3.32). The line is used six more times in the Iliad, of warriors who draw back after blows have been exchanged (for example 13.566, 16.817); Alexander draws back before any blow is struck. Hector's rebuke follows at once, and it begins with his brother's looks: “Evil Paris, best in looks” (3.39; see [[paris]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same line that opens this scene, “and when godlike Alexander caught sight of him”, introduces him again in Book 11, where the sight of an enemy sends him not back but to his bow: he shoots Eurypylus in the thigh (11.581–584). The serpent returns in a simile of the opposite kind when Hector waits for Achilles at the Scaean gates, “as a mountain serpent waits for a man at its hole”, full of poison and anger, coiling and glaring terribly (22.93–97). There the hero is the serpent, and he stands his ground, until Achilles comes near and he runs. The formula of trembling limbs is used of Priam when Iris appears to him (24.170), and of the beggar Irus as he is brought out to fight Odysseus (Odyssey 18.88). The only other word of the family of ὦχρος in Homer is in Odysseus' praise of Neoptolemus, who never went pale in the Wooden Horse (ὠχρήσαντα, Odyssey 11.528–530).

## Outside Homer

Virgil remembered the simile in the sack of Troy. The Greek Androgeos, finding himself in the dark among Trojans, recoils like a man who has trodden on a snake in rough briars (*Aeneid* 2.379–382).
