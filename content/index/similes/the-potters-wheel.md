## The simile

{{quote:298ed1c9-a647-54e3-81e5-852c2fbdda52}}

οἳ δʼ ὁτὲ μὲν θρέξασκον ἐπισταμένοισι πόδεσσι / ῥεῖα μάλʼ, ὡς ὅτε τις τροχὸν ἄρμενον ἐν παλάμῃσιν / ἑζόμενος κεραμεὺς πειρήσεται, αἴ κε θέῃσιν: “And now they would run on skillful feet / very easily, as when a wheel that fits his hands / is tried by a potter sitting at it, to see whether it will run” (18.599–601). θρέξασκον, “they would run”, is iterative, a thing done again and again, and ἐπισταμένοισι πόδεσσι are “skillful feet”, feet that know their business (ἐπίσταμαι, “know how”). The τροχός is a wheel, of a chariot elsewhere in the Iliad (6.42, 23.394); ἄρμενον ἐν παλάμῃσιν, “that fits his hands”, belong together, as Walter Leaf notes, comparing the axe that Calypso gives Odysseus, a great axe “that fitted his hands” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.600; *Odyssey* 5.234). κεραμεύς, “potter”, occurs only here in Homer. The potter sits, ἑζόμενος, and spins the wheel to test it, πειρήσεται, “to see whether it will run”; θέῃσιν is the verb of running, so that the wheel runs as the dancers do. Leaf notes that πειρήσεται with a direct object is unique (on 18.601).

The point of likeness is the circling, smooth and quick and without effort. Then the dance changes: ἄλλοτε δʼ αὖ θρέξασκον ἐπὶ στίχας ἀλλήλοισι, “and now again they would run in ranks toward one another” (18.602). Leaf finds the comparison very striking for lines of dancers wheeling round along the circles of a maze, and supposes that from time to time they left the maze and danced in long lines facing each other; ἐπὶ στίχας, he notes, is used elsewhere only of soldiers (on 18.601; see the translation's note on 18.590–606).

The figures on the Shield are often said to be like the real thing: the gods are tall “as gods should be” (18.518), the fighters fight “like living mortals” (18.539), the golden field “looked like plowed land” (18.548), and the dancing floor is like the one Daedalus made at Cnossus (18.590–592). This is the only comparison on the Shield that builds a scene of its own, and its scene is a craftsman at work, set in a picture made by the craftsman god.

## In context

The dance is the last scene of the Shield before the river Oceanus at its rim (18.607–608; see [[oceanus]]). Hephaestus inlays a dancing floor like the one that Daedalus fashioned for Ariadne in wide Cnossus (18.590–592; see [[the-dancing-floor-of-ariadne]], [[daedalus]] and [[ariadne]]). Young men and maidens “who would bring many oxen” dance holding one another's hands by the wrist, the girls in fine linen and garlands, the young men in tunics shining softly with oil and with golden knives on silver straps (18.593–598; see [[dance]]). After the running come the onlookers: “And a great throng stood around the lovely dance, / delighting in it”, with a divine singer and two tumblers leading the song and dance, whirling in the midst (18.603–606; see the translation's note on 18.604–606).

## Outside Homer

Strabo, reporting that the historian Ephorus counted the potter's wheel among the inventions of the Scythian sage Anacharsis, with the bellows and the two-fluked anchor, objects: how could the wheel be his invention, when Homer, who was older, knew it? (*Geography* 7.3.9). Leaf, citing the same passage, adds that remains of early Greek pottery show that the wheel was known in pre-Mycenaean times, a very ancient invention even to the oldest epic poets (on 18.600). Hesiod names the potter first among those who are jealous of their own kind, “and potter is jealous of potter, and craftsman of craftsman” (*Works and Days* 25; see [[potter-against-potter]]).
