## The simile

ἔγνω, φράσσατο δʼ ἵππον ἀριπρεπέα προὔχοντα, / ὃς τὸ μὲν ἄλλο τόσον φοῖνιξ ἦν, ἐν δὲ μετώπῳ / λευκὸν σῆμα τέτυκτο περίτροχον ἠΰτε μήνη: “he knew him; and he made out a horse, conspicuous, in the lead, / which was bay in all the rest, but on its forehead / there was a white mark, round like the moon” (23.453–455). μήνη is the moon, a word akin to μήν, the month (see [[moon]]). περίτροχος is “round”, circular, and Walter Leaf concludes that the moon meant must be the full moon, though the crescent seems to us the more natural likeness for such a mark (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.455). φοῖνιξ, “bay”, describes a horse only here in Homer; elsewhere it is the purple with which a woman stains ivory (4.141). σῆμα is a mark, as on a lot (7.189; see the translation's note on 23.326–333 and the key-word row for τύμβος / σῆμα).

The comparison makes the horse known at a distance. The Argives sit in the gathering and watch the horses fly on in the dust (23.448–449), but Idomeneus sits outside it, highest of all, at a lookout, and he knows the charioteer by his shout and the horse by its mark (23.450–453; see [[idomeneus]]). The mark tells him that the leader is not one of Eumelus' mares, which were in front at the turn, and he guesses that the leader is Diomedes (23.456–472).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The moon is the Iliad's likeness for a round, white light. When Achilles takes up his new shield, “from it, far off, came a blaze as of the moon” (19.373–374; see [[the-fire-seen-by-sailors]]), and the Trojan watch-fires shine like the stars around the bright moon (8.555–559; see [[stars-around-the-moon]]).
