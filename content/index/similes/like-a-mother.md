## The simile

ὢ πόποι ἦ μʼ ἔβλαψε θεὰ πόδας, ἣ τὸ πάρος περ / μήτηρ ὣς Ὀδυσῆϊ παρίσταται ἠδʼ ἐπαρήγει: “Ah, truly the goddess hindered my feet, she who always before / stands by Odysseus like a mother and helps him” (23.782–783). The comparison is a complaint, and a joke against himself: “So he spoke, and they all laughed heartily at him” (23.784). It is also true. Odysseus has just prayed to Athena, “Hear me, goddess, come as a good helper to my feet”, and she made his limbs light, and made Ajax slip where the dung of the slaughtered oxen lay (23.768–777; see [[athena]], [[odysseus]] and [[cattle]]). παρίσταται, “stands by”, is the word of Odysseus' own prayer to her in the night raid: “Hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, you who always / stand by me in all my toils” (10.278–279), and in the Odyssey Nestor tells Telemachus that he never saw the gods love anyone so openly as Pallas Athena stood by Odysseus (*Odyssey* 3.221–222). τὸ πάρος, “always before”, makes it a habit: the help is not new, and the loser knows it.

## Elsewhere in Homer

Athena is a mother in an earlier simile too: she turned Pandarus' arrow from the flesh of Menelaus “as a mother / brushes a fly from her child, when he lies in sweet sleep” (4.130–131; see [[mother-and-fly]]). Teucer goes back to Ajax's shield “like a child under its mother” (8.271; see [[like-a-child-under-its-mother]]), and Achilles likens himself to a mother bird who brings her chicks every morsel she finds (9.323–327; see [[the-bird-and-her-chicks]]). Here the likeness is turned to complaint, and the mother's favorite wins the race.
