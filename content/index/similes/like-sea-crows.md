## The simile

{{quote:4a65fb39-9ebe-523d-b5fb-040d55a3ce9f}}

οἱ δὲ κορώνῃσιν ἴκελοι περὶ νῆα μέλαιναν / κύμασιν ἐμφορέοντο, θεὸς δʼ ἀποαίνυτο νόστον: “And like sea crows around the black ship / they were carried on the waves, and the god took away their homecoming” (*Odyssey* 12.418–419). κορώνη is the crow. The crows of the sea are named once before, among the birds that roost in the wood around Calypso's cave, “owls and hawks and long-tongued crows / of the sea, whose concern is the works of the sea” (*Odyssey* 5.66–67; see [[birds]]). W. W. Merry refers here to his note on that verse (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.418), where he renders κορῶναι εἰνάλιαι “cormorants”, whose dark plumage probably gained for them the popular name of “sea-crows”; but there is nearly as much uncertainty about them as about the owls of the same verse, for the scholia identify them with the αἴθυιαι, the diving birds, and Hesychius with the λάροι, the gulls (on 5.66; see [[like-a-diving-bird]] and [[gull]]). ἴκελος is the epic form of εἴκελος, “like”. ἐμφορέοντο, “were carried”, is found in Homer only in this verse and its repetition. ἀποαίνυτο, “took away”, keeps the final vowel of ἀπο- before αἴνυμαι, which once began with a digamma, as in ἀποαίνυμαι (*Iliad* 13.262), where other places elide it, ἀπαινύμενον (*Iliad* 11.582, 17.85; Merry on 12.419).

The likeness is of what is seen from the ship. The men are black points on the water around the black hull, rising and falling with the waves as sea birds ride the swell; they do not swim, they are carried, ἐμφορέοντο. The next half-verse says what the likeness leaves out: “and the god took away their homecoming”. It is the end announced in the proem, where the companions perished, “fools, who ate up the cattle of Hyperion the Sun; / and he took away from them the day of their homecoming” (*Odyssey* 1.8–9; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[nostos]]).

## In context

When the companions had feasted six days on the cattle of the Sun, the wind dropped and they put out to sea; out of sight of land Zeus set a dark cloud over the ship, the West Wind broke the forestays, and the falling mast smashed the helmsman's skull, so that he fell from the deck “like a diver” (*Odyssey* 12.397–414; see [[like-a-diver]] and [[forestays]]). Then: “And at the same moment Zeus thundered and hurled his thunderbolt at the ship; / and she shook all over, struck by the thunderbolt of Zeus, / and was filled with sulfur, and my companions fell out of the ship” (*Odyssey* 12.415–417; see [[thunderbolt]] and [[zeus]]). Zeus keeps the promise he made to the Sun, to strike their swift ship with his thunderbolt and shatter it in the middle of the wine-dark sea (*Odyssey* 12.385–388; see [[helios]]). Odysseus alone stays aboard, pacing the ship until the sea tears her sides from the keel (*Odyssey* 12.420–421; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). The two likenesses, the diver and the sea crows, are the last pictures of his crew.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The verses return whole in the false tale that Odysseus tells Eumaeus, where the men in the sea are the crew of a Phoenician ship that Zeus wrecks off Crete (*Odyssey* 14.301–309; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]). The real wreck is told more briefly elsewhere, by Calypso to Hermes and by Odysseus to Arete: Zeus struck and shattered the swift ship with a gleaming thunderbolt, “There all his other noble companions perished” (*Odyssey* 5.131–133; 7.249–251). The birds of the sea are likenesses of gods in the fifth book: Hermes skims the waves like a gull, and Ino rises from the sea like a diving bird (*Odyssey* 5.51–53, 5.337; see [[the-gull]] and [[like-a-diving-bird]]). Here the sea birds are drowning men.
