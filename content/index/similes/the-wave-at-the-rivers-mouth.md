## The simile

{{quote:cd41fa76-e8a5-5d55-a22c-32643d11ce75}}

Τρῶες δὲ προὔτυψαν ἀολλέες· ἦρχε δʼ ἄρʼ Ἕκτωρ, “And the Trojans drove forward in a body, and Hector led them” (17.262): the verse that has brought on the Trojan charge twice before (13.136, 15.306). The simile gives the sound of it. ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἐπὶ προχοῇσι διιπετέος ποταμοῖο / βέβρυχεν μέγα κῦμα ποτὶ ῥόον, “And as at the mouth of a river fallen from Zeus / a great wave roars against the stream” (17.263–264). προχοαί are the outpourings of a river, its mouth; Odysseus swims into the mouth of a river on Scheria (ἐς ποταμοῦ προχοάς, *Odyssey* 5.453). διιπετής, “fallen from Zeus”, is said only of rivers, as fed by the rain of the sky (see [[fallen-from-zeus]] and the translation's note on 16.174). βέβρυχεν, “roars”, is used of waves and of the wave-beaten rock (*Odyssey* 5.412, 12.242), and of warriors roaring as they die (13.393, 16.486; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.264). The sea comes in at the river's mouth and meets the river coming down: two currents, and a great noise where they collide.

ἀμφὶ δέ τʼ ἄκραι / ἠϊόνες βοόωσιν ἐρευγομένης ἁλὸς ἔξω, “and all around the headlands / of the shore resound as the salt sea belches out” (17.264–265). βοάω, “shout”, is the verb of men's battle cries, here given to the shores. ἐρεύγομαι, “belch”, is said of the sea spewing against a rock (15.621; *Odyssey* 5.403, 5.438), and Leaf thinks it may mean no more than “roar” (on 17.265). He takes ἔξω as “beyond its own limits, on to the land”, and notes a variant, ἄκραι ἠϊόνος, “the headlands of the shore”, which a similar verse of *Iliad* 4 supports (ἀμφὶ δέ τʼ ἄκρας, 4.425); with the common reading he renders “the shores echo to their farthest points”.

The tenor: τόσσῃ ἄρα Τρῶες ἰαχῇ ἴσαν, “with so great a shout the Trojans came on” (17.266). The point of likeness is the noise of an army's onset. Against it the Achaeans hold still: they “stood around the son of Menoetius with one heart, / fenced with their bronze-fitted shields” (17.267–268).

Leaf reports two ancient stories about the simile. The scholia say that Solon burned his poems in despair of ever matching it, and they tell the same story of Plato, with more reason, Leaf observes, since Solon's poems survived; and the ancients held that the river was the Nile, for which, he says, there is no authority (on 17.263).

## In context

Menelaus has called the Achaean leaders to the body, and Ajax son of Oileus, Idomeneus and Meriones have come (17.246–261). The Trojans charge with Hector at their head, and Zeus pours a thick mist about the Achaeans' helmets, since he did not hate Patroclus while he lived (17.262–273). The Trojans push the Achaeans back and drag at the body, until Ajax rallies them and scatters the Trojan battalions like a wild boar scattering dogs and young men in the mountains (17.274–287; see [[like-a-boar]] and [[trojans]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The noise of an army is the noise of the sea in other similes. The Achaeans stream back to the assembly with a roar like a wave that crashes on a long beach (2.209–210; see [[surf-on-the-beach]]); they shout like a wave driven by the South Wind against a jutting crag (2.394–397; see [[wave-on-the-headland]]); and when Poseidon and Hector bring the armies together, their shout is louder than the sea's wave roaring on the shore under the North Wind (14.394–395; see [[louder-than-sea-fire-and-wind]]). In silence the Danaan battalions advance like waves that rise at sea and break on the land, curling around the headlands (4.422–427; see [[waves-on-the-shore]]), and they hold against Hector like a sheer rock by the sea that withstands the winds and “the swollen waves that belch against it” (15.618–622; see [[the-rock-by-the-sea]]).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle quotes two words of the simile, ἠιόνες βοόωσιν, “the shores roar”, in his chapter on poetic diction, to show what becomes of a verse when a standard word replaces an unusual one: said with the standard word, it would be “the shores shriek” (*Poetics* 1458b31; see [[the-shores-roar]]).
