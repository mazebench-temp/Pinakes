## The simile

{{quote:07bda45b-34a7-55df-b73d-53afb1aba7b0}}

δαῖέ οἱ ἐκ κόρυθός τε καὶ ἀσπίδος ἀκάματον πῦρ / ἀστέρʼ ὀπωρινῷ ἐναλίγκιον: the goddess “kindled from his helmet and shield a tireless fire, like the star of late summer”. ὀπώρη is the season when fruit ripens, the end of summer and the beginning of autumn, and ὀπωρινός means “of ὀπώρη”. ἐναλίγκιος is “like”. The star is not named. It is usually identified with Sirius from the longer simile of *Iliad* 22, where Achilles, running over the plain, gleams (παμφαίνοντα) like “the star that comes in late summer” (ὀπώρης), the brightest of all, which men call “the dog of Orion”; it is a sign of evil and brings much fever to wretched mortals (22.26–31). Sirius rises before the sun in the hottest weeks of the year, after a long time out of sight.

λαμπρὸν παμφαίνῃσι: it “shines out bright”, παμφαίνω being to shine all over, a word for stars and for polished bronze. λελουμένος ὠκεανοῖο, “having bathed in Oceanus”: the stars rise from the river that runs round the earth and set into it again. Only the Bear has no share in the baths of Oceanus (λοετρῶν Ὠκεανοῖο), because it never sets (18.489; Odyssey 5.275; see [[oceanus]]). The star in the simile has just come up out of the water.

One verb frames the simile, δαίω, which means to kindle or set ablaze: δαῖε before it (5.4), and after it τοῖόν οἱ πῦρ δαῖεν ἀπὸ κρατός τε καὶ ὤμων, “such was the fire she kindled from his head and shoulders” (5.7). The point of likeness is brightness. The fire that plays about the hero is as bright as the brightest star. But the star of late summer is also the star of heat and fever, and for the Trojans the light on Diomedes' helmet means danger.

## In context

*Iliad* 5 begins with a gift. Athena gives Diomedes “might and daring, so that he would stand out among all the Argives and win noble fame” (5.2–3). μένος, the might a god puts into a man, and θάρσος, “daring”, are her gifts (see [[menos]]); ἔκδηλος, “standing out, conspicuous”, and κλέος ἐσθλόν, “noble fame”, are their purpose (see [[kleos]]). The fire is the gift made visible, and it marks him out at once. Then she drives him “into the middle, where the most men were thrown into confusion” (5.8).

It is the first image of the day that ancient readers called his aristeia (see [[the-aristeia-of-diomedes]]). The fire is not mentioned again, and no Trojan speaks of it. Pandarus, not sure whether he is facing a god, knows Diomedes by his shield, his helmet and his horses (5.181–183).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Athena kindles the same fire on Achilles in *Iliad* 18. She puts the aegis around his shoulders, sets a golden cloud about his head and kindles from it a blazing flame, which rises to the sky like the beacon fires of a besieged island (18.203–214). The Trojan charioteers are struck with terror when they see “the tireless fire” (ἀκάματον πῦρ; see [[tireless]]) burning above his head, “the gray-eyed goddess Athena kindled it” (18.225–227). The words are those of 5.4.

Stars are the Iliad's image for the flash of armor. When Achilles arms, his helmet shines “like a star” (19.381), and when he runs toward Troy he gleams like the dog of Orion itself, bright and baneful (22.26–32); the point of his spear shines like the evening star, the fairest star in the sky (22.317–320; see [[the-evening-star]]). Hector moves among the ranks like a baneful star (οὔλιος ἀστήρ) that now shines out of the clouds and now hides in them again (11.62–65). The robe that Hecuba chooses for Athena “shone like a star” (6.295). The star of Book 5 is the first of them, and in this book the hero the goddess sets ablaze is Diomedes. See also [[shooting-star]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod names the star of the hot season: in summer, when the cicada sings in the trees, “Sirius parches head and knees” (*Works and Days* 587). Virgil joins the two Homeric pictures when Aeneas comes back to the battle. The crest blazes on his head and his golden shield-boss pours out fire, as comets glow blood-red on a clear night, or as Sirius rises, bringing thirst and sickness to wretched mortals, and saddens the sky with its baleful light (*Aeneid* 10.270–275).
