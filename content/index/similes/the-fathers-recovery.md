## The simile

{{quote:0487ef80-3639-5f23-b3df-80fd974aaaac}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἂν ἀσπάσιος βίοτος παίδεσσι φανήῃ / πατρός, ὃς ἐν νούσῳ κεῖται κρατέρʼ ἄλγεα πάσχων, / δηρὸν τηκόμενος, στυγερὸς δέ οἱ ἔχραε δαίμων, / ἀσπάσιον δʼ ἄρα τόν γε θεοὶ κακότητος ἔλυσαν: “And as when the life of a father appears welcome to his children, / a father who lies in sickness, suffering bitter pains, / long wasting away, and a hateful god has attacked him, / and welcome it is, when the gods free him from his misery” (*Odyssey* 5.394–397). The simile says “welcome” three times, ἀσπάσιος of the father's life, ἀσπάσιον of the gods' release, and ἀσπαστόν of the land in the tenor: ὣς Ὀδυσεῖ ἀσπαστὸν ἐείσατο γαῖα καὶ ὕλη, “so welcome did the land and the woods appear to Odysseus” (*Odyssey* 5.398).

βίοτος is life itself, as when Athena tells Telemachus to listen for news of πατρὸς βίοτον, “your father's life” (*Odyssey* 1.287; see the translation's table of key words). φανήῃ, “appears”, is the word, W. W. Merry notes, for the reappearance of what had been lost: the companions who escaped the Cyclops “appeared welcome” to their friends (ἀσπάσιοι … φάνημεν, *Odyssey* 9.466), and the timbers come up again out of Charybdis (*Odyssey* 12.441). So here the father's life had seemed lost beyond hope (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.394). The sickness here is a god's doing, and Merry recalls the Cyclopes' words to Polyphemus, that there is no escaping a sickness sent by great Zeus (*Odyssey* 9.411, a plain rendering; Merry on 5.394). ἔχραε, “has attacked”, is a gnomic aorist, the verb of the suitors who have “fallen upon” Penelope (ἐπέχραον, *Odyssey* 2.50; Merry on 5.396). Merry adds that human skill was not supposed in early times to reach inward ailments, and refers to Plato's remark that the sons of Asclepius did not use the present medicine that nurses diseases before the time of Herodicus (*Republic* 406a; on 5.394). The release is the gods' as the illness was a god's: θεοὶ κακότητος ἔλυσαν, “the gods free him from his misery” (*Odyssey* 5.397). κακότης, “misery”, is Poseidon's word for what he means to give Odysseus, “I will yet give him his fill of misery” (*Odyssey* 5.290; see the translation's table of key words).

The text prints κεῖται, “lies”; Merry's text has the subjunctive κῆται, after Hermann (Merry's critical note on 5.395; see the translation's note on 5.395).

The point of likeness is the joy of a life given back. The land is the father's life, seen again when it had seemed lost, and Odysseus has the children's part. It also looks beyond the moment: Odysseus is himself a father, lost and longed for, whose return his son has gone in search of (see [[telemachus]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).

## In context

Athena has bound the other winds and roused the North Wind to break the waves before Odysseus, “until he should come among the Phaeacians” (*Odyssey* 5.382–387; see [[athena]] and [[boreas]]). “There for two nights and two days on the swollen wave / he wandered, and many times his heart foresaw destruction” (*Odyssey* 5.388–389). On the third day the wind ceases and there is a windless calm, and he sees the land near at hand, “with a sharp look ahead, as he was lifted by a great wave” (*Odyssey* 5.390–393). Merry explains that the wind had fallen but the ground swell had not, so that Odysseus could only catch a glimpse of the coast as he rose out of the trough of the sea; a scholium says that when the winds have ceased the sea often goes on rolling with a dumb wave, κύματι κωφῷ, the words of the Iliad's heaving sea (on 5.393; *Iliad* 14.16; see [[the-heaving-sea]]). He compares Palinurus in Virgil, who after three nights in the sea saw Italy “high on the top of a wave”, *summa sublimis ab unda* (*Aeneid* 6.357; Merry on 5.393; see [[virgils-palinurus]]).

After the simile Odysseus swims on, “eager to set his feet on the land” (*Odyssey* 5.399), and hears the roar of the sea on the reefs (*Odyssey* 5.400–405; see [[shore]]). The welcome land is a coast of sharp crags and sheer rock, and he will reach it only at the mouth of a river (*Odyssey* 5.406–453; see [[the-octopus]] and [[the-river-of-scheria]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Odyssey turns the likeness around at its end. When Penelope at last knows her husband, he weeps holding her, and he is as welcome to her as the land to swimmers whose ship Poseidon has wrecked on the sea, “driven by the wind and the swollen wave”; few of them escape the gray sea to the land, the brine caked thick on their skin, and gladly they set foot on the land, having escaped misery (*Odyssey* 23.233–240, a plain rendering). The words are those of this book: ἀσπάσιος … φανήῃ, κύματι πηγῷ, κακότητα. In the fifth book the land is welcome to Odysseus; in the twenty-third Odysseus is the land, and Penelope the swimmer who reaches it (see [[penelope]]).

In the Iliad, Hector and Paris come out to the Trojans as welcome as a fair wind to sailors worn out at the oars (*Iliad* 7.4–7; see [[the-fair-wind]]). In the underworld Agamemnon tells Odysseus that he thought to come home “welcome to my children and my slaves” (ἀσπάσιος παίδεσσιν, *Odyssey* 11.431, a plain rendering), and was killed in his own house.
