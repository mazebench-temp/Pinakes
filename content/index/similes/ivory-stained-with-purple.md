## The simile

ὡς δʼ ὅτε τίς τʼ ἐλέφαντα γυνὴ φοίνικι μιήνῃ / Μῃονὶς ἠὲ Κάειρα παρήϊον ἔμμεναι ἵππων: “as when a woman stains ivory with purple, a Maeonian or a Carian, to be a cheekpiece for horses”. ἐλέφας is ivory ([[ivory]]), and φοῖνιξ, “crimson, purple-red”, is the dye ([[purple]]). μιαίνω is “stain, dye”, and in Homer it is also the verb for fouling with blood and dust. A παρήϊον is an ornament for the side of a horse's head; the same word means a cheek (16.159). The dyer is a woman of Maeonia or Caria, lands of Asia Minor whose men fight for Troy (2.864–875; see [[maeonians]] and [[carians]]).

The simile follows the thing after it is made: κεῖται δʼ ἐν θαλάμῳ, πολέες τέ μιν ἠρήσαντο / ἱππῆες φορέειν· βασιλῆϊ δὲ κεῖται ἄγαλμα, / ἀμφότερον κόσμός θʼ ἵππῳ ἐλατῆρί τε κῦδος, “it lies in a chamber, and many have prayed for it, horsemen who long to carry it, but it lies there as a delight for a king, both an ornament for the horse and a glory to the driver”. A θάλαμος is an inner room or storeroom. ἄγαλμα is a thing that gives pride and pleasure (ἀγάλλομαι, “exult in”); in Homer it is a treasure or an offering, and only later a statue. κόσμος is ornament, and κῦδος glory ([[kydos]]).

The tenor: τοῖοί τοι Μενέλαε μιάνθην αἵματι μηροὶ / εὐφυέες κνῆμαί τε ἰδὲ σφυρὰ κάλʼ ὑπένερθε, “so, Menelaus, your thighs were stained with blood, your shapely thighs, and your shins, and your fine ankles beneath”. The dyer's verb, μιαίνω, returns for the blood. The point of likeness is color, dark red on white skin, and the eye follows the blood down the leg from the thighs to the ankles. The simile also gives the stain a value. The dyed ivory is precious, desired by many and kept for a king, and Menelaus is a king: the wound is shown as an ornament.

## In context

{{quote:dae55fa8-05d3-5e31-a538-75e6553ebeb3}}

The arrow has gone through the belt, the corselet and the band, and grazed the outermost flesh (4.134–139; see [[mother-and-fly]]): “at once the dark blood flowed from the wound” (4.140). It is the first wound that the Iliad describes. The narrator speaks to Menelaus again (4.146), as he did when the gods did not forget him (4.127), and the address frames the simile. Then the watchers react. Agamemnon shudders “when he saw the black blood flowing down from the wound”, and Menelaus shudders too, until he sees that the binding and the barbs are outside, and his spirit is gathered back into his breast (4.148–152). The simile holds the moment between the wound and its effect. For seven lines the blood is beautiful before it is frightening.

The blood is called κελαινεφές, “dark” (4.140), and μέλαν, “black” (4.149); the ivory is dyed with φοῖνιξ, a dark red. κῦδος, the glory the ornament brings to its driver, is the word Athena used to tempt Pandarus, who would win “thanks and glory from all the Trojans” for the shot (4.95). And the woman who makes the ornament comes from the Trojan side of the sea. Two images of women frame the wounding. The goddess turns the arrow like a mother keeping a fly from her child (4.130–131), and the blood stains the leg as a craftswoman dyes ivory.

## Elsewhere in Homer

Ivory is rare in the Iliad. The only other ivory is on the reins, “white with ivory”, that fall into the dust when Antilochus strikes the charioteer Mydon (5.580–583). In the Odyssey it belongs to rich houses. Telemachus marvels at the bronze, gold, amber, silver and ivory in the palace of Menelaus at Sparta (Odyssey 4.71–75). Penelope's chair is inlaid with ivory and silver (19.55–57), Athena makes her “whiter than sawn ivory” (18.196), and false dreams come through a gate of ivory (19.562–567).

The verb of the dyer is the verb of defilement. The plumes of Achilles' helmet are fouled with blood and dust when Apollo strikes it from Patroclus' head (μιάνθησαν, 16.795–797), and the manes of Achilles' horses are fouled as they stand mourning Patroclus (17.439). Blood and ornament meet again in the death of Euphorbus, whose hair, like the Graces' hair, is wet with blood, with the locks bound in gold and silver (17.51–52). Agamemnon's wound in Book 11 is also given a woman's image: the pain is like the sharp pangs that the goddesses of childbirth send on a woman in labor (11.269–272).

## Outside Homer

Virgil turned the image into a blush. When Lavinia hears her mother plead with Turnus, the color floods her face “as if someone had stained Indian ivory with blood-red purple”, or as white lilies redden when they are mixed with roses (*Aeneid* 12.64–69). The Latin verb, *violaverit*, “has violated”, keeps the double sense of Homer's μιαίνω, to dye and to defile. The wound of a king has become the blush of a girl.
