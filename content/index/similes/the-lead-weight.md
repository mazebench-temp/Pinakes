## The simile

{{quote:4b341ac9-3c59-5192-ba21-4e44990a9310}}

ἣ δὲ μολυβδαίνῃ ἰκέλη ἐς βυσσὸν ὄρουσεν, / ἥ τε κατʼ ἀγραύλοιο βοὸς κέρας ἐμβεβαυῖα / ἔρχεται ὠμηστῇσιν ἐπʼ ἰχθύσι κῆρα φέρουσα: “And she sped down to the depths like a lead weight, / which, mounted on the horn of an ox of the fields, / goes down bringing doom to the fishes that eat raw flesh” (24.80–82). μολύβδαινα, the lead weight of a fishing line, is a derivative of μόλυβδος, “lead”, which Homer elsewhere calls μόλιβος (11.237; see [[like-lead]]); both the weight and βυσσός, “the depth”, are found only here in Homer. ὄρουσεν, “sped”, is the verb of a man who rushes first against the enemy (11.92) and of a spear that “had sped in vain from his strong hand” (13.505 = 16.615). ἐμβεβαυῖα, “mounted on”, is the perfect participle of ἐμβαίνω, “step on, stand on”: the lead sits on the horn.

βοὸς ἀγραύλοιο, “of an ox of the fields”, ends the verse four times in the Iliad: of oxhide (10.155, 23.684), of an ox struck behind the horns (17.521), and of the horn of the ox that Ajax son of Oileus wins in the footrace (23.780). The Odyssey parts the two words, with ἀγραύλοιο at the end of the verse, for the ox a lion has eaten (*Odyssey* 22.403) and for the angler's horn in Scylla's simile, βοὸς κέρας ἀγραύλοιο (*Odyssey* 12.253). Here the order is turned round, ἀγραύλοιο βοὸς κέρας. What the horn was is not certain. Walter Leaf reports the usual explanation, given by Aristarchus and, according to Plutarch, by Aristotle: a little tube of horn passed over the line just above the hook, so that the fish could not bite the line through, with molten lead run into it to sink it. He himself thinks that Haskins made it probable that the κέρας is an artificial bait of horn, weighted with lead and drawn through the water to attract the fish by its glitter, such as the South Sea Islanders used, and that this “undoubtedly suits the words best” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.80; see the translation's note on 24.80–82). The English, “mounted on the horn”, leaves the question open.

κῆρα φέρουσα, “bringing doom”, ends the verse of the cranes that fly toward the streams of Oceanus “bringing slaughter and doom to the Pygmy men” (3.6; see [[cranes-and-pygmies]]) and of the Argives who sailed for Troy “bringing slaughter and doom to the Trojans” (2.352). ὠμηστής, “that eat raw flesh”, is said in the Iliad of birds (11.454) and of dogs (22.67), here of the fish, and a little later in the book of Achilles, when Hecuba calls him “that eater of raw flesh” (24.207; see [[eating-raw-flesh]] and [[fish]]).

The point of likeness is the straight, swift drop: a lead weight goes to the bottom by the shortest way and does not turn aside. Iris' epithets are the epithets of wind, ἀελλόπος, “storm-footed” (24.77), and ποδήνεμος, “wind-footed” (24.95; see [[storm-footed]] and [[wind-footed]]); under the sea the poet gives her the weight of lead. The doom that the sinker carries belongs to the fish. At the bottom Iris finds Thetis, who “was weeping for the fate of her noble son, who was to / perish in deep-soiled Troy, far from his native land” (24.85–86).

## In context

Apollo has protested at the defiling of Hector's body, Hera has answered him, and Zeus has decided: the gods will not steal the body, but Thetis is to be called, so that Achilles may accept gifts from Priam and give Hector back (24.33–76; see [[apollo]], [[hera]] and [[zeus]]). Iris rises at once, in the verse with which she rose to stop Hera and Athena in *Iliad* 8 (24.77 = 8.409), and between Samos and rocky Imbros “she leapt into the dark sea; and the water groaned over her” (24.78–79; see [[samothrace]] and [[imbros]]). The simile follows. She finds Thetis in a hollow cave with the sea goddesses around her and gives her Zeus' summons in one verse: “Rise, Thetis; Zeus calls you, whose counsels are imperishable” (24.88). Thetis takes a veil, “dark blue, and no garment was ever blacker than this” (24.94), and the two go up together, Iris leading, while “around them the wave of the sea parted” (24.96), and so to Olympus, where Hera puts a golden cup in Thetis' hand (24.97–102; see [[thetis]] and [[iris]]). Iris has one more errand in the book, to Priam (24.143–188).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Iris' one other simile is also of a downward flight. Sent by Zeus to Poseidon, she goes from Ida to Ilios: “As when snow or hail flies from the clouds, / cold, under the rush of the North Wind, born of the clear sky, / so swiftly swift Iris flew in her eagerness” (15.170–172; see [[snow-and-hail]]). In the Odyssey the other messenger of the gods crosses the sea in a likeness taken from fishing: Hermes, coming down from Pieria, skims the waves like a gull that hunts fish in the gulfs of the sea and wets its thick wings in the brine (*Odyssey* 5.50–54). Thetis herself rises from the gray sea “like a mist” (1.359; see [[like-a-mist]]) and goes back to it with a leap (1.532).

The angler's tackle appears in two other similes. Patroclus drags Thestor over the rail of his chariot on his spear as a man on a jutting rock drags a fish out of the sea “with line and gleaming bronze” (16.406–408; see [[the-fisherman]]), and in the Odyssey Scylla lifts Odysseus' companions, gasping, up to the rocks, as an angler on a headland, who casts the horn of an ox of the fields into the sea with a long rod, flings a fish ashore gasping (*Odyssey* 12.251–255). In both of those the catch is a man. Here the likeness is taken not from the angler but from his tackle, and the goddess is the weight.

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates quotes the three verses in the *Ion* as a passage of Homer that the fisherman's art, not the rhapsode's, must judge, and Ion agrees (*Ion* 538c–d; see [[plato-the-fishermans-lead]] and [[plato-ion-the-seers-passages]]). Plato's text has ἵκανεν, “reached”, for ὄρουσεν, ἐμμεμαυῖα, “eager”, for ἐμβεβαυῖα, μετʼ for ἐπʼ, and πῆμα, “harm”, for κῆρα. Leaf notes that Didymus knew ἐμμεμαυῖα and πῆμα as variants and defended πῆμα on the ground that it is absurd to speak of κήρ for fish, a reason Leaf himself calls absurd; ἐμμεμαυῖα, he says, is quite as possible as the eagerness that the poem gives to deadly missiles (on 24.80; 4.126, 11.574, 21.70). It would give the weight the eagerness that Iris herself has in her other simile, μεμαυῖα (15.172).

[[plutarch|Plutarch]], discussing the craft of anglers, quotes the verses of the lead weight and the horn and cites Aristotle for the practice of protecting the lower part of the line, near the hook, with a small hollow piece of horn, so that the fish cannot bite it through; he rejects the view of those who took κέρας here for hair and supposed that the ancients made their lines of ox hair, since, he says, lines were made of horsehair (*De sollertia animalium* 24).
