## The simile

{{quote:58681409-1317-568e-a6f9-185710167e4f}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἀπὸ πλατέος πτυόφιν μεγάλην κατʼ ἀλωὴν / θρῴσκωσιν κύαμοι μελανόχροες ἢ ἐρέβινθοι / πνοιῇ ὕπο λιγυρῇ καὶ λικμητῆρος ἐρωῇ: “As from a broad winnowing shovel across a great threshing floor dark-skinned beans leap, or chickpeas, under the shrill wind and the swing of the winnower” (13.588–590). The πτύον, the winnowing shovel, is named only here (πτυόφιν, with the epic ending -φι), and so are κύαμοι, “beans”, ἐρέβινθοι, “chickpeas”, and the λικμητήρ, the winnower (see the translation's note on 13.588–590). The ἀλωή is the threshing floor, as in the winnowing simile of Book 5, where men are winnowing (λικμώντων) and the wind carries the chaff (5.499–501; see [[winnowing]]). The winnower throws the threshed crop into the wind with his shovel, and the light chaff blows off while the seeds fall back. Here the seeds themselves fly, hard and small, off the broad blade.

The tenor: ὣς ἀπὸ θώρηκος Μενελάου κυδαλίμοιο / πολλὸν ἀποπλαγχθεὶς ἑκὰς ἔπτατο πικρὸς ὀϊστός, “so from the corselet of glorious Menelaus the bitter arrow glanced far away and flew off” (13.591–592). Helenus' arrow has struck the plate of the corselet (θώρηκος γύαλον, 13.587; see [[corselet]]) and bounces off as the beans bounce off the shovel. The point of likeness is the rebound and its harmlessness: a small hard thing thrown at a broad surface, and flying away from it. The scene is a peaceful one, the work of the harvest, set in the middle of a duel.

## In context

Helenus has killed Deipyrus with a great Thracian sword, and grief seizes Menelaus, who comes at him with threats, brandishing his spear, while Helenus draws his bow (13.576–585; see [[helenus]] and [[menelaus]]). The arrow is shot first and glances off. Then Menelaus' spear goes through the hand that holds the bow and pins it to the bow, and Helenus draws back with his hand hanging at his side, “and the ash spear trailed along with it” (13.593–597). Agenor draws the spear out and binds the hand with a sling of sheep's wool (13.598–600; see [[sling]] and [[agenor]]). Menelaus kills Peisander next (13.601–619).

## Elsewhere in Homer

An arrow has struck Menelaus' corselet before. In Book 4 Pandarus' “bitter arrow” was driven through his belt and corselet and only grazed his flesh, because Athena “brushed it away from his flesh, as a mother brushes a fly from her child” (4.127–140; see [[mother-and-fly]]). The threshing floor returns when Achilles' horses trample the dead as oxen tread out white barley on a well-built threshing floor (20.495–497). In the Odyssey the winnowing shovel is the sign of Odysseus' last journey: he is to walk inland with an oar until a stranger calls it a winnowing shovel (ἀθηρηλοιγός, *Odyssey* 11.127–129, 23.274–276).
