## The simile

{{quote:6180bdea-92b7-581f-903d-72b48c8c08c1}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἀνὴρ χαλκεὺς πέλεκυν μέγαν ἠὲ σκέπαρνον / εἰν ὕδατι ψυχρῷ βάπτῃ μεγάλα ἰάχοντα / φαρμάσσων· τὸ γὰρ αὖτε σιδήρου γε κράτος ἐστίν· / ὣς τοῦ σίζʼ ὀφθαλμὸς ἐλαϊνέῳ περὶ μοχλῷ: “And as when a smith dips a great axe or an adze / in cold water, and it hisses loudly, / to temper it; for this in turn is the strength of iron; / so his eye hissed around the stake of olive wood” (*Odyssey* 9.391–394). χαλκεύς is a worker in metal, as the name is given also to the goldsmith Laerces, who comes with his tools to gild the horns of Nestor's heifer (*Odyssey* 3.425–433; see [[laerces-the-goldsmith]]). The axe and the adze, πέλεκυς and σκέπαρνον, are the carpenter's tools that Calypso gave Odysseus for his raft, “a great axe” (πέλεκυν μέγαν), there of bronze, and “a polished adze” (*Odyssey* 5.234–237; see [[axe]] and [[adze]]). ἰάχοντα, “hisses”, agrees with πέλεκυν only, ἠὲ σκέπαρνον being thrown in as it were in a parenthesis (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.392). βάπτω is “dip”, and φαρμάσσων, “to temper it”, is a verb of φάρμακον, the drug or charm, here of the treatment that hardens the metal. τὸ γάρ is the tempering: “for this in turn is the strength of iron” (Merry on 9.393; see [[iron]]).

The point of likeness is the sound, and the meeting of a burning thing with a wet one: the hot bar answers to the iron, and the water to the eye (Merry on 9.393). The explanatory verse adds an antithesis that Merry draws out: the dipping that makes iron strong is, for the Cyclops' eye, its destruction (on 9.393).

## In context

The second of the two likenesses of the blinding comes after the first without a pause. Odysseus twirls the stake “as when a man bores a ship's timber / with a drill” (*Odyssey* 9.384–385; see [[the-shipwrights-drill]]); the blood flows round the stake, “And the blast singed all his eyelids round about, and his brows, / as the eyeball burned, and its roots crackled in the fire” (*Odyssey* 9.388–390); then the smith. Both likenesses are taken from a craftsman's work, the shipwright's and the smith's, and the second is heard rather than seen. After it, “he groaned aloud terribly, and the rock rang round about” (*Odyssey* 9.395), and the Cyclops wrenches the stake out of his eye (*Odyssey* 9.396–397; see [[stake]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Iron in Homer is the metal of tools and of prizes more than of weapons: the iron axes through which Odysseus shoots in the contest of the bow (*Odyssey* 21.3, 21.81, plain renderings), and the lump of iron that Achilles sets as a prize, from which the winner's shepherd and plowman will have iron for five years (*Iliad* 23.826–835; see [[iron]]). The Iliad's smith is a god: Hephaestus at his bellows and anvil makes the new armor of Achilles (*Iliad* 18.468–477; see [[forge]] and [[hephaestus]]).

## Outside Homer

The verb of the simile gave Greek its word for the temper itself, βαφή, the “dipping” (Merry on 9.393). Aristotle uses it of warlike cities, which are safe as long as they are at war but perish once they have won their empire: “for they lose their temper, like iron, when they are at peace” (τὴν γὰρ βαφὴν ἀνιᾶσιν, ὥσπερ ὁ σίδηρος, εἰρήνην ἄγοντες, *Politics* 7.14, 1334a6–9; see [[aristotle]]). Merry also compares Ajax' words in Sophocles, softened “like iron in the dipping”, βαφῇ σίδηρος ὥς (*Ajax* 651, as Merry quotes it; see [[sophocles]]).
