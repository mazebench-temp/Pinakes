## The simile

{{quote:81c7a5d7-f7e9-5f96-aea4-8308deb5595e}}

οἳ δʼ ὦκα διέπρησσον πεδίοιο / νόσφι νεῶν ταχέως· ὑπὸ δὲ στέρνοισι κονίη / ἵστατʼ ἀειρομένη ὥς τε νέφος ἠὲ θύελλα: “and quickly they crossed the plain, / swiftly, away from the ships; and beneath their chests the dust / rose and stood like a cloud or a storm” (23.364–366). The dust rises from under the horses and stands in the air as a mass. ἵστατʼ ἀειρομένη, “rose and stood”, is said of the dust under the feet of the army as it rushed to the ships when Agamemnon tested it (2.150–151), and of the wave of the Scamander that stood lifted high over Achilles (ἵστατʼ ἀειρόμενον, 21.327). νέφος is a cloud. θύελλα is a storm wind, the gust that snatches things away: Helen wishes that on the day she was born “an evil storm of wind had carried me away / to a mountain or into the wave of the loud-roaring sea” (6.346–347). Walter Leaf compares the storms, ἄελλαι, that raise a great mist of dust in a simile of *Iliad* 13, and the whirling cloud of dust, κονίσαλος ἀελλής, that rose from under the feet of the marching Achaeans (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.366; 13.334–336, 3.13).

The comparison is given to the dust, not to the horses. What the men left behind see of the race is a cloud moving away over the plain, and the next verses keep the speed: “and their manes streamed in the blasts of the wind. / And the chariots at one moment skimmed the bountiful earth, / and at another bounded high in the air” (23.367–369). πίλνατο, “skimmed”, is to come close to the ground, and μετήορα, “high in the air”, is the word with which Zeus says that earth and sea, pulled up on his golden rope, would all hang in midair (8.24–26). The drivers stand in the chariots, the heart of each pounding as they strive for victory, and the horses “flew on, raising the dust of the plain” (23.370–372).

## In context

Five drivers have yoked their horses and cast lots for their places, and Achilles has pointed out the turning post far off on the plain and set Phoenix beside it to watch the running (23.287–361; see [[the-funeral-games-of-patroclus]] and [[horses]]). “And all together they raised their whips over their horses, / and struck them with the straps, and shouted at them with words, / eagerly” (23.362–364). The dust follows the race to its end. The Argives in the gathering watch the horses fly on “raising the dust of the plain” (23.449), the drops of dust strike Diomedes as he comes in first, and his chariot runs so lightly that it leaves no deep track in the fine dust (23.502–506).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Dust is the sign of an army or a team in motion. The Achaeans march out in a whirling cloud of dust, as thick as the mist the South Wind pours over the mountains, and that simile ends with the half-verse that begins this one, “very quickly they crossed the plain” (3.10–14, 23.364; see [[mist-on-the-mountain]]); they are whitened by the dust that the horses' hooves beat up into the brazen sky, as the heaps of chaff grow white at the winnowing (5.499–505; see [[winnowing]]); and when Patroclus routs the Trojans “high up a storm wind of dust / spread out under the clouds” as their horses strain back toward the city (16.374–376). The Trojans who follow Hector are “like a flame or a storm wind” (13.39; see [[like-a-flame]]). Ajax tells Hector that he will soon pray for horses swifter than hawks, “the horses that will carry you to the city, raising the dust of the plain” (13.819–820). The same verse is given to the runners of the Phaeacian games, who fly on from the start “raising the dust of the plain” (*Odyssey* 8.121–122).

## Outside Homer

Leaf compares Virgil's chariot race in the third *Georgics* (on 23.368). There the chariots seem now low, now raised on high, to be carried through the empty air and rise into the breezes; there is no delay and no rest, and a cloud of yellow sand is raised (*iamque humiles iamque elati sublime videntur / aera per vacuum ferri atque adsurgere in auras. / nec mora nec requies; at fulvae nimbus harenae / tollitur*, *Georgics* 3.108–111). In the footrace of the *Aeneid* the runners burst from the start “like a storm cloud” (*effusi nimbo similes*, *Aeneid* 5.317; see [[virgil]]).
