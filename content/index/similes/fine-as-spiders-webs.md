## The simile

{{quote:09b590aa-e30a-59b7-90df-e5afe8974f5e}}

πολλὰ δὲ καὶ καθύπερθε μελαθρόφιν ἐξεκέχυντο, / ἠύτʼ ἀράχνια λεπτά, τά γʼ οὔ κέ τις οὐδὲ ἴδοιτο, / οὐδὲ θεῶν μακάρων: “and many too were poured down from above, from the roof beam, / fine as spider's webs, which no one could even see, / not even one of the blessed gods” (*Odyssey* 8.279–281). The comparison takes half a verse, and its point is fineness: the bonds are too thin to be seen. οὐδέ is “not even”: the chains could not so much as be seen (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.280). In καθύπερθε μελαθρόφιν, “from above, from the roof beam”, the second and more exact word explains the first; μέλαθρον is the roof beam, which a scholium derives from μέλας, “black”, since it was blackened by the smoke (Merry on 8.279). The reason follows: πέρι γὰρ δολόεντα τέτυκτο, “for they were made exceedingly cunning” (*Odyssey* 8.281), where πέρι goes with δολόεντα, “exceeding cunning” (Merry on 8.281; see [[wily]] and the translation's table of key words).

What is strong is made to look like the weakest of things. On the great anvil Hephaestus forged the bonds “unbreakable, not to be loosed”, and he poured them in a circle all around the bedposts before he let more down from the beam (*Odyssey* 8.274–279; see [[the-bonds-of-hephaestus]], [[forge]] and [[hephaestus]]). The poem calls the whole contrivance δόλος, “snare” (*Odyssey* 8.276, 8.282; see [[dolos]]), and when the gods come to look at the lovers caught in it, they laugh as they see “the craft of Hephaestus of much wisdom” (*Odyssey* 8.326–327; see [[laughter-of-the-gods]]).

## In context

The simile belongs to Demodocus' second song (see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]] and [[demodocus]]). The Sun has seen Ares and Aphrodite lying together in Hephaestus' house and told him; he goes to his smithy “brooding evil deep in his mind”, forges the bonds, sets them around the bed, and makes as if to go to Lemnos (*Odyssey* 8.270–284; see [[helios]] and [[lemnos]]). When the lovers lie down, “around them the bonds / of Hephaestus of much wisdom poured, skillfully made, / and they could not move or lift any of their limbs” (*Odyssey* 8.296–298; see [[bed]]). Only Hephaestus can loose them, and he does so when Poseidon has pledged himself for Ares' fine (*Odyssey* 8.344–360).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Spider's webs are named once more in the Odyssey, and not in a simile: Telemachus, coming to the swineherd's hut, wonders whether his mother still waits in the halls or has married another man, “and the bed of Odysseus lies, for lack of sleepers, with foul cobwebs on it” (*Odyssey* 16.33–35, a plain rendering). Both times the webs are on a marriage bed.

## Outside Homer

Ovid keeps the spider in his version of the story, told by Leuconoe among the daughters of Minyas. When the Sun has shown him the lovers, Vulcan at once files “thin chains of bronze, / and nets and snares that could deceive the eyes”, *graciles ex aere catenas / retiaque et laqueos, quae lumina fallere possent*: “the finest threads would not surpass that work, / nor the spider's web that hangs from the top of the beam”, *non illud opus tenuissima vincant / stamina, non summo quae pendet aranea tigno*, and he makes them follow the lightest touch (*Metamorphoses* 4.176–181). Homer's bonds hang down “from the roof beam” and could not be seen even by the gods; Ovid's web hangs from the top of the beam, and the chains deceive the eyes (see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite-in-later-writers]] and [[ovid]]).
