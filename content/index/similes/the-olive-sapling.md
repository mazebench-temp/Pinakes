## The simile

{{quote:19d2d1c5-0ba9-56f2-a968-00aadbaeedc7}}

οἷον δὲ τρέφει ἔρνος ἀνὴρ ἐριθηλὲς ἐλαίης / χώρῳ ἐν οἰοπόλῳ, ὅθʼ ἅλις ἀναβέβροχεν ὕδωρ: “And as a man rears a flourishing sapling of olive / in a lonely place, where water has drenched it in plenty” (17.53–54). ἔρνος is a young shoot or sapling, and τρέφω, “rear”, is the verb for bringing up children as well as plants: Thetis says that she reared Achilles φυτὸν ὣς γουνῷ ἀλωῆς, like a plant on the high ground of an orchard, when he shot up ἔρνεϊ ἶσος, like a sapling (18.56–57; see [[like-a-sapling]]). ἐριθηλής, “flourishing”, is said elsewhere of orchards and of the shoots of a tamarisk (5.90, 10.467). χώρῳ ἐν οἰοπόλῳ, “in a lonely place”, is the half-verse of the boar that waits for the hunters in *Iliad* 13 (13.473; see [[lonely]]). The watering is told by ἀναβέβροχεν, “has drenched”, which Walter Leaf reports as the reading of Zenodotus; the common text has ἀναβέβρυχεν, which would make the water well up around the tree (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.54; see the translation's note on 17.54).

καλὸν τηλεθάον· τὸ δέ τε πνοιαὶ δονέουσι / παντοίων ἀνέμων, καί τε βρύει ἄνθεϊ λευκῷ, “a fine, thriving tree; and the breezes of all the winds / shake it, and it teems with white blossom” (17.55–56). τηλεθάω, “flourish, be in bloom”, comes once more in the Iliad, in Priam's lament for the sons whom Achilles killed in their bloom (22.423); βρύω, “teem”, is found only here in Homer. The breezes do the tree good. Leaf compares the “wind-nurtured” spear, ἀνεμοτρεφὲς ἔγχος (11.256), for the benefit that wind was thought to give a growing tree (on 17.55).

Then the wind turns: ἐλθὼν δʼ ἐξαπίνης ἄνεμος σὺν λαίλαπι πολλῇ / βόθρου τʼ ἐξέστρεψε καὶ ἐξετάνυσσʼ ἐπὶ γαίῃ, “but a wind comes suddenly with a great storm / and wrenches it out of its trench and stretches it on the ground” (17.57–58). λαῖλαψ is the squall of Zeus' storms (16.365, 16.384). The βόθρος is the trench in which the tree was planted (Leaf on 17.58), so the storm undoes the man's work. ἐξετάνυσσε, “stretched out”, is the verb of a body laid at full length, as the fallen Asius “lay stretched out” (κεῖτο τανυσθείς, 13.392; see [[the-felled-tree]]).

The tenor: τοῖον Πάνθου υἱὸν ἐϋμμελίην Εὔφορβον / Ἀτρεΐδης Μενέλαος ἐπεὶ κτάνε τεύχεʼ ἐσύλα, “such was the son of Panthous, Euphorbus of the good ash spear, / whom Menelaus, son of Atreus, killed, and began to strip of his armor” (17.59–60). The likeness is of youth and beauty cut off at the height of their growth. The verses just before have shown the dead man's hair: “His hair was drenched with blood, hair like the Graces', / and his braids, which were bound tight with gold and silver” (17.51–52; see [[graces]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). The blossom of the tree answers the bright hair, and the man who rears the sapling with such care answers the parents of whom Euphorbus has just spoken (17.34–40; see [[panthous]]).

Leaf compares Odysseus' words to Nausicaa, whom he likens to the young shoot of a palm that he once saw by the altar of Apollo at Delos (*Odyssey* 6.162–167). He also reports that Hehn, holding that only the wild olive was known in early Homeric times, took this cultivated tree as proof that the passage is late, a view he calls founded “on very weak grounds” (on 17.53).

## In context

Euphorbus, son of Panthous, was the first to strike Patroclus with a spear (16.806–815; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). Now he comes to the body, claims the honor of the first blow, and tells Menelaus to give up the bloody spoils or lose his life (17.9–17). Menelaus answers with the fate of Euphorbus' brother Hyperenor, whom he killed, and warns him to go back into the crowd: “a fool learns a thing once it is done” (17.19–32; see [[hyperenor]]). Euphorbus wants to avenge his brother and bring Menelaus' head and armor to his parents (17.34–42). His spear bends back on Menelaus' shield; Menelaus prays to Zeus and drives his spear through the young man's neck, “and he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him” (17.43–50; see [[euphorbus]] and [[menelaus]]). The olive tree follows the fall, and a lion simile follows the olive tree: no Trojan dares to come against Menelaus, as dogs and herdsmen dare not come near a lion that has seized the best cow of the herd (17.61–69; see [[lion-among-cattle]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Young men fall like trees in the Iliad's battles: Simoeisius like a poplar that a chariot-maker cuts down by a marsh (4.482–487; see [[the-poplar]]), the twin sons of Diocles like tall fir trees (5.559–560; see [[fir-trees]]), Imbrius like an ash cut down on a mountain peak (13.178–181; see [[the-felled-ash]]), Asius and Sarpedon like an oak, a poplar or a pine felled for ship-timber (13.389–391, 16.482–484; see [[the-felled-tree]]), and Hector, struck by a stone, like an oak uprooted by the thunderbolt of Zeus (14.414–417; see [[the-oak-struck-by-the-thunderbolt]]). Those trees are cut down by men or struck by Zeus. Euphorbus' tree alone was planted and tended by a man, and the wind takes it.

The sapling is a picture of a young life in Homer's speeches too. Thetis' son shot up like a sapling (18.56–57 = 18.437–438), and in the Odyssey Eumaeus says that the gods reared Telemachus like a sapling (*Odyssey* 14.175). This is the only olive tree in the Iliad, which elsewhere has the olive only as wood and oil: Peisander fights with an axe on a handle of olive wood (13.612; see [[olive]]).
