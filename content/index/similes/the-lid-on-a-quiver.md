## The simile

{{quote:be17afff-cb09-584f-9753-f782e9acf9b7}}

δειπνήσας δʼ ἄντρου ἐξήλασε πίονα μῆλα, / ῥηιδίως ἀφελὼν θυρεὸν μέγαν· αὐτὰρ ἔπειτα / ἂψ ἐπέθηχʼ, ὡς εἴ τε φαρέτρῃ πῶμʼ ἐπιθείη: “And when he had dined he drove his fat flocks out of the cave, / easily taking away the great door-stone; and then / he set it back again, as one sets the lid on a quiver” (*Odyssey* 9.312–314). πῶμα is a lid, as of the quiver from which Pandarus takes his arrow, “Then he removed the lid of his quiver” (*Iliad* 4.116; see [[quiver]] and [[bow]]), or of a chest (*Odyssey* 8.443). ὡς εἰ takes the optative here, as at *Iliad* 2.780, and the subject of ἐπιθείη is not expressed, “as (anyone) would set”, as in ὅσσον τε γέγωνε βοήσας, “as far as a man can make himself heard with a shout” (*Odyssey* 9.473; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.314).

The point of likeness is ease. The likeness says nothing of the stone itself; the measure of it was given when the Cyclops first set it in place: “two and twenty wagons, / good four-wheeled ones, could not have heaved it from the ground” (*Odyssey* 9.241–242; see [[door-stone]] and [[wagon]]). Merry takes that verse for a parody of the proverbial measure of the Iliad, where two men, the best of the people, could not easily have heaved Hector's stone from the ground onto a wagon, with something intentionally comic in the exact number twenty-two (on 9.241; *Iliad* 12.447–448). The Cyclops takes the stone away ῥηιδίως, “easily” (*Odyssey* 9.313), as Hector wields his stone easily, “as a shepherd easily carries the fleece of a ram” (*Iliad* 12.449–452; see [[the-shepherd-and-the-fleece]]); but Hector's stone was made light by Zeus, and the Cyclops needs no god.

## In context

On the second morning the Cyclops milks his flocks and takes two more men for his meal (*Odyssey* 9.307–311). The stone is the reason the men are still alive and still prisoners: the night before Odysseus had not killed him in his sleep, because “we could not have pushed away from the high doorway / with our hands the heavy stone that he had set against it” (*Odyssey* 9.304–305). The Cyclops drives his flocks off to the mountain “with much whistling” (*Odyssey* 9.315), and Odysseus is left “brooding evil deep within” (*Odyssey* 9.316), until he finds his plan in the Cyclops' club (*Odyssey* 9.318–330; see [[the-mast-of-twenty-oars]] and [[stake]]). On the last morning the blinded Cyclops takes the stone away from the doorway himself and sits in it with his hands spread out, to catch the men as they go (*Odyssey* 9.415–418).
