## The simile

{{quote:8eeb1e44-9409-5129-8371-c33459751088}}

ἀμφὶ δʼ ἄρʼ αὐτῷ βαῖνε λέων ὣς ἀλκὶ πεποιθώς: “he bestrode it like a lion trusting in his courage”. ἀμφὶ … βαῖνε, “went around, stood astride”, is the verb for standing over a fallen comrade to keep his body from the enemy (see [[fighting-over-the-body]]). ἀλκὶ πεποιθώς, “trusting in his courage”, names the strength that stands and defends (see [[alke]]). The phrase belongs to beasts at bay and to men over a body: to a mountain lion (17.61; Odyssey 6.130), to a boar that waits for the hunters or turns on the dogs (13.471, 17.728), and to Hector standing firm over the body of Patroclus (18.158).

The next lines show what the lion does. Aeneas held in front of the body “his spear and his shield, balanced on every side, eager to kill whoever came against him, shouting terribly” (5.300–302). The lion is not hunting. It stands over what is its own and dares anyone to take it. The point of likeness is courage in defense.

Why Aeneas stands over Pandarus is said plainly: “fearing that the Achaeans might somehow drag the body away from him” (5.298). A body left to the enemy was stripped of its armor and could be left unburied (see [[stripping-the-dead]]). Pandarus was his ally and his companion in the chariot, whom he had sought out and brought against Diomedes (5.166–240; see [[pandarus]]).

## In context

Diomedes' spear, guided by Athena, has gone through Pandarus' face, and he has fallen from the chariot (5.290–296). The lion's defiance lasts three lines. Diomedes answers it with a stone, “a huge thing, which two men could not carry, such as mortals are now; but he wielded it easily even alone” (5.303–304; see [[men-of-today]]), and crushes Aeneas' hip, “where the thigh turns in the hip joint, and men call it the cup” (5.305–307). The hero sinks to his knees, leans on the earth with his hand, and black night covers his eyes (5.308–310). The same two lines tell of Hector stunned by Diomedes' spear in Book 11 (5.309–310 = 11.355–356). Aeneas would have died there, had his mother not seen it (5.311–318; see [[diomedes-wounds-aphrodite]] and [[aeneas]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The defense of a body returns in the same words in Book 17, with a different animal. Menelaus stands over the body of Patroclus as a cow stands over her first calf, and holds in front of it his spear and his shield, eager to kill whoever comes against him (17.4–8, repeating 5.299–301). Telamonian Ajax covers the same body with his broad shield and stands over it like a lion over its cubs when hunters meet it in the forest (17.132–137). The lion of Book 5 defends a companion; the lion of Book 17, the young. See [[lion]].
