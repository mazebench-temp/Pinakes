## The simile

{{quote:2c3415b8-56e8-5296-acc3-fd28fe26aad5}}

βῆ δʼ ἴμεν ὥς τε λέων ὀρεσίτροφος ἀλκὶ πεποιθώς, “And he went on like a mountain-bred lion, trusting in his courage” (*Odyssey* 6.130; see [[lion]] and [[mountain-bred]]). The verse joins two of the Iliad's: Sarpedon goes at the Achaean wall βῆ ῥʼ ἴμεν ὥς τε λέων ὀρεσίτροφος, the same words but for a particle, and the mountain-bred lion that snatches the best cow from a herd is ἀλκὶ πεποιθώς, “trusting in his courage” (*Iliad* 12.299, 17.61; see [[the-mountain-lion-and-the-fold]] and [[alke]]). The Iliad has the dative ἀλκί five times, always in that verse-end (*Iliad* 5.299, 13.471, 17.61, 17.728, 18.158), and the Odyssey only here (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.130).

The lion is ὑόμενος καὶ ἀήμενος, “rained upon and blown by the wind” (*Odyssey* 6.131), two passive participles found only here in Homer; Nitzsch compared νιφόμενος, “snowed upon”, in Xenophon, as Merry reports (on 6.131). The weather is the lion's own, a beast of the open mountain. The night before, Odysseus had crept under two bushes so thick that neither “the wet might of the blowing winds” (ἀνέμων … ἀέντων, the verb of ἀήμενος) nor the rain could pass through them (*Odyssey* 5.478–480; see [[olive]]). ἐν δέ οἱ ὄσσε / δαίεται, “and his eyes / blaze” (*Odyssey* 6.131–132), takes the verb of fire, as Hector's eyes “blazed with fire” when he burst through the gates of the wall (πυρὶ δʼ ὄσσε δεδήει, *Iliad* 12.466); ὄσσε, properly a dual, is treated as a neuter plural and takes a singular verb, as there (Merry on 6.131). ἐν δέ οἱ ὄσσε ends the verse in which Achilles' eyes shine out terribly at the sight of his new armor (*Iliad* 19.16–17).

“and he goes after the cattle or the sheep / or after the wild deer” (*Odyssey* 6.132–133; see [[cattle]], [[sheep]] and [[deer]]): βουσὶ μετέρχεται, with the dative, is to go among the herd, and μετʼ ἀγροτέρας ἐλάφους, with the accusative, to go after the deer. The change of case, Merry notes, is strictly accurate: the lion pursues the flying deer, while his ravages in the farmyard are confined within a narrower circle (on 6.132). Then the drive: κέλεται δέ ἑ γαστήρ, “and his belly bids him / make an attempt on the sheep, and go even into the close-built fold” (*Odyssey* 6.133–134). The second verse is the Iliad's word for word (*Iliad* 12.301), but there it is the lion's “proud spirit”, θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ, that bids him (*Iliad* 12.300); here it is his belly. Merry compares Virgil's *suadet enim vesana fames*, “for mad hunger urges him” (on 6.133).

The tenor follows in two verses: “so Odysseus was about to mingle with the girls of the lovely tresses, / naked though he was; for need had come upon him” (*Odyssey* 6.135–136; see [[with-lovely-tresses]]). The main point of comparison, as Merry puts it, is that both are pushed by hunger into an act of unusual boldness, χρειὼ γὰρ ἵκανε (on 6.135). The lion has his belly; Odysseus has his need, of clothes and of a guide to the city, which he means to beg of her and asks for at the end of his speech (*Odyssey* 6.144, 6.178–179), and of food, which he eats greedily when it is set before him, “for he had long tasted no food” (*Odyssey* 6.248–250; see [[fasting]]). μίξεσθαι, “to mingle”, is the verb of coming among others, as the Phaeacians, Nausicaa says, live so far apart that no other mortal “mingles with” them (*Odyssey* 6.205, 6.241).

## In context

The ball falls into the eddy, the girls shout, and Odysseus wakes, wondering whether the people of this land are savage or kind to strangers, and whether the cry was of nymphs or of mortals (*Odyssey* 6.115–126; see [[nymphs]] and [[xenia]]). “So saying, brilliant Odysseus came out from under the bushes, / and from the thick forest with his strong hand he broke off a branch / of leaves, to cover the man's nakedness about his body” (*Odyssey* 6.127–129; see [[trees]] and [[strong-hand]]). θάμνων, “the bushes” (*Odyssey* 6.127), are the two olive bushes under which he slept, θάμνους (*Odyssey* 5.476–477); μήδεα is “nakedness”, the word used again when he girds himself with rags to fight Irus (*Odyssey* 18.67; see the translation's table of key words). Then the simile.

Its effect is told at once: “And terrible he appeared to them, afflicted by the brine, / and they fled in fear this way and that over the jutting beaches” (*Odyssey* 6.137–138; see [[sea]] and [[shore]]). The girls scatter like the herd the lion goes after. One does not: “only the daughter of Alcinous stood her ground; for Athena / put daring in her mind and took the fear from her limbs” (*Odyssey* 6.139–140; see [[nausicaa]], [[athena]] and [[tharsos]]). And the lion does not spring. Odysseus ponders whether to clasp her knees, and chooses to beg her “with soothing words, standing apart”, lest she be angry in her heart (*Odyssey* 6.141–148; see [[supplication]] and [[decision-scene]]). The simile gives the hunger and the boldness, and the scene that follows gives the restraint.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The lion is the commonest beast of the Iliad's similes, and Odysseus' lion is made from two of its verses (see above). The Odyssey keeps the lion for a few men and moments. Menelaus foretells Odysseus' revenge as the return of a lion to his lair, where a hind has laid her fawns (*Odyssey* 4.333–340; see [[the-hind-in-the-lions-lair]]); Penelope, afraid for her son, is a lion hemmed in by hunters (*Odyssey* 4.791–793; see [[the-lion-encircled]]). The Cyclops devours Odysseus' men “like a mountain-bred lion” (ὥς τε λέων ὀρεσίτροφος, *Odyssey* 9.292, a plain rendering), and when the suitors lie dead Eurycleia finds Odysseus spattered with blood and gore “like a lion” that comes from devouring an ox (*Odyssey* 22.401–406, a plain rendering). In the sixth book the lion is a hungry, wet and naked stranger.

The belly comes back in Odysseus' own mouth. At Alcinous' table he says, “for there is nothing more shameless than the hateful belly, / which bids a man remember it under compulsion” (*Odyssey* 7.216–217), where κύντερον, “more shameless”, is literally “more doglike”; as a beggar at his own door he says that a ravening belly cannot be hidden, and that for its sake men fit out ships against their enemies (*Odyssey* 17.286–289), and that “the evil-working belly” drives him to fight Irus (*Odyssey* 18.53–54); the renderings of the later books are plain renderings (see [[gaster]]). The γαστήρ of the lion is the first of these.

## Outside Homer

Virgil gave the hunger to the lion of a night raid. When Nisus kills the sleeping Rutulians in their camp, he is like “an unfed lion rampaging through full sheepfolds (for mad hunger urges him)”, that mangles and drags the soft flock, dumb with fear, and roars with bloody mouth: *impastus ceu plena leo per ovilia turbans / (suadet enim vesana fames)* (*Aeneid* 9.339–341; see [[virgil]]).

Pausanias, Merry notes, says that this scene was painted by Polygnotus in the Propylaea at Athens (on 6.138, citing Pausanias 1.22; see [[polygnotus]] and [[pausanias]]).
