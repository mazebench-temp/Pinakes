## The simile

{{quote:36554afe-84d9-5179-8d78-b687cb048d83}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ὀπωρινὸς Βορέης φορέῃσιν ἀκάνθας / ἂμ πεδίον, πυκιναὶ δὲ πρὸς ἀλλήλῃσιν ἔχονται: “As when the North Wind of late summer carries thistles / over the plain, and they cling thick together” (*Odyssey* 5.328–329). ἄκανθα, “thistle”, occurs only here in Homer. ὀπωρινός, “of late summer”, is from ὀπώρα, the ripening season; W. W. Merry reckons it from the rising of the Dog Star to the setting of the Pleiads, from the end of July to the end of October, the season that follows θέρος, summer (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.328; see the translation's note on 5.328–329). The North Wind of that season is the wind of an Iliad simile too, where it quickly dries a newly watered orchard (*Iliad* 21.346–347; see [[the-north-wind-dries-the-orchard]] and [[boreas]]). Here it is a wind of the dry plain at the end of summer, when the thistles have withered.

What the thistles do Merry explains from a description of the “wind-witch” of the Russian steppes, which he quotes from Schleiden's *The Plant*: in autumn the stem of the thistle rots off, and the globe of branches dries into a ball light as a feather, which the wind drives over the plain, hopping along the ground or rising suddenly into the air, and one ball hooks onto another until a whole mass rolls away before the wind (on 5.329). πυκιναί, “thick”, is the clinging of the dry heads to one another; the subjunctive of the wind's carrying passes into the indicative of the thistles' clinging, ἔχονται (Merry on 5.329).

The tenor follows: ὣς τὴν ἂμ πέλαγος ἄνεμοι φέρον ἔνθα καὶ ἔνθα, “so the winds carried it over the open sea this way and that” (*Odyssey* 5.330). The raft is the mass of thistles, its beams still held together, and the open sea is the plain. Then the four winds of the storm play with it: “now the South Wind would toss it to the North Wind to carry, / and now again the East Wind would yield it to the West Wind to chase” (*Odyssey* 5.331–332; see [[notus]], [[eurus]] and [[zephyrus]]). προβάλεσκε, “would toss”, and εἴξασκε, “would yield”, are iteratives, of a thing done again and again, and both are found only here. They are the four winds that fell on the raft together when Poseidon spurred on the storms: “The East Wind and the South Wind clashed together, and the stormy West Wind / and the North Wind” (*Odyssey* 5.295–296).

The point of likeness is lightness and helplessness. A raft that took four days to build is a bundle of dry stalks, carried about by every wind; Odysseus sits in the middle of it, the line before says, “avoiding the end of death” (*Odyssey* 5.326), and the great wave carries it with the current this way and that (*Odyssey* 5.327). Merry notes that a current, properly, sets one way, but in the conflict of winds Odysseus was in a maze of cross currents and eddies (on 5.327).

## In context

Poseidon, coming back from the Ethiopians, has seen Odysseus on his raft near the land of the Phaeacians and raised the storm: he gathered the clouds, threw the sea into confusion, and spurred on the storms of winds of every kind (*Odyssey* 5.282–296; see [[poseidon]]). Odysseus' knees were loosened, and he called the Danaans who died at Troy three times and four times blessed (*Odyssey* 5.297–312). A great wave struck him, whirled the raft around and threw him from it; his mast was broken and the sail and yard fell into the sea; the wave held him under, and the garments Calypso had given him weighed him down; but he came up, spat out the brine, caught the raft again and sat in its middle (*Odyssey* 5.313–326; see [[sea]]). The simile follows, and after it the daughter of Cadmus sees him (*Odyssey* 5.333; see [[like-a-diving-bird]] and [[leucothea]]). The next simile of the storm tells how the wave breaks the raft at last, scattering its beams as a wind scatters a heap of husks (*Odyssey* 5.368–370; see [[the-heap-of-husks]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Winds that fall on the sea together make a simile of the Iliad, where the North Wind and the West Wind from Thrace raise the dark wave and cast up seaweed, and so the hearts of the Achaeans are torn (*Iliad* 9.4–8; see [[two-winds-from-thrace]]). In the storm that wrecked Odysseus' ship after the island of the Sun, the storm that Calypso tells of in this book (*Odyssey* 5.130–134), he sat on the keel and the mast lashed together and was carried by the deadly winds, first the West Wind and then the South Wind (*Odyssey* 12.420–427, a plain rendering).
