## The simile

ὃ δʼ ἤϊε νυκτὶ ἐοικώς: “and he went, like the night”. νυκτί is the dative of νύξ, “night”, and ἐοικώς, the participle of ἔοικα, “be like”, takes the dative. A god is compared with the night itself. The simile does not say what the two share. Darkness, silence, the fear that comes with night: the listener supplies it.

## In context

{{quote:2dcda65d-12f3-5df4-9e33-86e73b78faf1}}

Apollo is heard, not seen: the arrows clatter on the shoulders of the angry god. Then he comes “like the night”, and the simile hides him at the moment he arrives. He sits apart from the ships and shoots, and the twang of the silver bow is terrible ({{ref:Iliad 1.48-49}}). No one in the camp is said to see him. The plague is known by what it does: first the mules and dogs, then the men, and the pyres of the dead, burning thick and constant ({{ref:Iliad 1.50-52}}; see [[wrath-of-apollo]]).

## Outside Book 1

In the underworld the shade of Heracles, another archer, stands “like dark night” (ἐρεμνῇ νυκτὶ ἐοικώς), holding his bare bow with an arrow on the string (Odyssey 11.606–607). Hector bursts through the Achaean gate “like swift night in his look” (Iliad 12.462–463).
