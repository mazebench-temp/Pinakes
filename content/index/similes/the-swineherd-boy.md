## The simile

{{quote:704d91ee-8776-560f-8632-2204af4ec88f}}

συφορβός, “swineherd”, occurs only here in the Iliad; in the *Odyssey* it is the word for Eumaeus and the men who keep Odysseus' swine (*Odyssey* 14.504, 16.154, 21.189). The boy is a παῖς, a child set to herd the pigs. ἔναυλος is a gully or the torrent that runs down it in winter, the gullies into which the Trojans would flee (16.71) and the torrents that the Scamander bids the Simoeis spur on (21.312; see [[simoeis]]). ἀποέρσῃ, “sweeps away”, is Helen's word when she wishes that on the day she was born a wave had swept her away (6.348), and Hera's when she fears that the river will sweep Achilles away (21.329); the ancient commentators on that verse call the word Cyprian, as Walter Leaf reports (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.283). Leaf takes the picture from the practice of sending swine to fatten in the oak woods of the mountains in autumn and driving them home as winter comes on (on 21.282).

The point is the death, not the danger. Achilles has just said that he would rather have been killed by Hector, “the best man bred here; / then a brave man would have been the killer, and he would have slain a brave man” (21.279–280). A boy drowned in a winter torrent dies for nothing and without a name, and his body is lost; the river means Achilles to die so, and to lie under its sand and silt with no tomb (21.316–323; see [[scamander]]). λευγαλέῳ θανάτῳ, “a wretched death”, is the death without glory, the opposite of the death in battle that brings a man fame (see [[kleos]] and [[death-in-battle]]).

## In context

The river has overtaken Achilles on the plain and is eating the ground from under his feet (21.263–271; see [[the-channel-and-the-gardener]]). He groans and looks up to the sky: no god has pitied him, and his mother deceived him when she told him that he would die under the wall of Troy by the arrows of Apollo (21.272–278; see [[thetis]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). At once Poseidon and Athena come to him in the likeness of men and assure him that it is not his fate to be brought down by a river (21.284–292; see [[achilles-and-the-river]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The verse of the wretched death returns in the *Odyssey*, in a storm at sea. When Poseidon raises a storm against his raft, Odysseus calls the Danaans who died at Troy three times and four times blessed (see [[three-times-blessed]]), and wishes that he had died on the day the Trojans hurled their spears at him over the body of Achilles: then he would have had his funeral rites, and the Achaeans would have spread his fame, νῦν δέ με λευγαλέῳ θανάτῳ εἵμαρτο ἁλῶναι, “but now it was fated that I be caught by a wretched death” (*Odyssey* 5.306–312 = *Iliad* 21.281). Both heroes, about to drown, wish for the death of a warrior at Troy.

The winter torrent is the Iliad's picture of force that nothing stops, but elsewhere it is the hero who is like it: Diomedes storms across the plain like a river in flood that scatters the dikes, and Ajax drives the Trojans as a torrent swollen by the rain of Zeus comes down from the mountains (5.87–92, 11.492–495; see [[river-in-flood]]). A traveler stops short at a swift river and runs back (5.597–600; see [[man-at-the-river]]). Only here does a man drown in one.
