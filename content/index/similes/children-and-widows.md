## The simile

ὥς τε γὰρ ἢ παῖδες νεαροὶ χῆραί τε γυναῖκες / ἀλλήλοισιν ὀδύρονται οἶκον δὲ νέεσθαι: “for like young children or widowed women they wail to one another about going home”. παῖδες νεαροί are “young children”, and χῆραι γυναῖκες “widowed women”, women bereft of their husbands. ὀδύρονται means “lament, wail”. οἶκον δὲ νέεσθαι (“to go home”) is a phrase of the book's argument about leaving; Nestor uses it twice (2.354, 2.357).

The vehicle is the household without its men, the helpless who can only grieve; the tenor, the army crying to go home. The point of likeness is complaint without action, and it is meant to shame.

## In context

{{quote:fdc47141-6727-5109-9a04-350b23074cd8}}

The Achaeans, Odysseus tells Agamemnon, are breaking their promise to sack Ilios before sailing home (2.284–288). The picture comes from the same assembly. Arguing for flight, Agamemnon had imagined “our wives, I suppose, and our little children” (ἄλοχοι καὶ νήπια τέκνα) waiting in their halls (2.136–137). Odysseus turns it round: the men now behave like the women and children they left behind. Then he softens. The toil is enough to make a man long for home, a month away from a wife is hard, and this is the ninth year; “but all the same, it is shameful to stay so long and go home empty-handed” (2.291–298; [[shameful-to-stay-long]]). The verb ὀδύρομαι (“lament”) comes back in the sign of Aulis that he recalls next, where the mother sparrow flutters around her nestlings, “lamenting her dear young” (ὀδυρομένη, 2.315; [[the-portent-at-aulis]]).

{{quote:008866c5-f953-520f-b78f-646b2ecc723a}}

[[Nestor]] takes up the comparison and makes it harsher: παισὶν ἐοικότες (“like children”), νηπιάχοις (“foolish little ones”), “who care nothing for the works of war” (πολεμήϊα ἔργα). νηπίαχος (“little child”) belongs with νήπιος (“foolish, childish”; [[nepios]]). Odysseus' children wail; Nestor's make speeches (ἀγοράασθε, “you talk in the assembly”).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Hector uses the same pair when he answers Ajax: do not test me as if I were a feeble boy, or a woman who knows nothing of the works of war (πολεμήϊα ἔργα, Iliad 7.235–236). Elsewhere the figure of weakness is the real cost of the war. Andromache tells Hector that he has no pity for their infant son or for her, who will soon be his widow (παῖδά τε νηπίαχον … χήρη, 6.407–409): the words of Odysseus and Nestor, in earnest. Her lament over his body begins with the same pair: he leaves her a widow in the halls, and the child is still an infant (24.725–726).
