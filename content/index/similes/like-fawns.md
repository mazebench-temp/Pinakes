## The simile

τίφθʼ οὕτως ἔστητε τεθηπότες ἠΰτε νεβροί, / αἵ τʼ ἐπεὶ οὖν ἔκαμον πολέος πεδίοιο θέουσαι / ἑστᾶσʼ, οὐδʼ ἄρα τίς σφι μετὰ φρεσὶ γίγνεται ἀλκή: “why do you stand like this, dazed, like fawns that, when they are worn out from running over a wide plain, stand still, and there is no courage in their hearts?” νεβροί are fawns, the young of the deer ([[deer]]). τεθηπότες, “dazed, stunned”, is the perfect participle of a verb of astonishment (τέθηπα). ἔκαμον is “grew weary”. ἀλκή is the strength to stand and defend oneself, the courage the fawns have lost ([[alke]]).

The tenor closes the simile with the words that opened it: ὣς ὑμεῖς ἔστητε τεθηπότες οὐδὲ μάχεσθε, “so you stand dazed and do not fight” (4.246). The repeated ἔστητε τεθηπότες (“you stand dazed”) frames the picture. The point of likeness is the stillness of an animal with no fight left in it. But the fawns have at least run until they dropped. The Argives stand still before they have fought at all.

## In context

{{quote:9c4a72cb-b02a-54fb-b64e-615ea9e0f48b}}

Agamemnon has left his chariot with his attendant and goes on foot through the ranks (4.223–231; see [[the-review-of-the-army]]). Those he sees eager he encourages: father Zeus will be no helper to liars, and vultures will eat the flesh of the oath-breakers (4.232–239; see [[vultures]]). Those he sees “holding back from hateful war” he rebukes “with angry words” (4.240–241), and this is the rebuke. It opens with abuse. ἐλεγχέες is “disgraces”, and ἰόμωροι, rendered “arrow-fighters”, is a puzzle. It occurs once more in Homer, when the Trojan Acamas taunts the Argives (14.479), and ancient readers took it either as “fighting with arrows”, at a safe distance, or as “braggarts” (see [[obscure-iomoroi]]). Then comes a question: οὔ νυ σέβεσθε; “have you no shame?” (see [[sebas]]). After the fawns, a taunt: are they waiting for the Trojans to reach the ships, to see whether the son of Cronus will hold his hand over them (4.247–249)?

The image has been used on Agamemnon himself. In Book 1 Achilles said he had “the eyes of a dog and the heart of a deer” (1.225; see [[dog-eyes-deer-heart]]). Now the king turns the deer on his own men. Similes of shame spoken in speeches go back to Book 2, where Odysseus said the Achaeans wailed to go home “like young children or widowed women”, and Nestor that they talked “like children” (2.289–290, 2.337–338; see [[children-and-widows]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same words describe the twelve Trojan youths whom Achilles takes alive in the river, to kill at Patroclus' pyre: he leads them out “dazed like fawns” (τεθηπότας ἠΰτε νεβρούς, 21.26–29). At the start of Book 22 the Trojans who have fled into the city like fawns (πεφυζότες ἠΰτε νεβροί) are drying off their sweat (22.1–2). Poseidon reminds the Achaeans that the Trojans used to be like frightened deer in a wood, the prey of jackals, leopards and wolves (13.101–104). Elsewhere the fawn is prey: an eagle sent by Zeus drops one beside his altar as a sign (8.247–250), and a dog hunts one through the glens of the mountains (22.189–192).
