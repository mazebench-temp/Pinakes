## The simile

{{quote:a6ec73a0-f38a-572b-8989-7a8ddeb51ace}}

The work has three stages. Athena makes Odysseus “taller to look upon and stouter”; she sends down from his head “the thick hair”, οὔλας … κόμας, “like the hyacinth flower” (*Odyssey* 6.229–231; see [[athena]], [[thick-and-woolly]] and [[hyacinth]]); and she pours grace upon him. The simile is for the last: ὡς δʼ ὅτε τις χρυσὸν περιχεύεται ἀργύρῳ ἀνὴρ / ἴδρις, “And as when a man pours gold around silver, / a skilled man” (*Odyssey* 6.232–233; see [[gold-and-silver]]). περιχεύεται is an aorist subjunctive, and ἀργύρῳ a locative, the ground on which the gold is laid or plated, as in χρυσὸν κέρασιν περιχεύας, “with gold poured around her horns”, the gilding of a heifer for sacrifice (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.232, citing *Odyssey* 3.384 and *Iliad* 10.294). At Pylos the gilding is done before the reader's eyes: the smith Laerces comes with anvil, hammer and tongs, “and the smith then poured it around the heifer's horns, / working it with skill, so that the goddess might rejoice to see the offering” (*Odyssey* 3.437–438; see [[laerces-the-goldsmith]]). The goddess there is Athena; here she is the gilder.

The craftsman is one “whom Hephaestus and Pallas Athena have taught / every kind of craft” (*Odyssey* 6.233–234; see [[hephaestus]]). The two gods of craft are paired in the *Hymn to Hephaestus*, who “with gray-eyed Athena taught men splendid works on the earth” (*Homeric Hymn* 20.2–3), and in Plato, where the arts are the gift of Hephaestus and “his fellow-craftswoman” (*Statesman* 274c–d), a passage Nitzsch quoted; Merry adds that Athena, the patroness of women's work, was also worshipped as Ergane, goddess of all artisans (on 6.233). τέχνην παντοίην is “art in all its branches” (Merry on 6.233). The last clause, χαρίεντα δὲ ἔργα τελείει, “and he accomplishes lovely works” (*Odyssey* 6.234), is coordinated with the rest but gives the result of the gods' teaching, where later Greek would have used ὥστε (Merry on 6.234).

The tenor answers the craftsman's χαρίεντα ἔργα with χάρις itself: ὣς ἄρα τῷ κατέχευε χάριν κεφαλῇ τε καὶ ὤμοις, “so she poured grace upon his head and shoulders” (*Odyssey* 6.235; see [[charis]]). The gold on silver is the grace laid over the man, and the poet comes back to the word at once: Odysseus goes apart along the shore, “shining with beauty and grace” (κάλλεϊ καὶ χάρισι στίλβων, *Odyssey* 6.237). What the gold stands for in the picture, Merry suggests, is the auburn hair around the fair forehead, “an aureole of auburn hair”, though he grants that this is hardly like the dark complexion given Odysseus at *Odyssey* 16.175 (on 6.232; see [[hair]]).

## In context

Odysseus has bathed apart from the girls, washing from his skin “the brine that covered his back and his broad shoulders” and from his head “the scurf of the barren sea”, and has anointed himself and put on the clothes Nausicaa gave him (*Odyssey* 6.224–228; see [[bathing]], [[sea]] and [[barren-sea]]). Then Athena's work, the simile, and the effect: he sits apart on the shore, “and the girl gazed at him” (*Odyssey* 6.236–237; see [[nausicaa]]). Nausicaa tells her handmaids that the man who seemed to her unsightly before “now … is like the gods who hold the wide sky”, and wishes that such a man might be called her husband (*Odyssey* 6.242–245; see [[wide-sky]] and [[posis]]).

The second time, the verses come at the end of the poem, and the same words make a different moment. The suitors are dead; the housekeeper Eurynome bathes Odysseus and anoints him with oil, “and Athena poured much beauty down over his head”, made him taller and stouter, sent down his thick hair like the hyacinth, and poured grace around his head and shoulders as a man pours gold around silver, and he came from the bath like the immortals and sat down again opposite his wife (*Odyssey* 23.153–165, a plain rendering; see [[penelope]] and [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). In the sixth book the verses prepare Odysseus to be seen by a girl who will wish for such a husband; in the twenty-third, by his wife, who still tests him with the secret of their bed before she will know him (*Odyssey* 23.173–206). The tenor's verb changes once: in the sixth book Athena κατέχευε, “poured” grace down upon him (*Odyssey* 6.235); in the twenty-third she περίχευε, poured it around him (*Odyssey* 23.162).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Athena's grace is poured on Odysseus once more, before the games of the Phaeacians: “Athena / poured a wondrous grace on his head and shoulders, / and made him taller and stouter to look on, / so that he might be dear to all the Phaeacians” (*Odyssey* 8.18–21, a plain rendering), and on Telemachus as he goes to the assembly (*Odyssey* 2.12; again 17.63). Before he fights Irus she fills out his limbs (*Odyssey* 18.69–70), and when Telemachus is to know him she makes him dark again, his jaws filled out and his beard dark about his chin (*Odyssey* 16.172–176). The disguise is the same work undone: on the shore of Ithaca she withers his skin and destroys “the auburn hair from his head” (ξανθὰς δʼ ἐκ κεφαλῆς … τρίχας, *Odyssey* 13.430–431, a plain rendering).

The craftsman taught by Athena is a figure of the Iliad's similes too. The carpenter who makes a ship's timber straight with a chalk line is one “who knows / well all his craft by the promptings of Athena” (*Iliad* 15.411–412; see [[the-chalk-line]]), and the builder's son Phereclus “knew how to make with his hands every kind / of intricate work; for Pallas Athena loved him above all others” (*Iliad* 5.60–61; see [[phereclus]]).

## Outside Homer

Virgil kept the picture for Aeneas, made beautiful by his mother before he shows himself to Dido: Venus breathes on him the hair and the light of youth, and he shines “as the grace that hands add to ivory, or when silver or Parian marble is set round with yellow gold”, *quale manus addunt ebori decus, aut ubi flavo / argentum Pariusve lapis circumdatur auro* (*Aeneid* 1.588–593; see [[virgils-dido-as-diana]] and [[virgil]]). Merry cites the lines on 6.232.

Strabo makes the gilder a figure for Homer's way with myth, laid over the truth as gold over silver (*Geography* 1.2.9; see [[strabo-gold-around-silver]]).
