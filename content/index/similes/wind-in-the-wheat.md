## The simile

ὡς δʼ ὅτε κινήσῃ Ζέφυρος βαθὺ λήϊον ἐλθὼν / λάβρος ἐπαιγίζων, ἐπί τʼ ἠμύει ἀσταχύεσσιν: “and as when the West Wind comes and stirs a deep field of grain, blowing hard and wild, and the field bows down with its ears”. λήϊον is standing grain, and βαθύ (“deep”) means tall and thick. λάβρος is “violent, boisterous”, and ἐπαιγίζων (“rushing upon it”) is of the family of καταιγίς (“squall”). ἠμύει means “bows, nods”, and ἀσταχύεσσιν “with its ears” of grain.

The tenor follows: ὣς τῶν πᾶσʼ ἀγορὴ κινήθη, “so their whole assembly was stirred”. After the storm at sea with its two winds ([[waves-of-the-icarian-sea]]) comes one wind on one field. The point of likeness is a mass that moves all at once and all one way. Every ear bends in the same direction, and the whole crowd turns for the ships.

## In context

{{quote:57a00aad-4926-58f3-aff0-4d594febbe5a}}

The two similes describe one moment, and the verb κινέω (“stir”) runs through both: κινήθη (2.144), κινήσῃ (2.147), κινήθη (2.149). The sea shows the upheaval, the field where it goes. Then the rush itself is told: the shout, the dust hanging in the air, the launching-trenches cleared, the props pulled from under the ships “as they longed for home” (2.149–154; see [[the-test-of-the-army]]). The Argives would have had their homecoming “against fate” if Hera had not spoken to Athena (2.155–156).

The West Wind ([[zephyrus]]) is not the gentle breeze of later poetry. In the Iliad it is usually a strong, stormy wind. The verb ἠμύω (“bow”) comes back once in the book: with ten counselors like Nestor, says Agamemnon, “the city of lord Priam would soon bow its head” (ἠμύσειε, 2.373). The clause of the simile also returns, with another wind, in the last sea simile of the assembly: ὅτε κινήσῃ Νότος ἐλθών, “when the South Wind comes and stirs it” (2.395; [[wave-on-the-headland]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Grain fields return in the battle similes. In Book 11 the two armies cut into each other like reapers driving their swaths through a rich man's field of wheat or barley, and the handfuls of grain fall thick (Iliad 11.67–71).
