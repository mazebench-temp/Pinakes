## The simile

{{quote:158c66e5-e44c-5db0-8032-e4851a9ccc2d}}

γεφύρωσεν δὲ κέλευθον / μακρὴν ἠδʼ εὐρεῖαν, ὅσον τʼ ἐπὶ δουρὸς ἐρωὴ / γίγνεται, ὁππότʼ ἀνὴρ σθένεος πειρώμενος ᾗσι: “and bridged a path long and wide, as far as the cast of a spear goes, when a man throws it to test his strength” (15.357–359). γεφυρόω is to make a causeway, from γέφυρα, an embankment: the γέφυραι that a river in flood breaks are dikes (5.88–89; see [[river-in-flood]]), and the elm that Achilles catches hold of in the Xanthus falls uprooted and bridges the river (γεφύρωσεν, 21.242–246). ἐρωή is a rush or force, here the flight of a thrown spear, as it is the swing of the winnower in a simile of Book 13 (13.590; see [[beans-on-the-threshing-floor]]). σθένεος πειρώμενος is “making trial of his strength”, and ᾗσι is the subjunctive of ἵημι, “throw”.

The comparison is a measure. The width of the causeway is given in the units of a man's sport: a spear thrown not at an enemy but to see how far it will go. Beside it stands the god's ease. Apollo kicks down the banks of the trench “easily” with his feet (ῥεῖʼ, 15.356), and the next comparison, for the wall, is a boy at play in the sand (15.362–364; see [[the-boy-and-the-sand]]). What the Achaeans dug with labor, a god fills in with his feet, and the poem measures the result by a man's best effort.

## In context

The ditch had stopped the Trojan chariots. In Book 12 Hector's horses would not dare to cross it and stood neighing at its edge, and the Trojans left their chariots there and attacked the wall on foot (12.50–59, 12.84–85; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). Now Apollo has promised Hector, “I will go on ahead, and the road for the horses I will make smooth all the way” (15.260–261), and he keeps his word. Hector drives his horses on with the whip, calling to the Trojans along the ranks, and they all follow “with a wondrous din” (15.352–355). Apollo goes before them, throws the banks into the middle of the ditch, and makes the path; “there they poured forward in battalions” (15.360), and the god, holding the aegis, throws down the wall itself (15.360–366).

## Elsewhere in Homer

A throw is the Iliad's measure of a short distance. The dust raised by the marching Achaeans is so thick that a man can see only as far as he can throw a stone (3.12; see [[mist-on-the-mountain]]). When Patroclus kills Sthenelaus with a stone, the Trojans give ground as far as the cast of a long javelin that a man throws to test himself, in the games or in war (16.586–592). Achilles leaps back from the river by the length of a spear's cast, ὅσον τʼ ἐπὶ δουρὸς ἐρωή, in the words of this simile (21.251). In the chariot race Antilochus' horses and Menelaus' mares run on together for the range of a discus that a young man throws to test his youth (πειρώμενος ἥβης), and then Menelaus holds his mares back for fear of a crash (23.429–437). In the night raid Odysseus and Diomedes let Dolon get as far past them as the furrow-length of mules before they run after him (10.351–354; see [[two-hounds]]).
