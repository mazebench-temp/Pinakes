## The simile

{{quote:4ea2e4fc-2885-5a44-a5bd-93b23be29e28}}

ἑζόμενος δὲ κατʼ αὖθι φίλων ἐν χερσὶν ἑταίρων / θυμὸν ἀποπνείων, ὥς τε σκώληξ ἐπὶ γαίῃ / κεῖτο ταθείς: “And sitting down there in the arms of his dear companions, breathing out his life, like a worm on the ground he lay stretched out” (13.653–655). σκώληξ, the earthworm, is named only here in Homer (see [[worm]]). θυμὸν ἀποπνείων, “breathing out his life”, is said of Diores as he dies in the dust in the first battle (4.524). κεῖτο ταθείς, “he lay stretched out”, and the rest of the verse, ἐκ δʼ αἷμα μέλαν ῥέε, δεῦε δὲ γαῖαν, “and the dark blood flowed and soaked the earth” (13.655), return word for word when Achilles kills Lycaon in the river (21.119).

The point of likeness is the body on the ground: long, limp and low, as a worm lies on the earth. The likeness is a humble one, and it is given to a king's son. Harpalion “had followed his dear father to make war at Troy, and did not come back again to his native land” (13.644–645); he attacked Menelaus at close range and then drew back, “glancing all around, lest someone graze his flesh with the bronze” (13.646–649). The arrow takes him from behind as he goes.

## In context

Menelaus has killed Peisander and stripped him (13.601–642). Harpalion, son of Pylaemenes, king of the Paphlagonians, stabs the middle of his shield and cannot drive the bronze through (13.643–647), and Meriones shoots him as he retreats (13.650–652; see [[meriones]] and [[harpalion]]). The great-hearted Paphlagonians lift him into a chariot and take him to Ilios, grieving, “and his father went with them, letting tears fall, and no penalty was paid for his dead son” (13.656–659). That father, Pylaemenes, was killed by Menelaus in Book 5 (5.576–579), and the poem does not explain how he walks behind his son's body here (see the translation's note on 13.643–659 and [[pylaemenes-dead-and-alive]]). Paris, whose guest-friend Harpalion was, kills Euchenor in his anger (13.660–672; see [[paris]] and [[euchenor]]).
