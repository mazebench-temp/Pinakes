## The simile

τὼ δʼ ἀνʼ ὅμιλον ἰόντε κυδοίμεον, ὡς ὅτε κάπρω / ἐν κυσὶ θηρευτῇσι μέγα φρονέοντε πέσητον· / ὣς ὄλεκον Τρῶας πάλιν ὀρμένω: “and the two went through the throng spreading tumult, as when two boars fall in high spirits on the hunting dogs; so they turned again and killed the Trojans” (11.324–326). The whole comparison is in the dual, the number for a pair: ἰόντε, κάπρω, φρονέοντε, πέσητον, ὀρμένω. κυδοιμέω, “spread tumult, throw into confusion”, is made from κυδοιμός, the din of battle. κύνες θηρευταί are hunting dogs, and μέγα φρονέοντε, “in high spirits, proud”, gives the boars the confidence of fighters.

The boar is usually the beast at bay, waiting for the dogs and the hunters (see [[boar]] and [[the-boar-at-bay]]). Here the two boars attack. The point of likeness is the turn: the hunted become the hunters, and the dogs scatter. The tenor makes it explicit with πάλιν ὀρμένω, “turning again”, and gives the result for the rest of the army: “and the Achaeans were glad to take breath as they fled from brilliant Hector” (11.326–327).

## In context

Hector has killed nine leaders and is driving the Achaeans toward their ships, and they would have fallen among the ships, had not Odysseus called on Diomedes to stand with him (11.310–315). Diomedes stays, though he knows that Zeus means to give power to the Trojans (11.316–319). The two kill Thymbraeus and his attendant Molion, and the simile follows (11.320–326; see [[thymbraeus]] and [[molion]]). Then they take the two sons of Merops and kill Hippodamus and Hypeirochus (11.328–335; see [[the-sons-of-merops]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same two men are a pair of beasts in Book 10 as well, where they set out on the night raid like two lions and run Dolon down like two hounds (10.297, 10.360–364; see [[two-lions]], [[two-hounds]] and [[odysseus-and-diomedes]]). In Book 11 they are boars, and the dogs are the enemy. Two Lapiths, Polypoetes and Leonteus, hold the gate of the Achaean wall like two wild boars in the mountains that await the din of men and dogs and break the timber around them (12.145–152). Boars with lions describe pairs of fighters in Books 5 and 7 (5.782–783, 7.255–257; see [[lions-and-boars]]).
