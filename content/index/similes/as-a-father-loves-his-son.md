## The simile

{{quote:0a17f63e-0109-59c3-9bae-48536b65dd1d}}

καί μʼ ἐφίλησʼ ὡς εἴ τε πατὴρ ὃν παῖδα φιλήσῃ / μοῦνον τηλύγετον πολλοῖσιν ἐπὶ κτεάτεσσι: “and loved me as a father loves his son, his only son, his darling, heir to great possessions”. The verb is said twice, ἐφίλησε of Peleus and φιλήσῃ of the father in the simile. μοῦνος τηλύγετος is “an only son, a darling”: τηλύγετος is used of a child on whom a house's hope rests (see [[darling]]), and Agamemnon has just used it of Orestes, who is reared at home “in great abundance” (9.143, 9.285; see [[orestes]]). πολλοῖσιν ἐπὶ κτεάτεσσι, “over great possessions”, makes him the heir of a rich house. The likeness measures Peleus' love by the strongest love the poem can name, a rich man's love for the one son through whom his house will go on.

The picture fits Phoenix's story at every point. His own father Amyntor cursed him “that he would never set upon his knees a dear son born of me” (9.455–456; see [[the-flight-of-phoenix]] and [[amyntor]]). The disinherited son comes to a stranger and is received as an heir: Peleus makes him wealthy and gives him a people to rule (9.483–484; see [[dolopians]]). Then the childless man makes the foster father's son his own. He fed Achilles on his knees, and since the gods gave him no child of his own, “it was you, Achilles, like the gods, that I made my son” (9.485–495). Peleus' house in fact has one son. Achilles tells Priam that the gods gave Peleus everything but sons to rule after him: “he begot one son, doomed to an untimely death” (24.538–540; see [[peleus]]). Phoenix is loved as an only son in the house of an only son, and he claims that son as his own. The simile is the ground of his appeal (see [[fathers-and-sons]] and [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Odyssey has the same picture in a longer simile. When Telemachus comes back from Pylos and Sparta, the swineherd Eumaeus runs to him and kisses him “as a father, with a loving heart, welcomes his son who has come from a distant land in the tenth year, his only son, his darling, for whom he has suffered many sorrows” (*Odyssey* 16.17–19). There too the man who gives the father's love is not the father: Odysseus is still in disguise in the hut. The Iliad turns the image to grief when Achilles mourns at the pyre of Patroclus as a father mourns as he burns the bones of his son, a bridegroom, whose death has brought sorrow to his parents (23.222–225).
