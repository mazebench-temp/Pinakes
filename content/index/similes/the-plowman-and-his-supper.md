## The simile

{{quote:5069bcd0-4d10-56f9-aefb-d63d210eb46a}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἀνὴρ δόρποιο λιλαίεται, ᾧ τε πανῆμαρ / νειὸν ἀνʼ ἕλκητον βόε οἴνοπε πηκτὸν ἄροτρον: “And as when a man longs for his supper, for whom all day long / two wine-dark oxen drag the jointed plow through the fallow field” (*Odyssey* 13.31–32). δόρπον is the evening meal (see [[feast]]), and λιλαίεται, “longs”, takes it in the genitive, the thing longed for. πανῆμαρ, “all day long”, is found only here in Homer. The νειός is the fallow, land plowed but not yet sown (see [[the-plowing]]), and the team is a pair: βόε is a dual, and so is the verb, ἕλκητον, “drag”. The second half of the verse is the Iliad's. In the simile of the two Ajaxes two wine-dark oxen pull the jointed plow through a fallow field, ἐν νειῷ βόε οἴνοπε πηκτὸν ἄροτρον (*Iliad* 13.703; see [[oxen-at-the-plow]], [[wine-dark]] and [[cattle]]). πηκτός, “put together”, is an epithet that describes the workmanship, as ποιητός is said of a house (*Odyssey* 13.306): we need not translate “well made”, though that may be implied, for the point is that the plow is a work of mechanical skill (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 13.32; see [[jointed]] and the translation's row νειός; πηκτὸν ἄροτρον).

ἀσπασίως δʼ ἄρα τῷ κατέδυ φάος ἠελίοιο / δόρπον ἐποίχεσθαι, βλάβεται δέ τε γούνατʼ ἰόντι: “and gladly for him the light of the sun sets, / so that he may go about his supper, and his knees are hindered as he goes” (*Odyssey* 13.33–34). φάος ἠελίοιο, “the light of the sun”, ends the verse of the poems' sunsets, κατέδυ λαμπρὸν φάος ἠελίοιο (*Iliad* 1.605; see [[sunset-lines]] and [[helios]]). Cobet and Nauck would have removed the adverb ἀσπασίως, Cobet reading the adjective ἀσπασίῳ and Nauck ἀσπάσιον, to match ἀσπαστόν in the tenor; Monro keeps it and compares the Iliad's men who are glad, ἀσπασίως, to bend the knee in rest or to take breath (Monro on *Odyssey* 13.33, citing *Iliad* 7.118 and 11.327). δόρπον ἐποίχεσθαι is “in that he can now go about his supper” (Monro on *Odyssey* 13.34). The phrase is made like ἔργον ἐποίχεσθαι, “to go about their work”, which opens the verse when Hector sends Andromache back to her loom and bids her tell her handmaids to go about their work, and when Telemachus sends his mother back to hers (*Iliad* 6.490–492; *Odyssey* 1.356–358): the plowman's work is done, and he goes about his supper. βλάβεται, “are hindered”, is an anomalous present, for which the aorist βλάβετο can always be read (Monro's critical note on *Odyssey* 13.34). The half-verse is Odysseus' own in the Iliad. Urging Achilles to let the army eat before it fights, he says that no man can fight all day until the sun goes down without food, for even if his heart is eager, “yet his limbs grow heavy unawares, and thirst and hunger / overtake him, and his knees are hindered as he goes” (*Iliad* 19.162–166; see [[food-and-wine-are-might-and-courage]] and [[gounata]]).

The tenor is a single verse: ὣς Ὀδυσῆʼ ἀσπαστὸν ἔδυ φάος ἠελίοιο, “so welcome to Odysseus did the light of the sun set” (*Odyssey* 13.35). It repeats the end of the plowman's verse, and the adverb ἀσπασίως, “gladly”, comes back in it as the adjective ἀσπαστόν, “welcome”. The point of likeness is the longing for the end of a day. The plowman wants his supper and his rest; Odysseus wants the night, for the Phaeacians sail by night, and the sunset is the beginning of his voyage home.

## In context

On the morning after the tale the Phaeacians bring their gifts to the ship, and Alcinous stows them himself under the thwarts; then they feast all day in his house, on an ox that he sacrifices to Zeus, and Demodocus sings (*Odyssey* 13.18–28; see [[possessions]], [[feast]] and [[demodocus]]). “But Odysseus / kept turning his head toward the all-gleaming sun, / in haste for it to set; for he was eager to go home” (*Odyssey* 13.28–30). δῦναι ἐπειγόμενος is “in haste, eager for (his) setting” (Monro on *Odyssey* 13.30). Alcinous had promised that on the day of the escort Odysseus would lie down overcome by sleep, and the young men would row him over the calm to his native land (*Odyssey* 7.317–320; see [[pompe]]). As soon as the sun has set he asks the king to send him on his way; the Phaeacians pour libations, and he puts the cup into Arete's hand with his farewell (*Odyssey* 13.36–62; see [[libation]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). That night, while the ship runs like four stallions under the whip, he sleeps “most like death” (*Odyssey* 13.79–92; see [[the-four-stallions]] and [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]]).

The guest of a king, at a feast with a divine singer, is likened to a laborer at the end of his day. On his first evening in Scheria Odysseus had asked for his supper in the same hall, “for there is nothing more shameless than the hateful belly” (*Odyssey* 7.215–216; see [[gaster]]). Now it is not supper that he wants. His long labor is summed up as he sails: he “before had suffered very many sorrows in his heart, … but now he slept motionless, forgetful of all he had suffered” (*Odyssey* 13.90–92).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The plow team is the image of hard labor, and of a pair that works as one. The two Ajaxes stand side by side like two oxen straining at the plow, with only the yoke between them (*Iliad* 13.701–708; see [[oxen-at-the-plow]]); mules are better than oxen at dragging the jointed plow through the deep fallow (*Iliad* 10.351–353); and on the shield of Achilles many plowmen wheel their teams across a broad fallow, and at each turn a man puts a cup of honey-sweet wine into their hands (*Iliad* 18.541–547; see [[the-plowing]]).

Homer tells the hours of the day by a man's work. The battle turns “at the hour when a woodcutter makes ready his meal / in the glens of a mountain” (*Iliad* 11.86–90), the afternoon is the time for unyoking oxen (*Iliad* 16.779; *Odyssey* 9.58), and the timbers come up from Charybdis at the hour when a judge rises from the assembly for his supper (*Odyssey* 12.439–441; see [[the-judge-rising-for-supper]]). The plowman's supper comes at sunset.

Odysseus himself claims the plowman's strength when the suitor Eurymachus mocks him. If there were oxen to drive, “the best there are, / great and tawny, both well fed with grass, / … then you would see me, whether I would cut a long furrow before me”, and he would mow with a sickle in spring, “fasting right until darkness” (*Odyssey* 18.366–375, a plain rendering).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells the farmer what kind of man should follow the team: a man of forty, “who has dined on a loaf of four quarters, eight portions”, and who will mind his work and drive a straight furrow, no longer looking about after his fellows, but with his heart on the work (*Works and Days* 441–445; see [[hesiod]]). Hesiod's plowman is a well-fed man who keeps his eyes on the furrow; Homer's longs for his supper at the end of the day.
