## The simile

αὐτὰρ ἐγὼν ἐπόρουσα κελαινῇ λαίλαπι ἶσος: “but I swept upon them like a black storm” (11.747). λαῖλαψ is a squall, a storm of wind and rain, and κελαινός is “black, dark”, the color of storm clouds and of night. ἐπόρουσα, “I rushed upon”, is the verb of an attack. The comparison is a half-verse and builds no scene. The point of likeness is sudden, overwhelming force, and the darkness that comes with it.

Nestor speaks it of himself: the old man tells his own youth in the language in which the narrator tells the deeds of warriors. The next verses give the storm's damage: “and I took fifty chariots, and around each two men bit the ground with their teeth, beaten down by my spear” (11.748–749).

## In context

Nestor is telling Patroclus of his war with the Epeians. Athena roused the Pylians by night; Neleus hid Nestor's horses, thinking him too young, but Nestor went on foot and stood out among the horsemen (11.714–721). At the Alpheus the battle began, and Nestor was the first to kill a man, Mulius, son-in-law of Augeas, the leader of the Epeian horsemen. He leapt into Mulius' chariot, the Epeians fled in fear this way and that, and he fell on them like a storm (11.737–749; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]], [[mulius]] and [[nestor]]). He would have killed the two Moliones too, had Poseidon not hidden them in mist (11.750–752; see [[the-moliones]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The half-verse is formulaic. The leaders of the Lycians climb onto the battlements of the Achaean wall like a dark storm (ἐρεμνῇ λαίλαπι ἶσοι, 12.375–376), and when the gods go down to battle in Book 20, Ares shouts like a dark storm from the top of the city and along the Simoeis (20.51–53). The squall itself drives other similes: the West Wind batters the clouds “with a deep squall” as Hector cuts down the multitude earlier in this book (βαθείῃ λαίλαπι, 11.306; see [[the-west-wind-and-the-clouds]]), and the goatherd's storm cloud “brings a great storm” (λαίλαπα πολλήν, 4.278; see [[storm-cloud]]).
