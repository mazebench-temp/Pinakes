## The simile

{{quote:bf145737-e042-5ebf-abf1-e6b9852401ad}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτε ταρφειαὶ νιφάδες Διὸς ἐκποτέονται / ψυχραὶ ὑπὸ ῥιπῆς αἰθρηγενέος Βορέαο: “And as when snowflakes fly thick and fast from Zeus, / cold, under the rush of the North Wind, born of the clear sky” (19.357–358). ταρφειαί, “thick and fast”, is the adjective of the snowflakes that a blustering wind pours down on the earth in the first snow simile of *Iliad* 12 (ταρφειάς, 12.158; see [[a-snow-of-stones]]). Διός is “from Zeus”: Walter Leaf finds in the genitive clear evidence of an early sense of the god's name, “sky”, as in the line where the roar of both armies “reached the upper air and the rays of Zeus” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.357; 13.837; see [[zeus]] and the translation's note on 19.357). ἐκποτέονται, “fly forth”, is found only here in Homer. The second verse is the verse of the snow or hail to which Iris is likened as she goes down from Ida, there in the singular, ψυχρή (15.171; see [[snow-and-hail]], [[boreas]] and [[born-of-the-clear-sky]]).

The tenor takes up the adjective: ὣς τότε ταρφειαὶ κόρυθες λαμπρὸν γανόωσαι / νηῶν ἐκφορέοντο καὶ ἀσπίδες ὀμφαλόεσσαι / θώρηκές τε κραταιγύαλοι καὶ μείλινα δοῦρα, “so then, thick and fast, the helmets, shining brightly, / were carried out from the ships, and bossed shields / and corselets with strong plates and ash spears” (19.359–361; see [[helmet]], [[shield]], [[corselet]] and [[spear]]). The list is the one with which Idomeneus answers Meriones in *Iliad* 13, in another order: “So I have spears and bossed shields / and helmets and corselets shining brightly” (13.264–265). ἐκφορέοντο, “were carried out”, is said of the arms but means the men who wear them; Leaf calls it a rather curious phrase, more epic in its simplicity than the paraphrases that would hide its baldness, and quotes Heyne's note that the arms are named for the armed (on 19.360). κραταιγύαλοι, “with strong plates”, occurs only here; Leaf takes the γύαλα as the solid breastplate and backplate of the corselet and the verse, from its awkward position, as a late addition (on 19.361; see the translation's note on 19.361).

The likeness is of number and motion. In the other snow similes the flakes fall thick and cover everything (12.156–158, 12.278–286); here they fly before the wind, and the arms stream out of the ships. The snow is cold, and the arms shine.

## In context

Achilles has refused to eat until sunset, and Zeus sends Athena to drip nectar and ambrosia into his breast. She goes down “like a long-winged, shrill-voiced bird of prey”, while “the Achaeans / were at once arming throughout the army”, and when she has gone back to her father's house, “they poured out away from the swift ships” (19.340–356; see [[like-a-bird-of-prey]] and [[athena]]). The simile pictures the pouring out.

After it come the light and the noise: “And the gleam reached the sky, and all the earth around laughed / under the flash of the bronze; and beneath rose a din from the feet / of men” (19.362–364). The gleam that reaches the sky is the gleam of the army going out in *Iliad* 2, which “went up through the upper air to the sky” (2.457–458; see [[forest-fire]], [[sky]] and [[upper-air]]). γέλασσε, “laughed”, is said of the shining earth: Leaf holds that laughing and shining pass so naturally into one another that the word cannot be confined to the shining, though that is its first sense (on 19.362; see [[earth]] and [[bronze]]). The whole wide sky above and the whole earth laughed, and the salt swell of the sea, at the scent of the narcissus that Earth made grow as a snare for Persephone (*Homeric Hymn to Demeter* 8–14), and in Hesiod the house of Zeus laughs at the voice of the Muses (*Theogony* 40–41). In the midst of the army Achilles arms (19.364; see [[arming-scene]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The snow of the Iliad is many things falling together. The stones from the Achaean wall fall like snowflakes that a blustering wind pours down thick (12.156–158; see [[a-snow-of-stones]]), and the stones of both sides fly as thick as the snow that Zeus pours down on a windless winter's day until it covers the land to the edge of the sea (12.278–289; see [[the-great-snowfall]]). Antenor remembers Odysseus' words “like the snowflakes of winter” (3.222; see [[snowflakes-of-winter]]). Iris alone is likened to a single fall of snow or hail, for the speed of her descent (15.170–172). Here the snow is the army's arms on the morning Achilles goes out to fight.

## Outside Homer

Lucretius gives the picture of 19.362–364 to legions on maneuvers: the gleam rises to the sky, the earth all around shines with bronze (*totaque circum / aere renidescit tellus*), and beneath, under the force of men, a sound is roused by their feet (Lucretius 2.323–327). Leaf quotes the line on 19.362, with Horace's house that laughs with silver (*ridet argento domus*, *Odes* 4.11.6).
