## The simile

{{quote:319c60ba-51dc-5d2c-abb6-8b532b7e7dde}}

ὃ δʼ ἀνέδραμεν ἔρνεϊ ἶσος· / τὸν μὲν ἐγὼ θρέψασα φυτὸν ὣς γουνῷ ἀλωῆς: “and he shot up like a sapling; / I reared him like a plant on the slope of an orchard” (18.56–57). ἔρνος is a young shoot or sapling, the word of the olive tree that a man rears with care in the simile for Euphorbus (17.53; see [[the-olive-sapling]]). ἀνέδραμεν, “shot up”, is literally “ran up”, and Walter Leaf notes that the metaphor of a growing shoot is a familiar one in English too (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.56). φυτόν is anything that grows, a plant; θρέψασα, “having reared”, is from τρέφω, the verb for bringing up children and for tending plants alike (τρέφει, of the man and his olive, 17.53). γουνῷ ἀλωῆς, “on the slope of an orchard”, is said once more in the Iliad, of the orchard of Oeneus, where he made no harvest offering to Artemis (9.534), and in the Odyssey, in the form γουνὸν ἀλωῆς, of the vineyard where old Laertes creeps about and sleeps on fallen leaves (*Odyssey* 1.193, 11.193). On this line Leaf refers to his note on 9.534, where he reports that γουνός is generally taken from γόνυ, “knee”, as the rise or swell of the land, the part most exposed to the sun and so the most fertile, but finds Hesychius' gloss, “a fertile place”, more reasonable. An ἀλωή is planted ground, an orchard here and a vineyard on the Shield (18.561; see [[the-vintage]]).

The likeness is of quick growth and of care. The son grew as a young tree grows, and the mother tended him as a gardener tends a plant in the best ground. The simile sits in the middle of a lament. Its first half tells what Thetis did: she bore a son, “preeminent among heroes”, and reared him, and sent him with the ships to Ilios (18.55–59). The second half tells what she will not have: “but I shall not welcome him again / returning home to the house of Peleus” (18.59–60; see [[nostos]]). The growth of the sapling is the measure of the life that will not grow old.

## In context

Achilles' groan at the news of Patroclus' death reaches his mother in the depths of the sea, where she sits beside her aged father; she wails, and the Nereids gather around her in the silver-white cave, beat their breasts with her, and she leads the lamentation (18.35–51; see [[thetis]]). She calls herself δυσαριστοτόκεια, “unhappy mother of the best of men”, a word found only here (18.54; see the translation's note on 18.54). It is a lament for a son who is still alive, as the women of Hector's house in Book 6 “lamented Hector in his own house while he was still alive” (6.500). Its last lines turn back to the present: “And while he lives and looks on the light of the sun / he grieves, and I can do nothing to help him by going to him” (18.61–62). Yet she goes, to hear what grief has come on him (18.63–64).

Thetis says the lines again in the house of Hephaestus (18.436–443 repeat 18.55–62, with a new first verse, “since he gave me a son to bear and to rear”). There they open the story of her son's grievances, the girl taken from him, the embassy refused, Patroclus sent out in his armor and killed (18.444–456), and they end in a request: “That is why I come now to your knees” for a shield and helmet, greaves and corselet for her swift-fated son (18.457–461; see [[the-making-of-the-arms]]). The lament has become the ground of a supplication.

She had said the same thing more plainly in Book 1: “Ah me, my child, why did I raise you, when I bore you to such sorrow?” (1.414), and there too she called him swift-fated (1.417; see [[swift-fated]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The sapling of Book 17 is Euphorbus, a young olive that a man has reared in a lonely place with water in plenty, until a storm tears it from its trench and stretches it on the ground (17.53–60; see [[the-olive-sapling]]). There the tree is felled; Thetis' simile stops before the fall, but the next verses say that the son will not come home.

Children are young shoots elsewhere in Homer. Hecuba, begging Hector not to face Achilles, calls him φίλον θάλος, “dear offshoot”, whom she bore herself (22.87). In the Odyssey Odysseus likens Nausicaa to the young shoot of a palm that he once saw by the altar of Apollo on Delos (*Odyssey* 6.162–167), and Eumaeus says that the gods reared Telemachus “like a sapling” (ἔρνεϊ ἶσον, *Odyssey* 14.175), the words of Thetis' simile.
