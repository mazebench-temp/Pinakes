## The simile

{{quote:b419ef62-ac58-5d67-901f-aca32c0e80e2}}

νὺξ δʼ ἄρʼ ἐπῆλθε κακὴ Βορέαο πεσόντος, / πηγυλίς· αὐτὰρ ὕπερθε χιὼν γένετʼ ἠΰτε πάχνη, / ψυχρή, καὶ σακέεσσι περιτρέφετο κρύσταλλος: “And an evil night came on, when the North Wind fell, / freezing; and from above came snow like hoarfrost, / cold, and ice grew thick on our shields” (*Odyssey* 14.475–477; see the translation's row πηγυλίς; πάχνη; κρύσταλλος). πηγυλίς, “freezing”, from the stem of πήγνυμι, “make solid, freeze”, and πάχνη, “hoarfrost”, are found nowhere else in Homer; the related verb παχνοῦται is said once in the Iliad, of the valiant heart of a lion that “grows chill” as dogs and men drive it from a farmyard (17.109–112; see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]). The Suda explains the one word by the other: στίβη, “frost”, is “πηγυλίς, or πάχνη” (Suda σ 1099), and πηγυλίς is “the cold” (Suda π 1492). Βορέαο πεσόντος is “when the North Wind fell”, that is, ceased to blow, as ἄνεμος πέσε in another false tale, the one that Odysseus tells Penelope (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 14.475, comparing *Odyssey* 19.202; see [[boreas]]).

The likeness is of snow to frost, and the poem does not say where it lies: the snow that comes down from above is likened to the frost that forms on the ground on a still night, and both are cold. The comparison has seemed weak to some readers: Naber proposed ἠΰτε λάχνη, “like wool”, an emendation that Monro thinks very plausible (on *Odyssey* 14.476); λάχνη is the woolly nap of a cloak and the down on a young man's cheek (10.134; *Odyssey* 11.320). The cold is told in three steps: the night is freezing, the snow is cold, and the ice grows on the shields. περιτρέφετο, “grew thick”, “solid”, is the verb of milk curdling and of brine caked on the skin (Monro on *Odyssey* 14.477, comparing *Odyssey* 9.246 and 23.237), and of the milk that sets in the simile of Paeëon's cure (5.902–903; see [[fig-juice]]). κρύσταλλος, “ice”, is named once more in Homer, in the cold spring of the Scamander, which “in summer flows forth like hail, / or cold snow, or ice formed from water” (22.151–152; see [[the-springs-of-the-scamander]]).

## In context

The tale is told on a night like the one it tells of. After the evening meal at Eumaeus' hut “an evil night came on, a night without a moon, and Zeus rained / all night long, and a great West Wind blew, always wet” (*Odyssey* 14.457–458), and Odysseus, “testing the swineherd, / whether he would take off his cloak and give it to him” (*Odyssey* 14.459–460), tells of an ambush beneath Troy, led by Odysseus and Menelaus, with the teller third in command (*Odyssey* 14.468–471; see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]] and [[ambush]]). The men lay among the reeds and the marsh under the city wall, and the snow fell. “Then all the others had cloaks and tunics, / and slept at ease, their shoulders wrapped in their shields”; the large shield swung round to the back served as a shelter, almost as an outer garment (*Odyssey* 14.478–479; Monro on *Odyssey* 14.479; see [[shield]]). The teller had left his cloak behind and come with only his shield and his kilt (*Odyssey* 14.480–482; see [[cloak]] and [[kilt]]), and in the third part of the night he told Odysseus, “the cold / is bringing me down; for I have no cloak” (*Odyssey* 14.487–488). Odysseus' device sent Thoas running to the ships, and the teller lay in Thoas' crimson cloak until dawn (*Odyssey* 14.490–502; see [[thoas]] and [[crimson]]). The snow and the ice make the need for a cloak; the rain and the West Wind of the night in the hut make the point of the tale, and Eumaeus takes it: he gives the stranger a cloak for the night, the thick one he keeps “to wear whenever some terrible storm arose” (*Odyssey* 14.508–522; see [[ainos]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Iliad's snow is a likeness for what falls thick and fast: stones from the Achaean wall like the snow of a winter's day (12.156–158, 12.278–289; see [[a-snow-of-stones]] and [[the-great-snowfall]]), helmets and shields pouring out of the ships like snowflakes from Zeus (19.357–361; see [[snowflakes-from-zeus]]), Iris flying like snow or hail (15.170–172; see [[snow-and-hail]]), and Odysseus' words like the snowflakes of winter (3.222; see [[snowflakes-of-winter]]). Here the snow is not a likeness but the weather of the story, and the comparison runs the other way, from snow to frost. In the Odyssey frost is the enemy of the castaway and of the beggar: cast up on the shore of Scheria, Odysseus fears that “the cruel frost and the fresh dew together” may overcome him if he keeps watch by the river (*Odyssey* 5.466–467), and in the town he asks to warm himself at the fire, since his clothes are bad and the frost of early morning may overcome him (*Odyssey* 17.23–25, a plain rendering).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod's winter has the same words. In the month of Lenaion, when the North Wind blows through Thrace, he tells the farmer to put on a soft cloak and a tunic reaching to his feet (*Works and Days* 504–537), “for the dawn is cold when the North Wind has fallen”, ψυχρὴ γάρ τʼ ἠὼς πέλεται Βορέαο πεσόντος (*Works and Days* 547). Aristotle explains hoarfrost as vapor that freezes before it has condensed into water, in clear and windless weather; snow and hoarfrost, he says, are the same thing, as rain and dew are, differing only in amount, for when the cloud freezes it is snow, when the vapor freezes it is hoarfrost (*Meteorology* 1.10, 347a15–28; 1.11, 347b15–24).
