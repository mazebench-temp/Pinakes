## The simile

{{quote:f1981b7f-6fc0-5b6c-aaca-71eee703010f}}

ἡ μὲν ἄρʼ ὣς εἰποῦσʼ ἀπέβη γλαυκῶπις Ἀθήνη, / ὄρνις δʼ ὣς ἀνόπαια διέπτατο: “So saying, gray-eyed Athena went away, / and like a bird she flew upward and away” (*Odyssey* 1.319–320). ὄρνις is the common word for a bird, and no kind is named. ὣς, “like”, stands after its noun and so takes an accent, as in ἴρηξ ὣς, “like a hawk”, of Thetis leaping down from Olympus (18.616; see [[like-a-hawk]]), and ὄρνιθες ὣς, “like birds”, of the Trojans coming on with clamor (3.2; see [[cranes-and-pygmies]]). διέπτατο, “flew”, is said in Homer only of goddesses, and each time in a comparison of speed: Hera flies to Olympus as swiftly as the mind of a man who has traveled over much of the earth (15.80–83; see [[the-travelers-thought]]), and Iris goes down to Poseidon as swiftly as snow or hail flies from the clouds (15.170–172; see [[snow-and-hail]]). ἀνόπαια is found only here.

The likeness is of the going, swift, sudden and upward, as no guest goes. It is told in four words, and its effect at once: Athena puts might and daring in Telemachus' heart and makes him think of his father more than before, and he perceives it and is amazed, “for he thought that it was a god” (*Odyssey* 1.320–323).

## The word ἀνόπαια

The word was disputed in antiquity, and W. W. Merry sets out the readings (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.320). The Homeric lexicon of Apollonius gives three: some took ἀνόπαια as the name of a bird, since elsewhere Athena goes “in the likeness of a sea eagle” (φήνῃ εἰδομένη, *Odyssey* 3.372); some as ἀνοπαίως, that is, “unseen”; and some as “up through the opening”, ὀπή, the window or smoke-hole. “Unseen” was the view of Herodian, according to the scholia on 18.318 and 21.417, and Merry finds some support for it in the contrast with Pylos, where the sight itself is told, “amazement seized all who saw it” (*Odyssey* 3.372), while here Telemachus only perceives that the goddess has gone. For the smoke-hole Merry cites a note in Cramer's *Anecdota Oxoniensia* (1.83), which gives Aristophanes' explanation of ὀπαία and ἀνόπαια as the smoke-vent, the old houses having their openings for air in the roof, and Cassius Longinus' reading of the verse as a swallow flying out by the hole; Gerlach took the ὀπαῖα to be the spaces between the ends of the roof beams.

Merry himself prefers the meaning that Eustathius drew from Empedocles' use of the word for fire, καρπαλίμως ἀνόπαιον, “swiftly upward”, and adds that Herodotus gives the name Anopaea to the mountain and the path over it by which the Persians were led around the pass of Thermopylae (Herodotus 7.216). The translation follows him (see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 1.320).

## Simile or transformation?

Those who took ἀνόπαια as the name of a bird held that the verse is no simile but tells of a phantom (εἴδωλον) of Pallas in a bird's form, and they pointed to two other departures of the goddess. At Pylos she leaves Nestor's company in the likeness of a sea eagle, to the amazement of all who see it (*Odyssey* 3.371–373), and in the slaughter of the suitors she perches on the roof beam of the smoky hall, like a swallow to look upon (*Odyssey* 22.239–240). Merry answers that ὄρνις ὥς cannot describe a phantom. It is a simile, compressed into two words, of the kind Odysseus uses of himself when he clings to the fig tree above Charybdis “like a bat” (ὡς νυκτερίς, *Odyssey* 12.433), and compression does without naming the kind of bird. He sets the long simile of Hera's flight (15.80–83) beside its brief echo in the Odyssey, where the ships of the Phaeacians are swift “as a wing or a thought” (*Odyssey* 7.36). He reports La Roche's observation that where Homer adds the kind to ὄρνις, an eagle or a falcon, the bird is an omen (12.200–201, 13.821–822; *Odyssey* 15.160–161, 15.525–526, 20.242–243).

The index reads the verse with Merry as a simile, as it reads the gods who sit “like birds, like vultures” on the oak of Zeus (7.58–61; see [[like-vultures]]). The question is the same for Athena's descent like a bird of prey (19.350–351; see [[like-a-bird-of-prey]]) and for Sleep hidden in a fir on Ida like a shrill bird (14.289–291; see [[chalcis-and-cymindis]]). The text says only what Telemachus perceives. Whether he saw a bird or saw the stranger vanish, he knows that a god has been with him (see [[divine-epiphany]] and [[thambos]]).

## In context

Athena has come to Ithaca in the likeness of Mentes, lord of the Taphians, been welcomed and fed, told her false tale and given her counsel; she refuses the guest-gift that Telemachus offers and asks him to give it on her way back (*Odyssey* 1.96–318; see [[athena]], [[mentes-the-taphian]] and [[xenia]]). Then she goes. What she does in going is what she told Zeus she would do in Ithaca, “urge his son on / the more, and put might in his heart” (*Odyssey* 1.88–89): “she put might and daring” in his heart (*Odyssey* 1.320–321; see [[menos]] and [[tharsos]]). “And at once he went among the suitors, a man equal to the gods” (*Odyssey* 1.324; see [[equal-to-the-gods]]).

The suitors see only a guest who left in haste. Eurymachus asks who the stranger was: “How he sprang up and was gone at once, and did not wait / to be known” (*Odyssey* 1.410–411). Telemachus gives him the name of Mentes, son of Anchialus, “but in his heart he knew the immortal goddess” (*Odyssey* 1.417–420; see [[telemachus]] and [[eurymachus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Athena leaves in a bird's likeness twice more in the Odyssey. At Pylos the verse that ends with her departure, ὣς ἄρα φωνήσασʼ ἀπέβη γλαυκῶπις Ἀθήνη, is followed by φήνῃ εἰδομένη, “in the likeness of a sea eagle”, and amazement seizes all who see; Nestor takes Telemachus by the hand and tells him that this was Athena, who honored his father among the Argives (*Odyssey* 3.371–379). In the battle in the hall she flies up to the roof beam and sits there like a swallow (*Odyssey* 22.239–240). Hermes skims the waves like a gull that hunts fish in the gulfs of the sea (λάρῳ ὄρνιθι ἐοικώς, *Odyssey* 5.51–54), and Ino rises from the sea and goes back into it like a diving sea bird (αἰθυίῃ ἐικυῖα, *Odyssey* 5.337, 5.353).

In the Iliad the gods go and come like birds of prey. Poseidon leaves the two Ajaxes as a hawk rises from a sheer rock to chase another bird across the plain, and the lesser Ajax knows him for a god by the movements of his feet and legs as he goes, “and gods are easy to know” (13.62–72; see [[the-hawk-from-the-rock]]). Apollo goes down from Ida like a hawk, a swift dove-killer (15.237–238; see [[like-a-hawk]]); Thetis leaps down from snowy Olympus like a hawk with the new armor of Achilles (18.616–617); and Athena leaps down from the sky like a long-winged, shrill-voiced bird of prey (19.350–351). As in Ithaca, the god's going is a sign: Ajax knows Poseidon from behind, and Nestor and Telemachus know Athena by her flight.
