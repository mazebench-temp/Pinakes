## The simile

{{quote:8c2f5daf-443b-5b65-bfad-82d832461789}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἀφʼ ὑψηλῆς κορυφῆς ὄρεος μεγάλοιο / κινήσῃ πυκινὴν νεφέλην στεροπηγερέτα Ζεύς: “And as when from the high peak of a great mountain / Zeus who gathers the lightning moves away a thick cloud” (16.297–298). κινέω, “move, set in motion”, is the verb of the West Wind stirring the deep grain and of the South Wind raising a wave against a headland (2.147, 2.395). The cloud is πυκινή, “thick, close”. στεροπηγερέτα, “gatherer of the lightning”, is found only here in Homer, made like Zeus' common title νεφεληγερέτα, “cloud-gatherer” (16.666; see [[cloud-gatherer]]): the god who gathers the clouds also takes them away.

Then the view: ἔκ τʼ ἔφανεν πᾶσαι σκοπιαὶ καὶ πρώονες ἄκροι / καὶ νάπαι, οὐρανόθεν δʼ ἄρʼ ὑπερράγη ἄσπετος αἰθήρ, “and all the lookout places and the high headlands stand out, / and the glens; and from the sky the boundless upper air breaks open” (16.299–300). σκοπιαί are the heights from which a man keeps watch, πρώονες the jutting spurs of the mountain, νάπαι its wooded hollows. The αἰθήρ is the bright upper air above the clouds, and it “breaks open” (ὑπερράγη) when the cloud is gone.

The tenor: ὣς Δαναοὶ νηῶν μὲν ἀπωσάμενοι δήϊον πῦρ / τυτθὸν ἀνέπνευσαν, πολέμου δʼ οὐ γίγνετʼ ἐρωή, “so the Danaans, when they had thrust the consuming fire from the ships, / drew breath a little, but there was no respite from the war” (16.301–302). ἐρωή is here “respite”, from ἐρωέω, “draw back”, not the ἐρωή that is the rush of a thrown spear (see the translation's note on 16.302 and [[the-cast-of-a-spear]]). The point of likeness is relief: the cloud that lay on the mountain is lifted, and everything stands clear. But the tenor takes back half of what the vehicle gives. The Danaans breathe only “a little”, for the Trojans are not yet fleeing in headlong rout from the black ships; they still stand against them and give way from the ships only under compulsion (16.303–305).

## In context

Hector had cut through Ajax's spear, the Trojans had thrown fire on the ship of Protesilaus, and Achilles, seeing the flame, sent Patroclus out (16.114–129; see [[ajax]] and [[protesilaus]]). Patroclus' first spear kills Pyraechmes, the leader of the Paeonians, beside the stern of that ship; the Paeonians flee, he drives the Trojans from the ships and puts out the blazing fire, and the half-burned ship is left there (16.284–294; see [[pyraechmes]] and [[patroclus]]). The ships are saved, and the simile marks the moment.

Its drawing of breath answers the book's first speeches. Patroclus asked for Achilles' armor in the hope that the Trojans might take him for Achilles and hold off, and “the warlike sons of the Achaeans may draw breath, / worn out as they are; and brief is the breathing space in war” (16.41–43, in the words of Nestor's advice, 11.800–801). Before the fire fell on the ships Ajax had no way at all to draw breath (16.110–111). Now the Achaeans breathe, and the breathing space is brief, as Patroclus said it would be. Achilles had spoken of the Trojans as “a dark cloud” settled about the ships (κυάνεον Τρώων νέφος, 16.66); in the simile Zeus moves the cloud from the peak.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The two verses of the clearing are those of the simile of the Trojan watch fires at the end of *Iliad* 8, which burn like the stars around the moon on a windless night, when every lookout, headland and glen stands out, the boundless air breaks open and all the stars are seen (8.555–559, with 8.557–558 = 16.299–300; see [[stars-around-the-moon]]). There the clear sky shows the Trojans' fires on the plain; here it shows the Achaeans' relief when the fire on the ships is out. The breaking open of the sky suits the moving of a cloud better than a night already windless, and the two verses are reported to have been rejected in Book 8 in antiquity (see the translation's note on 8.557–558). Walter Leaf reports that Aristarchus and Aristophanes athetized them there and Zenodotus left them out, as brought in from *Iliad* 16; he agreed that they belong more fitly here, where the clouds are burst open and the peaks are those of a mountain already named, though he thought they had stood in Book 8 from the first (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 8.557–558); of Book 16 he wrote, “There is no question of the appropriateness of these grand lines here” (on 16.299–300).

Zeus' clouds sit on the mountain tops in other similes: in *Iliad* 5 the Danaans stand like clouds that the son of Cronus has set motionless on the peaks in windless weather (5.522–527; see [[clouds-on-the-mountains]]). In Book 17, when a mist hides the fight over Patroclus, Ajax prays to Zeus to make clear sky, and to destroy the Achaeans in the light if he must; Zeus pities him, scatters the mist, and the sun shines out on the whole battle (17.645–650).
