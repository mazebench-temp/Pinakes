## The simile

{{quote:56711d88-149a-5054-8e1d-a37a7c71396b}}

The comparison comes in two steps. First the shield: τοῦ δʼ ἀπάνευθε σέλας γένετʼ ἠΰτε μήνης, “and from it, far off, came a blaze as of the moon” (19.374; see [[moon]]). Then the long simile: ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἂν ἐκ πόντοιο σέλας ναύτῃσι φανήῃ / καιομένοιο πυρός, τό τε καίεται ὑψόθʼ ὄρεσφι / σταθμῷ ἐν οἰοπόλῳ· τοὺς δʼ οὐκ ἐθέλοντας ἄελλαι / πόντον ἐπʼ ἰχθυόεντα φίλων ἀπάνευθε φέρουσιν: “And as when to sailors out at sea there appears the blaze / of a burning fire, which burns high up in the mountains / in a lonely farmstead; and storm winds bear them, unwilling, / over the fish-filled sea, far from their friends” (19.375–378). σέλας, “blaze”, is the word of the lights of this book: Achilles' eyes shine out “like a blaze” when he sees the armor, and “like the blaze of fire” when he puts it on (19.17, 19.366; see [[eyes-like-fire]]), and it is said three times in six verses of the shield and of the fire it is likened to (19.374, 19.375, 19.379). ἐκ πόντοιο, “out at sea”, places the sailors, not the fire: Walter Leaf explains it by the idiom in which the source of a perception is put in the one who perceives, so that the light appears to sailors “(seeing it) from the sea” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.375; see the translation's note on 19.375). The σταθμός is a farmstead or steading, very likely a sheep-station to Leaf (on 19.377; compare 18.589), and οἰοπόλος is “lonely”, as of the place where the boar of Idomeneus' simile stands at bay (13.471–473; see [[lonely]]). The sea is ἰχθυόεις, “fish-filled” (see [[fish-filled]] and [[sea]]).

The tenor: ὣς ἀπʼ Ἀχιλλῆος σάκεος σέλας αἰθέρʼ ἵκανε / καλοῦ δαιδαλέου, “so from the shield of Achilles the blaze reached the upper air, / the fine shield, intricately worked” (19.379–380; see [[upper-air]] and [[intricately-worked]]). It is the verse of the flame that Athena kindled on Achilles' head at the ditch, with “shield” in place of “head” (18.214; see [[the-beacon-fires]]).

Leaf finds the point of the comparison in the distance at which a light on a high hill can be seen at sea, and perhaps also the thought that the sight of Achilles in his shining armor cheers his men as a shepherd's fire cheers sailors driven off their course who do not know where they are (on 19.375). The simile gives the sailors' plight in its own words: the storm carries them “unwilling”, “far from their friends”. He also reports that 374, the moon, has been suspected since Hermann and Heyne, because it does not go well with the simile that follows: where comparisons are heaped up elsewhere, each shows a different aspect of the thing, and the moon suggests a round shield, which there is no ground for thinking that Achilles bore (on 19.374; compare 23.455, and see the translation's note on 19.369–373).

## In context

Achilles arms in the order of the other arming scenes: greaves, corselet, sword, and then the shield, “great and heavy” (19.369–373; see [[arming-scene]] and [[the-shield-of-achilles]]). The army has just poured out of the ships as thick as snowflakes, and its gleam reached the sky (19.357–363; see [[snowflakes-from-zeus]]); the shield's blaze reaches the upper air. The lights go on: the helmet shines like a star (19.381; see [[like-a-star]]), and when Achilles mounts the chariot he gleams in his armor “like shining Hyperion” (19.398; see [[like-the-sun]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Fire seen from far off is the Iliad's likeness for the gleam of bronze. As the army marches out in Book 2, its gleam is like a destroying fire that burns a forest on the peaks of a mountain, “and the glare is seen far off” (2.455–458; see [[forest-fire]]). At the ditch the flame on Achilles' head is like the beacon fires of a besieged island, lit at sunset so that the neighbors may see them and come with their ships (18.207–214). Hector has fires burned on the plain all night, so that their glare may reach the sky (8.508–509; see [[stars-around-the-moon]]), and Athena kindles a fire from the helmet and shield of Diomedes like the star of late summer (5.4–7; see [[the-autumn-star]]).

Sailors carried off by storm winds within sight of a fire are the matter of an episode of the Odyssey: on the tenth day out from Aeolus, Odysseus' men see the fields of Ithaca and men tending fires, and then, while he sleeps, they open the bag of winds, and the storm snatches them away to sea, weeping, far from their native land (*Odyssey* 10.28–49).
