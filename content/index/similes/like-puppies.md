## The simile

{{quote:8761b312-70fc-5a59-8f1f-6ecead2a3873}}

σὺν δὲ δύω μάρψας ὥς τε σκύλακας ποτὶ γαίῃ / κόπτʼ· ἐκ δʼ ἐγκέφαλος χαμάδις ῥέε, δεῦε δὲ γαῖαν: “and seizing two together he dashed them to the ground like puppies, / and their brains flowed out on the ground and soaked the earth” (*Odyssey* 9.289–290). σὺν … μάρψας is “seizing together”, the companion of ἐπὶ … ἴαλλε, “put out his hands to”, in the verse before (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.288). σκύλαξ is a young dog, a whelp (see [[dogs]]), and κόπτω is “strike, dash”. The comparison is two words long and gives no scene. Its point is the difference in size and in regard: two grown men are in the Cyclops' hands what two puppies are in a man's, and he kills them as if they were no more.

The second verse is made of the Iliad's words for brains and wine. ἐγκέφαλος χαμάδις, “brains … on the ground”, is the curse of the oath, “may their brains pour out on the ground like this wine” (*Iliad* 3.300; see [[like-this-wine]]), and δεῦε δὲ γαῖαν, “and soaked the earth”, is said of the wine that Achilles pours on the ground all night for Patroclus (*Iliad* 23.220). The next sentence finishes the meal: he cuts them limb from limb and eats them like a mountain-bred lion (*Odyssey* 9.291–293; see [[the-devouring-lion]]).

## In context

The Cyclops has asked where the strangers' ship is moored, and Odysseus, who sees the trap, has told him that Poseidon wrecked it on the rocks (*Odyssey* 9.279–286; see [[odysseus]] and [[poseidon]]). “So I spoke, and he answered me nothing, with pitiless heart, / but sprang up and put out his hands to my companions” (*Odyssey* 9.287–288; see [[pitiless]]). He had already refused Odysseus' plea that he respect the gods and Zeus the god of guests, saying that the Cyclopes care nothing for Zeus (*Odyssey* 9.269–278; see [[xenia]]); now he answers with the killing. The men can only weep and lift their hands to Zeus, “seeing the cruel deeds, and helplessness held our hearts” (*Odyssey* 9.294–295). At dawn and at evening the Cyclops takes two more each time (*Odyssey* 9.311, 9.344; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]).

## Outside Homer

In Euripides' satyr play the Cyclops is a cook. Odysseus tells the chorus how, when all was ready, the Cyclops seized two of his companions; one he slaughtered over a bronze cauldron, the other he caught by the tendon of the foot and dashed against the sharp point of a rock, spattering out his brains; then he roasted the flesh and threw the limbs into the cauldron to boil (*Cyclops* 396–404; see [[euripides]]). Merry calls the cooking a later refinement, not in the Homeric story (on 9.288). In Virgil, Achaemenides, left behind by Ulysses, saw the Cyclops, lying back in the middle of his cave, seize two of their number in his great hand and break them on the rock, so that the threshold swam with their gore (*vidi egomet duo de numero cum corpora nostro / prensa manu magna medio resupinus in antro / frangeret ad saxum, sanieque aspersa natarent / limina*, *Aeneid* 3.623–626; see [[virgil]]). Macrobius, as Merry reports, set the two tellings side by side: Virgil told the deed bare and brief, while Homer mixed feeling into it, πάθος, and with the grief of the telling matched the horror of the cruelty (Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 5.13; Merry on 9.288).
