## The simile

κινήθη δʼ ἀγορὴ φὴ κύματα μακρὰ θαλάσσης / πόντου Ἰκαρίοιο: “the assembly was stirred like the long waves of the sea on the Icarian deep”. κινήθη is the aorist passive of κινέω (“set in motion, stir”). φή (“like”) is a rare word. Homer has it once more, at Iliad 14.499, and ancient critics explained it as ὡς (“as”). κύματα μακρά are the “long waves”, the heavy swell of open water. The [[icarian-sea|Icarian sea]] lies around the island of Icaria, west of Samos. Two winds raise the waves, Εὖρος, the East Wind, and Νότος, the South Wind ([[eurus]], [[notus]]), “rushing down from the clouds of father Zeus”.

The tenor is the ἀγορή (“assembly”), the crowd of men; the vehicle, a sea heaved up by a storm. The point of likeness is the heaving of a great mass: the whole crowd is lifted and tossed at once, as the sea is by the two winds.

## In context

{{quote:56df7537-849d-5a7b-bd20-a451bb3fd6af}}

Agamemnon has put the army to the test by proposing flight: “let us flee with our ships to our dear native land” (2.140; see [[the-test-of-the-army]]). His words stir the heart of every man who had not heard the council (2.142–143), and the simile shows the whole assembly heaving before anyone moves. A second simile follows at once, the West Wind in a field of grain ([[wind-in-the-wheat]]), and one verb binds the pair: κινήθη (2.144), κινήσῃ (2.147), κινήθη (2.149). The sea gives the upheaval, the grain the direction it then takes, toward the ships.

This is the first of three sea similes in the assembly. The men come back like a wave roaring on a long beach ([[surf-on-the-beach]]), and they shout for war like waves crashing on a headland ([[wave-on-the-headland]]). The Icarian sea, like the [[cayster|Cayster]] of a later simile (2.461; [[birds-on-the-asian-meadow]]), belongs to the eastern Aegean, and this local knowledge is often taken as a sign of the poet's Ionian background.

## Elsewhere in Homer

At Iliad 14.499 Peneleos holds up the severed head of Ilioneus on his spear φὴ κώδειαν (“like a poppy head”).

Two winds stir the sea again at the start of Book 9. The North Wind and the West Wind heap up the dark waves, and so grief tears at the Achaeans' hearts (9.4–8). In the assembly that follows, Agamemnon proposes flight once more, repeating 2.111–118 and 2.139–141, and this time he means it (9.17–28).
