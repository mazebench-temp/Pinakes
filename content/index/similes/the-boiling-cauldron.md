## The simile

{{quote:c4e804ae-3d03-58eb-b1ab-630e00de56fc}}

λέβης is a cauldron of bronze, the vessel of the hearth and of the gifts and prizes of the Iliad (see [[cauldron]]). It is ἐπειγόμενος πυρὶ πολλῷ, “pressed by a great fire”, and it melts κνίσην, the fat, of a σίαλος, a fattened hog, ἁπαλοτρεφής, “tender-fed”, a word found only here (see [[fire]]). ἀμβολάδην, “bubbling up”, also occurs only here; κάγκανα, “dry”, is said again of the dry wood piled for light in Odysseus' hall (*Odyssey* 18.308). Walter Leaf notes that ζεῖ should be read ζέει, since the contracted form is neither Homeric nor metrical, and that the verb is used of the cauldron as we say “the kettle boils”; he reports the variant μελδομένου, “of a hog being melted down”, and a scholium that explains it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.362–363; see the translation's note on 21.362–364).

The river's streams are the cauldron and Hephaestus is the fire under it. The river spoke “burning with fire, and his fair streams bubbled up” (21.361), and after the simile they are “blazing with fire, and the water boiled; / and it would not flow on, but was held back” (21.365–366). The heat that seethes in the pot is the god's breath, ἀϋτμή, which wears the river down (21.366–367; see [[hephaestus]]). Water that has been the river's weapon is made a kitchen's water, and the god yields.

## In context

The river has risen against Achilles and nearly drowned him, and Hera has sent her son to burn him (21.324–341; see [[scamander]] and [[achilles-and-the-river]]). Hephaestus burns the dead on the plain and then the trees, the plants and the fish of the river, until the river cries out that no god can match him (21.342–360; see [[the-north-wind-dries-the-orchard]]). After the simile the river begs Hera to call her son off and swears never to save the Trojans (21.367–376), and the fire is quenched (21.381).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The *Odyssey* uses the same likeness for Charybdis. When she spews out the sea, it seethes up all churning “like a cauldron on a great fire”, λέβης ὣς ἐν πυρὶ πολλῷ, πᾶσʼ ἀναμορμύρεσκε κυκωμένη (*Odyssey* 12.235–238). The words are the river's: just before the fire the Scamander rushed on Achilles κυκώμενος, “churning”, and μορμύρων ἀφρῷ, “seething with foam” (21.324–325).

## Outside Homer

Aristarchus, as Leaf reports, noted at this verse that the poet knows the boiling of meat but never shows his heroes using it; Leaf objects that what the simile describes is the melting of fat, not the boiling of flesh (on 21.362). The remark answers to Plato's: Homer feasts his heroes on campaign not on fish nor on boiled meat but only on roast, since soldiers can more easily use the fire itself than carry pots about with them (*Republic* 3.404b–c; see [[plato]]).
