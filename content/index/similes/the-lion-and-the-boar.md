## The simile

{{quote:07f1838a-f3ec-5e88-baff-65f85edbb8e4}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτε σῦν ἀκάμαντα λέων ἐβιήσατο χάρμῃ, / ὥ τʼ ὄρεος κορυφῇσι μέγα φρονέοντε μάχεσθον / πίδακος ἀμφʼ ὀλίγης· ἐθέλουσι δὲ πιέμεν ἄμφω: “As when a lion overpowers a tireless boar in battle, / when the two fight in high spirits on the peaks of a mountain / over a little spring, and both want to drink” (16.823–825). σῦς is the wild boar (see [[boar]]) and λέων the lion (see [[lion]]). ἀκάμας, “tireless”, is given in this book to the river Spercheius (16.176), and later to the sun (18.239, 18.484). χάρμη is battle, and the joy of it (see [[charme]]). The beasts are a pair, and the dual gives it: μέγα φρονέοντε μάχεσθον, “the two fight in high spirits”. πῖδαξ, “spring”, is found only here, though it is in Ida's epithet πολυπῖδαξ, “of the many springs” (8.47, 14.157). The water is little and both want it, so the fight is to the death.

πολλὰ δέ τʼ ἀσθμαίνοντα λέων ἐδάμασσε βίηφιν, “and the lion brings him down by strength as he gasps hard” (16.826). ἀσθμαίνω, “gasp, pant”, is said in the Iliad of men gasping as they die (5.585, 13.399, 21.182) and of men out of breath from running (10.376). The boar is not beaten until he is spent. The tenor: ὣς πολέας πεφνόντα Μενοιτίου ἄλκιμον υἱὸν / Ἕκτωρ Πριαμίδης σχεδὸν ἔγχεϊ θυμὸν ἀπηύρα, “so, though the valiant son of Menoetius had killed many, / Hector, son of Priam, robbed him of his life with the spear at close range” (16.827–828). The tireless boar is Patroclus, and the strength that brings him down is Hector's; but the simile puts first what the tenor admits in a participle, that the boar had fought long and was gasping, and that Patroclus had killed many.

## In context

Three blows have come before Hector's. Apollo, wrapped in mist, struck Patroclus' back and shoulders with the flat of his hand, and knocked the helmet from his head; the spear broke in his hands, the shield fell from his shoulders, and the god loosened his corselet; blind folly seized him, and he stood amazed; Euphorbus struck him between the shoulders with a spear and ran back into the throng; and Patroclus drew back toward his companions, “avoiding doom” (16.787–817; see [[apollo]] and [[euphorbus]]). Hector came close through the ranks and stabbed him in the lowest part of the flank: “he fell with a thud, and greatly grieved the army of the Achaeans” (16.818–822). The simile follows, and then Hector's boast and Patroclus' answer: “deadly fate and the son of Leto have killed me, / and of men Euphorbus; and you are the third to slay me” (16.849–850; see [[patroclus]] and [[hector]]). The simile gives the victory to the lion's strength; the dying man gives it to the gods.

The simile is the last of the book's lions. Patroclus was the lion when he killed Sarpedon as a lion kills a bull among the herd (16.487–491; see [[lion-among-cattle]]), and when he leapt on Cebriones with the spring of a lion that is struck in the chest, “and its own courage destroys it” (16.751–754; see [[wounded-lion]]); over Cebriones' body he and Hector fought like two lions, both hungry, over a slain deer “on the peaks of a mountain” (16.756–758; see [[two-lions]]). The words of that simile come back here, ὄρεος κορυφῇσι … μέγα φρονέοντε μάχεσθον, but the two are no longer equal: Hector is the lion, and Patroclus is the boar. The book that began with a spring of dark water, when Patroclus came weeping to Achilles (16.3–4; see [[the-spring-of-dark-water]]), ends its similes with a little spring on a mountain, over which he dies.

## Elsewhere in Homer

Lion and boar are elsewhere a pair of beasts that do the same thing, not enemies. Warriors stand together “like lions that eat raw flesh or wild boars” (5.782–783, 7.256–257; see [[lions-and-boars]]); Hector turns among his enemies like a boar or a lion among hounds and hunters (12.41–48; see [[the-boar-or-lion-at-bay]]), and in other similes the hound or the hunter goes after “a wild boar or a lion” (8.338–342, 11.292–295). The boar's own likeness is the beast that stands at bay, as Idomeneus waits for Aeneas like a boar in the mountains trusting in its courage, its back bristling and its eyes blazing (13.471–475; see [[the-bristling-boar]]), and Odysseus, cut off among the Trojans, is beset like a boar that comes out of a deep thicket while dogs and young men rush at it from all sides (11.414–420; see [[the-boar-at-bay]]). In the fight over Patroclus' body the boar is Ajax, who darts through the champions and scatters the Trojan battalions as a boar scatters dogs and young men in the mountains (17.281–285).

## Outside Homer

The shield of Heracles in the *Shield* handed down under Hesiod's name shows herds of boars and lions glaring at one another in anger, neither side afraid; a great lion already lies there, and around him two boars, their lives taken, and the rest are roused still more to fight ([Hesiod], *Shield* 168–177). Walter Leaf, who compares the passage, finds the boar's courage in the simile no exaggeration, quoting a Mahratta proverb, “a boar will drink between two tigers” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.823).
