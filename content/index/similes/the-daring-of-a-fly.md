## The simile

{{quote:735a2793-7697-597a-a730-8dff95de2b8a}}

Athena, in the likeness of Phoenix, has told Menelaus that it will be a shame to him if the dogs tear the body of Achilles' companion, and he has answered that he would stand by Patroclus, “if only Athena / would give me strength” (17.553–566). ὣς φάτο, γήθησεν δὲ θεὰ γλαυκῶπις Ἀθήνη, / ὅττί ῥά οἱ πάμπρωτα θεῶν ἠρήσατο πάντων, “So he spoke, and the gray-eyed goddess Athena was glad / that he had prayed to her first of all the gods” (17.567–568). She puts strength into his shoulders and his knees, καί οἱ μυίης θάρσος ἐνὶ στήθεσσιν ἐνῆκεν, “and put in his breast the daring of a fly” (17.569–570). θάρσος is the courage that a god puts into a fighter (see [[tharsos]]), and here it is given an owner, the fly (μυῖα; see [[flies]]). Walter Leaf notes that the verse rhymes with the one before it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.570).

ἥ τε καὶ ἐργομένη μάλα περ χροὸς ἀνδρομέοιο / ἰσχανάᾳ δακέειν, λαρόν τέ οἱ αἷμʼ ἀνθρώπου, “which, however much it is driven from a man's skin, / keeps on trying to bite, and human blood is sweet to it” (17.571–572). ἰσχανάω is “hold on, persist”: Leaf renders it “holds on for biting”, comparing Athena's command to Menelaus a few verses before, ἔχεο κρατερῶς, “hold firm” (17.559), and notes that it may have been confused with a verb meaning “yearn”, which would suit the sense as well (on 17.572). The same verb, in another form, tells how the two Ajaxes hold back the Trojans at the end of the book (ἰσχανέτην, 17.747; see [[the-wooded-ridge]]). λαρός, “sweet”, is also said of food: Achilles remembers the “pleasant meal” that Patroclus used to set before him (19.316; see [[laros]]). The point of likeness is persistence. The fly is driven off again and again and always comes back; it does not fear the hand, and it wants the blood.

The tenor: τοίου μιν θάρσευς πλῆσε φρένας ἀμφὶ μελαίνας, “with such daring she filled his heart, dark on every side” (17.573). Menelaus goes to stand over Patroclus and throws his spear, and kills Podes, son of Eetion, Hector's dear companion at the feast, as he turns to run, and drags his body away (17.574–581).

## In context

The fighting over the body has been stretched tight again, and Athena has come down from heaven wrapped in a purple cloud, like the rainbow that Zeus stretches out as a portent of war or of storm (17.543–552; see [[like-rainbows]]). She speaks to Menelaus first, “in the likeness of Phoenix in form and in tireless voice” (17.555; see [[phoenix]] and [[athena]]). Menelaus' answer ends with the danger: “But Hector has the dread might of fire” (17.565; see [[hector]]). The fly's daring is the goddess's answer to his prayer. Its effect is measured at once by a god of the other side. Apollo, in the likeness of Phaenops, taunts Hector for shrinking from Menelaus, who “before now at least / was a soft spearman”, and now has killed Hector's companion and carried off his body (17.582–590; see [[menelaus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The fly first came near Menelaus in Book 4. When Pandarus shot at him, Athena turned the arrow from his skin: “She brushed it away from his flesh, as a mother / brushes a fly from her child, when he lies in sweet sleep” (4.130–131; see [[mother-and-fly]]). The words return here: there the goddess kept the fly away from his skin (ἔεργεν ἀπὸ χροός), and here the fly comes back to a man's skin however often it is driven off (ἐργομένη … χροός). Elsewhere flies are a crowd. They swarm around the milk pails in spring, as many as the Achaean army and as thick as the fighters around Sarpedon's body (2.469–473, 16.641–643; see [[flies-around-the-milk-pails]]); Achilles fears that they will breed worms in the wounds of Patroclus (19.23–27); and the gods call one another “dog-fly”, κυνάμυια, for boldness without shame (21.394, 21.421).
