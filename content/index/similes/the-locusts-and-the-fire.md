## The simile

{{quote:275fbafe-a739-578e-809a-c31d68f030cb}}

ἀκρίδες, “locusts”, are named only here in Homer. ῥιπῆς πυρός, “the rush of fire”, is a phrase made like ῥιπῆς … Βορέαο, the rush of the North Wind that drives the snow (15.171, 19.358); Walter Leaf notes the likeness, and takes ἠερέθονται as “take wing” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.12). The verb is used of the golden tassels of the aegis, which flutter in the air (2.448), and of the minds of younger men, which are always blown about (3.108). φευγέμεναι ποταμὸν δέ, “to flee to a river”, has the suffix of direction written apart, ποταμόνδε (see the translation's note on 21.3). The fire is ἀκάματον, “tireless” (see [[tireless]] and [[fire]]), and it springs up as suddenly, ὄρμενον ἐξαίφνης, as the fire that falls on a city in the simile of Book 17 (17.738; see [[the-burning-city]]). φλέγει, “burns”, may be transitive or intransitive, as Leaf notes (on 21.13).

The likeness is of men caught between two things. The locusts are driven by the fire into the water and cower there, and the Trojans, with Achilles behind them, are penned into a river too deep to cross: “so before Achilles deep-eddying Xanthus' / sounding stream was filled with horses and men, all mingled together” (21.15–16; see [[scamander]] and [[deep-eddying]]). The water is no refuge. In the second simile of the book the same men cower under the banks like fish before a dolphin, and the verb is the same, πτώσσουσι, “they cower” (21.14), πτῶσσον, “cowered” (21.26; see [[the-dolphin-and-the-fishes]]).

## In context

The Trojans have reached the ford of the Xanthus, and Achilles splits them; half flee toward the city, and half are penned into the deep-streaming, silver-eddying river, where they fall in with a great crash and swim, shouting, this way and that among the eddies (21.1–11; see [[achilles-and-the-river]]). After the simile Achilles leaves his spear against the tamarisks and leaps in after them with his sword (21.17–21; see [[tamarisk]]).

The fire of the simile returns as the book's own action. Later the river itself rises against Achilles, and Hera sends Hephaestus, whose fire burns the dead on the plain and then the river, until his streams boil (21.328–382; see [[hephaestus]]). The book begins with creatures driven by fire into water, and at its center fire comes to the water.

## Outside Homer

The scholia, as Leaf reports, call this way of dealing with locusts a practice of Cyprus (on 21.12). [[strabo|Strabo]] describes the locust-eaters of Aethiopia, who live on the locusts that the strong west and southwest winds of spring drive into their country: they throw smoky brushwood into the ravines and set it alight, and the locusts, flying over the smoke, are blinded and fall; then they pound them with salt and make them into cakes (*Geography* 16.4.12). He tells too of the gods honored for ending a plague of locusts: the Oetaeans worship Heracles Cornopion, from κόρνοπες, their word for locusts, for ridding them of the creatures, and the Aeolians of Asia sacrifice to Apollo Pornopion (*Geography* 13.1.64).
