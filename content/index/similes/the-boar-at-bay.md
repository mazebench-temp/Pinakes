## The simile

{{quote:17c4ea27-7136-57c7-95bf-1f63c3ae28da}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτε κάπριον ἀμφὶ κύνες θαλεροί τʼ αἰζηοὶ / σεύωνται: “and as when around a boar the dogs and young men in their prime rush”. κάπριος is the wild boar, and θαλεροὶ αἰζηοί are young men in their strength. ὃ δέ τʼ εἶσι βαθείης ἐκ ξυλόχοιο, / θήγων λευκὸν ὀδόντα μετὰ γναμπτῇσι γένυσσιν: “and he comes out of a deep thicket, whetting the white tusk in his curving jaws”. A ξύλοχος is a thicket, the lair of wild beasts. ἀμφὶ δέ τʼ ἀΐσσονται, ὑπαὶ δέ τε κόμπος ὀδόντων / γίγνεται: “and they rush at him all around, and a gnashing of tusks rises”. κόμπος is a clashing or rattling noise. οἳ δὲ μένουσιν ἄφαρ δεινόν περ ἐόντα: “and they stand their ground at once, terrible though he is” (11.414–418).

The simile is of the boar at bay, the beast that turns to face the hunt (see [[boar]]). The point of likeness is a lone fighter, surrounded and dangerous, who will not run, and the dogs and young men who do not run either. The tenor is short: “so then around Odysseus, dear to Zeus, rushed the Trojans” (11.419–420).

## In context

Diomedes has gone back to the ships with Paris' arrow through his foot, and Odysseus is left alone, “since rout had seized them all” (11.401–402). He debates with his own heart whether to flee, and decides: “it is cowards who leave the fighting, but whoever would be best in battle must by all means stand his ground strongly, whether he is struck or strikes another” (11.403–410; see [[pondering-two-ways]] and [[the-cowards-leave-the-fighting]]). While he is still thinking, the ranks of the Trojans close around him, “setting their own bane in their midst” (11.411–413). The simile shows the choice carried out. He stands, and he strikes: he wounds Deiopites, kills Thoon, Ennomus and Chersidamas, and wounds Charops, before Socus wounds him (11.420–438; see [[odysseus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Odysseus knew boars. In the Odyssey the scar by which Eurycleia recognizes him came from a boar on Parnassus, which charged out of its thicket (ἐκ ξυλόχοιο) against the hunters, its back bristling and fire in its eyes, and gashed his leg above the knee before he killed it (*Odyssey* 19.439–454). In the Iliad the boar at bay belongs to other defenders too. Idomeneus waits for Aeneas like a boar that whets its tusks to ward off dogs and men (13.471–477), the two Lapiths at the gate fight like two wild boars, and a gnashing of tusks rises from them (κόμπος ὀδόντων, 12.146–150), and Ajax goes through the champions “like a boar in courage” (17.281–285; see [[like-a-boar]]). Hunting dogs and their quarry recur through Book 11 (see [[the-hunter-and-his-hounds]] and [[two-boars]]).
