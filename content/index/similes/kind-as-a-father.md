## The simile

{{quote:a99d2cc0-c6f4-5f74-ad6b-c611e67df4f1}}

ἀλλʼ εἴ τίς με καὶ ἄλλος ἐνὶ μεγάροισιν ἐνίπτοι / δαέρων ἢ γαλόων ἢ εἰνατέρων εὐπέπλων, / ἢ ἑκυρή, ἑκυρὸς δὲ πατὴρ ὣς ἤπιος αἰεί, / ἀλλὰ σὺ τὸν ἐπέεσσι παραιφάμενος κατέρυκες: “no, if anyone else in the halls rebuked me, / one of my brothers-in-law or my husband's sisters, or his brothers' well-robed wives, / or my mother-in-law (but my father-in-law was always kind, like a father), / you would talk them round with words and hold them back” (24.768–771). Helen names everyone in the house who might rebuke her, the husband's brothers, his sisters, his brothers' wives and his mother, and spares only one, in a parenthesis of half a verse (see [[in-laws]]). The mother-in-law is Hecuba, who has just lamented before her (24.747–760); the father-in-law is Priam. πατὴρ ὣς, “like a father”, puts the noun before ὡς, as in λέων ὣς, “like a lion”.

ἤπιος is “kind, gentle”. It is the word of the “soothing” drugs that a healer lays on a wound (ἤπια φάρμακα, 4.218, 11.830), of the kindness that Zeus tells Athena he means toward her, “and I want to be kind to you” (8.40 = 22.184), and of Patroclus, “a kind one”, in Achilles' words about the charioteer whom his horses mourn (23.281). Helen uses it twice in this speech. Priam was always kind, and at the end, after Hector's death, “there is no longer anyone else in wide Troy / who is kind to me or a friend, but all shudder at me” (24.774–775). Beside it she sets Hector's ἀγανοφροσύνη, “by your gentleness and your gentle words” (24.772; see [[helen]]).

The point of likeness is a father's kindness to his child, shown by Priam to the woman over whom the war that has killed his sons was fought. The poem has shown that kindness in *Iliad* 3. Priam called her to sit beside him on the wall, “Come here, dear child, and sit in front of me”, and told her, “you are not to blame for anything in my eyes; it is the gods who are to blame” (3.162–164), and she answered, “You are one I respect, dear father-in-law, and one I fear” (3.172; see [[priam]] and [[the-view-from-the-wall]]).

## In context

Helen's lament is the third and last over Hector's body, after Andromache's and Hecuba's (24.761–776; see [[lament]]). He was the dearest to her of her husband's brothers, though her husband is Alexander, who brought her to Troy, and she wishes she had died before that (24.762–764). It is the twentieth year since she left her native land, and she never heard an evil or contemptuous word from him (24.765–767). The simile of the father stands in the middle of her praise of Hector, who held the others back, and after it she weeps for him and for herself, since no one in Troy is left who is kind to her. “So she spoke, weeping, and the countless people groaned with her” (24.776). Then Priam orders the Trojans to bring wood for the pyre (24.777–781).

## Elsewhere in Homer

πατὴρ ὣς ἤπιος ἦεν, “he was kind as a father”, ends four verses of the Odyssey, and three times it is said of Odysseus as king. Telemachus tells the assembly of Ithaca that he has lost his brave father, who was once king among them and was kind, like a father (*Odyssey* 2.46–47). Mentor in the same assembly, and Athena among the gods, say that no scepter-bearing king should be ready to be ἀγανὸς καὶ ἤπιος, “gentle and kind”, any more, since none of the people he ruled remembers Odysseus, who was kind, like a father (*Odyssey* 2.230–234 = 5.8–12). The fourth time Menelaus says it of Nestor: “for he was kind to me as a father, while we sons of the Achaeans fought at Troy” (*Odyssey* 15.151–153). The pair ἀγανός and ἤπιος are the two words of Helen's praise, the gentleness of Hector and the kindness of Priam.

In the Iliad the old king is a father to others too in the last book. Hermes, in the likeness of a young Myrmidon, tells Priam, “you look to me like my dear father” (24.371), and Priam begs Achilles to remember his own father, who is of his age (24.486–487; see [[peleus]]).
