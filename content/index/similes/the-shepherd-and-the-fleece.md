## The simile

{{quote:ab529b20-37e8-537d-ab6f-e5488029e681}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτε ποιμὴν ῥεῖα φέρει πόκον ἄρσενος οἰὸς / χειρὶ λαβὼν ἑτέρῃ, ὀλίγον τέ μιν ἄχθος ἐπείγει: “And as a shepherd easily carries the fleece of a ram, taking it in one hand, and the weight presses on him little” (12.451–452). The ποιμήν is the shepherd; πόκος, “fleece”, the shorn wool of a sheep, occurs only here in Homer, and ἄρσην ὄϊς, “a male sheep”, is a ram (see [[ram]] and [[sheep]]). ἄχθος is a load or a weight, and ἐπείγω “press, weigh on”.

The tenor: “so Hector lifted the stone and carried it straight at the doors” (12.453). The likeness is between two weights. The narrator has just said what the stone weighs. It stood in front of the gates, thick at the base and sharp at the top, and “not even two men, the best of the people, could easily heave it from the ground onto a wagon, such as mortals are now; but he wielded it easily even alone” (12.445–449; see [[men-of-today]]). The simile turns the stone into a fleece. ῥεῖα, “easily”, takes up ῥέα πάλλε, “wielded it easily” (12.449), and the shepherd carries his load in one hand, as Hector does alone what two men could not. Between the stone and the simile stands the verse that gives the lightness to a god: “The son of crooked-counseling Cronus made it light for him” (12.450). Hector's strength and Zeus' help are set side by side, and the poem does not choose between them (see the translation's note on 12.450).

## In context

Zeus has given Hector the higher glory, and he calls on the Trojans to break the wall and throw fire on the ships (12.437–443). Then the stone and the simile. He plants himself, hits the doors in the middle, and breaks off both hinges; the bars give way, the doors split, and he leaps in, “his face like swift night”, his eyes blazing (12.453–466; see [[the-gates-of-the-wall]], [[like-night]] and [[eyes-like-fire]]). The Trojans pour over the wall and through the gates, and the Danaans flee among the ships (12.467–471; see [[stone-throwing]] and [[the-fight-at-the-wall]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Diomedes and Aeneas each lift a stone “which two men could not carry, such as mortals are now”, and wield it easily alone (5.302–304, 20.285–287); only Hector's is made light by a god. Ajax's boulder at the same wall is too heavy for one man of today to hold in both hands (12.380–383; see [[like-a-diver]]). The breaking of the wall has a second simile of ease in Book 15. Apollo throws down the Achaean wall “very easily” (ῥεῖα μάλʼ), as a boy by the sea scatters with his hands and feet the sand he has heaped up in his childish play (15.355–366).
