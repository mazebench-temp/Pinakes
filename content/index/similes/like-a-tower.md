## The simile

ἤριπε δʼ ὡς ὅτε πύργος ἐνὶ κρατερῇ ὑσμίνῃ: “and he fell like a tower in the fierce battle”. ἤριπε, “fell, collapsed”, is from ἐρείπω, the verb of walls thrown down; ἐρείπια are ruins. πύργος is a tower, a defense of a city wall. ὡς ὅτε, “as when”, usually opens a scene of several lines. Here it has no verb of its own and no scene: the hearer supplies the tower's fall, and the half-line ends with the battle it explains.

The point of likeness is the fall of something tall and upright. A man in armor goes down all at once, as a tower collapses. A tower is also a defense, and Echepolus falls “among the champions” (ἐνὶ προμάχοισι, 4.458), the men who stand in front of the rest.

## In context

{{quote:8dab0342-96bd-5384-b5b4-db48a3e65915}}

Echepolus, son of Thalysias, is the first man killed in the Iliad (see [[echepolus]]). The poem marks the moment twice: Antilochus was “the first” (πρῶτος) to kill a Trojan (4.457), and was “first” (πρῶτος) to strike him on the ridge of his helmet (4.459). The spear point fixes in his forehead and pierces the bone, “and darkness covered his eyes” (4.461; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). Then he falls. The scene has the parts of the Iliad's killings: killer and victim, the victim's father, the wound, the death and the fall (see [[androktasia]]). The killer is Antilochus, son of Nestor, who is named here for the first time in the poem (see [[antilochus]]).

The book has used πύργος twice already, for a body of men. The Athenians and Cephallenians stood waiting “until another column of Achaeans should come on” (πύργος, 4.334), and Agamemnon told their leaders they would gladly watch “even if ten columns of Achaeans were fighting in front of you” (πύργοι, 4.347). The towers of the review were columns of soldiers. The first tower to fall in the battle is one man. The poem will never show the towers of Troy falling; its first death gives their fall to a man.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The tower is a figure for a defender. Ajax carries a shield “like a tower” (σάκος ἠΰτε πύργον, 7.219, 11.485, 17.128), and in the underworld Odysseus tells his shade that the Achaeans lost “such a tower” in him (Odyssey 11.556). Other men fall like trees. Imbrius falls like an ash on a mountain top cut down with the bronze (13.178–181). Asius and Sarpedon fall in the same three lines, which begin as this one does (ἤριπε δʼ ὡς ὅτε), “as an oak falls, or a white poplar, or a tall pine that carpenters in the mountains cut down with fresh-sharpened axes to be a ship's timber” (13.389–391, 16.482–484). In Book 4 itself, a few lines later, Simoeisius falls like a poplar (4.482–487; see [[the-poplar]]), and ἤριπε is used again when Leucus falls over the body he was dragging (4.493).
