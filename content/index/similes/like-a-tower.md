## The simile

The Iliad uses the tower for two opposite pictures. A man killed in battle falls “like a tower” (4.462), and Ajax, the great defender of the Achaeans, carries a shield “like a tower” (7.219). One is a tower falling, the other a tower standing.

The first is the death of Echepolus. ἤριπε δʼ ὡς ὅτε πύργος ἐνὶ κρατερῇ ὑσμίνῃ: “and he fell like a tower in the fierce battle”. ἤριπε, “fell, collapsed”, is from ἐρείπω, the verb of walls thrown down; ἐρείπια are ruins. πύργος is a tower, a defense of a city wall. ὡς ὅτε, “as when”, usually opens a scene of several lines. Here it has no verb of its own and no scene: the hearer supplies the tower's fall, and the half-line ends with the battle it explains.

The point of likeness is the fall of something tall and upright. A man in armor goes down all at once, as a tower collapses. A tower is also a defense, and Echepolus falls “among the champions” (ἐνὶ προμάχοισι, 4.458), the men who stand in front of the rest.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The tower is a figure for a defender. Ajax carries a shield “like a tower” (σάκος ἠΰτε πύργον, 7.219, 11.485, 17.128), and in the underworld Odysseus tells his shade that the Achaeans lost “such a tower” in him (Odyssey 11.556). Other men fall like trees. Imbrius falls like an ash on a mountain top cut down with the bronze (13.178–181). Asius and Sarpedon fall in the same three lines, which begin as this one does (ἤριπε δʼ ὡς ὅτε), “as an oak falls, or a white poplar, / or a tall pine, which on the mountains builders / cut down with newly sharpened axes to be ship-timber” (13.389–391, 16.482–484). In Book 4 itself, a few lines later, Simoeisius falls like a poplar (4.482–487; see [[the-poplar]]), and ἤριπε is used again when Leucus falls over the body he was dragging (4.493).
