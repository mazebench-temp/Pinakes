## The simile

{{quote:65f01ef6-4b08-5821-8757-5cfe98c55065}}

οἵη περ φύλλων γενεὴ τοίη δὲ καὶ ἀνδρῶν: “as is the generation of leaves, so is that of men”. The first line states the likeness before the picture is drawn: οἵη … τοίη, “such as … such”, and the picture ends with ὣς, “so” (6.149). φύλλα are leaves. γενεή, from the root of γίγνομαι (“be born, come to be”), is a birth, a generation, and a family line (see [[genee]]). The translation renders it “generation” in the simile and “lineage” in the rest of the speech (6.145, 6.151, 6.211).

The picture has two halves. ἄνεμος χαμάδις χέει, “the wind pours (the leaves) to the ground”; ὕλη τηλεθόωσα φύει, “the forest, in full leaf, puts forth (others)”. τηλεθάω is “flourish, be luxuriant”. ἔαρος δʼ ἐπιγίγνεται ὥρη, “and the season of spring comes on”. The last line applies it: ἣ μὲν φύει ἣ δʼ ἀπολήγει, “one grows, another passes away”. φύω, the verb of the forest's growth, is used again of the generations of men, and ἀπολήγω is “leave off, come to an end”.

The point of likeness is succession. The leaves of one year fall and others take their place; one generation of men grows while another ends. Nothing in the simile is individual. No leaf is told from another, and no man is named. The forest lasts, and the leaves change.

## In context

Diomedes has asked the stranger who faces him who he is. If he is a god he will not fight him; if he is one of the mortals “who eat the fruit of the field”, let him come nearer, “so that you may the sooner reach the bounds of destruction” (6.142–143; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]] and [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]]). Glaucus begins with a question of his own: “Great-hearted son of Tydeus, why do you ask about my lineage?” (6.145). The leaves are his answer. Against the threat of death he sets the common lot: the generations of men come and go like the leaves of a year.

Then he tells it anyway, “if you wish to learn this too, so that you may know well our lineage—and many men know it” (6.150–151). Sisyphus, Glaucus, Bellerophon and Hippolochus follow, sixty lines of them (see [[bellerophon-in-lycia]]), and the speech ends with the claim the simile seemed to set aside:

{{quote:355eb7a5-a8c1-5675-b8b5-f98c69a34fa6}}

The two parts of the speech do not cancel each other. The leaves say how little one generation amounts to; the lineage says what a man receives from those before him and owes them, “not to shame the race of my fathers” (6.209; see [[always-to-be-the-best]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). γενεή holds them together. It is Glaucus' word for what Diomedes asked, the word of the simile and the word of the claim, five times in the speech. And the lineage decides the meeting. It shows that Diomedes' grandfather Oeneus once entertained Bellerophon, and the two men do not fight (6.212–231; see [[xenia]]).

Glaucus is also the listener of the Iliad's other famous speech on mortality. Sarpedon tells him that if the two of them could live forever, ageless and immortal, he would not fight in the front ranks; but since the fates of death stand over men in thousands, and no mortal can escape them, let them go and win glory or give it to another (12.322–328; see [[sarpedon]]). Glaucus draws no conclusion from the leaves. Sarpedon draws the hero's conclusion from the same fact.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same comparison returns in the battle of the gods. Apollo will not fight Poseidon “for the sake of wretched mortals, who like leaves now blaze with life, eating the fruit of the field, and now waste away and die” (21.463–466). ζαφλεγέες, “full of fire”, is the flourishing of 6.148; ἀρούρης καρπὸν ἔδοντες is Diomedes' phrase for mortals, “who eat the fruit of the field” (6.142). The god sees men as Glaucus does, and for that reason will not fight about them.

Elsewhere Homer's leaves are a measure of number. The Achaeans in the meadow of the Scamander are as many “as the leaves and flowers that grow in their season” (2.468; see [[leaves-and-flowers]]); Iris warns that they are coming “like leaves or like the sands” (2.800; see [[like-leaves-or-sand]]); and the Cicones come against Odysseus' men as many as the leaves and flowers in their season (*Odyssey* 9.51). Only Glaucus and Apollo use the leaves for the brevity of life.

## Outside Homer

The simile was among the most admired passages of Homer (see [[the-man-of-chios]]). [[mimnermus|Mimnermus]] takes it up in a poem on the shortness of youth: “we, like the leaves that the flowery season of spring brings forth” (fr. 2 West). An elegy that West assigns to [[simonides|Simonides]] (it was once given to Semonides) quotes the first line as the finest thing “the man of Chios” said, and adds that few who hear it take it to heart, because every man has hope (fr. 19 West). The chorus of Aristophanes' *Birds* calls men “like the generation of leaves” (685–687). The same image is in the Wisdom of Ben Sira: some leaves fall and others grow, and so it is with the generations of flesh and blood (Ecclesiasticus 14.18).

Later poets joined Glaucus' leaves to Homer's leaves of number, and made them the dead (see [[leaves-of-the-dead]]). In [[virgil|Virgil]] the dead crowding the bank of the underworld river are as many as the leaves that fall in the woods at the first frost of autumn (*Aeneid* 6.309–310). [[dante|Dante]]'s souls drop from the shore of Acheron one by one, like autumn leaves until the branch sees all its spoils on the ground (*Inferno* 3.112–117), and [[milton|Milton]]'s fallen angels lie “thick as autumnal leaves that strow the brooks in Vallombrosa” (*Paradise Lost* 1.302–303).
