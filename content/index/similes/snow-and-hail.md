## The simile

{{quote:27f1a045-0559-5b9d-9c77-8e22fc6dfc9f}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτʼ ἂν ἐκ νεφέων πτῆται νιφὰς ἠὲ χάλαζα / ψυχρὴ ὑπὸ ῥιπῆς αἰθρηγενέος Βορέαο: “As when snow or hail flies from the clouds, cold, under the rush of the North Wind, born of the clear sky” (15.170–171). νιφάς is a fall of snow, and χάλαζα is hail, which the Iliad names only twice more: among the storms Zeus makes ready when he flashes his lightning (10.5–7), and in the cold of one of the two springs of the Scamander, which runs in summer like hail, or snow, or ice (22.151–152). πτῆται, “flies”, is from πέτομαι, the verb of birds and of the gods' flight. ῥιπή is a rush, the force of a thing hurled or blown, and the North Wind is αἰθρηγενής, “born of the clear sky” (αἴθρη): it is the cold wind that clears the sky, and in the Odyssey it is αἰθρηγενέτης when it joins the other winds against Odysseus' raft (*Odyssey* 5.296; see [[boreas]]).

The tenor: ὣς κραιπνῶς μεμαυῖα διέπτατο ὠκέα Ἶρις, “so swiftly swift Iris flew in her eagerness” (15.172). The half-verse is the one that closed Hera's flight to Olympus ninety verses before (15.83; see [[the-travelers-thought]]), with Iris' name and her epithet ὠκέα, “swift” (see [[swift-iris]]).

The likeness is of speed and of descent. Iris comes down from the heights of Ida to the plain as snow or hail is driven down out of the clouds by the wind, straight and fast. The thing she resembles is also cold and hard, and her errand is a threat: Poseidon is to leave the battle, or Zeus will come and fight him face to face, “since he says he is far stronger than you in strength, and the elder by birth” (15.176–182). Snow and hail are Zeus' weather, and the message is his too.

## In context

Hera has brought Iris and Apollo to Ida, and Zeus gives Iris her charge first: she is to tell Poseidon to cease from battle and war and go among the tribes of the gods or into the shining sea (15.143–167; see [[iris]] and [[messenger-scene]]). “So he spoke, and swift Iris, wind-footed, did not disobey, and she went down from the mountains of Ida to sacred Ilios” (15.168–169), in the two verses of her errand to Hector in Book 11 (11.195–196; see [[wind-footed]]). After the simile she stands near Poseidon and repeats Zeus' words (15.173–183). Poseidon answers with his claim to an equal share of honor, the three lots of the sons of Cronus (15.185–199; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]), and gives way when she reminds him that the Erinyes always follow the elder (15.200–219; see [[poseidon]]).

The simile is the second of three for the gods' journeys from Ida in the first part of the book: Hera flies like a traveler's thought (15.80–83), Iris like snow or hail, and Apollo goes down like a hawk (15.237–238; see [[like-a-hawk]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The second verse returns in Book 19 for the helmets, shields, corselets and spears that pour out of the Achaean ships as thick as the snowflakes that fly from Zeus, cold, under the rush of the North Wind (19.357–361; see [[snowflakes-from-zeus]]). There the snow is many things together; here it is one goddess. In the other snow similes of the Iliad the snow falls thick: Odysseus' words came like the snowflakes of winter (3.222; see [[snowflakes-of-winter]]), and the stones flew from both sides of the Achaean wall as thick as snow (12.156–158, 12.278–289; see [[a-snow-of-stones]] and [[the-great-snowfall]]). Iris has one more likeness, in Book 24, when she goes to call Thetis to Zeus: she plunges into the sea like a lead weight that goes down to bring death to the fish (24.77–82; see [[the-lead-weight]]).
