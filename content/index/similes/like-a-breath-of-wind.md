## The simile

{{quote:9906e557-f350-5c15-9392-fb792f4891eb}}

ἡ δʼ ἀνέμου ὡς πνοιὴ ἐπέσσυτο δέμνια κούρης, “And she rushed like a breath of wind to the girl's bed” (*Odyssey* 6.20; see [[athena]] and [[bed]]). The likeness is a single phrase, as when Thetis rises from the gray sea “like a mist” (*Iliad* 1.359; see [[like-a-mist]]); πνοιή is the breath or blast of the wind, rendered “breath” here (see the translation's table of key words). The verb, ἐπέσσυτο, “rushed to”, gives the speed, and the wind gives the manner of her coming.

The chamber she comes to is shut. It is “intricately worked”, Nausicaa sleeps in it, and “beside her two handmaids, who had their beauty from the Graces, / one on either side, by the doorposts; and the shining doors were closed” (*Odyssey* 6.15–19; see [[nausicaa]], [[handmaids]] and [[door]]). The handmaids slept in their young mistress' room, W. W. Merry explains, probably on mattresses on the floor, so near the door that it could not be opened without waking them (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.18). The goddess comes past the closed doors and the sleeping handmaids as a breath of wind would. For a parallel Merry turns to the *Hymn to Hermes*, where the god, coming home at dawn, slips sideways through the keyhole of the hall “like an autumn breeze, like mist” (*Hymn to Hermes* 145–147; Merry on 6.20).

## In context

Athena has gone to the house of Alcinous, “devising a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus” (*Odyssey* 6.13–14; see [[alcinous]] and [[nostos]]). At the bed she does what dreams do in Homer: she “stood above her head and spoke to her” (*Odyssey* 6.21 = 4.803; see [[dream-scene]]), “in the likeness of the daughter of Dymas, famed for ships, / who was of an age with her, and dear to her heart” (*Odyssey* 6.22–23; see [[dymas-the-phaeacian]]). She scolds the girl for her neglected clothes and sends her to the washing troughs at dawn (*Odyssey* 6.25–40), and goes back to Olympus, where no wind ever shakes the seat of the gods (*Odyssey* 6.41–47; see [[olympus]]). Nausicaa wakes and “marveled at the dream” (*Odyssey* 6.49; see [[dream]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The wind is the measure of the gods' travel. Athena's golden sandals carry her over sea and land “as fast as the blasts of the wind”, ἅμα πνοιῇς ἀνέμοιο (*Odyssey* 1.96–98), and Hermes' carry him in the same verses (*Odyssey* 5.44–46 = *Iliad* 24.340–342; see [[hermes]]). The immortal horses of Achilles “flew as fast as the winds” (*Iliad* 16.149; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]), and when Hector throws his spear at Achilles, Athena “turned it back from glorious Achilles with a breath, / blowing very gently” (*Iliad* 20.439–440).

The nearest scene is the dream of Penelope at the end of the fourth book, which Athena sends in the likeness of Penelope's sister: the phantom goes “into the chamber past the strap of the bolt, / and it stood above her head and spoke to her”, and when it has spoken “it slipped away past the bolt of the doorpost / into the blasts of the winds” (*Odyssey* 4.795–803, 4.838–839; see [[iphthime]] and [[penelope]]). There the image leaves by way of the winds; here the goddess comes like one.

## Outside Homer

Merry also compares Virgil's line on the shade of Anchises, which slips three times from Aeneas' arms, “like the light winds and most like a winged dream”, *par levibus ventis volucrique simillima somno* (*Aeneid* 6.700–702; Merry on 6.20; see [[virgil]]).
