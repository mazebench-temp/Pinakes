## The simile

{{quote:4dc3a21a-abd9-5eca-b526-496738183bb2}}

ἀλλʼ ἔμενον νεφέλῃσιν ἐοικότες ἅς τε Κρονίων / νηνεμίης ἔστησεν ἐπʼ ἀκροπόλοισιν ὄρεσσιν / ἀτρέμας: “but they stood their ground like clouds that the son of Cronus has set in windless weather on the tops of the mountains, motionless”. ἔμενον, “they stood their ground”, is the verb of the whole passage. νηνεμίη is a windless calm (from νη-, “not”, and ἄνεμος, “wind”), and ἀκρόπολα ὄρεα are high mountain tops. ἀτρέμας, “without trembling, still”, stands first in its line, set apart; the word is used of Zeus sleeping motionless on the peak of Gargarus (14.352).

The simile goes on to the winds that are not blowing: ὄφρʼ εὕδῃσι μένος Βορέαο καὶ ἄλλων / ζαχρειῶν ἀνέμων, οἵ τε νέφεα σκιόεντα / πνοιῇσιν λιγυρῇσι διασκιδνᾶσιν ἀέντες, “while the might of the North Wind sleeps, and of the other violent winds, which scatter the shadowy clouds with their shrill blasts when they blow” (5.524–526). Βορέης, the North Wind, is named here for the first time in the poem (see [[boreas]]). ζαχρειής, “violent”, occurs only here, and λιγυρός is “shrill, whistling”.

The tenor: ὣς Δαναοὶ Τρῶας μένον ἔμπεδον οὐδὲ φέβοντο, “so the Danaans awaited the Trojans steadfastly and did not flee” (5.527). μένον takes up the ἔμενον of the first line. The point of likeness is steadiness. Clouds are usually the image of movement and storm, as in the dark cloud that a goatherd sees coming over the sea in Book 4 (4.274–282; see [[storm-cloud]]). These are clouds that do not move, because the winds that would drive them are asleep. The stillness is the gift of Zeus, who sets them there (ἔστησεν). The condition of the simile, “while the might of the North Wind sleeps”, leaves open what will happen when it wakes.

## In context

Stung by Sarpedon, Hector has rallied the Trojans (5.493–497). The Achaeans have held their ground, whitened with dust like the chaff on a threshing floor (5.498–505; see [[winnowing]]), Ares has shrouded the battle in night, and Apollo has sent Aeneas back healed (5.506–518). The two Ajaxes, Odysseus and Diomedes urge the Danaans on, and they fear “neither the strength of the Trojans nor their onslaughts” (5.519–521; see [[onslaught]]). Two images of Achaeans standing firm follow each other: heaps of chaff under a wind, and clouds where no wind blows. Then Agamemnon goes through the throng with an exhortation, and the killing begins again (5.528–540; see [[exhortation]]).

The North Wind, asleep in the simile, blows in the story before the book is out. When Sarpedon faints under the oak of Zeus, “the breath of the North Wind blowing on him revived his spirit” (5.697–698; see [[tlepolemus-and-sarpedon]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Zeus sets other things in the sky as signs. The serpents on Agamemnon's corselet are “like the rainbows that the son of Cronus sets in the clouds, a portent for mortal men” (11.27–28), in a line built like this one. The Trojan watch fires burn like the stars around the moon on a windless night (νήνεμος αἰθήρ), when every lookout and headland stands out and the shepherd rejoices in his heart (8.555–559). In the Odyssey, as the ship nears the Sirens, the wind drops, “and there was a windless calm (νηνεμίη), and a god lulled the waves to sleep” (Odyssey 12.168–169).
