## The simile

{{quote:e47002dc-a533-5f88-80a5-cedd028c4f5e}}

ἀλλʼ ἔχον ὥς τε τάλαντα γυνὴ χερνῆτις ἀληθής: “but they held, as an honest working woman holds the scales” (12.433). τάλαντα are a pair of scales (see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). χερνῆτις, a woman who works with her hands for hire, occurs only here, and ἀληθής, “true”, is a woman who weighs honestly (see the translation's note on 12.433–435). ἥ τε σταθμὸν ἔχουσα καὶ εἴριον ἀμφὶς ἀνέλκει / ἰσάζουσʼ: “who, holding the weight and the wool, lifts them up on either side until they are equal” (12.434–435). σταθμός is the weight in one pan and εἴριον the wool in the other; whether she is weighing out wool to be spun or spun wool to be paid for is not said (see [[weaving]]). ἵνα παισὶν ἀεικέα μισθὸν ἄρηται: “so that she may win a meager wage for her children” (12.435). ἀεικής is “unseemly, mean”: the pay is poor. The poem does not say whether she has a husband.

The tenor: ὣς μὲν τῶν ἐπὶ ἶσα μάχη τέτατο πτόλεμός τε, “so evenly was their battle and war stretched tight” (12.436). The point of likeness is the balance: two pans held level, two armies neither of which can move the other. The woman holds them level because she must be exact; she is poor, and she is honest. The level scales are the moment before they tip.

## In context

The simile ends a deadlock. The Lycians cannot break through and the Danaans cannot drive them back (12.417–420); the battlements part them like the boundary between two men in a field (12.421–426; see [[the-boundary-dispute]]); the towers and battlements are spattered with the blood of both sides, “but even so they could not put the Achaeans to rout” (12.430–432). Then the scales tip: the battle hung even “until Zeus gave the higher glory to Hector, son of Priam, who was the first to leap inside the wall of the Achaeans” (12.436–438). Hector lifts the stone and breaks the gate (12.445–471; see [[hector]], [[the-shepherd-and-the-fleece]] and [[the-fight-at-the-wall]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The tenor's verse returns in Book 15, after another simile from a craft: the battle stays level as a carpenter's chalk line makes a ship's timber straight in the hands of a man skilled in all his craft by the promptings of Athena (15.410–413). The scales themselves are Zeus' in Books 8 and 22, where he sets two fates of death in his golden scales, lifts them by the middle, and one side sinks: the Achaeans' on the second day of battle, Hector's at the end (8.69–72, 22.209–213; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). The woman's scales are the same instrument in a poor house, and here too it is Zeus who tips the balance (12.437).

Wool-work is women's work in Homer. Aphrodite comes to Helen in the likeness of an old wool-comber who “used to work fine wool for her” at Lacedaemon (3.386–388), and in the Odyssey the slave women of Odysseus' house have been taught to card wool (*Odyssey* 22.422–423).
