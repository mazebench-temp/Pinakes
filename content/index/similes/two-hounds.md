## The simile

{{quote:6ea5eed3-1e05-5b43-982e-076b17ebb948}}

ὡς δʼ ὅτε καρχαρόδοντε δύω κύνε εἰδότε θήρης: “and as when two sharp-toothed hounds, skilled in the hunt”. The simile is in the dual, the grammatical number for a pair: καρχαρόδοντε, κύνε, εἰδότε, and the verb ἐπείγετον, “they press hard”. κύνε, the dual of κύων, “dog”, occurs only here. καρχαρόδους, “with jagged teeth”, describes dogs once more, the dogs from which two lions snatch a goat (13.198), and εἰδότε θήρης is “knowing the chase”.

The quarry is ἢ κεμάδʼ ἠὲ λαγωόν, “a fawn or a hare”. κεμάς, a young deer, occurs only here in Homer (see [[deer]]), and λαγωός, the hare, occurs in the Iliad only here and in a simile of Book 22 (22.310; see [[hare]]). Both are animals that live by speed. The hounds press it ἐμμενὲς αἰεί, “without letting up”, through wooded country, ὃ δέ τε προθέῃσι μεμηκώς, “and it runs on ahead, crying out”. μεμηκώς is a perfect of μηκάομαι, the bleating of sheep and goats (μεμακυῖαι, 4.435), here the cry of the hunted animal (see the translation's note on 10.362).

The tenor answers in the dual: ὣς τὸν Τυδεΐδης ἠδʼ ὃ πτολίπορθος Ὀδυσσεὺς / λαοῦ ἀποτμήξαντε διώκετον ἐμμενὲς αἰεί, “so the son of Tydeus and Odysseus, sacker of cities, cut him off from the army and chased him without letting up”. ἐμμενὲς αἰεί is said of the hunters in both halves, and ἀποτμήγω, “cut off”, is the hunting dogs' maneuver: Dolon is kept from his own lines and driven the other way, toward the Achaean ships, as Odysseus planned (10.346–348). Dolon was “swift-footed” (10.316), and the simile makes him the swiftest of quarry, run down by two.

## In context

Odysseus sees Dolon coming from the Trojan camp and proposes to let him run past, then catch him, or, if he outruns them, keep him pressed toward the ships (10.338–348). The two lie down among the dead, and Dolon runs past “in his thoughtlessness” (10.349–350). When he is a furrow-length of mules away, they run after him; hearing their feet, he stops, hoping for companions sent by Hector to call him back, until at a spear's throw or less he knows them for enemies and flees (10.351–359; see [[mules]] and [[dolon]]). The simile follows, and the chase ends where the hounds have driven it. As Dolon is about to fall in with the Achaean sentries, Athena puts might into Diomedes, so that none of the others may boast that he struck first (10.365–368). Diomedes throws his spear over Dolon's shoulder on purpose, and the quarry stands still, teeth chattering, and is taken alive (10.369–377; see [[ambush]] and [[odysseus-and-diomedes]]).

The dogs of this simile are hunting dogs, and those of the sentries' simile a little earlier are watchdogs at the fold (10.183–189; see [[the-watchdogs]]). The chase runs from one to the other: the two hunters drive their quarry toward the sentries, and Dolon, who wore a wolf's skin to go among the Achaeans (10.334; see [[wolves]]), is hunted down like a fawn. The two men set out from the camp “like two lions” (10.297; see [[two-lions]]); here, in pursuit, they are dogs.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The hunting dog of the similes chases deer. Antilochus springs on a Trojan like a dog on a fawn that a hunter has wounded (15.579–581). Achilles chases Hector around the walls of Troy as a dog chases a fawn through glens and ravines, tracking it even when it cowers under a bush (22.188–193). Andromache, hearing the cries from the walls, fears that Achilles has “cut off” Hector alone from the city and is driving him onto the plain, μοῦνον ἀποτμήξας, the verb of this simile (22.456). The hare is the eagle's prey in the other two Iliad similes that have it (17.674–678, 22.308–311; see [[hare]]). Hunting dogs are set on a boar or a lion, more dangerous game, when Hector urges on the Trojans like a huntsman (11.292–295; see [[the-hound-at-the-heels]]). In the Odyssey the young men used to take Odysseus' dog Argos out after wild goats, deer and hares (*Odyssey* 17.294–295). See [[dogs]].
