## The simile

{{quote:2af1c976-7a0a-5178-bb11-3bca0917812c}}

τεύχεσι παμφαίνων ὥς τʼ ἠλέκτωρ ἐβεβήκει: “(he) came down, gleaming in his armor like the shining sun”. παμφαίνω is “shine all over, gleam”, a word for polished bronze and for stars (see [[gleaming-all-over]]). ἠλέκτωρ is “the beaming one”, a name for the sun (see the translation's note on 6.513). It occurs in Homer only here and at 19.398, where it stands beside Hyperion, the sun's own name. It is usually connected with ἤλεκτρον, the bright amber or electrum of the Odyssey's palaces and necklaces (*Odyssey* 4.73, 15.460, 18.296); one of those necklaces, of gold strung with amber, shines “like the sun” (ἠέλιον ὥς, *Odyssey* 18.296).

The comparison is of brightness only: the sun on bronze, and the man in the bronze. It comes in the line after the simile of the stallion and completes it (6.506–511; see [[the-stallion]]). The horse runs proud of its beauty; the man gleams like the sun. Both pictures are of display. Paris has put on “his glorious armor, inlaid with bronze” (6.504), the armor he was handling in his chamber when Hector found him (6.321–322).

## In context

Paris is going to the war at last. Hector has rebuked him, Helen has scorned him, and Hector has gone to find Andromache (6.325–368). He catches up with Hector at the gate and apologizes for being late, and Hector tells him he is brave but holds back of his own will (6.515–525; see [[paris]] and [[hectors-visit-to-troy]]). In the fighting that follows he kills Menesthius, a son of Areithous (7.8–10).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The half-line returns once. Achilles, armed in the armor Hephaestus made for him, mounts his chariot behind Automedon, “gleaming in his armor like the beaming Hyperion” (τεύχεσι παμφαίνων ὥς τʼ ἠλέκτωρ Ὑπερίων, 19.398), and calls to his horses. The line joins two men whose fates are bound together: Paris, with Apollo, will kill Achilles at the Scaean gates, as the dying Hector foretells (22.359–360; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).

Achilles' bronze shines again “like the gleam of blazing fire or of the rising sun” when he comes at Hector (22.134–135). In the Odyssey the sun is the measure of splendor at peace: the halls of Menelaus and of Alcinous gleam “as of the sun or of the moon” (*Odyssey* 4.45, 7.84), and so does the shroud that Penelope wove and washed (*Odyssey* 24.147–148).
