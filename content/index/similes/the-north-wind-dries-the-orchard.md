## The simile

{{quote:57fecb58-0deb-5198-99ae-2b839b8583d0}}

ὀπωρινός, “of late summer”, is from ὀπώρα, the season when the fruit ripens. The Iliad's other things of late summer are the star that shines brightest after bathing in Oceanus, to which Athena's fire on Diomedes is likened, and the day of violent rain when Zeus is angry with men (5.5, 16.385; see [[the-autumn-star]] and [[the-storm-of-late-summer]]). The wind is the North Wind, Βορέης, cold and drying, “born of the clear sky” (15.171; see [[boreas]]); in the *Odyssey* the same ὀπωρινὸς Βορέης carries thistles over a plain, as the winds toss Odysseus' raft (*Odyssey* 5.328–330; see [[thistles-in-the-north-wind]]). The orchard is an ἀλωή, a planted ground of fruit trees or vines, the word for the orchard of Priam where Achilles caught Lycaon cutting fig shoots (21.36, 21.77; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). νεοαρδής, “newly watered”, and ἀγξηραίνω, “dry up”, occur only here. The meaning of ἐθείρῃ, “tends”, is unknown; Walter Leaf notes that the word recurs in Greek in only one later verse, which explains nothing, and that the context suggests “tills” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.347; see the translation's note on 21.347).

The likeness is of the drying. The river has flooded the plain, “and all of it was full of the water that had poured out” (21.300), and now the fire dries it as quickly as a dry wind dries a watered orchard. The second half of the simile, χαίρει δέ μιν ὅς τις ἐθείρῃ, “and whoever tends it rejoices”, turns the god's fire into a farmer's good: the drying that pleases a gardener is a burning of the dead (21.343–349). The pictures of the book's two farming similes answer each other: in the first a man leads water through his garden and it outruns him (21.257–262; see [[the-channel-and-the-gardener]]), in the second the wind dries what he has watered, and he is glad.

## In context

Hera has called on Hephaestus to save Achilles from the river (21.328–341; see [[hera]] and [[hephaestus]]). The fire blazes up on the plain, burns the many dead whom Achilles had killed and the river had cast out, and dries the plain; then Hephaestus turns the flame on the river itself, and its trees, plants and fish burn (21.342–355; see [[fire]] and [[achilles-and-the-river]]). Hera has promised to raise the West and the South Wind (21.334–335; see [[zephyrus]] and [[notus]]), but the only wind in the episode is the North Wind of the simile.
