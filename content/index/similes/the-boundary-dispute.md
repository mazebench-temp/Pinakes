## The simile

{{quote:b88bd160-b3e1-5e94-a41c-6476162f16c5}}

ἀλλʼ ὥς τʼ ἀμφʼ οὔροισι δύʼ ἀνέρε δηριάασθον / μέτρʼ ἐν χερσὶν ἔχοντες ἐπιξύνῳ ἐν ἀρούρῃ: “But as two men contend over boundary stones, holding measuring rods in their hands, in a common field” (12.421–422). οὖροι are the stones that mark the limit of a field, as the stone Athena throws at Ares had been set up by men of old “to be the boundary of a field” (οὖρον ἀρούρης, 21.405; see [[boundary-stones]]). μέτρα, “measures”, are here rods for measuring land, and ἐπίξυνος, “common”, occurs only here, of a field in which both men have a share. ὥ τʼ ὀλίγῳ ἐνὶ χώρῳ ἐρίζητον περὶ ἴσης: “and in a narrow space they strive for an equal share” (12.423). ἴση is the fair share of a division, as Neleus divided the spoil of Elis “so that no one might go away cheated of his fair share” (ἴσης, 11.705). The quarrel is in the dual: δύʼ ἀνέρε δηριάασθον, ὥ … ἐρίζητον, a pair of men.

The tenor: ὣς ἄρα τοὺς διέεργον ἐπάλξιες, “so the battlements kept these apart” (12.424), and over them they hacked at the oxhide shields around one another's chests (12.424–426; see [[battlements]]). The point of likeness is the line and the deadlock. Each side presses on a strip of ground that neither will give up, and the mark between them, a row of stones in a field or a parapet on a wall, stays where it is. The narrow space of the simile answers the cramped fighting over the battlements.

## In context

Sarpedon has pulled a battlement down and called on the Lycians to follow, “the work of more men is better” (12.397–412; see [[sarpedon]] and [[lycians]]). They bear down harder, and the Argives strengthen their battalions inside the wall: “for neither could the mighty Lycians break the Danaans' wall and make a path to the ships, nor could the Danaan spearmen ever thrust the Lycians back from the wall, once they had first come close to it” (12.413–420). The simile follows, and the slaughter on the towers and battlements, spattered with the blood of both sides (12.425–431). A second simile from peaceful life closes the deadlock, the working woman who holds her scales level (12.432–436; see [[the-working-womans-scales]] and [[the-fight-at-the-wall]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Disputes and boundaries belong to the life of peace that the similes bring into the war. On the shield of Achilles two men quarrel in the assembly over the blood-price of a dead man, and both go to an arbiter for a settlement (18.497–501). The shield shows the land being worked as well, the plowland, the king's domain and the vineyard (18.541–572). The stone that marked a field in peace becomes a weapon in Book 21, when Athena hurls it at Ares (21.403–406).
