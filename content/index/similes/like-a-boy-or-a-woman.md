## The simile

{{quote:b6836b37-7d54-5541-9139-6ca3b7a637d8}}

μή τί μευ ἠΰτε παιδὸς ἀφαυροῦ πειρήτιζε / ἠὲ γυναικός, ἣ οὐκ οἶδεν πολεμήϊα ἔργα. ἠΰτε, “as, like”, is the particle of Ajax's shield “like a tower” a few lines before (7.219). ἀφαυρός is “feeble, weak”; at the end of the book Zeus uses the comparative of a god “far feebler” than Poseidon (ἀφαυρότερος, 7.457). πειρητίζω is “test, make trial of”. πολεμήϊα ἔργα, “the works of war”, are what boys and women do not know. Nestor called the army “foolish little ones, who care nothing for the works of war” (2.337–338; see [[children-and-widows]]), and Zeus told Aphrodite, “Not to you, my child, are given the works of war” (5.428).

The comparison is short and has no scene. It works by denial: Hector is what a boy or a woman is not. The next four lines say what he is, in a list of skills, each beginning “I know” (οἶδα). He knows battles and the killing of men; how to swing the dry oxhide to the right and to the left; how to charge into the fray of swift horses; how to dance for Ares in the standing fight (7.237–241; see [[with-the-oxhide-shield]] and the translation's notes on 7.238–239 and 7.241). He will strike Ajax openly, not by lying in wait (7.242–243).

## In context

Ajax has come near with his shield like a tower and threatened him: Hector will learn “what kind of chiefs are among the Danaans too, even after Achilles” (7.226–232; see [[like-a-tower]]). He speaks with a champion's scorn, and Hector answers the scorn. Ajax had not called him a boy. Hector puts the insult into words himself, to deny it, and then throws his spear (7.244). See [[hector]] and [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]].

The book is full of such measures of manhood. Menelaus called the Achaeans who would not face Hector “Achaean women, Achaean men no longer” (7.96), in the words of Thersites (2.235; see [[achaean-women-no-longer-men]]). Hector imagined his challenge as a test of the best of the Achaeans (7.73–75), and at the end of the duel he praises Ajax for the gifts a god has given him, “stature and strength and good sense” (7.288–289).

## Elsewhere in Homer

To be likened to a child or a woman is an insult in the Iliad. Diomedes tells Paris, who has shot him in the foot, that he cares no more than if a woman or a senseless child had hit him (11.389–390). Aeneas and Hector both tell Achilles not to try to frighten them with words, “like a child” (νηπύτιον ὥς, 20.200–202, 20.431–433). Before his last fight Hector fears that Achilles will kill him unarmed, “like a woman” (22.124–125). The Ajax whom Hector tells not to treat him as a feeble boy fells him with a stone in Book 14, and the narrator says that the man who hit him was “not the feeblest of the Achaeans” (ἀφαυρότατος, 15.11).
