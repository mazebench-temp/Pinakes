## The simile

τῶν δʼ ὥς τʼ ὀρνίθων πετεηνῶν ἔθνεα πολλὰ / χηνῶν ἢ γεράνων ἢ κύκνων δουλιχοδείρων / Ἀσίω ἐν λειμῶνι Καϋστρίου ἀμφὶ ῥέεθρα: “and as the many tribes of winged birds, geese or cranes or long-necked swans, in the Asian meadow, by the streams of the Cayster”. ἔθνεα are “tribes, flocks”, πετεηνῶν “winged”, δουλιχοδείρων “long-necked”. The birds fly ἔνθα καὶ ἔνθα (“here and there”), ἀγαλλόμενα πτερύγεσσι (“glorying in their wings”), and settle κλαγγηδὸν προκαθιζόντων, “with loud cries, each ahead of the next”, and “the meadow resounds” (σμαραγεῖ δέ τε λειμών).

The tenor: the many tribes of men “poured out onto the plain of the Scamander; and the earth beneath rang terribly under the feet of the men and of their horses” (2.464–466). The birds give noise and motion: flock after flock comes down beyond the last, as the contingents take their ground on the plain. The meadow resounds under the birds, and the earth rings (κονάβιζε) under the men.

## In context

{{quote:d65fb152-00f9-5b63-bd8c-3537efdc1c0b}}

After the gleam of bronze ([[forest-fire]]), the second simile of the run turns from sight to sound. It also recalls the bees that brought the army to the assembly. ἔθνεα, their word (2.87), serves for the birds, and the line of application returns word for word: ὣς τῶν ἔθνεα πολλὰ νεῶν ἄπο καὶ κλισιάων, “so the many tribes of men, from the ships and huts” (2.91 and 2.464; [[swarming-bees]]). Then the men went to an assembly; now they go to war. The meadow of the simile becomes the meadow of the story: “they stood in the flowering meadow of the Scamander” (2.467; [[scamander]]).

The [[asian-meadow|Asian meadow]] lay along the lower [[cayster|Cayster]], near Ephesus. Like the [[icarian-sea|Icarian sea]] (2.145), it belongs to the eastern Aegean, and this local knowledge is often taken as a sign of the poet's Ionian background. For the birds, see [[geese]], [[cranes]] and [[swans]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the next book the Trojans advance with clamor like cranes flying over the streams of Ocean (Iliad 3.2–7). The line of the three birds, “geese or cranes or long-necked swans”, returns when Hector darts at a ship like a tawny eagle swooping on a flock of birds feeding by a river (15.690–693).

## Outside Homer

Virgil remembers the birds of the Cayster among his signs of weather (*Georgics* 1.383–384). In his catalogue of the Italian forces, the men of Messapus march singing like swans returning from their feeding, and the river and the “Asian marsh” resound (*Aeneid* 7.699–702).
