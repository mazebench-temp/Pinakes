## The simile

ὃς καὶ χρυσὸν ἔχων πόλεμον δʼ ἴεν ἠΰτε κούρη / νήπιος: “and he came to the war wearing gold, like a girl, the fool”. χρυσὸν ἔχων is “having gold”, wearing it as ornament. ἠΰτε is “like”, and κούρη “girl, young woman”. νήπιος (“fool”) is the narrator's word for a man who cannot see what is coming ([[nepios]]).

The vehicle is a girl in her gold jewelry; the tenor, a warrior who goes to war in the same finery. The point is display. Gold is for adornment, not for battle, and the next words say what it was worth: οὐδέ τί οἱ τό γʼ ἐπήρκεσε λυγρὸν ὄλεθρον, “and it did nothing to keep off grim destruction”.

## In context

{{quote:23b85784-5f14-5a0e-a864-4125ff74640c}}

The lines close the entry for the [[carians|Carians]], “of barbarous speech” ([[barbarophonos|βαρβαρόφωνοι]], 2.867), who were led by the brothers [[nastes|Nastes]] and [[amphimachus-of-caria|Amphimachus]], “the splendid children of Nomion” (2.870–871). The relative ὅς (“who”) could refer to either brother, and the Greek does not say which.

The gold did not save him: “he was brought down at the hands of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus in the river, and wise-hearted Achilles carried off the gold” (2.874–875). The same line foretold the death of the Mysian augur Ennomus, whose birds did not save him (2.858–861). The Iliad as we have it does not narrate the killing: the river fighting of Book 21 names neither brother (see [[the-river-battle-foretold]]). The gold meant for show becomes Achilles' spoil ([[gold-and-silver]]).

The narrator gave Agamemnon the same word, νήπιος, when he believed the Dream (2.38). Both men misjudge what lies ahead.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The comparison and the word come together again when Patroclus stands weeping before Achilles. Achilles asks why he is crying like a little girl (ἠΰτε κούρη / νηπίη) who runs beside her mother and begs to be picked up (Iliad 16.7–11).
