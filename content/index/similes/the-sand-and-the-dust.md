## The comparison

{{quote:9169bac7-9b25-53a5-bb68-9544ce6fb669}}

οὐδʼ εἴ μοι τόσα δοίη ὅσα ψάμαθός τε κόνις τε, “not if he gave me as much as the sand and the dust”. ψάμαθος is the sand of the shore, κόνις the dust of the plain, and the pair occurs only here in Homer. The comparison is of number, the number that cannot be counted.

It closes a sentence of refusals, each larger than the last. First come multiples: ten times and twenty times as much as Agamemnon has now, and whatever else might come to him from elsewhere (9.379–380). Then the two richest cities Achilles can name: all that comes in to Orchomenus, the Minyan city of Boeotia, and all that comes to Thebes in Egypt, where the most treasures lie stored, with a hundred gates and two hundred men driving out with horses and chariots through each (9.381–384; see [[orchomenus]] and [[thebes-in-egypt]]). Last comes the sand and the dust, which no one can count. Then the sentence turns back to its subject: “not even so would Agamemnon any longer persuade my heart, until he has paid me back for all his heart-grieving insult” (9.386–387). The list of gifts that Odysseus has just recited is long and exact (9.264–299; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). Achilles answers with sums that have no end, because what he asks is not a sum (see [[honor-and-dishonor]]). λώβη, “insult”, is the word of Book 1, where Achilles told Agamemnon that his insult would be his last, if he ruled over men worth anything (λωβήσαιο, 1.231–232).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Sand is the measure of the numberless once more in the Iliad. Iris, in the likeness of Polites, warns the Trojans that the Achaeans are coming across the plain “like leaves or like the sands, far too many” (2.800; see [[like-leaves-or-sand]]). Elsewhere sand is the stuff of the shore: the beach that Poseidon will cover with sand when he washes away the Achaean wall (7.461–462, 12.31), and the sand castle that a boy builds and knocks down, to which the narrator likens Apollo throwing down that wall (15.361–364).

## Outside Homer

The same measure of the countless runs through ancient poetry. Pindar ends his second Olympian ode: “since sand has escaped counting”, who could tell how many joys Theron has given to others? (*Olympian* 2.98–100). God promises to multiply Abraham's seed “as the stars of the heaven, and as the sand which is upon the sea shore” (Genesis 22.17). Archimedes wrote *The Sand-Reckoner* (Ψαμμίτης) to show that the grains of sand, which some thought past all number, could be counted, even the sand that would fill the universe.
