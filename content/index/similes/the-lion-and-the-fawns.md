## The simile

{{quote:8cac8130-edc5-5b5a-a53a-379d7c8cdc70}}

ὡς δὲ λέων ἐλάφοιο ταχείης νήπια τέκνα / ῥηϊδίως συνέαξε λαβὼν κρατεροῖσιν ὀδοῦσιν: “as a lion seizes the helpless young of a swift hind in his strong teeth and easily crushes them”. νήπιος is “young, helpless”, the word for a child who cannot fend for himself. συνέαξε, from συνάγνυμι, is “crushes, breaks together”, a gnomic aorist. The lion comes “into their lair” (εἰς εὐνήν) and “takes away their tender life” (ἁπαλόν … ἦτορ ἀπηύρα). Then the simile turns to the mother. ἣ δʼ εἴ πέρ τε τύχῃσι μάλα σχεδόν, οὐ δύναταί σφι / χραισμεῖν: “and the mother, even if she happens to be very near, cannot help them”, for “a dreadful trembling comes over her herself”, and she darts away through the brush and the woods, “hurrying and sweating, before the onrush of the mighty beast” (11.116–119).

The tenor takes up the mother's verb. “So none could ward off destruction from those two, none of the Trojans, but they themselves were fleeing before the Argives” (11.120–121): χραισμῆσαι answers χραισμεῖν. The point of likeness is less the lion's strength, which is taken for granted (ῥηϊδίως, “easily”), than the helplessness of those who ought to protect. The two brothers are the fawns, and the Trojans, who should save them, are the hind that runs.

## In context

Isus, a bastard son of Priam, was driving, and Antiphus, born in wedlock, stood beside him in the same chariot. Agamemnon strikes Isus in the chest with his spear and Antiphus beside the ear with his sword, throws him from the chariot, and strips them both in haste (11.101–110; see [[isus]] and [[antiphus-son-of-priam]]). He knows them. Achilles had once caught the pair herding sheep on Ida, bound them with willow shoots and let them go for ransom, and Agamemnon had seen them by the ships (11.104–112; see [[achilles-raids]]). Spared once by Achilles, they are not spared again.

The pair is the second of three that Agamemnon kills in this part of his day. After it come the two sons of Antimachus, also “in one chariot”, against whom he rushes “like a lion” (11.122–130; see [[like-a-lion]] and [[sons-of-antimachus]]). Diomedes killed two sons of Priam from one chariot in Book 5, as a lion breaks the neck of a heifer (5.159–165; see [[lion-among-cattle]]). See [[the-aristeia-of-agamemnon]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Odyssey turns the scene around. Menelaus, hearing of the suitors in Odysseus' house, likens them to fawns that a hind has laid to sleep in a mighty lion's lair while she grazes in the glens; the lion comes back to his lair and brings a shameful death on both, and so will Odysseus on the suitors (*Odyssey* 4.335–340; Telemachus repeats the lines to Penelope, 17.126–131). There the hind has put her young in the lion's bed; here the lion comes into hers. The deer is the coward's beast in the Iliad (1.225, 4.243, 13.102–104; see [[deer]] and [[like-fawns]]), and the mother who cannot save her young has a counterpart in the sparrow at Aulis, eaten with her nestlings by the serpent (2.311–316; see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]).
