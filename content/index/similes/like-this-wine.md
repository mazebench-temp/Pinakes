## The simile

ὁππότεροι πρότεροι ὑπὲρ ὅρκια πημήνειαν / ὧδέ σφʼ ἐγκέφαλος χαμάδις ῥέοι ὡς ὅδε οἶνος / αὐτῶν καὶ τεκέων, ἄλοχοι δʼ ἄλλοισι δαμεῖεν: “whichever side first does harm in defiance of the oaths, may their brains pour out on the ground like this wine, theirs and their children's, and may their wives be subdued by others”. ἐγκέφαλος is the brain (literally “what is in the head”); χαμάδις, “to the ground”; ῥέοι, “may it flow”, is an optative of wish. ὧδε … ὡς ὅδε, “so … as this”, points twice at the thing in the speaker's hand. ὑπὲρ ὅρκια, “beyond, in defiance of the oaths”, returns when the oaths are broken in Book 4 (4.67, 4.236). δαμεῖεν, “be subdued”, is said of a woman given to a man's bed, as Thetis says Zeus “subdued” her to a mortal husband (18.432).

This is a comparison of a special kind. The vehicle is not described but shown: the wine that the speakers are pouring out of their cups as they speak. The tenor is a future that the speakers call down on the oath-breakers. The point of likeness is the flow to the ground, and it is total: wine poured out cannot be gathered up again, and the oath-breakers' brains, and their children's, and their wives are to be lost the same way.

## In context

{{quote:e154f48d-ea03-5d7e-9f0f-16b367131580}}

Agamemnon has cut the throats of the lambs and laid them on the ground, gasping, “for the bronze had taken away their strength” (3.292–294; see [[the-oaths-sworn]]). Then the wine is drawn from the mixing bowl in cups and poured out, and the men of both armies pray. The narrator gives their prayer in the form he uses for what “a man would say” in a crowd (τις-speech; see [[tis-speech]]). The victims are killed and the wine is spilled in the sight of the gods called to witness, so that the fate of whoever breaks the oath is shown as well as spoken (see [[oath]] and [[libation]]).

The narrator adds a warning at once: “So they spoke, but the son of Cronus was not yet going to fulfill their prayer” (3.302). The same half-line refused Agamemnon's prayer for the fall of Troy in Book 2 (2.419). In Book 4 the Trojan Pandarus, urged on by Athena, shoots Menelaus during the truce. Agamemnon, over his wounded brother, recalls this rite: the oath, the blood of lambs and the unmixed libations are not in vain, and if Zeus does not fulfill them at once he will fulfill them late, and the Trojans will pay “with their own heads and their wives and their children” (4.155–168).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The curse takes its image from the battlefield, where a spear goes through helmet and bone and “the brain inside was all spattered” (11.96–98, 12.184–186, 20.398–400). In the Odyssey the same words describe the Cyclops' meal: he dashes two of Odysseus' men to the ground like puppies, “and their brains flowed out on the ground (ἐγκέφαλος χαμάδις ῥέε), and wet the earth” (Odyssey 9.288–290). The second half of that line is used of wine: Achilles, keeping vigil by Patroclus' pyre, draws wine from a golden bowl and pours it on the ground all night, “and wet the earth” (23.218–221). Wine and brains are poured out in the same words.

## Outside Homer

Curses that act out their own fulfillment are found in the treaty oaths of the ancient Near East, where wax is melted or an animal cut apart with the prayer that the oath-breaker may suffer the same, and in the Roman fetial rite, where the priest strikes a pig and asks Jupiter to strike the Roman people so if they break the treaty (Livy 1.24.7–9). Christopher Faraone set the wine of Book 3 among such rites (“Molten Wax, Spilt Wine and Mutilated Animals: Sympathetic Magic in Near Eastern and Early Greek Oath Ceremonies”, *Journal of Hellenic Studies* 113, 1993).
