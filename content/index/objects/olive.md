ἐλαίη is the olive tree, ἔλαιον its oil, and ἐλάϊνος, “of olive wood”, the adjective.

## In the Iliad

The tree itself is named once, in a simile. Euphorbus, killed by Menelaus, is like “a flourishing sapling of olive” that a man rears in a lonely place where water drenches it in plenty; it is shaken by every wind and teems with white blossom, until a sudden storm wrenches it out of its trench and stretches it on the ground (17.53–58; see [[the-olive-sapling]]). The tree is cultivated, planted in a trench and watered. Walter Leaf reports that Hehn held that only the wild olive was known in early Homeric times and took this tree as a sign that the passage is late, a view Leaf calls founded “on very weak grounds” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.53).

Its wood is the handle of Peisander's weapon: he takes from under his shield “a fine / axe of good bronze, on a handle of olive wood, / long and well polished” (13.611–613; see [[axe]] and [[peisander-killed-by-menelaus]]). Its oil anoints men after bathing: Odysseus and Diomedes, back from the night raid, “anointed themselves richly with olive oil” (10.577), and Hera anoints herself with an ambrosial oil before she goes to Zeus (14.171–172; see [[bathing]]). In the later books the oil is for the dead: the body of Patroclus is washed and anointed (18.350), and Aphrodite anoints Hector's body with rose oil (23.186). The river Titaressus flows on top of the Peneius “like olive oil” (2.753–754; see [[like-olive-oil]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The olive is a tree of the Odyssey. On Scheria Odysseus, washed ashore, sleeps under two bushes grown from one root, one of φυλίη and one of olive, so thick that neither wind nor sun nor rain comes through (*Odyssey* 5.475–480). Calypso gives him an axe with a handle of olive wood to build his raft (*Odyssey* 5.234–236). Olives grow in the orchard of Alcinous and among the fruit trees that hang over Tantalus (*Odyssey* 7.114–116, 11.588–590). The club of the Cyclops is a trunk of green olive wood, which Odysseus sharpens and heats in the fire and drives into his eye (*Odyssey* 9.319–320, 9.375–394). On Ithaca a long-leaved olive stands at the head of the harbor of Phorcys, beside the cave of the Naiads; the Phaeacians leave Odysseus' gifts at its root, and he and Athena sit at the foot of “the sacred olive” to plan the death of the suitors (*Odyssey* 13.102–104, 13.122, 13.372–373). His bed is an olive: a long-leaved olive grew in the courtyard, he built the bedchamber around it, lopped its leafy top, and trimmed the trunk from the root to make the bedpost, and this is the sign by which Penelope knows him (*Odyssey* 23.183–204). In Laertes' orchard not a fig, a vine, an olive or a pear is without care (*Odyssey* 24.245–247).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus tells of the olive on the Acropolis of Athens. In the temple of Erechtheus there were an olive and a sea, which the Athenians said Poseidon and Athena set there as witnesses when they contended for the land. The Persians burned the olive with the rest of the sanctuary, and on the second day after the burning the Athenians who went up to sacrifice saw that a shoot a cubit long had sprung from the stump (Herodotus 8.55). He also reports that when the Pythia told the Epidaurians to make images of Damia and Auxesia from the wood of a cultivated olive, they asked the Athenians for one, believing their olives the most sacred; it is said, he adds, that at that time there were olives nowhere else on earth but at Athens (5.82).
