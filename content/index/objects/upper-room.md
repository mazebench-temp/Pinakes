## The word

ὑπερώϊον, from ὑπέρ, “above”, is the upper floor of a house, usually in the plural, ὑπερώϊα, “the upper rooms”. A stairway leads up to it (κλῖμαξ, *Odyssey* 1.330, 21.5), and the women of the house live there, apart from the hall where the men feast (see [[house]] and [[handmaids]]).

## In the Iliad

The upper room is where a girl of a great house is kept, and where a god finds her. Astyoche, “a modest maiden, she went up to the upper room” in the house of Actor, and Ares lay with her in secret; she bore him Ascalaphus and Ialmenus (2.512–515; see [[astyoche]]). Hermes saw Polymele dancing in the chorus of Artemis, and “At once he went up to her upper room and lay with her in secret”; she bore him Eudorus (16.180–186; see [[polymele]] and [[the-birth-of-eudorus]]).

## In the Odyssey

The upper room is Penelope's. She comes down from it to the suitors with two handmaids (*Odyssey* 1.330–335, 18.206–211), and she goes back up to it with her women and weeps for Odysseus until Athena casts sleep on her eyelids. The verses of the first book return after she has talked with the stranger by night and when Telemachus sends her away from the contest of the bow, and with another first verse after she has rebuked Antinous for plotting her son's death (*Odyssey* 1.362–364, 19.602–604, 21.356–358, 16.449–451). She lies there fasting when she learns that Telemachus has sailed (*Odyssey* 4.787–788), and weaves there at her loom, seldom seen by the suitors (*Odyssey* 15.515–517). Her bed there, she says, has been a bed of groaning, wet with her tears ever since Odysseus went to Troy (*Odyssey* 17.101–104, 19.594–597). Eurycleia goes up to it to wake her with the news of his return, and she comes down from it to meet him (*Odyssey* 22.428–429, 23.1–9, 23.85–87).
