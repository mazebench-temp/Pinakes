## The word

τάλαντον is first the balance, and in the plural the pair of scales: Zeus holds out his golden scales, χρύσεια … τάλαντα, to weigh the fates of the Achaeans and the Trojans, and of Achilles and Hector (8.69, 22.209; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]), and in a simile an honest working woman holds her scales level, the weight on one side and the wool on the other (12.433–435; see [[the-working-womans-scales]]). The talent is a weight measured on them. In Homer it is always a weight of gold, χρυσοῖο τάλαντα or χρυσοῦ … τάλαντα, and the poems count it in whole talents and in halves, ἡμιτάλαντον.

## In Homer

Talents of gold go with tripods, cauldrons, horses and women among the greatest gifts. “Ten talents of gold” follow the seven tripods untouched by fire at the head of Agamemnon's gifts to Achilles (9.122, 9.264; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]), and when the gifts are brought, “Odysseus weighed out ten talents of gold in all / and led the way” (19.247–248). Priam weighs out ten talents of gold for the ransom of Hector (24.232; see [[apoina]]). On the Shield of Achilles, “in the middle lay two talents of gold, / to give to the one among them who spoke the straightest judgment” (18.507–508; see [[the-trial-on-the-shield]]). In the Odyssey Menelaus received ten talents of gold from Polybus of Egyptian Thebes, Aegisthus paid his watchman a wage of two, and Maron gave Odysseus seven (*Odyssey* 4.128–129, 4.524–526, 9.201–202).

How much a talent was worth is not said. Walter Leaf held that it was little (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.269), and the games for Patroclus bear him out: two talents of gold are the fourth prize of the chariot race, after a mare and a cauldron, and half a talent of gold the last prize of the footrace, after a silver mixing bowl and an ox (23.262–270, 23.740–751). On Odysseus' weighing of the ten talents Leaf notes that it shows the talent to have been a known weight of gold, though the talents were most probably wedges or bars that needed only counting; the stress laid on the weighing would imply a large sum, “whereas we know that it was but small”, and may be a sign of a later age, when the talent had become large (on 19.247).

## Outside Homer

In Herodotus the talent is a great weight of silver or gold, the unit in which the Persian king's tribute is reckoned. Darius fixed the tribute of the peoples who paid in silver by the weight of the Babylonian talent, and of those who paid in gold by the Euboean, the Babylonian talent being worth seventy-eight Euboean minas; the whole yearly tribute came, Herodotus reckons, to 14,560 Euboean talents (3.89.2, 3.95; see [[herodotus]]).
