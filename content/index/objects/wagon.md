## The words

Homer has two words for the wagon. ἅμαξα is the common one, the wagon of later Greek too. ἀπήνη is found in the Iliad only in the last book, and in the Odyssey only of Nausicaa's wagon; in both poems it names the same mule wagon as ἅμαξα (24.266, 24.275; *Odyssey* 6.72–73), and the translation renders both “wagon” (see the translation's row ἅμαξα / ἀπήνη). The wagon runs on four wheels, τετράκυκλος (24.324), where the war chariot has two (see [[chariot]]): in the Odyssey not even twenty-two good four-wheeled wagons could have levered the Cyclops' door-stone from the ground (*Odyssey* 9.241–242), as not even two men could easily heave Hector's stone onto a wagon (12.447–448). It is drawn by mules or by oxen (24.150, 24.782; see [[mules]] and [[cattle]]).

The πεῖρινς, rendered “wicker body”, is the box bound on the frame (24.190, 24.267). In the Odyssey Peisistratus stows Menelaus' gifts in the πεῖρινς of his chariot (*Odyssey* 15.131). Walter Leaf accepts Hesychius' explanation, a plaited body on a wagon, the square basket set on it, and thinks it probably the same as the ὑπερτερίη fitted on Nausicaa's wagon (*Odyssey* 6.70; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.190).

The wagon's epithets are ἐΰτροχος, “well-wheeled” (24.150, 24.179, 24.189, 24.266, 24.711; see [[well-wheeled]]), ἐΰξεστος, “polished” (24.275, 24.578, 24.590), and once πρωτοπαγής, “newly built” (24.267), the word of the eleven chariots that Pandarus left at home (5.194; see [[chariot]]).

## In the Iliad

Wagons belong to the work of peace and to the dead. During the truce of Book 7 the Trojans wash the blood from their dead and, “shedding hot tears”, lift them “onto the wagons” to burn them (7.424–426; see [[cremation]]). The stone with which Hector breaks the gate of the Achaean wall is one that not even two men, the best of the people, “could easily heave it from the ground onto a wagon, / such as mortals are now” (12.447–449; see [[stone-throwing]]). The last book is the wagon's book: Priam's mule wagon carries the ransom to the hut of Achilles and brings Hector's body home, and wagons drawn by oxen and mules bring in the wood for his pyre (24.149–151, 24.263–277, 24.578–590, 24.697–718, 24.782–784; see the note on Book 24 and [[the-ransom-of-hector]]). The Achaeans had brought the wood for Patroclus' pyre from Ida on their mules, the logs bound behind them (23.120–122; see [[ida]]).

The Great Bear has the wagon's name: “and the Bear, which they also call by the name of the Wagon” (18.487; see [[the-bear]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod's farmer must build his own. A wagon has a hundred timbers, which the man rich only in fancy does not know; it is easy to say “lend me two oxen and a wagon”, and easy to refuse, for the oxen have work to do (*Works and Days* 453–457). He gives the measures of its parts, an axle seven feet long and a wheel rim of three spans for a wagon ten palms wide (*Works and Days* 424–426), and warns against loading a wagon beyond measure, so that the axle breaks and the load is lost (*Works and Days* 692–693; see [[hesiod]]). In Solon's story in Herodotus the Argive brothers Cleobis and Biton, when the oxen did not come from the field in time for the festival of Hera, put themselves under the yoke and drew their mother's wagon forty-five stades to the temple; after the sacrifice and the feast they fell asleep in the temple and never rose again (Herodotus 1.31.2–5; see [[herodotus]]).
