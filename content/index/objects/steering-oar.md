## The word

The Homeric ship was steered not with a rudder hung on pivots, as a modern ship is, but with an oar at or near the stern, like the oar that steers a lifeboat; its blade was perhaps broader than a rowing oar's, as the name, from πηδόν, “oar-blade”, suggests, and the helmsman sat to work it (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix I §17). Carvings and coins show ships with two such oars, but Homer speaks of only one on a ship (*Odyssey* 3.281, 5.255, 5.270, 5.315; Merry, Appendix I §17). Merry takes μετὰ χερσί, “in his hands”, at the death of Menelaus' helmsman as proof that it was a paddle, since a tiller would not be grasped between the hands (on 3.281).

οἰήιον is sometimes the same thing, as in the verse told of the Cyclops' two throws, that the rock just missed the end of the steering oar (*Odyssey* 9.483, 9.540); the plural is used of many ships (19.43) and of one (*Odyssey* 12.218). Merry thinks the οἰήιον was properly the handle of the steering oar, or a ring through which its shaft passed, as οἴηκες are the rings of a yoke (24.269; Merry, Appendix I §17). The translation renders both words “steering oar”.

## In Homer

When Achilles calls the Achaeans to assembly, even those came who had always stayed in the gathering of the ships, “who were helmsmen and held the steering oars of the ships, / and were stewards beside the ships, dealing out the food” (19.42–44; see [[assembly]]). The helmsman's skill is Nestor's example of cunning against force: “by cunning again a helmsman on the wine-dark sea / steers a swift ship buffeted by the winds” (23.316–317; see [[metis]]).

## Elsewhere in the Odyssey

At Sunium Apollo kills Menelaus' helmsman Phrontis “as he held the steering oar of the speeding ship in his hands”, the best of men at steering a ship whenever storms rushed on (*Odyssey* 3.279–283; see [[phrontis-son-of-onetor]]). When Odysseus taunts the Cyclops from his ship, the blind giant throws the peak of a mountain and then a greater stone, which fall in front of the ship and behind it, and of each throw the same verse says that it just missed the end of the steering oar (*Odyssey* 9.480–486, 9.537–542, plain renderings; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). Before Scylla Odysseus bids his helmsman, “since you work the steering oars of the hollow ship”, keep the ship away from the smoke and the surf (*Odyssey* 12.217–220, a plain rendering). The ships of the Phaeacians need neither: “the Phaeacians have no helmsmen, / nor any steering oars, such as other ships have”, for the ships themselves know the thoughts of men and all their cities (*Odyssey* 8.557–560, a plain rendering; see [[phaeacians]]).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus describes the cargo boats of the Nile, built of short planks of acacia set like bricks around long pegs: they have a single steering oar, πηδάλιον, which passes through the keel, a mast of acacia and sails of papyrus (Herodotus 2.96.1–3; see [[herodotus]]).

Virgil's helmsman Palinurus keeps his hands on the tiller and his eyes on the stars until Sleep throws him overboard with it, and swims to Italy as Odysseus swims to Scheria (*Aeneid* 5–6; see [[virgils-palinurus]]).
