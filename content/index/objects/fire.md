## The word

πῦρ (pyr), genitive πυρός, dative πυρί, is fire. Its plural, πυρά, names the fires of a camp at night (8.509, 8.554, 8.562; see [[night-watch]]), and from the same stem come πυρή, the funeral pyre (see [[pyres]]), and πυρσοί, the beacons that a besieged island lights at sunset (18.211; see [[the-beacon-fires]]).

Fire has its standing epithets. It is ἀκάματον, “tireless” (5.4, 15.731, 16.122, 18.225; see [[tireless]]), θεσπιδαές, “wondrous” (12.177, 15.597, 20.490; see [[wondrous]]), δήϊον, “consuming” (9.347, 16.127; see [[destructive]]), κήλεον, “blazing” (8.217, 15.744, 18.346), μαλερόν, “ravening” (9.242, 20.316; see [[ravening]]), ἀΐδηλον, “destroying” (2.455, 9.436, 11.155), and ὀλοόν, “deadly” (13.629, 15.605). Its strength is its μένος, “might”: the Chimaera breathes out “the terrible might of blazing fire” (6.182; see [[chimaera]]), and Menelaus says that “Hector has the dread might of fire” (17.565; see [[menos]]).

## In the Iliad

**The hearth and the altar.** Fire cooks the meal and carries the offering to the gods. When the envoys come to Achilles' hut, he sets down a chopping block “in the light of the fire”, Patroclus makes the fire blaze high, and when it has burned down he spreads the embers and roasts the meat; then he throws the offerings into the fire (9.206–220; see [[sacrifice]]). At Agamemnon's sacrifice the entrails are held “over the flame of Hephaestus” (2.426). When the body of Patroclus is brought in, a tripod is set over the fire to heat the water for washing him (18.344–348; see [[tripod]] and [[bathing]]).

**Watch fires.** On the night after the second day of battle the Trojans camp on the plain: “A thousand fires were burning on the plain, and beside each / sat fifty men in the glare of the blazing fire” (8.562–563; see [[night-watch]]). In the city the women are to burn a great fire in every hall, against a surprise attack while the army is away (8.519–522).

**The pyre.** The dead are given to the fire. Hector asks that, if he falls, his body be given back, “so that when I am dead / the Trojans and the wives of the Trojans may give me my portion of fire” (7.79–80); Agamemnon will not grudge the dead “the quick comfort of fire” (7.410; see [[the-comfort-of-fire]]); and in the truce both sides heap their dead on the pyre and burn them (7.428–432; see [[cremation]] and [[pyres]]). The burning of Patroclus is told in Book 23 (23.110–257).

**Fire against the ships.** From the second day of battle Hector's aim is to burn the ships: “let there be some remembrance of consuming fire, / so that I may set fire to the ships and kill the men themselves” (8.181–182). In the embassy Achilles bids Agamemnon consider how to ward off “the consuming fire from the ships” (9.347). At the ships Hector cries “Bring fire” (15.718), and Ajax drives back every Trojan “whoever brought tireless fire” (15.731), until Hector shears off the head of Ajax's spear and the Trojans throw tireless fire on Protesilaus' ship, and “an unquenchable flame poured over it” (16.114–123). Achilles sees the blaze and sends out Patroclus, who drives the Trojans from the ships “and put out the blazing fire” (16.124–129, 16.293; see [[the-battle-at-the-ships]] and [[the-death-of-protesilaus]]).

**The burning of cities.** Fire ends a siege. In Phoenix's story of Meleager, when the enemy is already setting fire to the city, Meleager's wife begs him to fight, recounting the sorrows that come to men whose city is taken: “they kill the men, and fire levels the city” (9.588–594; see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). Agamemnon prays that he may burn the doors of Priam's hall “with consuming fire” (2.415), and Hera and Athena have sworn never to ward off the evil day from the Trojans, “not even when all Troy blazes with ravening fire” (20.315–317). The Iliad does not tell the burning of Troy; it foretells it (see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]] and [[the-fall-of-troy]]).

**The forge.** Hephaestus turns his bellows toward the fire and casts bronze, tin, gold and silver into it to make the arms of Achilles (18.468–475; see [[forge]] and [[the-making-of-the-arms]]).

**Fire from the gods.** Athena kindles a tireless fire from the helmet and shield of Diomedes (5.4–7; see [[the-autumn-star]]) and a flame from the head of Achilles at the ditch, and the Trojan charioteers are struck with terror “when they saw the tireless fire / blazing terribly above the head of the great-hearted son of Peleus” (18.205–227; see [[the-beacon-fires]]). Eyes blaze like fire: Agamemnon's in his anger (1.104; see [[eyes-like-fire]]), Hector's as he leaps through the gates (12.466), and Achilles' as he arms (19.365–366).

**In the similes.** Fire is the poem's likeness of destruction spreading. The bronze of the marching army flashes like a destroying fire on a mountain, and the Trojans fall before Agamemnon, and later before Achilles, like woodland before a fire driven by the wind (2.455–458, 11.155–159, 20.490–494; see [[forest-fire]]). The war around the body of Patroclus is “savage as fire, which, rushing on a city of men, / springs up suddenly and blazes” (17.737–738; see [[the-burning-city]]), and armies fight on “like blazing fire” (11.596; see [[like-blazing-fire]]). Hector leads the Trojans “like a flame” (13.53; see [[like-a-flame]]), and Achilles' hands are like fire (20.371–372; see [[hands-like-fire]]).

**Hephaestus.** Fire is the element of [[hephaestus|Hephaestus]], and the poem can call it by his name: the entrails are held “over the flame of Hephaestus” (2.426), where the Greek has only Ἡφαίστοιο, and hogs are stretched to singe “across the flame of Hephaestus” (9.468), while Hector shouts “like the flame of Hephaestus, / unquenchable” (17.88–89). In Book 21 the god himself goes to war as fire against the river (21.328–382).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the Odyssey fire is kept and carried. Odysseus, cast ashore on Scheria, hides himself under the leaves as a man on a lonely farm hides a firebrand in black ashes, σπέρμα πυρὸς σώζων, “saving the seed of fire”, so that he need not kindle it from elsewhere (*Odyssey* 5.488–491). In the Cyclops' cave he heats the olive stake in the embers until it glows, and he and his companions grind it into the Cyclops' eye (*Odyssey* 9.375–390). Proteus, seized on the shore of Egypt, will try to become everything that creeps on the earth, καὶ ὕδωρ καὶ θεσπιδαὲς πῦρ, “and water and wondrous fire” (*Odyssey* 4.417–418).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells how men came to have fire. Angry at Prometheus' deceit in the dividing of the sacrifice, Zeus would not give men πυρὸς μένος ἀκαμάτοιο, “the might of tireless fire”, but Prometheus stole it in a hollow fennel stalk, and in return Zeus made woman, an evil for men (*Theogony* 561–570; *Works and Days* 47–58; see [[hesiod]], [[prometheus]] and [[the-punishment-of-prometheus]]). The phrase joins two of Homer's words for fire, its might and its tirelessness. In the *Homeric Hymn to Hermes* the newborn god makes fire with a stick of laurel, Ἑρμῆς τοι πρώτιστα πυρήια πῦρ τʼ ἀνέδωκε, “it was Hermes who first gave fire-sticks and fire”, and as the fire blazes up the poem calls it “the might of glorious Hephaestus” (βίη κλυτοῦ Ἡφαίστοιο, *Homeric Hymn to Hermes* 108–115; see [[hermes]]).

[[strabo|Strabo]] reports that the Persians, who honor fire among their gods, sacrifice above all to fire and to water. To fire they lay dry wood stripped of its bark with fat on top and kindle it by pouring oil, fanning the flame and never blowing on it; whoever blows on the fire, or puts a corpse or dung on it, they put to death (*Geography* 15.3.14).
