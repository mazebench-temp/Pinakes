## In Homer

Thetis finds Hephaestus “sweating as he turned about among his bellows” (18.372). Before he greets her he rises from the anvil block, sets the bellows away from the fire and gathers his tools into a silver chest (18.410–413); when she has made her request he goes back to them, turns them to the fire and “bade them work” (18.468–469). The twenty bellows blow on the crucibles with every degree of force, at hand when he hurries and quiet when he does not (18.470–473): Walter Leaf calls them, like the tripods, intelligent automata that obey the god's will (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.469; see [[tripod]] and [[the-golden-handmaids]]). He casts bronze, tin, gold and silver into the fire, sets the great anvil on its block and takes the hammer in one hand and the tongs in the other (18.474–477; see [[bronze]], [[tin]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). Leaf notes that χόανοι, “crucibles”, occur again in Hesiod, and that Aristarchus' feminine κρατερήν with ῥαιστήρ, “hammer”, is without parallel (on 18.470, 18.477).

The Odyssey shows the same tools twice more. Laerces, the goldsmith of Pylos, comes with anvil, hammer and well-made tongs to gild the horns of the heifer that Nestor sacrifices to Athena (*Odyssey* 3.432–438; see [[laerces-the-goldsmith]]), and in Demodocus' song Hephaestus sets the great anvil on its block to forge the bonds for Ares and Aphrodite (*Odyssey* 8.273–275). In *Iliad* 15 Zeus reminds Hera of the two anvils he hung from her feet (ἄκμονας, 15.19).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod likens the earth melting under Zeus' thunderbolts to tin melted by the skill of young men “in well-bored crucibles”, or iron softened by fire in the hands of Hephaestus (*Theogony* 862–866). See [[hephaestus]] and [[the-making-of-the-arms]].
