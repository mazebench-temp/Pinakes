μυρίκη is the tamarisk, a shrub or small tree of sandy and wet ground. In the Iliad it grows on the plain of Troy and along its river, and each time it is named it is part of the battlefield.

**On the plain.** The horses of the Trojan Adrastus, bolting in terror across the plain, catch the chariot in a tamarisk branch (ὄζῳ … μυρικίνῳ) and break it at the end of the pole, and Menelaus takes Adrastus alive (6.37–41; see [[adrastus-the-trojan]]). At night Odysseus lifts Dolon's spoils and sets them “on a tamarisk bush”, and marks the place, “gathering together reeds and the luxuriant branches of the tamarisk”, so that he and Diomedes will find it as they come back (10.465–468; see [[dedication-of-spoils]] and [[the-doloneia]]).

**By the river.** When Achilles drives the Trojans into the Xanthus, he leaves his spear on the bank, “leaning against the tamarisks”, and leaps in with his sword (21.17–18; see [[achilles-and-the-river]]). The tamarisks are among the trees of the bank that burn when Hephaestus turns his fire on the river: “The elms and the willows and the tamarisks were burning” (21.350; see [[elm]], [[willow]], [[scamander]] and [[fire]]).

## Outside Homer

The newborn Hermes, driving off Apollo's cattle, weaves himself sandals on the sand of the shore, συμμίσγων μυρίκας καὶ μυρσινοειδέας ὄζους, “mixing tamarisks and twigs like myrtle”, to hide his tracks (*Homeric Hymn to Hermes* 79–83; see [[hermes]]). [[herodotus|Herodotus]] tells that at Callatebus in Lydia craftsmen make honey from tamarisk and wheat (*Histories* 7.31), and that the boats that come down the Nile are towed by the current with a door made of tamarisk wood, stitched over with a mat of reeds and let float ahead of the boat on a rope, while a stone drags behind to steer (*Histories* 2.96). [[strabo|Strabo]] says that the Persian Magi chant their incantations holding a bundle of thin tamarisk rods (*Geography* 15.3.14).
