**The request.** The ghost of Patroclus asks Achilles not to lay their bones apart, but together, as the two were reared together in Peleus' house (23.82–90; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]]). σορός, “urn” (23.91), is found only here in Homer. ἀμφιφορεύς, “two-handled jar”, is elsewhere a jar for stores: the jars of honey and ointment that Achilles leans against the bier of Patroclus (23.170), and in the Odyssey jars of wine (*Odyssey* 2.290, 2.349, 9.204). This one is of gold, and the gift of Thetis (see [[thetis]] and [[gold-and-silver]]).

**A rejected verse.** Walter Leaf reports from the T scholia that the verse naming the jar (23.92) was athetized, as carried over from the Odyssey, where Thetis brings the jar after her son's death: to give it to him from home, before he sailed, would have been an evil omen, and a σορός is not an ἀμφιφορεύς. Leaf thinks the athetesis “unquestionably right”, and reports that the scholia, after Stesichorus, make the jar a gift of Hephaestus to Dionysus, who gave it to Thetis in return for the shelter she gave him (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.92; 6.135–137; see the translation's note on 23.91–92 and [[stesichorus]]).

**The golden bowl.** When the pyre has burned out, Achilles bids the leaders gather the bones of Patroclus “in a golden bowl and a double fold of fat, / until I myself am hidden in Hades” (23.243–244), and they gather them and lay them in the huts under a linen cloth (23.252–254; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]). Leaf takes this φιάλη to be the same vessel as the jar of the rejected verse, something unlike the shallow saucer of classical art, since it holds bones; the fat is a double layer to keep out air and moisture (on 23.243; see [[phiale]]). The bowl stays in the hut, and the mound heaped on the shore is empty: Leaf concludes that the bones were to be taken home with those of Achilles (on 23.254; see [[tomb]]).

**In the Odyssey.** The shade of Agamemnon tells the shade of Achilles how the Achaeans gathered his white bones at dawn in unmixed wine and ointment: his mother gave a golden amphora, which she said was a gift of Dionysus and the work of Hephaestus, and in it his bones lie mingled with those of Patroclus, and apart from them those of Antilochus; over them the army heaped a great tomb on a headland by the Hellespont (*Odyssey* 24.72–84; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]] and [[antilochus]]).

**Hector's chest.** At the end of the Iliad Hector's brothers and companions gather his white bones, wrap them in soft purple robes and lay them in a golden chest, χρυσείην … λάρνακα, which they put in a hollow grave under close-set stones (24.793–798; see [[hector]] and [[cremation]]).
