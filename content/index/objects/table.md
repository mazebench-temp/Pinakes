## Meaning

τράπεζα is usually explained as “four-footed”, from τρα-, a shortened form of τετρα-, “four”, and πέζα, “foot”. The word is already Mycenaean: tables are listed as *to-pe-za* among the furniture on the Linear B tablets from Pylos. πέζα, “foot”, is also the second half of κυανόπεζα, “with feet of dark blue enamel”, the epithet of Nestor's table (11.629; see [[with-feet-of-dark-enamel]] and [[kyanos]]), and of ἀργυρόπεζα, “silver-footed”, the epithet of Thetis (see [[silver-footed]]).

The Homeric table was small and light, one for each diner or for two: it is pushed up before the guest (ἐπιπροΐηλε, 11.628), drawn up beside him (Odyssey 1.138), and carried away when the meal is over (Odyssey 19.61).

## In Homer

In Nestor's hut Hecamede “first pushed up before the two of them a table, a fine one, well polished, with feet of dark blue enamel”, and set on it a bronze basket with an onion as a relish for the drink, pale honey and barley meal, and Nestor's great cup; another man could move the cup from the table only with toil when it was full (11.628–637; see [[hecamede]], [[barley-meal]], [[the-cup-of-nestor]] and [[kykeon]]). In Achilles' hut the bread is set out on the table in fine baskets, by Patroclus for the envoys of Book 9 and by Automedon for Priam (9.216–217, 24.625–626), and when Priam comes in, Achilles has just finished eating and drinking, “and the table still stood beside him” (24.476). The dogs that a lord feeds at his table are τραπεζῆες, “table-dogs” (22.69, 23.173).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the Odyssey the table is part of every reception of a guest. A whole verse brings it: παρὰ δὲ ξεστὴν ἐτάνυσσε τράπεζαν, “and beside them she drew up a polished table”, after the water for washing the hands (Odyssey 1.136–138, 4.52–54, 7.172–174, 10.368–370, 15.135–137, 17.91–93; see [[hand-washing]]). Odysseus in disguise, and the seer Theoclymenus, swear “by Zeus first of the gods, and the table of hospitality, and the hearth of noble Odysseus” (Odyssey 14.158–159, 17.155–156, 20.230–231; see [[xenia]]). Odysseus and Iphitus exchanged a bow for a sword and a spear, but never knew each other at table, for Heracles killed Iphitus first (Odyssey 21.31–38; see [[guest-gifts]]). When Odysseus begins to shoot, the suitors are told to hold up the tables against his arrows (Odyssey 22.74), and after the killing the tables are washed (Odyssey 22.438, 22.452).
