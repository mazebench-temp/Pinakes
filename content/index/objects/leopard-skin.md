παρδαλέη, with δορά (“skin”) understood, is the adjective of πάρδαλις, “leopard”, used as a noun.

When the armies meet, Paris steps out in front of the Trojans with a leopard's skin on his shoulders, a curved bow and a sword, brandishing two spears, and challenges the best of the Argives:

{{quote:553b8de8-3760-5a18-a4d2-79fd88e6b536}}

He has the bow of an archer and the spears of a front-line fighter at once, and a pelt where a corselet would be. When he arms for the duel he borrows his brother's corselet (3.332–333; see [[arming-scene]], [[bow]] and [[single-combat]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In Book 10 the leaders roused in the night throw the skins of beasts over their shoulders: Agamemnon a lion's skin (10.23–24), Menelaus a leopard's skin over his broad back, “dappled” (10.29–30), Diomedes another lion's (10.177–178), and the Trojan spy Dolon a wolf's (10.334).
