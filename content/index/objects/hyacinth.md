ὑάκινθος is a flower; which flower the Greeks meant by the name is not certain, and it need not be the garden hyacinth that bears the name now. In the Iliad it grows once. When Zeus takes Hera in his arms on the peak of Gargarus, “under them the shining earth sent up fresh-sprung grass, and dewy lotus, and crocus, and hyacinth, thick and soft, which held them up off the ground” (14.347–349; see [[crocus]], [[lotus]] and [[earth]]). The lovers lie on the flowers under a golden cloud, and Zeus sleeps there, overcome by sleep and love (14.350–353; see [[the-golden-cloud]] and [[the-deception-of-zeus]]).

In the Odyssey the flower is a likeness. Twice Athena makes Odysseus taller and stronger to look at and sends down from his head thick curls “like the hyacinth flower”, ὑακινθίνῳ ἄνθει ὁμοίας: when he has bathed in the river on Scheria and Nausicaa is about to see him, and when he has bathed in his own house and sits down before Penelope (*Odyssey* 6.229–231, 23.156–158; see [[thick-and-woolly]]). What the hair and the flower share, their color or the curl of the petals, is not said.

## Outside Homer

In the *Hymn to Demeter* the hyacinth is among the flowers that Persephone and the daughters of Oceanus are gathering when the earth opens and Hades carries her off (*Hymn to Demeter* 6–7, 425–426; see [[crocus]]). In later myth the flower has a story of its own. Hyacinthus of Amyclae, whom Apollo loved, was killed by the god's discus; from the boy's blood sprang a flower like a lily but purple, on whose petals Apollo wrote his own lament, AI AI, and Sparta kept the Hyacinthia every year in his honor (Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 10.162–219; see [[amyclae]], [[discus]] and [[ovid]]).
