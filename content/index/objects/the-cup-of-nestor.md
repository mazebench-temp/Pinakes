## Description

δέπας is Homer's commonest word for a cup (see [[cup]]), and this one is περικαλλές, “beautiful” (11.632). Hecamede sets it on the table in Nestor's hut with the onion, the honey and the barley, and mixes the drink for Nestor and Machaon in it (11.628–641; see [[hecamede]] and [[kykeon]]). It is big enough to serve as a mixing bowl for two.

- **Golden nails.** It is χρυσείοις ἥλοισι πεπαρμένον, “studded with golden nails” (11.633), the words used of the scepter that Achilles throws to the ground (1.246); golden studs also gleam on Agamemnon's sword (11.29–30).
- **Four handles and the doves.** Its οὔατα, “ears”, are handles, as on Hephaestus' tripods (18.378). There are four, and “around each two doves / of gold were feeding” (11.634–635; see [[doves]]). The verb νεμέθοντο, “were feeding”, is found only here.
- **Two bases.** “And below were two bases” (δύω δʼ ὑπὸ πυθμένες ἦσαν, 11.635). πυθμήν is the base or bottom of a vessel; Hephaestus sets wheels “beneath each base” of his tripods (18.375). Whether the two are two feet, or two supports beneath the bowl, and how they were joined to the handles, has been much debated (see the translation's note on 11.624–641).

**The weight.** “Another man could move it from the table only with toil / when it was full, but Nestor, the old man, lifted it without toil” (11.636–637). μογέων, “with toil”, is set against ἀμογητί, “without toil”, a word found nowhere else in Homer. A few verses later Nestor tells Patroclus that his strength “is not such as it was before in my supple limbs” (11.668–669; see [[nestor]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]).

**From home.** It is the cup “which the old man had brought from home” (ὃ οἴκοθεν ἦγʼ ὁ γεραιός, 11.632). Achilles too keeps a cup of his own in the chest his mother put on his ship, from which no other man drank, and from which he poured to no god but Zeus (16.221–227). No other cup in Homer is described so closely, and Nestor's is not heard of again.

## Outside Homer

**Ancient readers.** The longest ancient discussion of the cup, its nails, handles, doves and bases, and of how the old man lifted it, is preserved by Athenaeus (see [[athenaeus-nestors-cup]]).

**The gold cup from Mycenae.** In 1876 Heinrich Schliemann found in Shaft Grave IV at Mycenae a gold cup with two handles, each with a bird perched on it and a strip running down to the base, and it has often been called “Nestor's cup” since. The birds and the supports beneath them recalled 11.633–635. The cup has two handles, not four, and is centuries older than the poems, from the sixteenth century BC; it shows at most that cups with birds on their handles had once been made.

**The clay cup from Pithekoussai.** A clay drinking cup of the late eighth century BC, found in a grave on Pithekoussai (Ischia), carries a short verse inscription that is usually read as a drinker's joke: it claims to be “Nestor's cup”, good to drink from, and promises that whoever drinks from it will be seized by desire for Aphrodite. It is among the earliest Greek alphabetic inscriptions, and two of its lines are hexameters. Whether it alludes to the Iliad, or to a story of Nestor's cup that was older than the poem, is debated, and the readings of its first line differ.
