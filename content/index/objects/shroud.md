## The words

ταφήϊος, from τάφος, “burial”, is “for burial”; Penelope's ταφήϊον is the φᾶρος, the great mantle she is weaving, to be the old man's shroud (*Odyssey* 2.97–99; see [[cloak]]). σπεῖρον is any piece of cloth, a sail, the clothes to be washed, the rags of a beggar, and in Penelope's words the sheet in which a dead man is wound (*Odyssey* 2.102, 4.245, 5.318, 6.179). Elsewhere in Homer the dead are covered with the cloths of the living: linen, mantles, robes.

## In the Iliad

When the body of Patroclus is washed and anointed, “laying him on a bier they covered him with a soft linen cloth / from head to foot, and over it with a white cloak” (18.352–353; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]] and [[cloak]]). When Achilles gives back Hector's body, his men unload the ransom but leave two mantles and a tunic, “so that he could wrap the corpse and give it to be carried home” (24.578–581), and the slave women who have washed and anointed it “put a fine mantle and a tunic around him” before Achilles lays him on the bier (24.582–589; see [[the-ransom-of-hector]]). Walter Leaf supposes that one of the two mantles was put as a cover on the bier, and that the body, clothed in the tunic, was wrapped in the other (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.587). The bones are wrapped in their turn: those of Patroclus are covered with a soft linen cloth in their golden bowl (23.254; see [[the-golden-urn]]), and those of Hector with soft purple robes in a golden chest (24.795–796; see [[the-funeral-of-hector]]).

## In the Odyssey

Penelope tells the suitors that she must finish a mantle as a shroud for Laertes, “against the time when / the deadly fate of death that lays men low takes him down”, so that none of the Achaean women may blame her “if he were to lie without a winding-sheet, who won great possessions” (*Odyssey* 2.97–102; see [[the-web-of-penelope]], [[laertes]] and [[achaean-women]]). The seven verses are said again by Penelope herself and by the shade of Amphimedon (*Odyssey* 19.141–147, 24.131–137). In the last book the shade of Agamemnon tells how the daughters of the old man of the sea stood around the dead Achilles and clothed him in immortal garments (*Odyssey* 24.58–59; see [[nereids]]); the rendering is a plain one.
