Homer has four words for the helmet and uses them without clear distinction. κόρυς gives the verb κορύσσω, “to helmet, to arm”, and the standing epithet of Hector, κορυθαίολος, “of the gleaming helmet” ([[of-the-gleaming-helmet]]). Even spears can be κεκορυθμένα χαλκῷ, “helmeted with bronze”, that is, tipped with it (3.18). κυνέη is formed from κύων, “dog”, as if the helmet had first been a cap of dogskin, but a Homeric κυνέη can be “bronze-fitted” (χαλκήρης, 3.316). τρυφάλεια is of uncertain origin; it has been explained as a helmet with a φάλος. πήληξ is the fourth.

The parts are the φάλος, a ridge or boss on the crown whose exact form is disputed; the crest (λόφος) of horsehair, from which the helmet is ἵππουρις, “with a horsehair crest”, and ἱπποδάσεια, “thick with horsehair” (see [[horsehair-crested]]); and the strap (ἱμάς) that fastened it under the chin. The nodding crest was meant to frighten. When Hector reaches for his baby son, the child shrinks back from the bronze and the horsehair crest nodding terribly on the top of the helmet, and Hector takes it off and lays it on the ground (6.466–473).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The only helmet whose history the Iliad tells is the one covered with boar's tusks that Meriones lends Odysseus for the night raid (see [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]]). Autolycus stole it from Amyntor of Eleon, and it passed from hand to hand as a gift before it came to Meriones (10.261–271). When Apollo strikes Patroclus, the helmet he wears, Achilles' own, rolls under the horses' hooves and its crest is fouled with blood and dust, as it had never been before (16.793–800). Achilles' new helmet, made by Hephaestus, shines like a star, with golden plumes (19.380–383). Lots are shaken in a helmet (3.316, 7.175–182).

## Outside Homer

Helmets covered with rows of plates cut from boar's tusks, like the one Meriones lends Odysseus, are known from Mycenaean graves and pictures of the Late Bronze Age. They had long gone out of use when the poems took their final form, and the helmet of Book 10 is often counted among the memories of the Bronze Age that Homer preserves.
