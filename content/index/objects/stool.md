δίφρος is first the car of the chariot, the platform on which the driver and the fighter stand, and the word is usually explained as “carrying two” (δι-, “two”, with the root of φέρω, “carry”). It is also a light seat without a back, humbler than the chair of honor ([[throne]]).

Book 3 uses the word both ways. Priam and Antenor step into the δίφρος of the chariot to drive to the oath and back (3.262, 3.310, 3.312; see [[chariot]]). And when Helen comes into Paris' high-roofed chamber, the goddess herself fetches her a seat:

{{quote:23b23e3f-dc2a-58df-b128-e067ee9d4b04}}

Laughter-loving Aphrodite does a servant's work: “the goddess carried it and set it down facing Alexander”. Helen sits where the goddess puts her, but turns her eyes away as she speaks (see [[eyes]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the same house in Book 6, Helen asks Hector to come in and sit on a δίφρος (6.354).
