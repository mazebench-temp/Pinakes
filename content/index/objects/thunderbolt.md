## Meaning

κεραυνός is the bolt that strikes. Homer keeps it apart from the flash, ἀστεροπή or στεροπή, and from the thunder, βροντή, though the three come together when Zeus stops Diomedes: “Thundering terribly, he let fly a gleaming thunderbolt” (8.133). The etymology of the word is uncertain. The weapon belongs to Zeus alone, and his epithets come from it: he is τερπικέραυνος, rendered “who delights in thunder” and formed from κεραυνός itself (see [[delighting-in-thunder]]), and ἀστεροπητής, “lord of the lightning” (see [[lord-of-the-lightning]]).

The bolt is ἀργής, “gleaming” (8.133; Od. 5.128, 5.131, 7.249, 12.387), or ψολόεις, “smoky” (Od. 23.330, 24.539). Where it falls it leaves the smell of sulfur (θέειον). A flame of burning sulfur rises where it strikes the ground before Diomedes' horses (8.135); an oak felled by Zeus gives off “a dread smell of sulfur”, and no one who sees it close at hand keeps his courage, “for the thunderbolt of great Zeus is hard” (14.414–417); Odysseus' ship, struck at sea, is filled with sulfur (Od. 12.415–417, and in his Cretan tale, Od. 14.305–307).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The gods fear it. Ares will avenge his son “even if it is my fate to be struck by the thunderbolt of Zeus and lie among the dead in blood and dust” (15.117–118). Achilles, boasting over the grandson of a river, says that not even Oceanus can fight against Zeus, but “he too fears the thunderbolt of great Zeus and his dread thunder” (21.193–199). Athena's aegis is a thing “which not even the thunderbolt of Zeus overcomes” (21.400–401; see [[aegis]]).

In the Odyssey it strikes men. Zeus killed Iasion with it when Demeter lay with him in a thrice-plowed field (Od. 5.125–128), and he shattered Odysseus' ship after the companions ate the cattle of the Sun (Od. 5.131–132, 7.249–250, 12.415–419, 23.330). At the end of the poem he throws a smoky thunderbolt that falls before Athena, and she stops the fighting on Ithaca (Od. 24.539–544). Like the bolt before Diomedes' horses (8.133–136), it is a warning that ends a fight.

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod's *Theogony* the Cyclopes, Brontes, Steropes and Arges, “Thunder”, “Lightning” and “Bright”, gave Zeus thunder and made him the thunderbolt (139–141); the third name answers to ἀργής, the bolt's Homeric epithet. Freed by Zeus from their father's bonds, they gave him thunder, the smoking thunderbolt and lightning, and “relying on these he rules over mortals and immortals” (501–506). With them he fights the Titans (687–712) and burns the heads of Typhoeus (853–856).
