λέβης is a metal vessel for water, a cauldron to set over the fire or a basin to wash in. It stands beside the tripod, the cauldron on three legs (see [[tripod]]), and the two are named together as treasure: “seven tripods untouched by fire, and ten talents of gold, / and twenty gleaming cauldrons, and twelve horses” head the list of Agamemnon's gifts to Achilles (9.122–123 = 9.264–265; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]] and [[tawny]]). In Book 19 the twenty cauldrons are carried out of Agamemnon's hut with the seven tripods (19.243–244).

In the later books the cauldron is a prize and a gift. Achilles brings cauldrons and tripods out of the ships as prizes for the funeral games of Patroclus (23.259); the third prize of the chariot race is a cauldron untouched by fire, holding four measures and still bright (23.267–268), and Idomeneus offers to wager a tripod or a cauldron on which team is leading (23.485). Priam takes two gleaming tripods and four cauldrons out of his treasure for Hector's ransom (24.233; see [[possessions]]). In a simile of the river Xanthus, set on fire by Hephaestus, the water boils like a cauldron on a great fire, melting the fat of a fattened hog (21.361–365; see [[the-boiling-cauldron]]).

In the Odyssey the λέβης is above all the basin of the guest's washing. A maid pours water from a golden jug over a silver basin for the guest to wash his hands (*Odyssey* 1.136–138, and often), at Nestor's sacrifice his son Aretus brings the water for washing in a flowered basin (*Odyssey* 3.440), and the old nurse Eurycleia takes the gleaming basin to wash the stranger's feet; when she knows the scar, the leg drops into the basin and the bronze rings out (*Odyssey* 19.386–388, 19.468–470). Cauldrons are also gifts there: Alcinous bids each of the Phaeacian lords give Odysseus a great tripod and a cauldron (*Odyssey* 13.13–14), and Odysseus counts them on the shore of Ithaca (*Odyssey* 13.217–218).
