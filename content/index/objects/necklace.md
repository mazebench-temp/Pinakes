summary: Necklaces, ὅρμοι in Homer, among the jewelry Hephaestus forged in Thetis' cave and the gifts of Penelope's suitors. Aristotle lists the necklace among the tokens of recognition, with the little boat of Tyro; the most famous necklace of legend is Harmonia's, with which Polynices bribed Eriphyle.
aliases: [necklaces, the necklace of Harmonia]

Tokens left with an exposed child, necklaces, rings and the like, are the stock means by which the child is later recognized, in tragedy and above all in New Comedy, whose plots often turn on them (Menander's *Arbitration*, Plautus' *Rope*). The necklace of legend was the one that Hephaestus made for Harmonia when she married Cadmus; Polynices bribed Eriphyle with it to send her husband to Thebes, and it brought ruin on all who owned it (Apollodorus 3.4.2, 3.6.2; see [[alcmaeon-kills-eriphyle]] and [[cadmus]]). In the Odyssey the suitor Eurymachus gives Penelope a golden necklace strung with amber, “like the sun” (*Odyssey* 18.295–296).
