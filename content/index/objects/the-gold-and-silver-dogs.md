## In Homer

{{quote:5ab6d01f-07b8-5513-99ea-7c351d201fc8}}

The dogs stand ἑκάτερθε, “on either side”, at the house of Alcinous; the poem names them after the golden doors, the silver doorposts and lintel and the golden handle, and before the chairs of the hall within (*Odyssey* 7.88–95; see [[the-house-of-alcinous]] and [[door]]). Hephaestus made them “with his cunning skill”, ἰδυίῃσι πραπίδεσσι, the phrase that Homer uses only of his work, as of the houses of the gods on Olympus and the shield of Achilles (*Iliad* 1.608, 18.482; see [[with-cunning-skill]] and [[hephaestus]]). They have a dog's task, δῶμα φυλασσέμεναι, “to guard the house” (see [[dogs]]), and a god's nature: ἀθανάτους … καὶ ἀγήρως ἤματα πάντα, “immortal and ageless all their days”, is the state of the gods, which Calypso offered Odysseus (*Odyssey* 5.136; see [[immortal-and-ageless]] and [[ageless]]).

**Images or living things.** W. W. Merry thinks it improbable that the poet means anything more than images, of dogs here and of boys in the golden youths who hold the torches in the same hall (*Odyssey* 7.100–102). Homer, he argues, tends to speak of works of imitative or mechanical art in hyperbole, as a tribute to the artist's skill: the tripods of Hephaestus move “of themselves”, the golden handmaids are, after all, only “like living young women”, and Odysseus, telling of the golden baldric worn by the shade of Heracles, with its bears, boars and lions, its battles and slaughters, wishes that the man who made it may never make another (*Iliad* 18.376, 18.418; *Odyssey* 11.609–614, a plain rendering). And works of imitative art had not yet received names of their own, so that they borrowed the names of what they imitated, as the golden herdsmen walk with the cattle on the shield of Achilles (*Iliad* 18.577; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.91; see [[tripod]], [[the-golden-handmaids]] and [[the-shield-of-achilles]]). The Odyssey has one more golden dog that seems to live, the hound on the golden brooch of the cloak that Odysseus wore on his way to Troy, as he describes it to Penelope in disguise: all who saw it wondered how, though made of gold, it gazed at a dappled fawn as it throttled it, while the fawn struggled with its feet to get away (*Odyssey* 19.225–231, a plain rendering; see [[brooch]]).
