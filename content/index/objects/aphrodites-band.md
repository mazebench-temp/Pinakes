## The word

ἱμάς is a strap or thong of leather: the reins of a chariot (23.324, 23.363), the boxer's thongs (23.684), the strap of a helmet (3.371, 3.375), and in the Odyssey the thong that draws the bolt of a door (*Odyssey* 1.442, 21.46; see [[chariot]] and [[helmet]]). κεστός, found in Homer only here, is a verbal adjective of κεντέω, “prick, stitch”: “stitched, embroidered”. The helmet strap that chokes Paris under his throat is πολύκεστος, “richly worked” (3.371), and the Suda explains the κεστός as “the strap pricked through and embroidered all over” (κ 1428). The strap is also ποικίλος, “richly worked” (14.215, 14.220), the word for Athena's robe (5.735; see [[robe]]). What kind of strap it was the poem does not say, a band worn across the breast or a girdle (see the translation's note on 14.214–223). Aphrodite looses it “from her breast” (ἀπὸ στήθεσφιν), and Hera puts it “in her bosom” (κόλπῳ), the fold of the robe at the breast (14.214, 14.219, 14.223). The English keeps “strap” for ἱμάς, “girdle” for ζώνη, which Hera puts on separately (14.181; see [[girdle]]), and “band” for μίτρη (see [[band]]).

## In Homer

Hera, adorned in her chamber (see [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]]), calls Aphrodite apart from the other gods and asks her for “love and longing, with which you / overcome all the immortals and mortal men”, inventing a reason: she is going to reconcile Oceanus and Tethys (14.187–210; see [[hera]], [[aphrodite]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). θελκτήρια are “enchantments”, things that charm and bewitch (θέλγω, “charm, beguile”). The word is found elsewhere in Homer only in the Odyssey, of the songs that Phemius knows, “enchantments of mortals” (*Odyssey* 1.337), and of the wooden horse, which some Trojans wanted to keep as an offering to charm the gods (*Odyssey* 8.509). Three powers are named, φιλότης, “love” (see [[philotes]]), ἵμερος, “longing” (see [[himeros]]), and ὀαριστύς, “intimate talk” (see [[oaristys]]), and the next verse calls the third πάρφασις, a persuasion of words (see [[peitho]] and [[the-persuasion-that-steals-the-mind]]). Aphrodite puts it into Hera's hands: “Here, now, put this strap in your bosom, / richly worked, in which all things are wrought; and I do not think / you will come back with nothing done” (14.219–221), and Hera smiles and puts it in her bosom (14.222–223; see [[smiling-at-him]]).

The strap is not mentioned again. When Zeus sees Hera on Gargarus, “desire wrapped his shrewd mind” (14.294; see [[eros]]), and he swears that he has never desired any goddess or woman as he desires her now (14.315–328). The poem leaves the strap's working to be inferred from its effect (see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle quotes the strap's last verse, with the participle in the singular, “persuasion, which steals the mind even of one who thinks shrewdly” (φρονέοντος). Desire, he argues, is more unjust than anger because it schemes: anger is open, but desire is crafty, “as they call Aphrodite guile-weaving”, and as Homer says of the embroidered strap (*Nicomachean Ethics* 7.6, 1149b13–18; see [[aristotle]]).

The Suda glosses the phrase as “an embroidered strap, in which, the story went, were all the enchantments for deceit” (κ 1427), and the word κεστός as the embroidered strap, “or the garment of Aphrodite”, and by extension any showy garment of women (κ 1428). Proclus, explaining the story allegorically, says that Aphrodite is said to carry the κεστός at her breast, with its powers displayed, while Hera hides it under her bosom, since she has it in herself, and by it joins herself to the creator of the world (*On the Republic* I 139 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).

In Latin the adjective became a noun, *cestos*, Venus' girdle. Martial gives two couplets to it among his gifts, the second ending “ussit amatorem balteus iste Iovem”, “that belt set Jove on fire with love”, the story of Book 14 in one verse (*Epigrams* 14.206–207).
