## In Homer

The word is used three times in the Odyssey and never in the Iliad. Telemachus, gazing at Menelaus' halls, names it between gold and silver, “of gold and amber and silver and ivory” (*Odyssey* 4.73; see [[gold-and-silver]] and [[house]]). In the later books it is an ornament of necklaces: in the story of Eumaeus' childhood, the messenger of the Phoenician traders comes to his father's house with a golden necklace strung with amber (*Odyssey* 15.458–460), and Eurymachus gives Penelope a richly worked golden necklace strung with amber, like the sun (*Odyssey* 18.295–296; both plain renderings).

## Amber or electrum

ἤλεκτρον later named both amber and electrum, the pale alloy of gold and silver. W. W. Merry, following Buttmann, reports that Pliny took Homer's word for the metal, a natural compound in which silver is a fifth part, and cited this verse: Homer bears witness that Menelaus' palace shone with gold, electrum, silver and ivory (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.73, quoting Pliny, *Natural History* 33.4.23). Merry finds the view tempting where the word stands between the two metals that make the alloy, but the two necklaces require amber, since a golden necklace would not be strung with pieces of a paler gold; and he cites a scholium on Aristophanes' *Clouds*, that Homer does not know the word ὕαλος, “glass”, but does know ἤλεκτρος. He decides for amber in Homer, and takes the metal to be meant in Sophocles (*Antigone* 1037) and Virgil (*Aeneid* 8.402), where the name of amber was borrowed for a metal that resembled it in its pale brightness (Merry on 4.73; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 4.73). Buttmann derived the word, like ἠλακάτη, “distaff”, from ἕλκω, “draw”, for the power of rubbed amber to attract light things; Merry thinks this too fanciful, and reports Curtius' derivation of ἤλεκτρον, ἠλέκτωρ, “shining” (of the sun), and Ἠλέκτρα from a root meaning “flash” (see [[elektor]] and [[distaff]]).

## Outside Homer

The *Shield* ascribed to Hesiod describes Heracles' shield as gleaming all round with gypsum, white ivory and ἤλεκτρον, and shining with bright gold (*Shield* 141–143; see [[shield-of-heracles]]), which Merry reads as amber. Herodotus, writing of the western ends of Europe, doubts that there is a river called Eridanus flowing into the northern sea, from which amber is said to come, and concludes only that tin and amber come to the Greeks from the ends of the earth (Herodotus 3.115; see [[herodotus]]).
