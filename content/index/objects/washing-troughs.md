## The word

πλυνοί, from πλύνω, “wash”, the verb for washing clothes, as λούω is the verb for washing the body (see [[bathing]]), are the places where the washing was done. The Suda glosses them as tubs, πύελοι, “in which they wash” (π 1819), and distinguishes πλυνός accented on the last syllable, the vessel itself, from πλύνος, the thing washed (π 1817). Homer's πλυνοί are not vessels to carry but fixed places at running water. Those of Troy are “wide, / fair, and of stone” (*Iliad* 22.153–154), and those of Scheria are “unfailing” (ἐπηετανοί), with “much fair water” welling up through them “to clean even what is very dirty” (*Odyssey* 6.86–87). W. W. Merry takes them for tanks dug at the side of the river, open to it above and below, so that the water was always passing in and out (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.86; see the translation's row πλυνοί). The clothes were trodden in them, in the “pits” (βόθροι, *Odyssey* 6.92), and spread to dry in the sun.

## In Homer

**At Troy.** Achilles chases Hector past the springs of the Scamander, “And there beside them are the washing troughs, wide, / fair, and of stone, where the glossy clothes / the wives of the Trojans and their fair daughters used to wash / in former days, in time of peace, before the sons of the Achaeans came” (*Iliad* 22.153–156; see [[the-springs-of-the-scamander]] and [[trojan-women]]). The troughs lie near the city, and the poem names them as a thing of the peace that the war has ended.

**On Scheria.** The troughs of the Phaeacians lie by the river, “far from the city” (*Odyssey* 6.40), so that Nausicaa must ask her father for the mules and the wagon. The washing is the errand on which Athena sends her, and the book's meeting happens because of it: the clothes are trodden and spread on the shore, the girls bathe, eat and play at ball while they dry, and their shout wakes Odysseus (*Odyssey* 6.25–117; see [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]], [[the-river-of-scheria]], [[wagon]] and [[ball]]). The clean clothes become the stranger's: he begs for “a rag to throw around me, / if you had perhaps some wrapping for the clothes”, and the handmaids lay beside him a mantle and a tunic (*Odyssey* 6.178–179, 6.214; see [[cloak]] and [[tunic]]).

**Later in the Odyssey.** The verb returns twice. The Phoenician woman in the house of Eumaeus' father was seduced by one of the Phoenician traders as she was washing clothes beside their ship (*Odyssey* 15.417–420), and in Amphimedon's tale among the dead Penelope showed the great web of the shroud when she had woven it and washed it, shining like the sun or the moon (*Odyssey* 24.147–148; see [[the-web-of-penelope]]). These renderings are plain renderings, not the translation. A mantle given as a gift is ἐυπλυνές, “freshly washed”: so are the mantles of the Phaeacians' gifts (*Odyssey* 8.392, 8.425; again 13.67, 16.173).

## Outside Homer

The Suda knows the Πλυντήρια, which it calls a festival of the Athenians (π 1818); the name is formed from the same verb. Eustathius, as Merry reports him, said that Sophocles wrote a satyr play, the *Washerwomen* (Πλύντριαι), in which he himself played Nausicaa (Merry on 6.115; see the translation's note on 6.115).
