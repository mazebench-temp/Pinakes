## The bonds

δεσμός is anything that binds: a halter, a rope, a fetter, a knot (see [[chest]]). Hephaestus' δεσμοί, also δέσματα (*Odyssey* 8.278), are forged like metalwork, “and set the great anvil on the anvil block, and forged bonds / unbreakable, not to be loosed, so that they might stay there steadfast” (*Odyssey* 8.274–275; see [[forge]]). ἀρρήκτους ἀλύτους, “unbreakable, not to be loosed”, are the words of the rope of war that Zeus and Poseidon stretch over both armies, “unbreakable and not to be loosed” (*Iliad* 13.360; see [[the-rope-of-war]]), and Zeus' golden bond on Hera's hands was “unbreakable” too (*Iliad* 15.19–20; see [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]]). κόπτε δὲ δεσμούς ends a verse of the Iliad, where Hephaestus is “forging the rivets” of his tripods (*Iliad* 18.379): the same half verse, of the god's ordinary craft.

They are fine and pliant, poured rather than fastened: Hephaestus “poured the bonds in a circle on every side” around the bedposts, “and many too were poured down from above, from the roof beam, / fine as spider's webs, which no one could even see, / not even one of the blessed gods; for they were made exceedingly cunning” (*Odyssey* 8.278–281; see [[bed]] and [[fine-as-spiders-webs]]). W. W. Merry notes that the verbs χέε and ἐξεκέχυντο, “poured”, express the subtle and pliable nature of the net, and that οὐδέ is “not even”: the chains could not so much as be seen (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.278, 8.280). They are δολόεντα, “cunning” (see the translation's row δολόεις, and [[wily]]), τεχνήεντες, “skillfully made” (*Odyssey* 8.297), and as a whole they are δόλος, “snare” (*Odyssey* 8.276, 8.282, 8.317), the word that the poem also uses of the wooden horse (*Odyssey* 8.494; Merry on 8.276; see [[dolos]] and [[the-wooden-horse]]). The gods who see them laugh at the τέχναι, “the craft”, of Hephaestus (*Odyssey* 8.327; see [[hephaestus]]).

## Bonds and the gods

The gods know bonds. Ares had been bound before: the sons of Aloeus, Otus and Ephialtes, bound him “in strong bonds” and kept him in a bronze jar for thirteen months, until Hermes stole him away (*Iliad* 5.385–391; see [[ares]]). Hera, Poseidon and Athena once wanted to bind Zeus (*Iliad* 1.399–406; see [[binding-of-zeus]]), and Zeus hung Hera from the sky with a golden bond on her hands (*Iliad* 15.18–21). In the song it is Hermes who says he would gladly lie in three times as many bonds (*Odyssey* 8.339–342; see [[hermes]]).

## Outside Homer

Merry calls Demodocus' song “this Lay of the Net of Hephaestus” (on 8.267; see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]]). Plato's Socrates, listing the stories of the gods that the young should not hear, names the deception of Zeus by Hera and, beside it, “the binding of Ares and Aphrodite by Hephaestus”, Ἄρεώς τε καὶ Ἀφροδίτης ὑπὸ Ἡφαίστου δεσμόν (*Republic* 390b–c; see [[plato-zeus-deceived]] and [[plato]]).
