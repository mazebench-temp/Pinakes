Ἄϊδος κυνέη, “the helmet of Hades”, is named only here in Homer. κυνέη is one of Homer's words for a helmet (see [[helmet]]). The line seems to play on the god's name, which the Greeks explained as “the unseen” (see [[hades]]): Ἄϊδος … μή μιν ἴδοι, “of Hades … so that he should not see her”.

It does what the gods' mist and cloud usually do. In this book Hephaestus hides Idaeus in night (5.23) and Apollo hides Aeneas in a dark cloud (5.344–345); in Book 3 Aphrodite snatches Paris away in a thick mist (3.380–381). Here it is Athena who must be hidden, from a god. Ares sees Diomedes and lunges at him over the horses' yoke, and Athena, unseen, catches the spear and pushes it aside (5.846–854). Diomedes can see her, because she took the mist from his eyes (5.127–128; see [[achlys]]). She has already put on her own golden helmet (5.743–744), and the poem does not say how the two go together. When Ares remembers the wound in the battle of the gods, he accuses her of thrusting at him herself with a spear “seen by all” (πανόψιον ἔγχος, 21.397). See [[diomedes-wounds-ares]].

## Outside Homer

In the *Shield of Heracles*, Perseus flies from the Gorgons with the helmet of Hades on his head, “holding the dread darkness of night” ([Hesiod], *Shield* 226–227). [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] says that the Cyclopes gave Zeus thunder and lightning, Pluto the helmet and Poseidon the trident (*Library* 1.2.1), and that the nymphs gave Perseus the helmet with the winged sandals and the bag for the Gorgon's head (2.4.2). Plato sets it beside the ring of Gyges as the means of doing wrong unseen (*Republic* 10.612b).
