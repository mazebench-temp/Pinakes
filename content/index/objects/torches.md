## The words

δαΐς is a torch, from δαίω, “kindle”. δάος, in the same sense, appears in Homer only with μετὰ χερσὶν ἔχουσαι, “with torches in their hands” (*Iliad* 24.647), or ἔχουσα, of a single woman (*Odyssey* 23.294). Torches are the light of a house after dark, carried by its servants, and αἰθομένας δαΐδας, “blazing torches”, is their phrase (*Odyssey* 1.428, 1.434). The Odyssey has other lights as well: the braziers (λαμπτῆρες) set up in the hall and fed with dry wood and torches (*Odyssey* 18.307–311), and a golden lamp (λύχνος) carried once, by Athena (*Odyssey* 19.34).

## In the Iliad

On the Shield of Achilles, in the city at peace, “they were leading the brides from their chambers under blazing torches / through the city, and the bridal song rose loud” (18.492–493), and the women watched from their doorways (18.491–496; see [[marriage]] and [[the-two-cities]]). In Achilles' hut, when the beds are to be spread for Priam and his herald, “the women went out from the hall with torches in their hands” (24.647; see [[handmaids]] and [[bed]]).

## In the Odyssey

Torches belong to the night of the house. The same verse brings the women out with torches to make the beds for guests at Sparta and in the palace of Alcinous (*Odyssey* 4.300, 7.339), and to come to Odysseus when the suitors are dead (*Odyssey* 22.497). The bed of Odysseus and Penelope is made under blazing torches, and Eurynome leads them to it with a torch in her hands (*Odyssey* 23.289–294). In Alcinous' hall golden statues of youths hold blazing torches on their pedestals to light the feasters by night (*Odyssey* 7.100–102). Penelope unwove her web by torchlight (*Odyssey* 2.104–105), and Telemachus goes to his chamber “under blazing torches” on the night before the killing of the suitors, as in the first book (*Odyssey* 19.47–50). Eurymachus mocks the beggar that the gleam of the torches seems to come from his bald head (*Odyssey* 18.353–355).
