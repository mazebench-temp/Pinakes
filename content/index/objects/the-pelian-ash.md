## Description

μελίη is the ash tree and the spear made of its wood, as the shaft of every spear was (see [[ash-spear]] and [[spear]]); Πηλιάς is “of Pelion”, the mountain of Magnesia where the Centaur Chiron lived (see [[pelion]] and [[chiron]]). The spear is described in the verses with which Patroclus arms. He takes the rest of Achilles' armor and two spears of his own, “Only the spear of the noble grandson of Aeacus he did not take, / heavy, huge, and massive”: no other Achaean could wield it, and Achilles alone knew how (16.139–142; see [[arming-scene]]). βριθὺ μέγα στιβαρόν, “heavy, huge, and massive”, are the words of Athena's spear, with which she overpowers the ranks of men (5.746 = 8.390).

Its history is a gift. Chiron gave it to Achilles' “dear father / from the peak of Pelion, to be death for heroes” (16.143–144; see [[peleus]]). The other gifts to Peleus came from the gods: the immortal horses, “the splendid gifts that the gods gave to Peleus” (16.381, 16.867; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]), and the armor, which the gods gave him on the day they married him to Thetis (18.84–85; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]]). Peleus gave the armor to his son (17.195–197), and Hector strips it from Patroclus' body; the spear stays behind with Achilles.

## In the later books

When Achilles arms in the armor Hephaestus has made, he takes up the spear last, drawing “his father's spear” from its case (πατρώϊον ἔγχος, 19.387), and the verses of Book 16 describe it again (19.388–391). With it he strikes Aeneas' shield (Πηλιὰς ἤϊξεν μελίη, 20.277) and fights Asteropaeus, who tries three times to pull it from the river bank where it has stuck and cannot (21.161–179; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]). He comes at Hector “brandishing the Pelian ash over his right shoulder” (22.133), and after his first cast Athena gives the spear back to him unseen (22.273–277); he drives it through Hector's neck where the armor of Patroclus leaves the throat bare (22.317–327). The spear Patroclus could not take kills the man who killed him.

## Outside Homer

Strabo sets the Pelian ash among the throwing spears. The Euboeans, he says, fought hand to hand with thrusting spears, “with outstretched ash spears” (2.543), but there were other spears for throwing, “such as the Pelian ash is likely to have been”, which “Achilles alone knew how to wield” (πῆλαι, from πάλλω, “brandish, hurl”); and Odysseus meant the throwing spear when he boasted that he could throw a spear farther than another man could shoot an arrow (*Odyssey* 8.229; Strabo, *Geography* 10.1.13; see [[strabo]]).
