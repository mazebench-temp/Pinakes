Homer names it in two ways. When Dolon arms, he “put on over it the skin of a gray wolf” (ἕσσατο δʼ ἔκτοσθεν ῥινὸν πολιοῖο λύκοιο, 10.334). ῥινός is a hide or skin, the word for the ox hide Diomedes sleeps on and for the leather of the helmet Meriones gives Odysseus (10.155, 10.262); πολιός, “gray”, is the color of the sea and of old men's hair, and of Homer's wolves (see [[wolves]]). When Dolon is stripped, it is λυκέη, “the wolfskin” (10.459), the adjective of λύκος, “wolf”, used as a noun with “skin” understood, as παρδαλέη is the leopard's skin (see [[leopard-skin]]).

He wears it as the Achaean chiefs of the same night wear their lion skins and Menelaus his leopard's skin, over the shoulders in place of a cloak (10.23, 10.29, 10.177; see [[lion-skin]]). It goes on “over” (ἔκτοσθεν) the curved bow he has slung about his shoulders, and with it he takes a cap of ferret skin and a sharp javelin (10.333–335; see [[helmet]], [[bow]] and [[arming-scene]]). Homer does not say that it is a disguise. Dolon goes eagerly along the road and runs past the two men lying among the dead (10.339, 10.350).

When Odysseus and Diomedes have killed him, they take from him “the cap of ferret skin, and the wolfskin, and the back-bent bow, and the long spear” (10.458–459): the first three pieces of his arming in reverse order, and the spear last, as it was when he armed. Odysseus holds them up to Athena (10.460–464; see [[dedication-of-spoils]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]).

## Outside Homer

In the *Rhesus*, the tragedy transmitted among the plays of Euripides (see [[euripides]]), Dolon makes the skin a disguise. He will fasten a wolf's hide about his back and set the beast's gaping jaws over his head, fit its forefeet to his hands and its legs to his legs, and imitate the four-footed path of a wolf, hard for the enemy to track, rising onto two feet where the ground is empty: “this is the trick (δόλος) I have arranged” (208–215). The chorus prays that Hermes, “lord of thieves”, will bring him there and back (216–217). Later in the play Rhesus' charioteer tells of a dream in which wolves mounted the backs of the mares he drove and lashed them on, and the mares snorted and reared in fear (780–788).
