## The word

ζώνη is formed from ζώννυμι, “gird”, like the warrior's belt, ζωστήρ, and the kilt beneath it, ζῶμα (see [[belt]] and [[kilt]]). It is the girdle that gathers a woman's robe at the waist, and three epithets of women are made from it: ἐΰζωνος, “fair-belted”, βαθύζωνος, “deep-girdled”, and καλλίζωνος, “lovely-girdled” (see [[fair-belted]], [[deep-girdled]] and [[lovely-girdled]]). In the Iliad the word also names a man's waist, where the girdle goes: Agamemnon, marshaling the army, is “in waist like Ares” (2.479; see [[like-zeus-ares-and-poseidon]]), and Iphidamas stabs him “at the waist, below the corselet” (11.234). The translation keeps “girdle” for ζώνη and “belt” for ζωστήρ, and Aphrodite's embroidered strap, which some later readers called a girdle, is another thing (see [[aphrodites-band]]).

## In Homer

**Hera's girdle.** Dressing in her chamber to go to Zeus, Hera puts on her ambrosial robe and fastens it with golden pins, “And she girded herself with a girdle fitted with a hundred tassels” (ζώσατο δὲ ζώνῃ ἑκατὸν θυσάνοις ἀραρυίῃ, 14.181), before the earrings, the veil and the sandals (14.178–186; see [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]] and [[hera]]). The only other tasseled thing in Homer is the aegis, from which “a hundred tassels hang floating, all of gold, / all finely woven, and each worth a hundred oxen” (2.448–449; see [[aegis]]), and whose regular epithet is θυσσανόεσσα, “tasseled” (5.738, 15.229, 17.593, 18.204, 21.400).

**Goddesses' girdles.** In the Odyssey Calypso and Circe dress at dawn in the same three verses: a great robe, silver-white, “fine and graceful, and about her waist she put a girdle / fine and golden, and on her head a veil” (*Odyssey* 5.230–232 = 10.543–545; see [[veil]]).

**Loosing the girdle.** In the catalogue of heroines Poseidon, taking the shape of the river Enipeus, lies with Tyro: “he loosed her maiden girdle, and poured sleep down upon her” (λῦσε δὲ παρθενίην ζώνην, κατὰ δʼ ὕπνον ἔχευεν, *Odyssey* 11.245). The loosed girdle and the poured sleep are the pair of *Iliad* 14, where Zeus is overcome by love and sleep (14.353; see [[hypnos]]).

## Outside Homer

In the *Hymn to Aphrodite* Anchises, taking the goddess to bed, first takes off her shining adornment, her brooches, spirals, buds and necklaces, then “loosed her girdle” and her glittering clothes (*Hymn* 5.161–165). Proclus reads the tassels of Hera's girdle as symbols: they have come forth from the goddess but are not cut off from her, and their number signifies the multitude of souls that her life-giving power brings forth (*On the Republic* I 137 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).
