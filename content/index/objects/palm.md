# φοίνικος (Od. 6.163), the only palm in Homer; the exclusion keeps out Φοίνικος, the father of Europa (Il. 14.321).
grc: [φοίνικος, "!^Φοίνικος"]
en: [palm tree]

φοῖνιξ is the palm, the date palm of the eastern lands. The word has other senses in Homer: φοῖνιξ is the purple-red dye with which a Maeonian or Carian woman stains ivory, and the bay color of a horse (*Iliad* 4.141, 23.454; see [[purple]] and [[crimson]]); Φοῖνιξ is a Phoenician (*Odyssey* 14.288; see [[phoenicians]]) and the name of Achilles' old tutor (see [[phoenix]]). The tree is named only once.

## In Homer

Odysseus, begging Nausicaa for help on the shore of Scheria, tells her of the wonder he once felt on Delos: “Once in Delos, beside the altar of Apollo, such a thing / I saw, a young sapling of a palm tree springing up” (*Odyssey* 6.162–163; see [[delos]], [[apollo]] and [[the-palm-on-delos]]). He saw it on the journey “on which I was to have evil sorrows”, the voyage to Troy, and stood astounded for a long time, “since never yet had such a tree come up from the earth”; so he marvels at Nausicaa now (*Odyssey* 6.164–168; see [[nausicaa]]). The palm is young, νέον ἔρνος, a sapling, and it stands by a god's altar. W. W. Merry thinks that a palm may have been kept always in the precinct of Delos, like the sacred olives of the Academy at Athens (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.162; see [[olive]]). The trees of the Iliad's similes for the young are the olive sapling and the plant of an orchard (*Iliad* 17.53–58, 18.56–57; see [[the-olive-sapling]] and [[like-a-sapling]]); the palm is the Odyssey's.

## Outside Homer

The Delian palm is the tree of Apollo's birth. In the *Hymn to Apollo* Leto labors on Delos for nine days and nine nights, and when Eileithyia comes, “she clasped her arms around the palm and pressed her knees / into the soft meadow”, and the god leaped forth into the light (*Hymn to Apollo* 91–92, 115–119; see [[leto]] and [[eileithyia]]). In Euripides' *Hecuba* the captive Trojan women wonder whether they will be carried to the island “where the first-born palm and the bay raised their sacred branches for dear Leto” (*Hecuba* 458–461; see [[euripides]]). A scholium on the Odyssey, Merry reports, refers Odysseus' palm to that first palm (on 6.162). Cicero's brother Quintus names “the tall and slender palm that Homer's Ulysses said he saw at Delos” beside the everlasting olive of the Athenian citadel, among the trees that live longer in memory than they could stand by nature (*On the Laws* 1.2; see [[cicero-the-palm-of-delos]]).

Herodotus describes the palms of Babylonia, which grow over the whole plain, most of them bearing fruit, from which the people make food, wine and honey; they tend them like fig trees, and tie the fruit of the palms the Greeks call male to the date-bearing palms, so that the gall-insect may enter the date and ripen it (Herodotus 1.193.4–5; see [[herodotus]]).
