## In Homer

**Nausicaa's game.** When the washing is spread to dry and the meal is over, Nausicaa and her handmaids throw off their veils and play at ball, and she leads the μολπή, the “song and dance” (*Odyssey* 6.99–101; see [[washing-troughs]] and [[veil]]). The scholia took μολπή here for the game itself; W. W. Merry sees no reason to doubt that the play went with a measured chant and a dance, the throwing and catching keeping time (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.101). When it is time to go home, Athena turns the game to her purpose: the princess throws the ball to one of her handmaids and misses her, it falls into a deep eddy of the river, the girls shout, and Odysseus wakes (*Odyssey* 6.112–117; see [[nausicaa]] and [[athena]]).

**The Phaeacian ball dance.** At the games of *Odyssey* 8 Alcinous bids Halius and Laodamas dance alone, since no one could rival them. They take a fine purple ball that skilled Polybus had made for them: one of them, bending backward, throws it up toward the shadowy clouds, and the other leaps from the ground and catches it before his feet touch the earth; then they dance on the ground, passing it quickly between them, while the other young men stand in the place of contest and beat time (*Odyssey* 8.370–380, a plain rendering; see [[phaeacians]]). Merry takes this as the same union of ball play and dance (on 6.101).

**A head like a ball.** The Iliad has no game of ball. It has only the adverb σφαιρηδόν, “like a ball”, of the head of Imbrius, which Ajax son of Oileus cuts off and sends flying through the throng to land at Hector's feet (*Iliad* 13.202–205; see [[like-a-ball]]).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus reports the Lydian claim to have invented the games that they and the Greeks played, dice and knucklebones and the ball and all the rest except πεσσοί, in a famine of the reign of Atys son of Manes (*Histories* 1.94.2–3; see [[game-pieces]] and [[herodotus]]). A grammarian of Corcyra gave the honor to Nausicaa: Agallis, a woman grammarian, ascribed the invention of the ball to her, as a favor to a woman of her own city (Athenaeus, as Merry quotes him on 6.101), and the Suda tells the same of Anagallis of Corcyra (σ 1720).

Eustathius, as Merry reports him, guessed that Nausicaa's game was one in which the thrower made a show of tossing the ball to one player and then flung it to another, and added that the Lacedaemonians excelled all other people at ball play, that Alexander was the most skillful of kings at it, and that among private men the most skillful was Sophocles, who played Nausicaa in his own satyr play, the *Washerwomen* (Merry on 6.115; see the translation's note on 6.115). The Suda names Aristonicus of Carystus as Alexander's partner at the ball (σ 1719). Plato's Socrates, starting a discussion, proposes the rule “as the children playing ball say”: whoever misses, and whoever keeps missing, will sit down as “donkey”, and whoever comes through without a miss will be king and give the orders (*Theaetetus* 146a; see [[plato]]).
