## Meaning

ζωστήρ is formed from ζώννυμι (“gird”), like ζῶμα, the [[kilt]] worn beneath it, and ζώνη, a woman's girdle and the waist it goes round. To gird oneself was to arm. Agamemnon “ordered the Argives to gird themselves” for battle (ζώννυσθαι, 11.15–16), and Nestor's gleaming belt lay beside his bed, the belt with which the old man girded himself whenever he armed for war (10.77–79).

## Descriptions

Menelaus' belt is described more fully than any other. Its clasps are golden: ὀχῆες, “holders”, the word used of the strap that held Paris' helmet under his chin (3.372). It is δαιδάλεος, “elaborate”, from δαιδάλλω (“work with skill”), and παναίολος, “gleaming” (πᾶν, “all”, + αἰόλος, “quick-moving, glittering”; see [[panaiolos]]), and it is ἀρηρώς, from ἀραρίσκω (“fit together”), and so “close-fitted” (4.132–135, 4.186, 4.213). Nestor's belt is παναίολος too (10.77).

## Use

The belt was the first defense of the belly. Athena guides Pandarus' arrow “to where the golden clasps of the belt held it together, and the corselet met it in a double layer” (4.132–133). It goes through the belt, the corselet and the band beneath, and grazes the skin (4.134–139; see [[corselet]] and [[band]]). The same words return when Achilles' spear strikes the young Polydorus in the back (20.413–415). Menelaus gives the belt the credit: “in front the gleaming belt stopped it” (4.185–186). Machaon draws the arrow “out of the close-fitted belt” and loosens it to reach the wound (4.213–215; see [[healing]]). A thrown spear could pierce one: Agamemnon's goes through Deicoon's shield and is driven “through the belt” into the belly (διὰ ζωστῆρος, 5.539).

A fine belt was a gift between heroes. Oeneus gave Bellerophon “a belt bright with purple” (ζωστῆρα … φοίνικι φαεινόν, 6.219), and after their duel Ajax gave Hector a belt in the same words, and Hector gave him a silver-studded sword (7.303–305; see [[purple]]).

Homer does not say what the belt was made of. It lay over the lower edge of the corselet, where the arrow met a double thickness (4.133), and it could turn an arrow. A broad belt of leather faced with bronze has often been supposed, but no Homeric line describes one.

## Outside Homer

Herodotus heard from the Greeks of the Black Sea that Heracles, leaving the half-snake woman who had borne him three sons, gave her one of his bows and his belt, which had a golden cup hanging from the end of its clasp. The son who could draw the bow and gird himself with the belt was to stay in the land. Only the youngest, Scythes, could do it, and since then, Herodotus says, the Scythians have worn cups on their belts (4.9–10). The ninth labor of Heracles was to fetch the belt (ζωστήρ) of the Amazon queen Hippolyte, a gift to her from Ares (Apollodorus, *Library* 2.5.9).
