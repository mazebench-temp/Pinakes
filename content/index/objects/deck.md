## The word

Homer always uses the plural, ἴκρια, or the form ἰκριόφιν with a preposition, “on the deck”, “from the deck”. The derivation is uncertain: Eustathius offered ἄκρον, “top”, or ἱκνέομαι, “come”, and Curtius, with misgivings, connected the word with a root seen in Latin *ico* (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix I §5).

What the ἴκρια were is disputed, and Merry settles it from the passages. Ajax goes “up and down the decks of the ships” as they lie side by side on the shore; Odysseus mounts the deck at the bow to meet Scylla; Telemachus would have slept on the deck of his ship, having no cabin; the helmsman sits at the stern on a raised deck, from which the falling mast strikes him down; the Phaeacians spread a blanket and linen for Odysseus on the deck at the stern; and Telemachus lays his guest's spear on the deck when the guest comes aboard, and takes up a spear from the deck when he goes ashore (Merry, Appendix I §5, on *Iliad* 15.676, 15.685; *Odyssey* 3.353, 12.229–230, 12.411–414, 13.73–75, 15.283, 15.552). The plural fits a deck that was not continuous: there was a small deck at the bow and another at the stern, and the waist of the ship between them, the ἄντλος, was open (*Odyssey* 12.230, 12.411, 13.75; Merry, Appendix I §5). Merry cites Thucydides for ships of that age that were “not covered in” (Thucydides 1.10, as Merry reports him).

Others took the word otherwise. Some ancient interpreters, followed by Brieger and by Hentze in his edition of Ameis' Odyssey, made the ἴκρια the upright ribs of the hull and the σταμῖνες their stays; Grashof limited the word to the bulwarks, and Nitzsch understood the whole inner lining of the sides (Merry, Appendix I §5). Merry finds strong evidence for a level platform in Herodotus, whose Paeonians of Lake Prasias live on ἴκρια joined together on tall piles in the middle of the lake, each man with a hut on the ἴκρια and a trapdoor through them down to the water (Herodotus 5.16.1–3; Merry, Appendix I §5): the piles stand under the platforms as the ribs of a ship stand under the decks. The translation renders the word “deck”, as in the Iliad (see its row ἴκρια and its note on *Odyssey* 5.252–253).

## In Homer

**At the ships.** When the Trojans reach the Achaean ships, Ajax will not stand back with the others, “but he went up and down the decks of the ships with long strides”, wielding a great spear for fighting at sea, twenty-two cubits long, leaping from deck to deck like a rider who leaps from horse to horse (15.674–686; see [[ajax]], [[the-rider-of-four-horses]] and [[the-battle-at-the-ships]]). At last, “hard pressed by missiles”, he draws back “to the seven-foot bench, and left the deck of the balanced ship” (15.728–729); Merry takes the bench, θρῆνυς, for the raised seat of the helmsman (Appendix I §17; see [[footstool]]).

## Elsewhere in the Odyssey

Nestor will not let Telemachus “lie down on the deck of a ship” while he has a house with blankets for guests (*Odyssey* 3.353; see [[xenia]]). Odysseus, against Circe's advice, puts on his armor, takes two spears and goes up onto the deck at the bow, where he expects Scylla to appear first (*Odyssey* 12.226–231, a plain rendering; see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). In the storm after Thrinacia the mast falls back and crushes the helmsman's skull, and he plunges from the deck like a diver (*Odyssey* 12.409–414). On the ship of the Phaeacians the crew spread a blanket and linen for Odysseus on the deck at the stern, so that he may sleep unwaking (*Odyssey* 13.73–75; see [[phaeacians]]), and when Theoclymenus comes aboard Telemachus' ship, Telemachus lays his guest's bronze spear on the deck, and in Ithaca he binds on his sandals and takes up a stout spear from the deck before he goes ashore (*Odyssey* 15.282–283, 15.550–552).
