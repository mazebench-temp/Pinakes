## Description

A χηλός has a lid (πῶμα, 16.221) and stands in a hut, a storeroom or a ship. Achilles' chest is “fine and intricately worked” (καλῆς δαιδαλέης), and his mother had put it on his ship herself, “filling it well with tunics / and cloaks to keep off the wind and woolly rugs” (16.222–224; see [[thetis]], [[tunic]] and [[cloak]]): a mother's packing for a son going far from home (see [[mothers-and-sons]]). In it he keeps his own cup, from which no other man drank the wine and from which he poured libations to no god but Zeus. Before he sends Patroclus out he takes the cup from the chest, cleanses it with sulfur and water, prays to Zeus of Dodona, and puts it back in the chest (16.225–232, 16.253–254; see [[cup]] and [[libation]]).

The other word, φωριαμός, is found only twice in Homer, both times of chests that hold woven things. Priam opens the fine lids of his chests and takes out twelve robes, twelve single cloaks and as many rugs, mantles and tunics for Hector's ransom (24.228–231; see [[the-ransom-of-hector]] and [[apoina]]), and at Sparta Helen stands beside the chests where her robes lie, which she made herself (*Odyssey* 15.104–105; see [[robe]]). The Suda explains the word as κιβωτός, “box” (φ 668). For the λάρναξ, the chest of Hephaestus' tools and of Hector's bones, see [[larnax]].

## In the Odyssey

Chests keep the wealth of a house. In Odysseus' storeroom, beside the gold, the bronze and the jars of old wine, lies “clothing in chests” (*Odyssey* 2.337–342; see [[the-storeroom-of-odysseus]]), and Penelope climbs to the storeroom where the chests stand, with fragrant garments in them, to take down the bow (*Odyssey* 21.51–53). Among the Phaeacians the queen gives the stranger a chest for the gifts of the kings, and he ties its lid with a knot that Circe taught him (*Odyssey* 8.424–448); the chest goes with him to the ship (*Odyssey* 13.10–12, 13.68).
