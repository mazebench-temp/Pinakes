## Meaning

μίτρη (Attic μίτρα) has no certain etymology. In Homer it is a piece of a warrior's armor. In later Greek it is a band of cloth worn around the head, a headband or turban: the Babylonians, says Herodotus, bind their heads with μίτραι (1.195). The word passed into Latin as *mitra*, and from Latin into English as “mitre”, the bishop's headdress.

## In Homer

It was the last thing between Pandarus' arrow and Menelaus' body:

{{quote:a7f48efd-9a91-5c3b-b0a6-7c2613822677}}

The band was worn next to the skin: it guarded his “flesh” (χροός), and the arrow reached it after the belt and the corselet. It is ἔρυμα, “a guard”, and ἕρκος ἀκόντων, “a barrier against javelins”. ἕρκος (“fence”) is a word of defense: Achilles is for the Achaeans “a mighty bulwark against evil war” (1.284), Ajax “the bulwark of the Achaeans” (3.229), and Nestor sets his foot soldiers “to be a bulwark against war” (4.299). Twice the band is said to be bronze-work: “the band that the bronze-smiths made” (4.187, 4.216). The relative pronoun τήν is feminine and singular, so it goes with μίτρη, not with the neuter ζῶμα (see [[kilt]]).

Elsewhere in the Iliad the μίτρη is worn at the lowest part of the belly. Diomedes' spear strikes Ares “in the lowest part of the belly, where he was girded with the μίτρη” (ὅθι ζωννύσκετο μίτρῃ, 5.857). Oresbius of Hyle, one of the Achaeans killed by Hector and Ares, is αἰολομίτρης, “with gleaming μίτρη” (5.707). And the Lycians who follow Sarpedon are ἀμιτροχίτωνες (16.419), a word found only there, perhaps “fighting in tunics without the μίτρη”; its sense is disputed.

## What it was

Three things are clear from Book 4: the band was worn at the belly beneath belt and corselet, it was made of bronze, and it gave the best protection. Beyond that there is no agreement. Semicircular bronze plates of the seventh century BC, some with engraved figures, have been found in Crete; they hung from a belt to cover the lower belly, and archaeologists call them mitrai after this passage. Whether Homer's μίτρη was such a plate, or a band of bronze around the body, is not known.

The narrator names three defenses when the arrow strikes: belt, corselet and band (4.132–138). Menelaus and Machaon name belt, kilt and band, and not the corselet (4.185–187, 4.215–216). See [[belt]], [[corselet]] and [[kilt]].
