Homer has two words for the axe. πέλεκυς is the common one, and it is usually compared with Sanskrit *paraśú-*, “axe”. ἀξίνη, whose origin is unknown, occurs only twice, both times in battle at the ships (13.612, 15.711). The handle is a πέλεκκον, a word made from πέλεκυς and found only in Peisander's axe, “on a handle of olive wood, / long and well polished” (13.612–613), or a στειλειόν (Od. 5.236). The prizes for archery at Patroclus' games are ten πελέκεις and ten ἡμιπέλεκκα, “half-axes” (23.851), usually taken as double-bladed and single-bladed axes. The axe Calypso gives Odysseus is “sharpened on both sides” (ἀμφοτέρωθεν ἀκαχμένον, Od. 5.235).

## Elsewhere in Homer

**The woodcutter and the shipwright.** The men sent to Ida for the wood of Patroclus' pyre carry “woodcutting axes” (ὑλοτόμους πελέκεας) and ropes, and fell the high oaks with the bronze (23.114–119). The axe in the shipwright's hand gives two comparisons. Paris tells Hector that his heart is always unyielding, “like an axe” that goes through a beam in the hand of a man who shapes a ship's timber, and adds to his force (3.60–63; see [[heart-like-an-axe]]). And a warrior falls like a tree that builders cut down on the mountains “with newly sharpened axes to be ship-timber” (13.389–391 = 16.482–484; see [[the-felled-tree]]). Calypso gives Odysseus a great bronze axe, fitted to his hands, with a handle of olive wood, and an adze; he fells twenty trees and hews them with the bronze for his raft (πελέκκησεν, “trimmed with the axe”, Od. 5.234–245).

**The sacrifice.** At Nestor's sacrifice of a heifer to Athena at Pylos his son Thrasymedes stands by holding a sharp axe, strikes, and the axe cuts the sinews of the heifer's neck (Od. 3.442–450; see [[sacrifice]]). Aretus, struck by Automedon's spear, leaps forward and falls as an ox of the field falls when a strong young man with a sharp axe strikes it behind the horns and cuts through the sinew (17.520–523).

**Iron.** The axes are sometimes of iron. Achilles sets out dark iron as the prize for archery, ten axes and ten half-axes; Meriones wins the axes and Teucer the half-axes (23.850–883). In the Odyssey the hiss of the Cyclops' eye is the sound of a smith dipping a great axe or an adze in cold water to temper it, “for that is the strength of iron” (Od. 9.391–393; see [[iron]]). Odysseus' twelve axes, which he used to set up in a row in his hall and shoot through, become the contest for Penelope's hand (Od. 19.572–581, 21.120–123, 21.421–423). How the arrow passed through them is much disputed.

**In battle.** The axe is rare as a weapon. Apart from Peisander's, the only battle-axes of the Iliad are swung at Protesilaus' ship, where the two sides no longer wait for the rush of arrows and javelins, but standing close, “with one spirit”, fight “with sharp axes and battle-axes, and with great swords and double-pointed spears” (ὀξέσι δὴ πελέκεσσι καὶ ἀξίνῃσι, 15.709–712; see [[sword]] and [[spear]]).

## Outside Homer

In Herodotus the battle-axe, σάγαρις, is a weapon of the peoples of the steppe. The Massagetae are archers and spearmen who carry battle-axes, and they make their spear-points, arrowheads and battle-axes all of bronze (1.215.1). The Scythians tell that in the reign of the three sons of Targitaus, their first man, golden things fell from the sky: a plow and a yoke, a battle-axe and a cup (4.5.3; see [[herodotus]]).
