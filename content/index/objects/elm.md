πτελέη (Attic πτελέα) is the elm. The Iliad names it in three places.

**The tomb of Eetion.** Andromache tells Hector how Achilles sacked her city and killed her father, but did not strip him; he burned him with his armor and heaped a mound over him, “and elms were planted around it / by the mountain nymphs, daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus” (6.416–420; see [[eetion]] and [[nymphs]]). The trees are the nymphs' honor to the dead king, as the burning with his armor is Achilles'.

**The bank of the Xanthus.** When the river rises against Achilles and he cannot keep his footing, he catches hold of an elm, “well-grown and great”; it falls, torn out by the roots, tears away the whole bank, holds back the streams with its thick branches, and bridges the river, falling full length into it (21.240–246; see [[scamander]] and [[achilles-and-the-river]]). γεφύρωσεν, “bridged”, may rather mean “dammed”, as Walter Leaf notes (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.245); the γέφυραι that a river in flood sweeps away are dikes (5.88–89; see [[obscure-gephyrai]]). The fallen tree does not save Achilles; he leaps out of the eddy and runs, and the river comes after him (21.246–250).

**In the fire.** When Hephaestus turns his flame on the river, the trees of its banks burn: “The elms and the willows and the tamarisks were burning” (21.350; see [[willow]], [[tamarisk]] and [[fire]]). Leaf reports Barker Webb's observation, by way of Schliemann, that all the trees and plants named in these verses are still found in the plain of Troy, so far as they can be identified (on 21.350).

## Outside Homer

[[hesiod|Hesiod]] advises the farmer that poles of laurel or elm are the least worm-eaten for a plow (*Works and Days* 435). [[strabo|Strabo]] tells that the Salassi of the Alps, who robbed those who crossed their mountains, made Messala, wintering near them, pay for his wood, both for firewood and for the elm-wood javelins used in exercise (*Geography* 4.6.7).
