The θώρηξ (Attic θώραξ) protected the chest and back, over the [[tunic]]. Homer's corselets are usually of bronze, “shining” and “richly wrought” (πολυδαίδαλος). The compound λινοθώρηξ, “with linen corselet”, names another kind (see [[with-linen-corselet]]). The word has no certain etymology. Later Greek also used θώραξ for the chest itself, whence English *thorax*.

The verb θωρήσσω means “put on the θώρηξ”, but Homer uses it for arming in general, of single warriors and of whole armies: Zeus bids the Dream tell Agamemnon to “arm the long-haired Achaeans” (2.11). The Achaeans are also χαλκοχίτωνες, men with “tunics of bronze” (see [[bronze-armored]]).

Bronze body armor was made in Mycenaean Greece. The best-known example is the suit of bronze plates found in a chamber tomb at Dendra in the Argolid, of about 1400 BC. A bronze corselet of a later type, in two halves for front and back, was found in a warrior's grave at Argos of the late eighth century BC. Which, if either, lies behind Homer's descriptions is debated.

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the arming scenes the corselet comes second, after the greaves: “second, he put the corselet around his chest” (δεύτερον αὖ θώρηκα περὶ στήθεσσιν ἔδυνεν, 3.332, 11.19, 16.133, 19.371). Paris borrows his brother Lycaon's (3.333), and Patroclus puts on Achilles', “spangled and starry” (16.134). Agamemnon's was a guest-gift from Cinyras of Cyprus, with bands of dark κύανος, gold and tin, and three serpents rising toward the neck on either side (11.19–28). When Pandarus' arrow strikes Menelaus, it pierces his belt, his corselet and the guard (μίτρη) worn beneath, and only grazes the skin (4.132–140).

## Outside Homer

The Egyptian king Amasis dedicated a linen corselet to Athena at Lindos, and sent another, woven with figures in gold and cotton, to the Spartans; the Samians seized it on the way (Herodotus 2.182, 3.47).
