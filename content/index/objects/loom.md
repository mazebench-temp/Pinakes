The Greek loom stood upright. The warp hung from a crossbeam, held taut by clay weights, and the weaver walked back and forth along it to pass the weft. Hence its name ἱστός, “the upright”, the same word as the ship's [[mast|mast]] ({{ref:Iliad 1.434}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.480}}).

The loom is the work of the women of a house, from queens to captives. Agamemnon foresees Chryseis at his loom in Argos, and Hector Andromache weaving at another woman's bidding (1.29–31, 6.456; see [[captive-women]]); Helen weaves the trials of Trojans and Achaeans into a purple web (3.125–128; see [[helens-web]]); Hector sends Andromache back to “the loom and the distaff” (6.490–492), and Telemachus sends Penelope back in the same words (*Odyssey* 1.356–358). Penelope's own loom is her stratagem: the shroud for Laertes, woven by day and unwoven by night, kept the suitors waiting for three years (*Odyssey* 2.93–110; see [[the-web-of-penelope]], [[shroud]], [[weaving]] and [[penelope]]).
