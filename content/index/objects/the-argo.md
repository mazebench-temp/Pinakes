## The name

Ἀργώ is declined like Λητώ: genitive Ἀργοῦς, dative Ἀργοῖ, as the Suda notes of the dative (α 3785), and as Euripides and Herodotus write them (*Medea* 1; Herodotus 4.179.3). Homer has only the nominative, in the one verse that names the ship.

## In Homer

Circe names the Argo when she warns Odysseus of the Wanderers, the rocks past which no ship of men has ever come safely: “Only one seafaring ship has sailed past there, / the Argo, in the thoughts of all, sailing from Aeetes”, and Hera sent it past “since Jason was dear to her” (*Odyssey* 12.69–72; see [[jason]], [[aeetes]], [[hera]] and [[seafaring-ship]]). Homer gives the ship no builder and no crew but its captain, and says nothing of a fleece or of Medea. παρʼ Αἰήταο πλέουσα, “sailing from Aeetes”, sets it on the voyage home from the king who is Circe's own brother (*Odyssey* 10.135–139; see [[circe]]), as in the *Theogony* the son of Aeson leads Aeetes' daughter away παρʼ Αἰήτεω, “from Aeetes” (*Theogony* 994). The Iliad knows Jason only as the father of Euneus, king of Lemnos (*Iliad* 7.468–469; see [[euneus]] and [[the-lemnian-women]]).

## Outside Homer

**The voyage.** In the *Theogony* the son of Aeson, having finished the grievous labors that Pelias laid on him, came to Iolcus “bringing the bright-eyed girl on a swift ship” (*Theogony* 992–999; see [[hesiod]], [[medea]] and [[pelias]]); the ship is not named. The Hesiodic *Catalogue*, as the scholia on Apollonius report it, brought the Argonauts home by another way: they sailed into Ocean by the Phasis, came by Ocean to Libya, and carried the Argo overland to our sea (fr. 45 Evelyn-White; see [[oceanus]]). Herodotus tells that when the Argo had been built for Jason under Pelion, he put a hecatomb and a bronze tripod on board and sailed round the Peloponnese for Delphi, until the North Wind caught him off Malea and carried him into the shallows of Lake Tritonis in Libya, where Triton showed him the way out in return for the tripod (Herodotus 4.179; see [[herodotus]] and [[malea]]). He names Aphetae in Magnesia as the place where Heracles was left behind, sent for water, when Jason and his companions sailed in the Argo for the fleece to Aea in Colchis (Herodotus 7.193.2), and tells of the grandsons of the Argo's crew, driven from Lemnos to Lacedaemon (Herodotus 4.145.2). Strabo says that Pelias sent out Jason and the Argo from Iolcus, and that some derived the name of Pagasae from the building of the Argo (*Geography* 9.5.15; see [[iolcus]] and [[strabo]]).

Euripides' *Medea* opens with the nurse's wish that the hull of the Argo had never flown through the dark-blue Clashing Rocks to the land of the Colchians, and that the pine had never been felled in the glens of Pelion to give oars to the hands of the best of men, who went for the golden fleece for Pelias (*Medea* 1–6; see [[euripides]] and [[pelion]]). Medea reminds Jason that the Greeks who sailed with him in the Argo know how she saved him, and foretells that he will die struck on the head by a remnant of the Argo (*Medea* 476–477, 1386–1388; see [[medea-play]]).

**Homer's Argo.** Strabo held that Homer took the wanderings of Odysseus from historical accounts and added myth to them. Among the facts the poet kept he counts the naming of Aeetes and of Jason and the Argo; and he finds Ἀργὼ πασιμέλουσα, “the Argo, of concern to all”, well said if the voyage went through familiar and populous places, as it would not be if Aeetes had lived in Ocean toward the sunrise, where Demetrius of Scepsis, citing Mimnermus, put him (*Geography* 1.2.38–40). The Wanderers themselves, he thought, were modeled on the Cyanean or Clashing Rocks at the mouth of the Pontus, and so made Jason's voyage through them plausible (*Geography* 1.2.10, 3.2.12); Herodotus speaks of the Cyanean Rocks, “which the Greeks say were formerly the Wanderers” (Herodotus 4.85.1; see [[the-wanderers]]). On the island of Aethalia, Strabo reports, a harbor was called Argous after the Argo, where Jason put in seeking Circe's dwelling, because Medea wished to see the goddess (*Geography* 5.2.6). For the Argo's passage of the Wanderers W. W. Merry refers to the *Argonautica* of Apollonius (4.924 and after, as Merry cites it: *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 12.70; see [[apollonius]]).
