Homer's usual word for the bow is τόξον, often in the plural τόξα for a single weapon. The rarer βιός is an old word, cognate with Sanskrit *jyā́*, “bowstring”. In the Iliad the bow belongs to a few specialists such as Teucer, Paris and Pandarus, and Diomedes sneers at Paris as a mere archer (Iliad 11.385). For the gods it is a weapon of power: Apollo and his sister Artemis strike from afar.

## In Book 1

[[Chryses]] invokes Apollo as ἀργυρότοξος, “god of the silver bow” ({{ref:Iliad 1.37}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.451}}; see [[god-of-the-silver-bow]]). The god comes down from Olympus in anger “with his bow on his shoulders and his close-covered [[quiver|quiver]]”, sits apart from the ships, and shoots; “terrible was the twang of the silver bow” (ἀργυρέοιο βιοῖο; see [[gold-and-silver]]). For nine days his [[arrows|arrows]] carry the plague (see [[wrath-of-apollo]]).

{{quote:31d08fe5-a463-52cf-9a08-54b301c0c853}}

The same god holds the [[lyre|lyre]] at the gods' feast at the end of the book ({{ref:Iliad 1.603}}). The Homeric Hymn to Apollo gives him both at birth: “let the lyre and the curved bow be mine” (line 131).

## Outside Homer

Heraclitus of Ephesus played on βιός (“bow”) and βίος (“life”): “the bow's name is life, but its work is death” (fragment B48 Diels–Kranz).
