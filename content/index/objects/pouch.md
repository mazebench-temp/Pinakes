## The word

πήρη is a bag for food, carried on the shoulder: the Suda explains it as “the case for loaves”, ἡ θήκη τῶν ἄρτων (π 1534). Homer names it only in the Odyssey, and only as a beggar's. It is ἀεικής, “shabby” (*Odyssey* 13.437, 17.197, 18.108), or ἀεικελίη, “unsightly” (*Odyssey* 17.357), πυκνὰ ῥωγαλέη, “rent in many places”, and it hangs from a στρόφος ἀορτήρ, “a twisted cord to sling it by” (*Odyssey* 13.438 = 17.198 = 18.109; see the translation's row σκῆπτρον, of a beggar; πήρη; στρόφος ἀορτήρ). ἀορτήρ is any strap that carries a thing, from ἀείρω, “lift, hang”: the golden baldric of Heracles' phantom and the straps of Agamemnon's scabbard are ἀορτῆρες too (*Odyssey* 11.609; *Iliad* 11.31; see [[the-baldric-of-heracles]]). The pouch goes with the staff, σκῆπτρον, which is the beggar's stick as it is the king's scepter (see [[scepter]]).

The man who carries it lives on what is put into it. In the hall of Odysseus the beggar is a προΐκτης, a man who lives on doles (*Odyssey* 17.352), a word that D. B. Monro sets beside προικός, “as a free gift”, in Alcinous' words about the gifts of the Phaeacians: προΐκτης is “a gift-man”, “one who lives on doles” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 13.15; see [[the-gifts-of-the-phaeacians]]).

## In the Odyssey

**The disguise.** Athena gives Odysseus the pouch with his staff when she makes him an old beggar on the shore of Ithaca (*Odyssey* 13.437–438; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]], [[athena]] and [[shape-changing]]).

**On the road and in the hall.** When he sets out with the swineherd for the city, “around his shoulders he slung a shabby pouch, / rent in many places; and on it was a twisted cord to sling it by”, and Eumaeus gives him a staff (*Odyssey* 17.197–199, a plain rendering; see [[eumaeus]]). In his own hall he takes the bread and meat that Telemachus sends him and sets it down “before his feet, on his unsightly pouch”, and eats while the singer sings (*Odyssey* 17.343–358, a plain rendering; see [[telemachus]]). Begging from the suitors, he finds that “all the others gave, and they filled his pouch / with bread and meat”, only Antinous gives nothing, and in the end strikes him with a footstool; he goes back to the threshold “and set down his pouch, / well filled” (*Odyssey* 17.411–412, 17.462–467, a plain rendering; see [[antinous]] and [[the-suitors]]). When he has beaten the beggar Irus, he slings the pouch on again in the verses of Book 17 and sits down once more on the threshold (*Odyssey* 18.108–110; see [[threshold]]).

## Outside Homer

The Suda records a proverb, “a beggar's pouch is not filled”, πτωχοῦ πήρα οὐ πίμπλαται, said of the insatiable (π 1534), and quotes a verse on “a ragged goatherds' pouch, all full of stale bread and meat”, ῥωγὰς αἰπόλων πήρα, ἄρτων ἑώλων πᾶσα καὶ κρεῶν πλήρης (ρ 242). It also cites Aristophanes for “Telephus from his little pouch”, Τήλεφος ἐκ πηριδίου, explaining that Telephus was a poor man (π 1535; see [[telephus]]).
