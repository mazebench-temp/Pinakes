## The word

κηρός is the wax that bees make. Homer gives it one epithet, μελιηδής, “honey-sweet” (*Odyssey* 12.48), which elsewhere is said of wine and wheat and fruit, of homecoming and of life itself (see [[honey-sweet]] and [[honey]]), and names it in no other passage. The wax comes in a τροχός, a “round” or disc, because, W. W. Merry supposes, it had been melted, clarified and poured into a pan or bowl, whose shape it kept when it cooled (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 12.174). The Iliad has bees and honey, but no wax (see [[bees]]).

## Outside Homer

**The ears stopped.** Nitzsch, as Merry reports, quoted an allusion to the scene from Alcman, καὶ τότʼ Ὀδυσσῆος ταλασίφρονος ὦτά θʼ ἑταίρων Κίρκη ἐπαλείψασα, “and then Circe, having anointed the ears of the companions of stout-hearted Odysseus”, in which Circe herself stops the ears, and called it the earliest testimony to any particular scene of the Odyssey (Merry on *Odyssey* 12.47; see [[circe]]). Ovid, teaching that a sweet voice can win love, recalls the Sirens, monsters of the sea who held back ships however swift, and how the son of Sisyphus almost loosed his limbs to them, *nam sociis inlita cera fuit*, “for wax was smeared on his companions” (*Ars Amatoria* 3.311–314; see [[ovid]] and [[sirens]]); Merry quotes the half-verse in his note on Odysseus' nod (on *Odyssey* 12.194).

**Bees and tablets.** Aristotle, who thought that honey falls from the air and is only gathered by the bee, held that the bee makes its wax from flowers and carries it on its legs (*History of Animals* 5.22; see [[aristotle]]). Wax covered the writing tablet: Demaratus, warning Sparta of Xerxes' plan, scraped the wax from a folding tablet, wrote on the wood and melted the wax back over the writing, so that the tablet went past the guards on the road as if blank, until Gorgo, the wife of Leonidas, told the Spartans to scrape the wax away (Herodotus 7.239.3–4; see [[herodotus]] and [[writing]]).

**The wax of the soul.** In Plato's *Theaetetus* Socrates supposes a block of wax in every soul, larger or smaller, purer or dirtier, harder or softer, a gift of Mnemosyne, the mother of the Muses, on which we stamp what we perceive and think, as with the seal of a ring, and remember it as long as the image lasts (*Theaetetus* 191c–d). He plays on Homer's word for the heart, κῆρ, which he writes in the old form κέαρ: when the wax is deep and smooth, impressions are stamped clearly “into this heart of the soul”, which Homer named so, “hinting at its likeness to wax”; and the “shaggy heart” that “the all-wise poet praised” is a block of rough wax, which takes unclear impressions (*Theaetetus* 194c–e; *Iliad* 2.851, 16.554; see [[plato]] and [[ker-heart]]).
