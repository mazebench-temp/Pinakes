## Meaning

κυκεών is formed from κυκάω, “stir, mix”: a drink that is stirred up, since the barley and cheese in it do not dissolve. Hecamede “made a mixture” (κύκησε, 11.638), and Circe “mixed” her drink (ἐκύκα, Odyssey 10.235). The same verb is used of men and horses thrown into confusion, as the sons of Antimachus are when the reins slip from their hands (κυκηθήτην, 11.129). The translation renders the noun “mixed drink”. Heraclitus made an image of it: “even the kykeon separates if it is not stirred” (fragment B125 Diels–Kranz).

## In Homer

After the battle Nestor and Machaon dry their sweat in the sea breeze and go into Nestor's hut, and Hecamede, the woman the Achaeans chose for him from the spoils of Tenedos, makes them a κυκεών (11.618–624; see [[hecamede]]). She sets out a table with a bronze basket, an onion “as a relish for the drink”, pale honey and barley meal, and Nestor's great cup (11.628–637; see [[table]] and [[the-cup-of-nestor]]). The grater, κνῆστις, is named only here in Homer. The two men drink, drive off their thirst, and take pleasure in talking (11.642–643). The drink is a refreshment after toil, and the poem does not call it a remedy; but one of the two who drink it is a wounded healer, and later readers asked what a doctor would say of it (see below and [[healing]]). ## Elsewhere in Homer

Circe's drink in the Odyssey has the same ingredients and more: she mixes cheese, barley meal and pale honey in Pramnian wine for Odysseus' companions, and adds baneful drugs to make them forget their country, and they become swine (Odyssey 10.233–240). Hermes warns Odysseus, “She will make you a kykeon, and put drugs in the food” (Odyssey 10.290), and she makes him one in a golden cup, but his own drug protects him (Odyssey 10.314–318). The honey that stands beside Nestor's drink is in Circe's (μέλι χλωρόν, 11.631; Odyssey 10.234).

## Outside Homer

In the Homeric Hymn to Demeter the grieving goddess, in the house of Celeus at Eleusis, refuses a cup of honey-sweet wine, saying that it was not lawful for her to drink red wine, and asks instead for barley and water mixed with tender pennyroyal. Metaneira makes the kykeon and gives it to her, and Demeter accepts it “for the sake of the rite” (ὁσίης ἕνεκεν, *Hymn to Demeter* 206–211). The words are usually taken to make her drink the origin of the kykeon drunk in the rites of Eleusis.

Plato's Socrates quotes the verses of Nestor's drink in the *Ion*: when Homer says that Hecamede, “Nestor's concubine”, gave the wounded Machaon a kykeon to drink, with Pramnian wine, grated goat's cheese and an onion as relish, is it the doctor's art or the rhapsode's that judges whether he speaks rightly? Ion answers, the doctor's (*Ion* 538b–c). In the *Republic* Socrates remembers the drink as given to Eurypylus, Pramnian wine with barley meal and grated cheese, and says that the sons of Asclepius did not blame the woman who gave it, though such things are thought to breed phlegm (*Republic* 3.405e–406a; see [[plato-asclepiads]]).
