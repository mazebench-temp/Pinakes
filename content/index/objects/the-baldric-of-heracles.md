## The words

ἀορτήρ, from ἀείρω, “lift, hang”, and τελαμών, from the root of τλῆναι, “bear”, both mean the strap that carries something, as W. W. Merry explains; here, by its place in the verse, τελαμών is the more specific of the two: the baldric was a golden strap (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.609; see the translation's row ἀορτήρ, τελαμών; χαροπός). Elsewhere the ἀορτῆρες are the golden straps that hold Agamemnon's silver scabbard (*Iliad* 11.31), and ἀορτήρ the cord of the beggar's pouch that Athena gives Odysseus, “a twisted cord to sling it by” (*Odyssey* 13.438, a plain rendering); τελαμών is the strap of a shield or a sword, as when Agamemnon threatens that the strap about a man's chest will sweat under his shield (*Iliad* 2.388; see [[shield]] and [[sword]]). The baldric that carries a sword is common; what makes this one a thing of its own is its gold and its images.

## The images

“Bears and wild boars and lions with flashing eyes, / combats and battles and slaughters and killings of men” (*Odyssey* 11.611–612; see [[boar]] and [[lion]]). The second of these verses is the line in which Hesiod lists the children of Strife, Ὑσμίνας τε Μάχας τε Φόνους τʼ Ἀνδροκτασίας τε, “Combats and Battles and Slaughters and Killings of Men” (*Theogony* 228; see [[hesiod]] and [[eris]]), and the Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles* sets herds of boars and lions glaring at one another among its terrors, with Pursuit and Rally, Din and Fear and Killing of Men (*Shield* 154–175; see [[shield-of-heracles]]). Achilles' shield, too, has its lions and its battles (see [[the-shield-of-achilles]]).
