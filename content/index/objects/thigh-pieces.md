The god's share of an animal sacrifice is the thigh-bones, wrapped in fat and burned on the altar. Book 1 calls them μηρία ({{ref:Iliad 1.40}}), μηροί ({{ref:Iliad 1.460}}) and, in the form printed at {{ref:Iliad 1.464}}, μῆρε. μηρός is also the ordinary word for a thigh: Achilles' sword hangs “beside his thigh” (παρὰ μηροῦ, {{ref:Iliad 1.190}}).

## In Book 1

[[Chryses]] rests his first prayer on sacrifices past: “if ever I burned for you the fat thigh-pieces of bulls and goats” ({{ref:Iliad 1.40-41}}; see [[prayer|prayer]]). At Chryse the Achaeans cut out the thigh-bones, wrap them in a double fold of fat, and lay raw flesh on them. The old priest burns them on split wood and pours wine over them, and only when they are burned up do the men taste the entrails and roast the rest for themselves (see [[sacrifice|sacrifice]]).

## Outside Homer

[[Hesiod]] explains the division. At Mecone, Prometheus tricked Zeus into choosing the white bones wrapped in glistening fat, and “since then the tribes of men on earth burn white bones for the immortals on fragrant altars” (*Theogony* 535–557).
