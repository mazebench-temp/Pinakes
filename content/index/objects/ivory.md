## Meaning

In Homer ἐλέφας is always ivory; the elephant itself is named only by later writers. The word is older than Homer. The Linear B inventories of furniture from Pylos describe tables and chairs inlaid with ivory, using an adjective made from it (*e-re-pa-te-jo*, “of ivory”). Its origin is uncertain, and it is probably a loan from the languages of the East, from where the ivory itself came.

## In Homer

The Iliad's first ivory is a simile. Menelaus' blood runs down his thighs from Pandarus' arrow:

{{quote:dae55fa8-05d3-5e31-a538-75e6553ebeb3}}

A Maeonian or Carian woman stains the ivory with purple to make a cheekpiece (παρήϊον) for a horse. It lies in a chamber, and many horsemen have prayed to carry it, but it is kept as a delight for a king, “both an ornament for the horse and a glory to the driver” (4.143–145). The women and the craft belong to Asia Minor (see [[maeonians]] and [[carians]]). Carved ivory horse trappings, blinkers and frontlets, are among the ivories found in the Assyrian palaces at Nimrud, of the ninth and eighth centuries BC. See [[ivory-stained-with-purple]] and [[purple]].

The other ivory of the Iliad belongs to horses too. When Antilochus strikes the charioteer Mydon with a stone, “the reins, white with ivory” (ἡνία λεύκʼ ἐλέφαντι), fall from his hands into the dust (5.580–583).

The Odyssey has ivory in the houses of kings. Menelaus' palace gleams with bronze, gold, electrum, silver and ivory (Odyssey 4.71–73). Penelope's chair is inlaid with ivory and silver (Odyssey 19.55–56), the key to the storeroom has an ivory handle (Odyssey 21.7), and Odysseus inlaid his bed with gold, silver and ivory (Odyssey 23.200). The Phaeacian Euryalus gives Odysseus a sword with a sheath of newly sawn ivory (Odyssey 8.403–405), and Athena makes Penelope “whiter than sawn ivory” (Odyssey 18.196). Dreams, Penelope says, come through two gates, one of horn and one of ivory, and those that come through the ivory deceive (ἐλεφαίρονται), a play on the word (Odyssey 19.562–567).

## Outside Homer

Virgil ends the sixth book of the *Aeneid* at the two gates of Sleep, of horn and of ivory: Anchises sends Aeneas and the Sibyl out through the gate of ivory, through which false dreams are sent (*Aeneid* 6.893–898).
