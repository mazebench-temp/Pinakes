## The horse

The Odyssey calls it a horse “of wood”, δουράτεος (*Odyssey* 8.493, 8.512), “the polished horse” (*Odyssey* 4.272; see [[polished]]), and “the hollow ambush”, κοῖλος λόχος (*Odyssey* 4.277, 8.515; see [[ambush]]). Epeius made it “with Athena's help” (*Odyssey* 8.493; see [[epeius]] and [[athena]]), and Odysseus “brought up into the citadel as a snare” (*Odyssey* 8.494; see [[dolos]]): a scholium observes that the horse is why Odysseus is called “sacker of cities”, since the making was the work of Epeius and Athena but the enterprise was his (as W. W. Merry quotes it, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.494; see [[sacker-of-cities]]). In it sat “all the best / of the Argives, bringing slaughter and doom to the Trojans” (*Odyssey* 4.272–273 = 8.512–513; see [[ker-doom]]), among them Menelaus, Diomedes and Odysseus, while the rest of the army burned their huts and sailed away (*Odyssey* 4.280–281, 8.500–503; see [[hut]]). The Trojans themselves dragged it into the citadel and debated whether to split the timber, throw it from the heights, or keep it as an offering to the gods; they kept it, and the city fell (*Odyssey* 8.504–515; see [[the-fall-of-troy]] and [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The three accounts look at it from three sides. In Menelaus' tale Helen walked three times around it and called the chieftains by name in the voices of their wives, and Odysseus held back the men who would have answered (*Odyssey* 4.271–289; see [[helen]] and [[anticlus]]). In Demodocus' song it is the snare that ends the war (*Odyssey* 8.492–520). In the underworld Odysseus tells Achilles that he was in charge of opening and closing the ambush, and that Neoptolemus alone neither paled nor wept inside it, but begged to go out (*Odyssey* 11.523–532, a plain rendering; see [[neoptolemus]]). The Iliad does not name the horse; Epeius there wins the boxing at Patroclus' games and is laughed at for his throw of the weight (*Iliad* 23.664–699, 23.838–840).

## Outside Homer

In the prologue of Euripides' *Trojan Women* Poseidon says that the Phocian Epeius of Parnassus, by the devices of Pallas, fitted together a horse pregnant with arms and sent it within the walls, a deadly image; two lines that Murray's text brackets add that later men will call it the Wooden Horse, Δούρειος ἵππος (*Trojan Women* 9–14, a plain rendering; see [[euripides]]). Aristotle counts the *Sack of Troy* and the *Sailing Away*, with the *Sinon*, among the tragedies made from the *Little Iliad* (*Poetics* 1459b5–7, where the *Sinon* and the *Trojan Women* are bracketed). Merry reports that Düntzer quoted from the summary of Arctinus' poem that some held that the horse should be dedicated to Athena (on 8.509).
