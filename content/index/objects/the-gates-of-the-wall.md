## Description

The gates were part of the wall from the first. Nestor proposed high towers by the common tomb of the dead, “And in them let us make gates that fit well, / so that through them there may be a way for driving horses” (7.339–340), and the Achaeans built them in the same words (7.438–439; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[the-building-of-the-wall]]).

πύλαι, always plural, is a gateway with its gates; σανίδες are the doors, made of planks, and δικλίδες, “double”, are folding doors in two leaves, the word for the doors of Odysseus' storeroom and court (Od. 2.344–345, 17.267–268). An ὀχεύς is a bar laid across the doors to hold them shut; a κληΐς is a bolt or pin, and in the Odyssey also a key (Od. 21.6, 21.47). θαιροί, named only once, are taken as the pins or pivots on which the doors turned (see the translation's note on 12.453–462).

## The fight at the gates

**Several gates.** The wall has more than one. The gate that Asius drives at lies “toward the left of the ships, where the Achaeans / were coming back from the plain with their horses and chariots” (12.118–119); “others were fighting their battle around other gates” while the Lapiths held this one (12.175); and when the Lycians reach Menestheus' tower, “all of them had been shut” (πᾶσαι γὰρ ἐπώχατο, 12.340). ἐπώχατο occurs only here, and is explained as “had been shut” or as “were assailed” (see the translation's note on 12.340). Aristarchus held that the wall had only one gate: he read πᾶσαι there as “the whole” gate and the plural πύλαι as a plural for one (scholia A on 12.340 and 12.470), and he athetized the lines on the “other gates” (12.175–180; scholium A). Which gate Hector breaks, the poem does not say.

**The open gate.** At the gate Asius found neither “the doors shut” nor “the long bar in place”, “but men held them wide open, in case some companion / fleeing from the war they might bring safe to the ships” (12.120–123; see [[asius]] and [[chariot]]). The gate kept open for the army's own fugitives lets in the enemy's chariot, but “in front of the high gates” stand the two Lapiths, Polypoetes and Leonteus, like oaks rooted in the mountains (12.127–136; see [[polypoetes]], [[leonteus]] and [[oaks-on-the-mountains]]). They had been inside, rousing the Achaeans, and darted out to fight in front of the gates when the Trojans rushed the wall (12.141–145), and Asius complains to Zeus that the two of them “will not give way from the gates” (12.171). Priam gives the same order at Troy when the army flees before Achilles: the gatekeepers are to hold the gates open until the people are inside, and then to set the close-fitted doors back in place (21.529–536).

**The gate Hector breaks.** The Trojans would not have broken “the gates of the wall and the long bar” but for Sarpedon (12.290–293). When Zeus gives Hector the glory, he carries a stone straight at the doors, “fitted close and strong, / double doors, high”, held from inside by two bars set across each other and fastened with a single bolt (12.453–456; see [[stone-throwing]] and [[hector]]). The stone breaks both hinges; the gates groan loudly all around, as the gates of heaven groan when they open of themselves for Hera's chariot (μύκον, 12.460; 5.749 = 8.393); the bars give way, and the doors split apart (12.457–462). Hector “leapt in, / his face like swift night”, and no one but a god could have stopped him “when he leapt through the gates”; the Trojans pour over the wall and “through the well-built gates themselves” (12.462–470; see [[like-night]] and [[the-fight-at-the-wall]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The breaking is remembered. Poseidon, rousing the Achaeans in Book 13, tells them in the words of the narrator's warning (12.291) that Hector “has broken the gates and the long bar” (13.124), and Hector later holds his ground “where he had first leapt in at the gates and the wall” (13.679). The gates are opened once more, by a god: Hermes brings Priam past “the towers of the ships and the ditch”, puts the sentries to sleep, “and at once opened the gates and pushed back the bars”, and brings him in with the ransom (24.443–447; see [[hermes]] and [[priam]]).
