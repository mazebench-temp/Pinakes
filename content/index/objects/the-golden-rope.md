## The challenge

Zeus has just threatened any god who helps either side with a beating or with Tartarus (8.10–17; see [[tartarus]] and [[zeus]]). The rope is his proof: "Come, gods, try it, so that you may all know" (8.18). It is a trial of strength, all the gods and goddesses hanging on one end and pulling down, Zeus alone at the other. They could not drag "Zeus, the highest counselor" from the sky to the plain (8.21–22; see [[most-high]]). He, if he pulled in earnest, would haul them up "earth itself and sea itself with you", bind the rope around a peak of Olympus, and leave everything hanging in midair (8.23–26; see [[olympus]]). μετήορα, "aloft", is used elsewhere in Homer only of chariots bounding up off the ground in the race (23.369). The point is stated last: "So far am I above the gods, and above men" (8.27).

σειρή is a rope or cord. In the Odyssey it is the plaited rope with which Melanthius is hoisted up a pillar to the rafters (*Odyssey* 22.175, 22.192). Here it is golden, the metal of the gods' possessions, like the gold Zeus puts on and the whip he takes up for his drive to Ida a few lines later (8.43–44). No rope is hung: the challenge is a boast that no one takes up.

## In the poem

The challenge belongs with the stories of the gods' early days, which show Zeus' power tested and confirmed. Once Hera, Poseidon and Athena tried to put him in bonds, and Thetis brought Briareus to sit beside him (1.396–406; see [[binding-of-zeus]]). He in turn hung Hera from the sky with two anvils at her feet and "a golden bond, unbreakable" on her hands (δεσμὸν … χρύσεον ἄρρηκτον, 15.18–21), and the gods could not set her free. Here the gods do not answer the dare. They fall silent (8.28–29), and Athena speaks for them (8.30–37; see [[athena]]). Within the book the boast is tested only in words. Hera asks Poseidon to join the gods who favor the Danaans in holding off Zeus, and he refuses, "since he is far the stronger" (8.205–211; see [[hera]] and [[poseidon]]).

In Book 13 a rope of the gods stands for the battle itself. Zeus and Poseidon, their minds set on different sides, take “the rope of mighty strife and of war common to all”, knot it crosswise and stretch it tight over both armies, “unbreakable and not to be loosed, which loosened the knees of many” (13.358–360; see [[the-rope-of-war]]). The golden rope is a dare that no god takes up. The rope of war is drawn tight, and men die under it.

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates, arguing that motion preserves things and rest destroys them, brings in the golden rope "as the crowning point": by it Homer means nothing else but the sun, and shows that as long as the heavens and the sun go round, all things among gods and men exist and are kept safe, but if the sun stood still, "as though bound", everything would be destroyed and turned upside down (*Theaetetus* 153c–d; see [[plato-golden-rope]]). Aristotle quotes 8.20–22, in the order 8.21–22, 8.20, as words that would seem well spoken to those who hold that what is wholly unmoved cannot be moved by anything (*Movement of Animals*, chapter 4; see [[aristotle-golden-rope]]). Later readers made the rope the golden chain that links heaven and earth (see [[homers-golden-chain]]).
