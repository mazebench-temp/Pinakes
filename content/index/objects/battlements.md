## Description

ἔπαλξις is a defense, and usually connected with ἀλέξω, “ward off”. On a wall it is the breastwork along the top, behind which the defenders stand and over which they fight (12.263–264, 12.424–425). The Achaean wall has battlements along its length and on its towers (12.258, 12.430; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). They are named in Homer only in Book 12 and once of Troy, where after the rout of Book 21 the Trojans cool their sweat and drink, “leaning on the fair battlements” (κεκλιμένοι καλῇσιν ἐπάλξεσιν, 22.3).

**Copings.** κρόσσαι occur only in Book 12. The Trojans “tore at the copings of the towers” (12.258) and later “climbed onto the copings, holding their sharpened spears” (12.444). The word has been explained as copings, as battlements, or as courses of stone that project like steps; the related προκρόσσας describes ships drawn up on the beach in rows (14.35; see the translation's note on 12.258–260, 12.444).

**Buttresses.** The Trojans also “pried at the jutting buttresses, which the Achaeans / had set first in the ground to be the props of the towers”, and dragged them out (12.259–261). στῆλαι are posts or pillars, the word for the pillar on a grave (11.371; see [[tomb]]); προβλῆτες, “jutting”, is said in the Odyssey of headlands (Od. 5.405, 10.89, 13.97). πρώτας has been taken as “first”, laid as the foundation, or as “in front” of the towers, and ἔχματα are props, as the stones that propped the ships are ἔχματα νηῶν (14.410).

## The fight at the battlements

**Behind the battlements.** When the Trojans pull at the battlements, the Danaans do not give ground: “they fenced the battlements with oxhides / and shot from them at the enemy as they came up under the wall” (12.263–264; see [[shield]]). The Lycian leaders climb onto them “like a dark storm” (12.375; see [[like-a-black-storm]]), and Ajax kills Epicles with a boulder that lay ready inside the wall, “uppermost beside the battlement” (12.381; see [[stone-throwing]] and [[epicles]]).

**The first breach.** Zeus rouses Sarpedon to rush at the wall “and break through the battlements” (12.308), and he does:

Teucer's arrow and Ajax's spear drive him back only “a little from the battlement” (12.406; see [[sarpedon]]). Patroclus will call him the man “who first leapt inside the wall of the Achaeans” (16.558), the words the narrator uses of Hector when he breaks the gate (12.438; see [[the-gates-of-the-wall]]).

**Neither side gives.** When the Lycians reach the wall, the battlements hold the armies apart as a boundary holds apart two men disputing it in a common field: “so the battlements kept these apart; and over them / they hacked at the oxhide shields around one another's chests” (12.424–425; see [[the-boundary-dispute]] and [[boundary-stones]]). “Everywhere the towers and battlements were spattered with the blood of men / on both sides, from Trojans and Achaeans” (12.430–431), until Zeus gives the glory to Hector (12.437).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus uses both words. The wall that the Spartans were building across the Isthmus “was already receiving its battlements” when the Athenian envoys came to Sparta (καὶ ἤδη ἐπάλξις ἐλάμβανε, 9.7.1), the last course of a wall nearing completion. And he says that the pyramid of Cheops was built “like steps, which some call κρόσσαι and others βωμίδες” (2.125.1), a sense close to one of the explanations of the Homeric κρόσσαι.
