In the Iliad λωτός is a plant of damp ground and meadows, good fodder, usually taken to be a kind of clover or trefoil. While Achilles keeps out of the war, his horses stand idle beside their chariots, “munching lotus and marsh-grown parsley” (2.775–777; [[horses]]). It grows with crocus and hyacinth in the soft grass that the earth sends up under Zeus and Hera on Ida (14.347–349), and it burns by the river with rushes and galingale when Hephaestus' fire attacks the Xanthus (21.350–351). The plains of the snow simile are λωτοῦντα (12.283), a contracted form of λωτόεις, “full of lotus,” which occurs only there. In the Odyssey Menelaus' broad plain at Sparta has “much lotus” beside galingale, wheat and barley, as he offers Telemachus horses (Od. 4.601–604).

The lotus of the Lotus-eaters seems to be another plant: a fruit, “the honey-sweet fruit of the lotus,” which makes those who eat it forget their way home (Od. 9.82–97). Odysseus' men “browse on lotus” there in the words used of Achilles' horses (λωτὸν ἐρεπτόμενοι, Od. 9.97; 2.776).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus gives the name to two other plants. In Egypt it is the lilies that grow in the Nile flood, which the Egyptians call lotus; they dry them and make bread from the poppy-like center, and the root too can be eaten (2.92.2–3). On the coast of Libya the Lotus-eaters live on the fruit of the lotus alone, a fruit the size of a mastic berry and as sweet as a date, from which they also make wine (4.177).
