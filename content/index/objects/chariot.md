ἅρμα (“chariot”) is one of several words for it. ὄχεα, used only in the plural, is another, and δίφρος is the car itself, the platform on which the two men stood. ἵπποι, “horses”, often means the team and chariot together. ἅρμα is usually connected with ἀραρίσκω (“fit together”). The Linear B tablets inventory chariots and their parts, and there the related word *a-mo* means “wheel”.

The chariot carried two men: the driver (ἡνίοχος), who held the reins, and the fighter beside him. In the Iliad the fighters ride to the battle and fight mostly on foot, while the driver keeps the chariot close behind for a quick escape. A warrior sometimes throws or thrusts his spear from the chariot, and many fall from it when they are hit. Nestor, marshaling his Pylians, tells the charioteers to keep their line and to thrust with the spear from their chariots: “this is how the men of old sacked cities and walls” (4.297–309). How far Homer's picture reflects real warfare, whether of the Bronze Age or of the poet's own time, is much debated.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The gods drive chariots of gold and bronze. Hebe fits the bronze wheels to the iron axle of Hera's chariot (5.720–723), Zeus drives his to Mount Ida (8.41–50), and Poseidon's runs over the sea without wetting its axle (13.23–30). At Patroclus' funeral games the first contest, with the richest prizes, is the chariot race (23.262–650).
