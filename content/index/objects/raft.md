## The word

σχεδίη is properly the feminine of the adjective σχέδιος, used as a noun, as ὑγρή, “the waters”, and ἀμβροσίη are (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.97, where it stands in his list of such words). Merry gives two explanations of the adjective. It may mean “made of joined planks”, from σχεδόν, “close”, a sense to which the epithet πολύδεσμος, “of many bindings”, points (see [[of-many-bindings]]), and the verse in which Odysseus hammers the raft together “with pegs and fastenings” (*Odyssey* 5.248); a scholium explains it so, as a craft fixed with pegs. Or it may be a vessel “hastily made”, as another scholium explains it, with the later σχεδιάζειν, “do offhand” (Merry on 5.33). Homer has the noun only of this one craft, in the book in which it is built and wrecked (*Odyssey* 5) and in Odysseus' account of it to Arete (*Odyssey* 7.264, 7.274). The translation renders it “raft” throughout, and πολύδεσμος “of many bindings” (*Odyssey* 5.33, 5.338, 7.264; see the translation's row σχεδίη, πολύδεσμος and its note on *Odyssey* 5.33).

## Raft or boat

What Odysseus builds is more than a raft. Its breadth is measured by a ship's: as wide as the floor of a broad freight ship that a man skilled in carpentry marks out (*Odyssey* 5.249–251; see [[ships]]). It has a deck set up on close-set ribs, long side planks, a mast with its yard, a steering oar, a fence of osier withes against the waves, and sails made fast with braces, halyards and sheets (*Odyssey* 5.252–260; see [[deck]], [[mast]], [[steering-oar]] and [[sails]]). No oars or rowing benches are named: one man sails it, sitting at the steering oar (*Odyssey* 5.270–271). Odysseus himself measures it against real ships when Calypso bids him cross the sea on it: “over it not even balanced ships / cross, swift-sailing, glorying in the wind of Zeus” (*Odyssey* 5.175–176; see [[balanced-ships]] and [[swift-sailing-ships]]). The contrast, Merry says, is between good ships properly built and such a one as Odysseus might be able to make for himself (on 5.175).

Merry, treating the building in his appendix on the Homeric ship, holds that it involves fewer incongruities to take the σχεδίη as a real ship or boat, more or less like the ordinary ship of the time, than to describe it as a raft, or rather a flat-bottomed box, which was the view of Brieger, accepted by Hentze in his edition of Ameis' Odyssey and by Autenrieth in his Homeric dictionary. He warns against pressing the story for impossibilities: it comes in the middle of a marvelous tale, so that one need not ask how a man working alone, without a shipwright's tools, made a vessel ready for sea in four days, and the poet may elaborate the idea as he goes, so that the details at the end are out of keeping with the simplicity of the beginning (Merry, Appendix I §1). The verb of the measuring decides the matter for him: τορνώσεται, “marks out”, is the striking of a curve with a pencil on a string, which points to curved sides and “disposes at once of the square-box plan” (Merry on 5.249; Appendix I §6). The English keeps “raft”.

## Elsewhere in the Odyssey

Odysseus tells Arete of the raft without saying that he built it. Calypso, he says, sent him off “on a raft of many bindings”, gave him bread and sweet wine, clothed him in immortal garments and sent a fair wind; on the eighteenth day he saw the mountains of Scheria, but Poseidon roused the winds against him, bound up his path and stirred up the sea, and the waves would not let him ride on the raft as he groaned, until the storm scattered it and he swam through the gulf (*Odyssey* 7.264–277, a plain rendering; see [[calypso]] and [[poseidon]]). The verses of the fair wind and of the seventeen days at sea are, with a single word changed, those of *Odyssey* 5 (*Odyssey* 7.266–268, as 5.268, 5.278–279).

## Outside Homer

Plato took the makeshift craft for an image of human reasoning. In the *Phaedo* Simmias says that on such questions as the survival of the soul a man must learn or find out how things are, or, if that is impossible, take the best and least refutable of human accounts and, carried on it “as on a raft” (ὥσπερ ἐπὶ σχεδίας), sail through life at hazard, unless he could make the crossing more safely and with less risk on a firmer vessel, some divine account (*Phaedo* 85c–d; see [[plato]]). Merry quotes the passage for the sense “hastily made” (on 5.33).

Aelian's list of the parts of the Odyssey once sung on their own names “the events about the raft” (see [[the-title-of-odyssey-5]]).
