## The word

μεσόδμη is formed from μέσος, “middle”, and the root of δέμω, “build”, the root of δόμος, “house”: “the thing built in the middle”. Homer uses it twice of a ship and twice of a house. On the ship it is the hollow box in which the mast stands: “The mast of fir, within the hollow mast-box, / they raised and set up, and made it fast with forestays” (*Odyssey* 2.424–425, again at 15.289–290; see [[mast]] and [[forestays]]). How it was built the poem does not say. W. W. Merry, giving weight to κοίλη, which in Homer always describes something that encloses a space, pictures a box of three sides standing upright, open toward the stern: when the mast was down it lay along the ship toward the stern, free of the box except at its foot, and when it was raised it stood in the box, enclosed on three sides for some two feet of its height, with a pin through two sides to close the open one, as on the barges of the Thames and the Medway in his own day. He prefers this to the common view of a cross-timber with a hole, into which a heavy mast could hardly have been dropped with the ship in a seaway, and which would have been called τρητός, “pierced”, not κοίλη (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix I §12). He takes the step of the mast, ἱστοπέδη, in which Odysseus stands bound upright to hear the Sirens (*Odyssey* 12.51), for virtually the same thing (see [[mast]]). The Iliad names the other end of the work, the crutch in which the lowered mast is laid when the ship comes into harbor (1.434; see [[mast-crutch]]).

In the house the plural μεσόδμαι stands with the walls, the beams of fir and the pillars of the hall. Telemachus sees them shine as if with blazing fire when Athena goes before him with a golden lamp (*Odyssey* 19.33–40), and [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]], in his vision of the suitors' death, sees the walls and the μεσόδμαι spattered with blood (*Odyssey* 20.351–357; see [[house]]). The renderings of Books 19 and 20 of the Odyssey are plain renderings, not the translation.
