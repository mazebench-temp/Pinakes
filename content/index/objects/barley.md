κριθή is barley, in Homer usually in the plural, κριθαί, or in the short, uninflected form κρῖ. κρῖ λευκόν, “white barley”, is a fixed phrase, found six times (5.196, 8.564, 20.496; *Odyssey* 4.41, 4.604, 12.358). Barley grows with wheat: reapers drive their swaths “through a rich man's field / of wheat or barley” (11.68–69), and in the Odyssey the earth bears the two together, unsown in the land of the Cyclopes and in the land of a just king (*Odyssey* 9.108–111, 19.111–113, plain renderings; see [[wheat]]).

It is fodder for horses, with spelt: Pandarus' horses and the Trojan horses at the watch fires munch “white barley and spelt” (5.196, 8.564; see [[spelt]] and [[horses]]). It is threshed by oxen: Achilles' horses trample the dead and their shields as bulls yoked on a well-built threshing floor tread out white barley (20.495–499; see [[bulls-on-the-threshing-floor]]). And it is needed for sacrifice: on the island of the Sun Odysseus' companions, who have no white barley left on board, use tender oak leaves in its place (*Odyssey* 12.357–358, a plain rendering). The meal ground from barley is ἄλφιτα (see [[barley-meal]]), and the whole grains scattered at a sacrifice are the οὐλαί or οὐλοχύται (see [[barley-grains]]).

## Outside Homer

[[Aristotle]] takes κρῖ as an example of a word from which something has been taken away, κρῖ for κριθή, as δῶ for δῶμα (*Poetics* 1458a3–5; see [[hyphairemenon]]). Herodotus says that the Egyptians, who think it a disgrace to live on wheat and barley, drink a wine made from barley, since they have no vines (*Histories* 2.36.2, 2.77.4; see [[spelt]] and [[herodotus]]).
