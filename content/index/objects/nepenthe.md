## In Homer

The drug is named once, at the supper in Menelaus' house, when the company has wept for Odysseus and for Antilochus and has turned back to the meal:

{{quote:227037ed-580c-5c33-951c-2aae4db62a94}}

It is an Egyptian gift, one of many drugs that Helen had from the land where the earth bears them most:

{{quote:26e5ad21-3be0-5751-803f-bcab26f849e0}}

The giver is Polydamna, the wife of Thon (see [[polydamna]] and [[thon]]), and the land is the Egypt where Menelaus gathered his wealth, where Polybus and his wife Alcandre gave him and Helen their gifts in Thebes, and where he was held back on his way home (*Odyssey* 4.81–85, 4.125–132, 4.351–586; see [[egypt]] and [[thebes-in-egypt]]). Its people are healers “of the stock of Paeëon”, the gods' physician (*Odyssey* 4.231–232; see [[paeeon]] and [[healing]]). W. W. Merry notes that the mention of the earth suggests that the substance was a plant, and that βάλε, “cast”, rather than χέε, “poured”, points to a solid and not a liquid (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.220); μεμιγμένα, “intermixed”, means that the wholesome and the harmful drugs grow together (Merry on 4.230).

The drug works only in the wine. Whoever swallowed it, “once it was mixed in the mixing bowl” (ἐπὴν κρητῆρι μιγείη, *Odyssey* 4.222), would shed no tear for that day: Eustathius, as Merry reports him, took the condition to mean that the drug was not νηπενθές at all unless it was mixed in the bowl (Merry on 4.222; see [[mixing-bowl]] and [[wine]]). In Ithaca the suitors had imagined the opposite, that Telemachus might fetch from Ephyra “drugs that destroy life, / and put them in the mixing bowl, and destroy us all” (*Odyssey* 2.329–330).

## The words

νηπενθής is “without grief”, from the negative prefix νη- and πένθος (see [[penthos]]); ἄχολος, “without anger”, from χόλος (see [[cholos]]); ἐπίληθος, “bringing forgetfulness”, from the root of λήθω, “escape notice”, and λανθάνομαι, “forget”. All three are found only here in Homer (see the translation's row νηπενθής, ἄχολος, ἐπίληθος and its note on *Odyssey* 4.220–232). ἐπίληθον, added without a conjunction, explains the two adjectives before it (Merry on 4.221). Aristarchus accented it as an adjective, ἐπίληθον, while Ptolemy of Ascalon took it as a participle, ἐπιλῆθον, wrongly in Eustathius' judgment (Merry's critical note on 4.221).

The Odyssey has other drugs. Ilus would not give Odysseus a man-slaying drug for his arrows (*Odyssey* 1.259–264; see [[odysseus-seeks-poison]] and [[ilus]]); Circe mixes baneful drugs into the food she serves Odysseus' companions “so that they might utterly forget their native land” (*Odyssey* 10.234–236, a plain rendering; see [[circe]]), and Hermes gives Odysseus a good drug against her (*Odyssey* 10.287–306). Circe's drugs make men forget their native land, Helen's makes them forget their grief.

## Outside Homer

Herodotus, who tells the Egyptian priests' story that Helen came to Proteus in Egypt, thought that Homer knew it and let it go because it was less fitting for epic than the story he used. As evidence he quotes the lines on the drugs Polydamna gave her (*Odyssey* 4.227–230) and Menelaus' words that the gods held him back in Egypt (*Odyssey* 4.351–352; Herodotus 2.116.1–5; see [[herodotus]]). Strabo, describing the coast east of Alexandria toward Canopus, reports that there was once a city Thonis there, named for the king who received Menelaus and Helen as guests, and quotes the verse on Polydamna, the wife of Thon (*Geography* 17.1.16; see [[strabo]]).

What the drug was is an old question. Merry reports that Dioscorides, in the chapter he cites as 4.28, ascribes a like effect to bugloss, which put into wine is thought to cheer; that Sprengel and others, with Mure, took it for opium; and that Dr. Hayman quotes Sir Henry Halford's view that it was henbane, still used in Greece and Turkey. Older writers, Plutarch (*Table Talk* 1.1.4) and Macrobius (*Saturnalia* 7.1), made an allegory of it, taking the drug for Helen's bewitching eloquence (Merry on 4.220; see [[plutarch]]).
