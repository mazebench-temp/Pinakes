ἑανός is a woman's fine robe, often a goddess's. The noun is usually connected with ἕννυμι, “clothe”. An adjective ἑανός, “soft, fine”, describes Athena's robe (5.734) and the tin of Achilles' greaves (18.613), and whether it is the same word is disputed.

Book 3 has two. Aphrodite comes to Helen in the likeness of an old wool-worker from Lacedaemon, and “took hold of her fragrant robe and plucked at it with her hand” (νεκταρέου ἑανοῦ ἐτίναξε λαβοῦσα, 3.385), the gesture of the old servant she pretends to be. νεκτάρεος, “nectar-sweet”, is formed from [[nectar]] (see [[fragrant]]). When the goddess has frightened her into obeying, Helen “went, wrapping herself in her shining white robe, in silence, and escaped the notice of all the Trojan women” (3.419–420). She had veiled herself to go out to the wall (3.141; see [[veil]]). Now she covers herself to go back unseen.

Helen is also τανύπεπλος, “of the long robes” (3.228; see [[long-robed]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Hera, dressing to beguile Zeus, puts on an ambrosial robe that Athena had made for her and worked with many figures (14.178–179).
