ἑανός is a woman's fine robe, often a goddess's. The noun is usually connected with ἕννυμι, “clothe”. The commoner word is πέπλος, the large woven robe that Athena lets fall on her father's floor and that Hecuba offers to Athena, which also served to cover chariots and the bones of the dead (see the notes on *Iliad* 5, 6 and 24). An adjective ἑανός describes Athena's robe (5.734), the linen cloth over Patroclus' body (18.352) and the tin of Achilles' greaves (18.613); it means “soft, fine”, and whether it is the same word as the noun is disputed (see [[heanos]]).

*Iliad* 3 has two. Aphrodite comes to Helen in the likeness of an old wool-worker from Lacedaemon, and “took hold of her fragrant robe and plucked at it with her hand” (νεκταρέου ἑανοῦ ἐτίναξε λαβοῦσα, 3.385), the gesture of the old servant she pretends to be. νεκτάρεος, “nectar-sweet”, is formed from [[nectar]] (see [[fragrant]]). When the goddess has frightened her into obeying, Helen “went, wrapping herself in her shining white robe, in silence, and escaped the notice of all the Trojan women” (3.419–420). She had veiled herself to go out to the wall (3.141; see [[veil]]). Now she covers herself to go back unseen.

Helen is also τανύπεπλος, “of the long robes” (3.228; see [[long-robed]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Hera, dressing to beguile Zeus, puts on an ambrosial robe that Athena had made for her and worked with many figures (14.178–179).

In the Odyssey πέπλοι are among the woven things of a house: Nausicaa's wagon carries them to the washing (*Odyssey* 6.38; see [[washing-troughs]]), and in the hall of Alcinous fine cloths, “the work of women”, are thrown over the chairs along the walls (*Odyssey* 7.96–97; see [[throne]]). At Sparta Helen takes from her chests the largest and most richly embroidered of the robes she has made herself, in the verses of Hecuba's robe for Athena, and gives it to Telemachus for his bride to wear (*Odyssey* 15.104–130; *Iliad* 6.289–295; see [[like-a-star]] and [[the-robe-for-athena]]). Among the suitors' gifts to Penelope is a great robe, richly worked, with twelve golden brooches (*Odyssey* 18.292–294, a plain rendering; see [[brooch]]).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus quotes the verses of Hecuba's robes, the work of Sidonian women whom Alexander brought from Sidon on the voyage on which he brought Helen home, to show that Homer knew of Paris' wandering to Sidon (Herodotus 2.116.2–3; *Iliad* 6.289–292; see [[herodotus]] and [[sidon]]), and Strabo sets the same verses beside the silver bowl of Phaedimus as evidence of Sidonian craftsmanship and of Helen's stay among the Sidonians with Alexander (*Geography* 1.2.33; see [[strabo]] and [[mixing-bowl]]). In Virgil Helen's finery outlives Troy: among the gifts saved from the ruins of Ilium that Aeneas sends to Dido are a mantle stiff with figures and gold and a veil bordered with saffron acanthus, *ornatus Argivae Helenae*, which Helen brought from Mycenae when she sailed for Troy, the gift of her mother Leda (*Aeneid* 1.647–652; see [[virgil]] and [[leda]]). And Andromache, parting from Aeneas at Buthrotum, gives Ascanius garments embroidered with gold and a Phrygian cloak to be *manuum … monimenta mearum*, remembrances of her hands (*Aeneid* 3.482–488; see [[virgils-andromache]] and [[ascanius]]), in words close to Helen's at her gift to Telemachus (*Odyssey* 15.125–126).
