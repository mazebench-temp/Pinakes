## The words

εἷμα, a garment, in the plural clothes, and ἐσθής, clothing taken together, are both formed from the root of ἕννυμι, “clothe”. The garments have their own names and entries: the tunic worn next to the skin, the cloak and the mantle over it, the woman's robe, girdle and veil (see [[tunic]], [[cloak]], [[robe]], [[girdle]] and [[veil]]).

## In Homer

**The wealth of a house.** Clothes are made by the women of a house and kept in it. When Hector is dead Andromache tells him that “there are clothes laid up for you in the halls, / fine and lovely, made by the hands of women”, and that she will burn them all, since he will not lie in them (*Iliad* 22.510–514; see [[andromache]]). The storeroom of Odysseus holds “clothing in chests” with the gold and the oil (*Odyssey* 2.339; see [[the-storeroom-of-odysseus]] and [[chest]]), and if Odysseus came home, Telemachus says, the suitors would all pray to be nimbler of foot “rather than richer in gold and clothing” (*Odyssey* 1.163–165). Clothes are kept clean by washing at the troughs by running water, at Troy in time of peace and on Scheria (*Iliad* 22.153–156; *Odyssey* 6.85–98; see [[washing-troughs]]).

**The guest's due.** A guest is bathed and dressed (*Odyssey* 3.467, 4.50; see [[bathing]] and [[xenia]]), and clothes are among the gifts of hosts: the Phaeacians will give Odysseus “bronze and gold in plenty, and clothing” (*Odyssey* 5.38), and Calypso clothes him in fragrant garments that weigh him down in the sea (*Odyssey* 5.264, 5.321, 5.372). Nausicaa tells the naked stranger that he “shall not lack clothing” (*Odyssey* 6.192).

**Gods, the dead, beauty and shame.** The gods' clothes are immortal or golden: Hebe dresses the healed Ares “in lovely clothes” (*Iliad* 5.905), Apollo puts “immortal clothing” on the body of Sarpedon (*Iliad* 16.670, 16.680), and on the shield Ares and Athena lead the army “clad in golden clothing” (*Iliad* 18.517). The shade of Patroclus comes to Achilles wearing “such clothes about its body as he had worn” (*Iliad* 23.67), and Priam's sons sit about their father and soak their clothes with tears (*Iliad* 24.161–162). Fine clothes go with beauty: Aphrodite tells Helen that Paris is in his chamber, “shining with beauty and fine clothes”, as if he came from a dance and not from a duel (*Iliad* 3.390–394; see [[paris]]). To strip a man of them is to shame him: Odysseus threatens to strip Thersites of “your cloak and your tunic, which cover your nakedness” (*Iliad* 2.261–262; see [[thersites]]).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle uses clothes for the fitness of style: “as a scarlet cloak suits a young man, so what suits an old one? (For the same clothing is not appropriate to both.)” (*Rhetoric* 1405a13–14; see [[aristotle]]). Among the things that make suffering seem near and pitiable he counts the gestures, voice and dress of those who act it out, and signs such as “the clothes of those who have suffered” (*Rhetoric* 1386a32–b2).
