## Meaning

τέρμα is a limit or end: the post at the far end of a course, around which the horses turn, ἀεθλοφόροι περὶ τέρματα μώνυχες ἵπποι, “prize-winning single-hoofed horses round the turning posts” (22.162; see [[racehorses-at-the-turning-post]]), and in the Odyssey the marks of a throw, which Athena sets for Odysseus' discus (ἔθηκε δὲ τέρματʼ Ἀθήνη, Od. 8.193). νύσσα is a post. In the chariot race of *Iliad* 23 it is the turning post (23.332, 23.338, 23.344), and in the footrace and in the games of the Phaeacians the line from which the runners start, τοῖσι δʼ ἀπὸ νύσσης τέτατο δρόμος, “for them the running was strained to the full from the post” (23.758 = Od. 8.121). Walter Leaf, quoting Merry and Riddell on the Odyssey, compares the English “scratch” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.758).

The chariot race at Patroclus' funeral games is run out from the ships across the plain, around the post and back. Leaf takes it as a single double course, with no earlier laps, and notes that the narrative, after all Nestor's advice, says nothing of the turn itself (on 23.373).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates asks the rhapsode Ion to recite what Nestor says to his son, “advising him to be careful about the turn in the horse race in honor of Patroclus” (περὶ τὴν καμπὴν ἐν τῇ ἱπποδρομίᾳ τῇ ἐπὶ Πατρόκλῳ), and Ion recites 23.335–340, with ἐυξέστῳ ἐνὶ δίφρῳ, “in the well-polished chariot”, where our text has ἐϋπλέκτῳ, “well-woven”. Who would know better whether Homer speaks rightly here, Socrates asks, a doctor or a charioteer? A charioteer, Ion answers, because he has the art (*Ion* 537a–c; see [[nestors-lesson-in-driving]], [[plato]] and [[chariot]]).

Aristotle names Nestor's words about the stump, “part of which rots in the rain”, among the difficulties that Hippias of Thasos solved by an accent (*Poetics* 1461a21–23; see [[the-stump-that-rots]]).

In Sophocles' *Electra* the false report of Orestes' death is a chariot race at Delphi lost at the turning post (see [[the-false-report-of-orestes-death]]). Leaf compares that race for the verb of driving close, ἐγχρίμπτω, and for the danger of the stone (on 23.334 and 23.340).
