δέρμα λέοντος, “the skin of a lion”, occurs twice in Homer, both times at the end of a verse, and both times the next verse describes it in the same words: αἴθωνος μεγάλοιο ποδηνεκές, “a great tawny lion, reaching to his feet” (10.23–24, 10.177–178). αἴθων, “tawny” or “blazing”, is the lion's color in the similes too (11.548, 18.161; see [[tawny]]). ποδηνεκής, “reaching to the feet”, is said elsewhere only of the shield of Periphetes of Mycenae, which reached to his feet, “a wall against javelins”, and tripped him (15.645–647). Agamemnon's skin is also δαφοινός, “blood-red” (10.23), the word for the serpent of the portent at Aulis and for jackals (2.308, 11.474; see [[blood-red]]).

The skin takes the place of a cloak. Agamemnon puts it on after his tunic and sandals, in place of the great mantle he threw around him when he dressed in Book 2 (10.21–24; 2.42–44; see [[dressing-scene]]), and Nestor, dressing in the same verses as Agamemnon, puts on a crimson cloak (10.131–134; see [[cloak]]). Diomedes, woken on the ox hide where he has slept beside his armor, throws his lion skin over his shoulders before he goes to rouse the other chiefs (10.155, 10.177–179). Each man then takes up his spear, the last piece of an arming (see [[arming-scene]]). Menelaus puts on a leopard's skin in the same night, and the Trojan spy Dolon a wolf's (10.29–30, 10.334; see [[leopard-skin]] and [[wolf-skin]]).

Diomedes, who wears one, is twice compared to a lion in the same night: he and Odysseus go out “like two lions through the black night” (10.297), and he falls on the sleeping Thracians as a lion falls on flocks that have no herdsman (10.485–488; see [[lion]] and [[lion-among-the-flocks]]).

## Outside Homer

The lion skin of later poetry and art is Heracles'. Homer's Heracles is an archer: his shade in the underworld holds a bare bow with an arrow on the string, and wears a golden baldric worked with bears, boars and lions (Od. 11.601–614). The Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles* still arms him like a Homeric hero, with greaves, corselet, sword, quiver, spear, helmet and shield, and no lion skin (122–139). In Euripides the skin of the lion of Zeus' grove is his armor. The chorus sings how he covered his head with the beast's gaping jaws (*Heracles* 359–363), and Megara remembers how he threw over his son the lion's skin “in which he armed himself” (465–466). Herodotus tells a Greek story from the Black Sea in which Heracles, overtaken by winter and frost in Scythia, drew the lion's skin over himself and fell asleep, and while he slept the mares of his chariot, put out to graze, vanished by some divine chance (4.8.3).
