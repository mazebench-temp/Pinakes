Homer always uses the plural, πέδιλα (“sandals”). The word comes from the root of πούς, ποδός (“foot”), like πέδη (“fetter”). Sandals were soles tied on under the foot: the swineherd Eumaeus makes his own, cutting and fitting a well-colored oxhide (*Odyssey* 14.23–24).

One verse serves whenever someone puts them on: ποσσὶ δʼ ὑπὸ λιπαροῖσιν ἐδήσατο καλὰ πέδιλα, “and under his shining feet he bound his fine sandals”. λιπαρός means “sleek, glistening”, as with oil. The verse belongs to scenes of dressing, not of arming. The arming scenes begin with the greaves and say nothing of footwear (3.330, 11.17, 16.131, 19.369).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same verse binds sandals on Agamemnon and Nestor when they rise in the night in Book 10 (10.22, 10.132), on Hera as she dresses to beguile Zeus (14.186), and on Telemachus on the morning of the assembly of Ithaca (*Odyssey* 2.4).

The gods' sandals are golden and immortal. When Hermes sets out, he binds under his feet “his fine sandals, immortal, golden, which carried him over the water and over the boundless earth with the breath of the wind” (24.340–342; *Odyssey* 5.44–46). Athena's are described in the same words (*Odyssey* 1.96–98). Homer does not say that they have wings.
