θρύον is the rush, a plant of wet ground. Homer names it only once. When Hephaestus has burned the elms, the willows and the tamarisks along the Xanthus, the low plants of the bank burn too:

{{quote:ac3d0b83-6290-5fac-861a-ed50a5daeb5e}}

The rushes grow with the lotus and the galingale “in plenty about the fair streams of the river” (21.352; see [[lotus]], [[galingale]], [[scamander]] and [[fire]]). Walter Leaf reports Barker Webb's observation, by way of Schliemann, that all the trees and plants named in these verses are still found in the plain of Troy, so far as they can be identified (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.350).

The word gave its name to a place. Thryum, Θρύον, is a town of Nestor's kingdom at the ford of the Alpheus (2.592), and in Nestor's story it is Thryoessa (11.711), Θρυόεσσα, “rushy” (see [[thryum]]).

The other word for the rush, σχοῖνος, is also found once in Homer. When Odysseus has let Ino's veil go into the river of Scheria, “he, withdrawing from the river, / sank down among the rushes, and kissed the grain-giving earth” (*Odyssey* 5.462–463; see [[the-river-of-scheria]]); the singular, σχοίνῳ, stands for the bed of rushes by the water. The Boeotian Schoenus is named from it (2.497; see [[schoenus]]), and the Asopus is βαθύσχοινος, “deep in rushes” (4.383; see [[deep-in-rushes]]).

## Outside Homer

[[strabo|Strabo]] reports how Demetrius of Scepsis explained the cults of Demeter, Kore and Hades in Triphylia, by the Alpheus: the land is fruitful, but it also breeds blight in the grain, and rushes, so that instead of great harvests there are often failures (*Geography* 8.3.15).
