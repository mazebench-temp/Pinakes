## Meaning

κύανος is a material, dark blue, which a craftsman sets into metal or wood. The noun occurs only three times in Homer, always in the genitive, “of dark κύανος” (μέλανος κυάνοιο, 11.24, 11.35) or “a coping of κύανος” (Odyssey 7.87). The adjective κυάνεος, “of κύανος”, is used of things made of it, and far more often of anything dark: the brows of Zeus (1.528), a cloud (5.345, 23.188), Thetis' mourning veil, blacker than any garment (24.93–94), and in compounds the dark prows of ships and the dark hair of Poseidon (see [[dark-brows]]). The translation renders the noun “dark blue enamel” and the adjective, where it describes a thing made of it, “dark blue”.

What the substance was is not certain. It has been taken for blue glass paste, for the blue frit called Egyptian blue, and for lapis lazuli, the blue stone that the cheaper materials imitated. The word has no Greek etymology. It is already Mycenaean: the Linear B inventories of furniture from Pylos describe pieces inlaid with *ku-wa-no*. Blue glass paste was used for inlay in the Mycenaean palaces, as in the alabaster frieze from the palace at Tiryns.

## In Homer

The Iliad names κύανος only in Book 11, and most of it is on Agamemnon. His corselet has ten bands of κύανος beside twelve of gold and twenty of tin, and on it serpents of κύανος rise toward the neck (11.24–28; see [[corselet]]). His shield has twenty bosses of white tin round a central one of κύανος, and a serpent of κύανος on its silver strap (11.33–40; see [[shield]]). The table that Hecamede sets before Nestor and Machaon is κυανόπεζα, “with feet of dark blue enamel” (11.629; see [[table]] and [[with-feet-of-dark-enamel]]). The three materials of the corselet, gold, κύανος and tin, return in the vineyard on the shield that Hephaestus makes for Achilles, a vineyard of gold with a ditch of κύανος and a fence of tin around it (18.561–565).

In the Odyssey the walls of Alcinous' palace are bronze, “and around them a coping of κύανος”, with doors of gold, doorposts and lintel of silver, and a golden handle (Odyssey 7.86–90). Like Agamemnon's armor, it is a work of precious materials laid side by side.
