## The word

θρῆνυς is a footstool. It goes with the high seat of honor, the θρόνος, and with the chair, κλισμός (see [[throne]]). A verse of seating ends, “and a footstool was under her feet” (ὑπὸ δὲ θρῆνυς ποσὶν ἦεν), when Charis seats Thetis on a silver-studded throne in the house of Hephaestus (18.389–390), and four times in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 1.131, 4.136, 10.315, 10.367). Once in the Iliad the word names a bench in a ship: Ajax, driven back by missiles at the ships, gives ground “onto the seven-foot bench” and leaves the deck (θρῆνυν ἐφʼ ἑπταπόδην, 15.729).

## In Homer

Hera's first offer to Sleep for lulling Zeus is a throne made by her son: “a fine throne, imperishable forever, / golden; and Hephaestus, my son, the god lame in both legs, / will make it with skill, and set a footstool beneath it for your feet, / on which you may rest your shining feet at the banquet” (14.238–241; see [[doron]], [[hephaestus]] and [[sleep]]). The footstool makes the gift a seat of honor at the feasts of the gods. Sleep refuses it (14.243–262).

In the Odyssey the footstool belongs to the welcome of guests and to its abuse. Telemachus seats the disguised Athena on a throne with a footstool (*Odyssey* 1.130–131), and the craftsman Icmalius made Penelope's chair “and set a footstool under it for the feet”, fastened to it, in the words of Hera's promise (ὑπὸ θρῆνυν ποσὶν ἧκε, *Odyssey* 19.56–58). Antinous takes from under his table the footstool “on which he rested his shining feet as he feasted”, again the words of Hera's promise (ᾧ ῥʼ ἔπεχεν λιπαροὺς πόδας εἰλαπινάζων, *Odyssey* 17.409–410; 14.241), and shows it to the beggar; then he throws it and hits him on the right shoulder (*Odyssey* 17.462–463), and Penelope, hearing of it, tells of the blow with a footstool (θρήνυι, *Odyssey* 17.504). The footstool of the gods' banquet is the suitors' weapon.
