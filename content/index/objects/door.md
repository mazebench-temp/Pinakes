## The door and its fastening

θύρη is the door, usually in the plural, θύραι, since a door commonly had two leaves (δικλίδες, *Odyssey* 2.345, 17.268). Before the house is the πρόθυρον, the doorway or gateway of the courtyard, where a visitor stands and waits to be received (see [[threshold]] and [[xenia]]), and inside, the doors of the hall and of the chambers. The leaves are hung on doorposts (σταθμοί; “he fitted close doors to the doorposts”, 14.167).

A door is shut by a bolt, κληΐς, or by a bar, ὀχεύς or ἐπιβλής. W. W. Merry, on the chamber door of Odysseus' house, explains the fastening of a single door: a bolt, probably of wood, slid on the inside into a hole in the doorpost; a strap tied to it passed out through a slit in the door, so that someone outside could pull the door shut by its handle, a hook (κορώνη), and then draw the bolt home by the strap; to secure the door, the loose end of the strap was tied round the hook. To open it again from outside, the strap was untied, and the key, also called κληΐς, which Merry takes for a hooked instrument, was put through the slit to draw the bolt back (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.441). So Penelope, going to the storeroom for the bow, loosed the strap from the hook, put in the key and knocked back the bars, and the doors roared as a bull roars in a meadow (*Odyssey* 21.42–50).

## In the Iliad

The doors of the Iliad are those of temples, chambers and huts. Hecuba is to take the robe to Athena's temple and “open with the key the doors of the sacred house” (6.89), and Theano opens them (6.298; see [[robe]]). Hephaestus built Hera a chamber with close-fitting doors and “a secret bolt, which no other god could open” (14.166–169; see [[the-deception-of-zeus]]). Phoenix broke “the closely fitted doors of the chamber” to escape from his father's house (9.474–477; see [[the-flight-of-phoenix]]). Achilles' hut was shut by a single bar of fir that it took three Achaeans to drive home and three to draw back, but Achilles worked it alone, and no sentry, he tells Priam, could easily push back the bar of his doors (24.452–456, 24.566–567; see [[hut]]). Priam imagines the dogs he reared at his table tearing his body at his outer doors (22.66–71; see [[priam]]).

## In the Odyssey

The doors of Odysseus' house are watched and shut. The suitors play before them (*Odyssey* 1.107), and before the killing of the suitors Eurycleia locks the doors of the women's hall and Philoetius slips out to bar the gates of the courtyard, binding them with a ship's cable (*Odyssey* 21.380–391). Penelope's storeroom, with its oak threshold, its doorposts and its shining doors, is opened with a bronze key with an ivory handle (*Odyssey* 21.5–7, 21.42–50).
