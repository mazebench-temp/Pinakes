# ἴου (Od. 5.72), the only form of the flower in Homer; not ἰόν, ἰοῦ "arrow".
grc: [ἴου]
en: [violets]
---

ἴον, the violet, is named once in Homer, in the description of Calypso's cave: “And all around, soft meadows of violets and parsley / were in bloom. There even an immortal, if he came, / would gaze at the sight and delight his mind” (*Odyssey* 5.72–74; see [[parsley]], [[vine]] and [[calypsos-cave]]). λειμῶνες … ἴου ἠδὲ σελίνου, “meadows of violets and parsley”, has the genitive of what the meadows are made of, as a grove of poplars is αἰγείρων ἄλσος (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.72). Merry takes ἴον to be the ordinary blue scented violet, ἴον μέλαν, “the dark violet”, as distinguished from the λευκόιον, the white (on 5.72).

**The variant σίου.** The ancients disputed the flower. The reading σίου, “of marsh parsley”, is said by Athenaeus to be the emendation of King Ptolemy Euergetes, who insisted that the violet was out of place here; Eustathius, without telling where it came from, says that some wrote σίου and that many of the ancients liked it (Merry's critical note on 5.72; see the translation's note on 5.72). If the reading had any value, Merry thinks, it would refer to the *Sium nodiflorum*, a marsh plant that grows freely in Greece (on 5.72).

The flower gives the sea one of its epithets, ἰοειδής, “violet-looking”, from ἴον and εἶδος: Hermes steps onto Calypso's island out of “the violet sea” (*Odyssey* 5.56; see [[violet-sea]]). The wool on Helen's distaff is ἰοδνεφής, “violet-dark” (*Odyssey* 4.135).
