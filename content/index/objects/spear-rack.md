grc: [δουροδόκης]

## The word

δουροδόκη is a “spear-holder”, from δόρυ, “spear”, and δέχομαι, “receive”, and it is found only here in Homer. When Telemachus takes the stranger's spear at the doorway, he stands it inside the house “against a tall pillar / inside a polished spear-rack, where many other / spears of stout-hearted Odysseus stood” (*Odyssey* 1.127–129; see [[spear]] and [[house]]). How it was made the poem does not say. W. W. Merry reports two views. Eustathius took it for a fluting cut in the pillar, in which the shafts rested: the simplest explanation, Merry thinks, though it suits the epithet ἐΰξοος, “polished”, badly. Rumpf took it for a rack against the wall between two engaged columns at the entrance of the hall, because Telemachus, coming home, “carried the spear and stood it against a tall pillar” and then crossed the stone threshold into the hall (*Odyssey* 17.29–30; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.128).

## The spears of Odysseus

The spears that stand in the rack are the arms of the absent master, kept in the hall where the suitors feast. When Odysseus is home, he tells Telemachus to carry all the weapons of war that lie in the halls into an inner corner of the high chamber, and to tell the suitors that he has put them away out of the smoke, since they are no longer like the arms Odysseus left when he went to Troy, and that a god has warned him that the men might quarrel in their wine and wound each other, “for iron of itself draws a man on” (*Odyssey* 16.281–298, repeated at 19.4–13). That night father and son carry out the helmets, bossed shields and pointed spears, while Athena lights their way with a golden lamp (*Odyssey* 19.31–34; see [[torches]]). Only two spears, two swords and two shields are to be left for themselves (*Odyssey* 16.295–296).
