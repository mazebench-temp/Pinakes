δένδρεον, in the plural δένδρεα, is a tree, of whatever kind. Homer also names the kinds, among them the olive, the plane, the elm, the willow and the tamarisk (see [[olive]], [[plane-tree]], [[elm]], [[willow]] and [[tamarisk]]).

In the Iliad the tree is what the woodsman and the fire see. A woodcutter in the glens of a mountain cuts tall trees until his arms have had their fill and he longs for his meal (11.86–89); the boar of Calydon throws many tall trees to the ground in the orchard of Oeneus, roots, blossoms and all (9.538–542; see [[the-calydonian-boar]]); and Hera bids Hephaestus burn the trees along the banks of the Xanthus (21.337–338; see [[achilles-and-the-river]]). It is also a likeness: the old men on the wall talk like cicadas that sit on a tree in the woods (3.150–152; see [[like-cicadas]]), and a man who stands still before the spear is like a pillar or a tree with high foliage (13.437; see [[like-a-pillar]] and [[with-high-foliage]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the Odyssey trees grow tall on Calypso's island, alder, poplar and fir, and Odysseus fells twenty of them for his raft (*Odyssey* 5.238–244); pear, pomegranate, apple, fig and olive grow in the orchard of Alcinous (*Odyssey* 7.114–116); trees with high foliage pour down their fruit over Tantalus, and the wind tosses it away whenever he reaches for it (*Odyssey* 11.588–592); and in the land of a just king the trees are heavy with fruit (*Odyssey* 19.111–113). In his father's orchard Odysseus proves who he is by naming the trees that Laertes gave him as a boy, thirteen pear trees, ten apple trees and forty fig trees (*Odyssey* 24.336–342). These are plain renderings. The farm of Laertes is πολυδένδρεος, “of many trees” (*Odyssey* 23.139, 23.359).
