## Description

It is a κυνέη, one of Homer's words for a helmet (see [[helmet]]), but it is “made of leather” (ῥινοῦ ποιητήν). ῥινός is a hide or skin: the same night Diomedes sleeps on “the hide of an ox of the fields” and Dolon goes out in “the skin of a gray wolf” (10.155, 10.334). Inside, it is stretched taut with thongs; outside, a boar's white teeth are set close, “this way and that”; in the middle is a lining of felt (πῖλος, found nowhere else in Homer). ἀργιόδους, “white-tusked”, is the boar's standing epithet, and the boar of Calydon has it too, “with white tusks” (9.539; see [[white-tusked]] and [[boar]]). No crest or ridge is mentioned.

The spies go out in leather. Diomedes' helmet is “of bull's hide, without ridge or crest, the kind called a skullcap” (10.258; see [[thrasymedes]]), and Dolon wears a cap of ferret skin (10.335), while elsewhere in the book helmets are bronze (10.30–31, 10.76; see [[arming-scene]]). The poem does not say why, though it notices that same night how far bronze shines in the dark (10.153–154).

## History

{{quote:c1004c2c-a396-5bee-99a8-43069d2053b3}}

The narrator stops the arming to tell where the helmet came from, as in Book 2 he told how Agamemnon's scepter came down from Hephaestus through Zeus, Hermes, Pelops, Atreus and Thyestes (2.100–108; see [[the-scepter-of-agamemnon]]). Few things in Homer have such a history: Agamemnon's corselet, a guest-gift from Cinyras of Cyprus (11.19–23), Meges' corselet, a gift to his father from a guest-friend at Ephyra (15.529–533), the silver mixing bowl made in Sidon (23.740–749), and in the Odyssey Odysseus' bow, the gift of Iphitus (*Odyssey* 21.11–38).

This one changes hands four times, in four ways, before it reaches Odysseus. Autolycus stole it, “boring through” (ἀντιτορήσας) Amyntor's house: the verb is used of Diomedes' spear piercing the skin of Aphrodite's hand (5.337), and here it is a burglar's. At Scandeia he gave it to Amphidamas of Cythera; why, the poem does not say (see [[scandeia]] and [[cythera]]). Amphidamas gave it to Molus “to be a guest-gift”, ξεινήϊον εἶναι, the half-verse of Cinyras' corselet (10.269; 11.20; see [[guest-gifts]]). Molus gave it to his son “to wear”, φορῆναι, the word with which Thyestes left the scepter to Agamemnon (10.270; 2.107). Now Meriones puts it on Odysseus' head for the night.

The route runs south, from Eleon in Boeotia (2.500; see [[eleon]]) to Cythera, the island between the Peloponnese and Crete, and to the house of Molus, whose son leads the Cretans with Idomeneus (2.650–651; see [[meriones]] and [[molus]]). The first owner named, Amyntor son of Ormenus, has the name and patronymic of Phoenix's father (9.448), but the poem does not say that they are one man (see [[amyntor]]). Nor does it say that the thief is Odysseus' grandfather. In the Odyssey Autolycus is his mother's father, who surpassed all men “in thievery and the oath” (*Odyssey* 19.394–396; see [[autolycus]]), so the helmet he stole comes in the end to his grandson's head. The Odyssey's Autolycus has a boar of his own: Odysseus' scar was dealt by a boar's “white tusk” (λευκῷ ὀδόντι) on a hunt with Autolycus' sons (*Odyssey* 19.393–394), and the helmet is set with a boar's “white teeth” (λευκοὶ ὀδόντες, 10.263).

## Outside Homer

**The Bronze Age.** Helmets faced with plates cut from boars' tusks and sewn in rows onto a cap of leather or felt are known from Mycenaean graves, and warriors wearing them appear in the art of the same age. They were in use for some centuries of the Late Bronze Age and had long gone out of use when the poems took their final form. The description of 10.261–265 fits them closely, and ἔνθα καὶ ἔνθα may describe rows laid in alternate directions. Many scholars count the helmet among the memories of the Bronze Age that the tradition kept, like the tower shield of Ajax (see [[the-shield-of-ajax]]); whether the description survived in song alone, or because an old helmet itself survived, is debated.

**Ancient critics.** Crates of Mallos used the helmet's history to make Phoenix a Phocian: Eleon, he said, was a small town on Parnassus, where Autolycus lived, and a burglar robs his neighbors. Demetrius of Scepsis answered that no Eleon was shown on Parnassus (Strabo, *Geography* 9.5.18; see [[hellas-or-ormenium]] and [[strabo]]). Strabo calls it “the helmet of Meges, which Odysseus used in the night-rousing” (κατὰ τὴν νυκτεγερσίαν), naming Meges where the Iliad has Meriones; νυκτεγερσία is his name for the night action of Book 10 (see [[title-of-book-10]] and [[the-doloneia]]).
