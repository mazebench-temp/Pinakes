## The word

ἀμφιφορεύς is formed from ἀμφί, “on both sides”, and φέρω, “carry”: a jar carried by a handle on either side. Later Greek shortened it to ἀμφορεύς, the amphora.

## In Homer

Most of the jars hold wine. When Odysseus' men took the city of the Cicones, each crew drew off much wine into jars, and they were still drinking it on the island off the Cyclopes' land; Maron, priest of Apollo at Ismarus, gave Odysseus twelve jars of a sweet unmixed wine, “a divine drink”, the wine that later overcame the Cyclops (*Odyssey* 9.161–165, 9.196–205; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). Telemachus takes twelve on his voyage, filled by Eurycleia from the stores of his father's house (*Odyssey* 2.290, 2.349–353, 2.379; see [[provisions]]). At the funeral of Patroclus Achilles leans two-handled jars of honey and ointment against the bier on the pyre (23.170; see [[honey]] and [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]]). In the cave of the Naiads on Ithaca there are mixing bowls and two-handled jars of stone, where the bees store their honey (*Odyssey* 13.105–106).

The golden one is a jar for bones. The ghost of Patroclus asks that one urn hold the bones of both, “the golden two-handled jar that your lady mother gave you” (23.91–92), and in the Odyssey the shade of Agamemnon tells the shade of Achilles that his bones lie with those of Patroclus in a golden amphora that Thetis gave, the gift of Dionysus and the work of Hephaestus (*Odyssey* 24.72–77; see [[the-golden-urn]]). The renderings of other books of the Odyssey are plain renderings, not the translation.
