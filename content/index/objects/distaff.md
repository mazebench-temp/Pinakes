## In Homer

The distaff is a woman's tool and, with the loom, the name of her work. Hector sends Andromache home from the wall to “the loom and the distaff” and to her handmaids, “and war will be the concern of men” (*Iliad* 6.490–492); Telemachus sends Penelope back to her room with the same verses, making speech the men's concern (*Odyssey* 1.356–359; see [[weaving]] and [[handmaids]]), and he repeats them on the day of the bow (*Odyssey* 21.350–353). Helen's distaff is of gold, given to her in Egyptian Thebes by Alcandre, the wife of Polybus, with a silver basket on wheels, and when she comes into the hall her handmaid sets the basket beside her with the distaff lying across it, “laden with violet-dark wool” (*Odyssey* 4.125–135; see [[basket]] and [[helen]]). In the Odyssey the queens sit spinning: Arete at her hearth among her handmaids, “turning the sea-purple yarn” (ἠλάκατα στρωφῶσʼ ἁλιπόρφυρα, *Odyssey* 6.53, 6.306; see [[hearth]] and [[purple]]), and Penelope in her chair, turning the fine yarn (*Odyssey* 17.97, a plain rendering). ἠλάκατα, found only in the plural, is the yarn spun off the distaff (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.53; see the translation's row ἠλάκατα).

## The word

The ancient scholars gave ἠλακάτη two senses, as W. W. Merry reports a scholium: the arrow, and the woman's tool from which the thread is drawn. The word is generally derived from ἕλκω, “draw”; Curtius referred it to another root, seen perhaps in ἀράχνη, “spider” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.122). The two senses meet in Artemis' epithet χρυσηλάκατος: Merry renders it “with golden arrows”, since a distaff confuses the picture of the huntress, but the translation keeps “of the golden distaff”, beside Helen's own golden distaff (Merry on 4.122; see [[of-the-golden-distaff]], [[artemis]] and the translation's note on *Odyssey* 4.122).
