δίσκος (“discus”) is the noun of δικεῖν, “throw”: the thing thrown. In the Odyssey it is a stone (*Odyssey* 8.190); the discuses of later Greek athletics were usually of bronze. Homer's heroes throw it for pleasure in idle hours, together with the javelin and the bow, and in contests. Its range served as a measure of distance: “as far as a discus flies, swung from the shoulder” (23.431).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The verse that describes the Myrmidons at play also describes the suitors: before Odysseus' house they “amused themselves throwing the discus and the javelin” (*Odyssey* 4.625–627, 17.167–169). At the games of the Phaeacians, stung by a taunt, Odysseus picks up a stone bigger and heavier than theirs and throws it past all their marks, and Athena, in the likeness of a man, marks the spot (*Odyssey* 8.186–198). At Patroclus' funeral games the weight thrown is a σόλος, a lump of raw iron, which is also the prize (23.826–849).

## Outside Homer

The discus was one of the five events of the pentathlon, and Myron's bronze Discus-thrower, known from Roman copies, is its most famous image. In myth a discus kills by accident: Apollo's kills Hyacinthus (Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 10.162–219), and Perseus' kills his grandfather Acrisius (Apollodorus 2.4.4).
