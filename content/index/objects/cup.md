Homer's words for the drinking cup are δέπας and κύπελλον, and they seem to be interchangeable: the cup Hephaestus gives his mother is a δέπας at {{ref:Iliad 1.584}} and a κύπελλον at {{ref:Iliad 1.596}}.

## In Book 1

At Chryse, after the feast, the young men fill the [[mixing-bowl|mixing bowls]] and serve everyone, first pouring a few drops into the cups for the gods (ἐπαρξάμενοι δεπάεσσιν, {{ref:Iliad 1.471}}; see [[libation|libation]]).

On Olympus, [[Hephaestus]] springs up and puts a δέπας ἀμφικύπελλον, a “two-handled cup”, into his mother's hand, and begs her to endure. [[Hera]] smiles and takes it, and he goes round pouring nectar for the other gods (see [[cupbearing|cupbearing]]). The cup turns the quarrel into a feast (see [[quarrel-on-olympus]]).

The meaning of ἀμφικύπελλον was disputed in antiquity. It may mean a cup with a handle on each side, as the translation takes it, or a double cup with a bowl at each end.

## Outside Homer

Heinrich Schliemann gave the name *depas amphikypellon* to a tall two-handled goblet of the Early Bronze Age that he found at Troy, and archaeologists still use it for the type. Nestor's four-handled cup (Iliad 11.632–637) lent its name to “Nestor's Cup”, a clay cup of the late eighth century BCE from a grave on Pithekoussai (Ischia), whose inscription jokingly claims to be Nestor's cup.
