## The word

ῥάκος is a rag, a torn piece of cloth, and the beggar's garment. The Iliad never uses it; in the Odyssey it belongs to Odysseus in disguise. With it goes ῥωγαλέος, “rent”, said of the tunic under the rag and of the beggar's pouch (*Odyssey* 13.435, 13.438, 14.343; see [[tunic]] and [[pouch]]). λαῖφος, “rag”, is a rarer word for the same dress (*Odyssey* 13.399, 20.206; see [[clothing]]). A rag and a tunic are a beggar's whole dress, as a cloak and a tunic are a guest's (see [[cloak]]).

## In the Odyssey

Naked on the shore of Scheria, Odysseus asks Nausicaa for “a rag to throw around me” (*Odyssey* 6.178; see [[nausicaa]]). On Ithaca Athena turns him into an old beggar and puts on him “a vile rag and a tunic, / rent, dirty, stained with foul smoke”, with a deerskin, a staff and a pouch (*Odyssey* 13.434–438; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]] and [[athena]]). In his own house he girds up his rags to fight the beggar Irus, and the suitors marvel at “such a thigh the old man shows out of his rags” (*Odyssey* 18.67, 18.74; see [[the-suitors]]); when the nurse has washed his feet he covers the scar with his rags by the fire, and later he draws them back from the scar to show it to the swineherd and the cowherd (*Odyssey* 19.507, 21.221; see [[the-scar-of-odysseus]]). “But Odysseus of many wiles stripped off his rags” (*Odyssey* 22.1) is the beginning of the killing of the suitors (see [[the-suitors]]), and when it is over the nurse would not have him stand in his hall “covered like this in rags” (*Odyssey* 22.488; see [[eurycleia]]). The renderings of Books 18–22 are plain renderings, not the final translation.

## Outside Homer

The Suda glosses ῥάκος as τριβώνιον, a threadbare cloak, and as the mask, because it was glued together of rags, quoting Aristophanes (ρ 29). [[Aristotle]] gives as an example of a good likeness, one that is also a metaphor, “a ruin to the rag of a house”, τὸ ἐρείπιον ῥάκει οἰκίας (*Rhetoric* 1413a6–7).
