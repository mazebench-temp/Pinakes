## In Homer

When Thetis comes to his house, Hephaestus puts away his bellows and tools, washes, takes his staff and goes out limping to meet her, and he is not alone (see [[hephaestus]] and [[thetis]]):

{{quote:4830ed16-7a51-5d8e-ba77-c204eb47b5b8}}

They are ἀμφίπολοι, the handmaids who wait on a lord or a lady (see [[handmaids]]), but made of gold, χρύσειαι, like the things of the gods (see [[gold-and-silver]]). They have what makes a person: νόος, a mind in their hearts (see [[noos]] and [[phrenes]]), αὐδή, a voice, and σθένος, strength, and they know ἔργα, handiwork, from the immortal gods, as the craftsman of the Odyssey's simile has his skill from Hephaestus and Athena (*Odyssey* 6.232–234). ἐποίπνυον, “they bustled about”, is the verb of Hephaestus himself bustling through the hall as the gods' cupbearer in Book 1 (ποιπνύοντα, 1.600; see [[unquenchable-laughter]]). ζωῇσι νεήνισιν εἰοικυῖαι, “like living young women”, is the language of the shield that he makes the same night, whose figures fight “like living mortals” (ὥς τε ζωοὶ βροτοί, 18.539), and whose golden field grows dark behind the plow like plowed earth, “though it was made of gold; that was a wonder beyond all” (18.548–549; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]]). Walter Leaf calls εἰοικυῖαι a very doubtful form for ϝεϝικυῖαι, which occurs everywhere else in Homer (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.418).

The house of Hephaestus has other works that move of themselves: the twenty tripods on golden wheels that go to the assembly of the gods and come back (18.373–377; see [[tripod]]), and the bellows that he turns to the fire and bids work (18.469), which Leaf calls intelligent automata obeying the god's will (on 18.469). Leaf finds the only other likeness in Homer in the gold and silver dogs that Hephaestus made to guard the palace of Alcinous, immortal and ageless (*Odyssey* 7.91–94); the golden youths who hold torches in the same palace (*Odyssey* 7.100–102) he takes for statues. He sees in the handmaids a relic of the tradition that gives magical powers everywhere to the mythical founders of metalwork, such as the Telchines of Rhodes, the Dactyls and the bronze man Talos of Crete and Wayland the Smith, and notes how sparingly Homer uses such marvels, even on Olympus (on 18.376, 18.418).

## Outside Homer

When Zeus has Hephaestus make the first woman, he bids him mix earth with water and put into it “the voice and strength of a human being”, ἀνθρώπου … αὐδὴν / καὶ σθένος, the gifts of the golden handmaids (Hesiod, *Works and Days* 60–62); in the *Theogony* the golden crown that the god makes for her is worked with creatures of land and sea, “wondrous, like living things with voices” (*Theogony* 578–584; see [[hesiod]]). Pindar says that Athena gave the sons of the Sun on Rhodes every art, and that their streets bore works like living and moving creatures (*Olympian* 7.50–52; see [[pindar]]). Aristotle, explaining why a household needs slaves, imagines tools that could work at a command, like the tripods of Hephaestus in the same scene, but does not name the handmaids (*Politics* 1.4, 1253b33–1254a1; see [[aristotle-the-tripods-of-hephaestus]]).
