## In Homer

Hermes meets Odysseus on his way to Circe's house, “in the likeness of a young man / with the first down on his lip, when youth is at its most graceful” (*Odyssey* 10.278–279; see [[hermes]]). He warns him that his companions are penned like swine and that he will not come home himself, but will stay where the others are (*Odyssey* 10.281–285), and gives him the remedy with his counsel:

{{quote:4ee995ce-0931-526a-80ee-a9db91993d23}}

When he has told him the rest, to draw his sword when Circe strikes him with her wand, to accept her bed and to make her swear the great oath of the gods (*Odyssey* 10.293–301; see [[circe]] and [[oath]]), he gives it:

{{quote:970c20ba-74bf-5084-b5f8-4e15d4f00c4c}}

**The plant.** Hermes pulls it up himself, ἐκ γαίης ἐρύσας, and shows Odysseus “its nature”: φύσις is found only here in Homer, and means the outward form of the plant, like the commoner φυή, as the next verse, which describes it, and the verb ἔδειξε, “showed”, confirm; the scholion explains it as τὸ εἶδος, “its look” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.303; see the translation's row φύσις; μῶλυ). The description is one verse, a black root and a flower “like milk” (*Odyssey* 10.304; see [[milk]]).

**The name.** μῶλυ δέ μιν καλέουσι θεοί, “moly the gods call it” (*Odyssey* 10.305). Elsewhere Homer gives a thing both the name the gods use and the name men use; here, as for the Wandering Rocks, he gives only the gods' word (*Odyssey* 12.61; see [[language-of-gods-and-men]]). Merry takes the “divine” name to be the word of poetry as against the word of common life, and notes that the moly naturally has no human name, the plant being unknown to men; he doubts D. B. Monro's rule that, where two names are given, the divine name is the one with the clearer meaning (on 10.305). The word itself he would connect, with a “perhaps”, with Latin *mollis* and with μαλάχη, *malva*, “mallow” (on 10.305).

**Hard to dig.** “and it is hard to dig up / for mortal men; but the gods can do all things” (*Odyssey* 10.305–306; see [[the-gods-can-do-all-things]]). It is uncertain, Merry says, whether χαλεπόν means only, as a scholion explains it, that the plant is hard to dig out of the earth and to find, which suits the half-verse that follows better, or points to some danger in it: it is said, he quotes, that if it is pulled up by the end of its root it brings death to the one who pulls it. He compares the bed that it would be hard for a man to move unless a god came himself (*Odyssey* 23.184–186, a plain rendering) and Anticleia's words to her son among the dead, “It is hard for the living to see these things” (*Odyssey* 11.156, a plain rendering), where the sense seems to lie between difficulty and danger (on 10.305). At 10.306 the best manuscripts read ἴσασι, “know”, perhaps a memory of “for the gods know all things” (*Odyssey* 4.379 = 4.468; see [[the-gods-know-all-things]]); δύνανται, “can do”, which a scholion gives and which may be a gloss suggested by χαλεπόν, “hard”, Merry thinks on the whole the better (his critical note on 10.306; see the translation's note on 10.306).

**A good drug against evil ones.** The moly is a φάρμακον, as Circe's drugs are, and the book sets them against each other: Circe gives her beasts “evil drugs” and mixes “grim drugs” into the companions' food (*Odyssey* 10.213, 10.236), the drug that makes them swine is “the ruinous drug” (*Odyssey* 10.394), and Hermes' gift is “the good drug” (*Odyssey* 10.287, 10.292; see [[drugs]]). The same pair of words describes the drugs of Egypt, from which Helen had hers, “many good and many grim, growing intermixed” (*Odyssey* 4.229–230; see [[nepenthe]]). Hermes bids Odysseus go into the house “with” the drug, ἔχων, and says that it will not let Circe bewitch him (*Odyssey* 10.287–292); the poem does not say whether he ate it or carried it. It works as promised. Circe gives him her mixed drink with the drug in it, strikes him with her wand, and finds him unchanged: “Wonder holds me, how you drank these drugs and were not bewitched at all”, and “there is a mind in your breast that cannot be bewitched” (*Odyssey* 10.316–329; see [[kykeon]], [[wand]] and [[thelgo]]). The moly does not do the rest of Hermes' work: the sword, the oath and the bed are Odysseus' own part (*Odyssey* 10.321–347).

## Outside Homer

What the plant was is an old question. Theophrastus, as Merry quotes him, reports that moly is said to grow about Pheneus and on Cyllene, and to be like the plant Homer describes, with a round root like an onion's and a leaf like a squill's; it is used against drugs and in magic, but it is not hard to dig up, as Homer says (*Historia plantarum* 9.15, quoted by Merry on 10.305). Cyllene is the Arcadian mountain (*Iliad* 2.603) from which Hermes is called “Cyllenian” (*Odyssey* 24.1, a plain rendering). Sprengel took Theophrastus' moly for *Allium nigrum*; a scholion, citing Hippocrates and Galen, made it the wild rue (Merry on 10.305), and so does the Suda: “Moly: a counter-agent; or a herb that wards off drugs, that is, wild rue” (Suda μ 1326). Merry, with a guess of his own, thought of hellebore, for the white flower and the dark root and for its old fame as a remedy for madness; but he finds it most natural to take the moly for a creation of the poet, and reports Eustathius' allegory, in which it stands for the counsel given to Odysseus to resist sorcery (on 10.305; see the translation's note on 10.305).

Ovid keeps Homer's two colors. Before Ulysses goes to Circe, “the peace-bringer of Cyllene had given him a white flower: the gods call it moly; it is held by a black root” (*pacifer huic dederat florem Cyllenius album: / moly vocant superi, nigra radice tenetur*, *Metamorphoses* 14.291–292; see [[ovid]]).
