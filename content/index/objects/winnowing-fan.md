## Meaning

ἀθηρηλοιγός is a compound of ἀθήρ, the awn or beard of the ear of grain, and λοιγός, “ruin” (*Iliad* 1.67, 1.97): the tool that is the ruin of the chaff. ἀθήρ is not found in Homer, but the Hesiodic *Catalogue* has the swift Iphiclus run “upon the awns of the wheat” without harming the crop (fr. 84 Evelyn-White; see [[iphiclus]]). The Iliad calls the tool πτύον: “As from a broad winnowing shovel across a great threshing floor / dark-skinned beans leap, or chickpeas, / under the shrill wind and the swing of the winnower” (*Iliad* 13.588–590; see [[beans-on-the-threshing-floor]] and [[winnowing]]).

Both the oar and the winnowing shovel, W. W. Merry notes, may be called πλάτη, “blade”, as Eustathius says: πλάτη of the sea is the oar, πλάτη of the land the winnowing shovel. So a man who had never seen the sea would make the mistake without absurdity (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.128; see [[oars]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

On the night of their reunion Odysseus tells Penelope the prophecy in nearly the same words: Teiresias bade him go to many cities of men carrying a well-fitted oar, until he comes to men who know nothing of the sea, “and when another wayfarer meets me / and says that I have a winnowing fan on my shining shoulder”, he is to fix the oar in the ground and sacrifice to Poseidon (*Odyssey* 23.264–284, a plain rendering; see [[penelope]] and [[poseidon]]).

## Outside Homer

Sophocles took the scene into his *Odysseus Wounded by the Spine*, Merry reports, calling the winnowing shovel ἀθηρόβρωτον ὄργανον, “a chaff-devouring instrument” (on *Odyssey* 11.128, citing fr. 404 Dindorf; see [[telegonus-kills-odysseus]]).
