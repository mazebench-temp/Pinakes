## Description

τάλαντα, a plural, is a pair of scales, the beam with its two pans. The singular τάλαντον later became a weight and a sum of money, and in Homer gold is already counted in τάλαντα (9.122, 18.507, 23.269). Zeus' scales are golden (χρύσεια, 8.69). He holds them out (ἐτίταινε) and sets in them δύο κῆρε τανηλεγέος θανάτοιο, "two fates of death that lays men low" (8.70; see [[that-lays-men-low]]). A κήρ is death as a man's allotted doom, and the dual gives one to each side (see [[ker-doom]]). He takes the beam by the middle and raises it (ἕλκε δὲ μέσσα λαβών), and the pan that sinks (ῥέπε) carries death: "the fated day of the Achaeans sank" (αἴσιμον ἦμαρ, 8.72; see [[aisa]]). Their fates settle on the bountiful earth, and the Trojans' are lifted toward the wide sky (8.73–74; see the translation's note on 8.69–74).

## In Homer

The weighing is told twice, in the same verses. In Book 8 it comes at midday, "when the sun stood astride the middle of the sky" (8.68; see [[helios]]), after a morning in which "the missiles of both sides struck home" (8.67). It decides the day. Zeus thunders from Ida and sends a blazing flash among the Achaeans, and their leaders flee (8.75–79; see [[zeus]] and [[ida]]). From then until sunset the day belongs to the Trojans (8.485–488).

In Book 22 Achilles has chased Hector three times around the walls of Troy, "but when for the fourth time they came to the springs", Zeus holds out the golden scales again, with one fate for Achilles and one for Hector. Hector's fated day sinks "and went to Hades, and Phoebus Apollo left him" (22.208–213). The first weighing gives Hector his day of victory; the second ends his life (see [[hector]]).

Elsewhere the scales are a figure of speech. When Zeus puts a cowardly heart into Hector, he turns his chariot to flight, "for he knew the sacred scales of Zeus" (16.656–658). Odysseus tells Achilles that men soon have their fill of battle, where the bronze strews the most straw and the harvest is smallest, "when Zeus inclines his scales, he who is the steward of war for men" (19.221–224; the last verse also at 4.84). In a simile the fight at the Achaean wall hangs even, like the scales of an honest working woman weighing wool for a meager wage, until Zeus gives the higher glory to Hector (12.433–438; see [[kydos]] and [[the-working-womans-scales]]).

## Fate and the will of Zeus

Whether the scales show Zeus a fate that stands apart from his will, or are an image of his own decision, the poem does not say, and readers have long disagreed. Both weighings come after the decision is made. In Book 8 Zeus has promised Thetis to give the Trojans the upper hand (1.524–527), and the weighing confirms the promise; in Book 22 he has asked the gods whether to save Hector and has let Athena have her way (22.167–185). He thinks of saving a man "long ago doomed by fate", and refrains, for Sarpedon and for Hector alike, when another god objects that the rest of them would not approve (16.439–443, 22.178–181; see [[moira]]). The poem never shows the scales overruling Zeus, or Zeus overruling the scales. They make visible, at the turning point of a day or of a life, what has been settled.

## Outside Homer

Aeschylus built a tragedy on the scene, the lost *Weighing of Souls* (*Psychostasia*), in which Zeus weighed the souls of Achilles and Memnon while their mothers, Thetis and Eos, pleaded for them; Plutarch, who reports it, treats the scene as a poet's invention (*How the Young Man Should Study Poetry* 16f–17a). Virgil's Jupiter weighs the fates of Aeneas and Turnus at the end of the *Aeneid* (12.725–727), and Milton hangs God's golden scales in the sky to part Gabriel and Satan (*Paradise Lost* 4.996–1004; see [[the-weighing-of-souls]]).
