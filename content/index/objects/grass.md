ἄγρωστις is a grass of the fields, a word Homer uses only once. W. W. Merry reports three identifications: it is often rendered “clover”, which the epithet μελιηδής, “honey-sweet”, seems to suit; others take it for couch grass (*Triticum repens*), which has a peculiarly sweet root; others for dog's-tooth grass (*Cynodon dactylon*), the principal pasturage of India, where it is called *doorba* (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.90).

## In Homer

When Nausicaa and her handmaids reach the washing troughs, they unyoke the mules and set them free from the wagon, “and drove them along the eddying river / to graze on the honey-sweet grass” (*Odyssey* 6.88–90; see [[mules]], [[wagon]] and [[the-river-of-scheria]]). τρώγειν is “to graze”, to munch raw plants. ἄγρωστιν μελιηδέα, “the honey-sweet grass” (*Odyssey* 6.90), gives the fodder the epithet of wine and of the fruit of the lotus (*Odyssey* 3.46, 9.94; see [[lotus]]), and of the wheat on which the horses of Diomedes feed at their manger in the Iliad, “eating honey-sweet wheat” (μελιηδέα πυρὸν ἔδοντες, *Iliad* 10.569; see [[wheat]]). The mules graze while the girls wash, eat and play, and are yoked again when it is time to go home (*Odyssey* 6.252–253).

Homer's other grass is ποίη, the herbage that the earth sends up beneath Zeus and Hera on Ida, “fresh-sprung grass” (*Iliad* 14.347; see [[hyacinth]]); in the Odyssey the Cyclops' ram was always the first to crop “the tender flowers of the grass” (*Odyssey* 9.449, a plain rendering). The horses of Achilles' men stand beside their chariots “munching lotus and marsh-grown parsley” (*Iliad* 2.775–776; see [[parsley]]), and Menelaus' plain has lotus and galingale (*Odyssey* 4.602–603; see [[galingale]]).

## Outside Homer

In [[Aristotle]]'s *History of Animals*, the ticks are among the creatures that come into being from other matter: they come from the ἄγρωστις (*History of Animals* 5.19).
