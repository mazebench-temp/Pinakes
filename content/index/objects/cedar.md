# κέδρου (Od. 5.60) and the adjective κέδρινον "of cedar" (Il. 24.192), the only forms in Homer.
grc: [κέδρου, κέδρινον]

κέδρος is named once in Homer, as firewood on Calypso's hearth, and its adjective κέδρινος once, of Priam's storeroom.

**On Calypso's hearth.** When Hermes comes to the cave, “A great fire was burning on the hearth, and far off the scent / of cedar that splits easily and of thyine wood spread over the island / as they burned” (*Odyssey* 5.59–61; see [[fire]], [[thyine-wood]] and [[calypsos-cave]]). W. W. Merry holds that the Homeric κέδρος is not the cedar of Lebanon but a kind of juniper, probably the Spanish juniper, *Juniperus oxycedrus*, which grows freely on the shores of the Mediterranean. εὐκέατος, which the translation renders “that splits easily”, he refers to κεάζω, “split”, rather than to κηώεις, “fragrant”, as Düntzer would, and takes it either as “easily split”, Virgil's *fissile lignum*, or more likely as “ready split”, cut into billets for burning (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.60; see the translation's note on 5.60). The fire and its scent, which reaches “far off”, over the island, are the first things told of the goddess's dwelling.

**Priam's storeroom.** When Priam prepares the ransom for Hector, he goes down into his storeroom, θάλαμον … κηώεντα, / κέδρινον ὑψόροφον, “into the fragrant chamber, / high-roofed, of cedar, which held many bright trinkets” (*Iliad* 24.191–192; see [[the-ransom-of-hector]]). κηώεις, “fragrant”, is the word of the scented chambers of Paris and of Hecuba (*Iliad* 3.382, 6.288), and here the cedar may be the reason for the scent.

## Outside Homer

Merry quotes Athenaeus, who says that the fragrance of the cedar, the θύον and the cypress is a matter for the history of plants (Athenaeus 5.207e), and Pliny, who says that in the same verse as the θύον Homer has cedar and larch burned (on 5.60; see [[thyine-wood]]).
