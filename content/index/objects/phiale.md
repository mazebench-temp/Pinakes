## The word

The φιάλη of Homer is not the vessel of later Greece. The word occurs four times, all in the funeral of Patroclus and its games (23.243, 23.253, 23.270, 23.616): the bowl is golden, it holds bones wrapped in fat, and the prize is ἀμφίθετος and ἀπύρωτος, “untouched by fire”, as tripods and cauldrons are that have never been set on it (9.122, 23.267). Walter Leaf, after Helbig, takes ἀμφίθετος in all probability for “two-handled”, and concludes that the Homeric φιάλη could have borne no resemblance to the shallow, saucer-like φιάλη of classical art, which is unsuitable for keeping bones and could not be used on the fire (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.243). The translation renders it “bowl”.

The classical phiale was the vessel of libation, held flat on the palm and tipped so that the wine ran out; vase painters show gods and mortals holding it over an altar (see [[libation]]). Its round shape and central boss make the comparison with a round shield natural (see [[the-cup-of-ares]] and [[shield]]).
