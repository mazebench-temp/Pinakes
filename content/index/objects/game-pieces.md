πεσσοί are the pieces of a board game, and by extension the game itself. Homer names them once, in the dative plural πεσσοῖσι, “with game pieces” (*Odyssey* 1.107), and says nothing of the board or the rules. In Attic the pieces are πεττοί and the game is πεττεία, the game of skill that Plato and Aristotle use as an image (see Outside Homer). The suitors' own game, as Athenaeus describes it in the account that Merry quotes, was one of aiming pebbles at a mark.

{{notes}}

## Elsewhere in Homer

The other games of the poems are knucklebones, over which the boy Patroclus killed the son of Amphidamas “in anger over the knucklebones” (ἀμφʼ ἀστραγάλοισι, 23.85–88; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]] and [[amphidamas-of-opus]]), and the ball that Nausicaa and her maids throw by the river (*Odyssey* 6.100, 6.115–117) and that the Phaeacian youths throw in their dance (*Odyssey* 8.370–380). Dice are not named in Homer.

## Outside Homer

Herodotus reports that the Lydians claimed to have invented the games that they and the Greeks then played, dice, knucklebones, the ball and every other kind, in a famine of eighteen years under King Atys, when they played all of one day so as not to look for food and ate on the next; πεσσοί alone they did not claim (*Histories* 1.94.2–4; see [[herodotus]]).

In Plato's *Phaedrus* Socrates tells of Theuth, the Egyptian god of Naucratis, who first invented number and calculation, geometry and astronomy, draughts and dice (πεττείας τε καὶ κυβείας), and letters (*Phaedrus* 274c–d). In the *Republic* Adeimantus says that those who answer Socrates' questions are led on step by step until they find themselves shut in and with nothing to say, like unskilled players who are shut in by good players at draughts and cannot move, “not with pebbles but with words” (*Republic* 6.487b–c); in the *Laws* the god who orders the whole is a player (πεττευτής) who has only to move each soul to a better or a worse place as its character changes (*Laws* 10.903d; see [[plato]]). Aristotle says that the man who is without a city by nature is at once a lover of war, “being like an isolated piece in a game of draughts” (ἄζυξ ὥσπερ ἐν πεττοῖς, *Politics* 1.2, 1253a5–7; see [[aristotle-man-without-a-city]]).

In Euripides the game belongs to men at leisure. At Aulis the chorus of women saw Protesilaus and Palamedes at their seats, “delighting in the intricate figures of the game pieces” (πεσσῶν ἡδομένους μορφαῖσι πολυπλόκοις, *Iphigenia at Aulis* 192–199; see [[protesilaus]] and [[palamedes]]), and in the *Medea* the children's old attendant overhears the news of their banishment where the oldest men sit at the draughts, by the holy water of Peirene in Corinth (*Medea* 67–72; see [[euripides]]).

How the suitors played their game Homer does not say. Athenaeus gives an account of it, which W. W. Merry quotes in his note on the game pieces (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.107): the hundred and eight suitors set out pebbles in two equal rows, fifty-four on each side, with one more in the space between, which they called Penelope, and each in turn aimed at it with his own; whoever struck Penelope without touching any other pebble won, and had high hopes of marrying her, and Eurymachus had won the most (see [[the-game-of-penelope]], [[eurymachus]] and [[the-suitors]]).
