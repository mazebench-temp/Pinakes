## The list

Agamemnon names the gifts “before you all”, to the elders at his table, after he has admitted his blind folly and promised “a boundless ransom” (9.115–121; see [[agamemnon]] and [[ate]]). They come in three stages.

**At once.** Seven tripods “untouched by fire”, new, like the prizes Achilles sets out at Patroclus' games (23.267, 23.885); ten talents of gold; twenty gleaming cauldrons; and twelve prize-winning horses, whose prizes alone would keep a man from being “landless” (9.122–127; see [[untouched-by-fire]], [[prize-winning]], [[tripod]] and [[horses]]). Seven women of Lesbos, “skilled in flawless handiwork”, whom Agamemnon chose out for himself “when he himself took well-built Lesbos”, so that the women are Achilles' own spoil given back to him (9.128–130; see [[lesbos]] and [[captive-women]]). And Briseis, with a great oath that Agamemnon never went into her bed (9.131–134; see [[briseis]] and [[oath]]). “All these things shall be at hand at once” (9.135).

**After the sack.** When the Achaeans divide the spoil of Troy, Achilles may load his ship with gold and bronze, and choose twenty Trojan women, “the most beautiful after Argive Helen” (9.135–140).

**At home.** If they come home to Achaean Argos, he will be Agamemnon's son-in-law, honored equally with Orestes; he may take whichever of the three daughters he likes, without bride-price, with a dowry greater than any father ever gave; and he will have seven cities near Pylos to rule (9.141–156; see [[orestes]], [[chrysothemis]], [[laodice-daughter-of-agamemnon]], [[iphianassa]], [[marriage]] and [[the-seven-cities]]).

All this on one condition, “if he ceased from his anger”, and with one demand: “let him submit to me, inasmuch as I am more kingly” (9.157–161).

## What is offered

Agamemnon calls the gifts ἀπερείσια ἄποινα, “a boundless ransom” (9.120), the phrase for what Chryses brought to free his daughter and Priam to free Hector's body (1.13, 24.502; see [[apoina]]). They answer Athena's promise in the first quarrel, that “someday three times as many splendid gifts will be laid before you” (1.213; see [[triple-gifts-foretold]]); the verb is the same, παρέσσεται (1.213, 9.135). Nestor judges them “no longer to be scorned” (9.164). Odysseus repeats the list in Agamemnon's verses, changing only the persons and leaving out the demand (9.262–299; see [[message-repeated]]).

Modern readers have asked what the offer would have made of Achilles. The gifts are more than he could ever return; they would make him Agamemnon's son-in-law and the lord of towns in Agamemnon's gift; and they come with the demand that he yield. Walter Donlan read the offer as a contest in giving meant to assert Agamemnon's superiority (“Duelling with Gifts in the Iliad: As the Audience Saw It”, *Colby Quarterly* 29, 1993), and Donna F. Wilson as ἄποινα offered on the giver's terms rather than the compensation Achilles demands (*Ransom, Revenge, and Heroic Identity in the Iliad*, 2002).

## Refused

Achilles answers the gifts with scorn: “His gifts are hateful to me, and I honor him at a hair's worth” (9.378). Not ten or twenty times as much, nor the wealth of Orchomenus or of Egyptian Thebes, nor as much as the sand and the dust would move him, “until he has paid me back for all his heart-grieving insult” (9.379–387; see [[the-sand-and-the-dust]] and [[thebes-in-egypt]]), and he will not marry Agamemnon's daughter (9.388–392; see [[achilles]]). Phoenix argues that the gifts change everything: if Agamemnon “were not bringing gifts, and naming more to come”, he would not ask Achilles to give up his wrath (9.515–519), and his tale of Meleager, who saved his city too late for the gifts promised him, ends with the warning that Achilles will no longer be held in the same honor if he fights “without the gifts” (9.574–605; see [[phoenix]] and [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). Ajax sets them beside the blood-price that a man accepts even for a brother or a son: “now we offer you seven, by far the best, and many other things besides” (9.632–639; see [[poine]]). Odysseus reports that Achilles “rejects you and your gifts” (9.679), and Diomedes wishes the king had never entreated him, “offering countless gifts” (9.698–699; see [[diomedes]]).

## Given

The gifts return when Achilles has lost Patroclus. Earlier he had told Patroclus to win great honor, so that the Danaans would give the girl back “and add splendid gifts” (16.84–86), as if none had been offered; whether such lines show that Book 9 was added late, or only Achilles' own view of that night, is disputed (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]). Thetis tells Hephaestus that the elders of the Argives begged him and named many glorious gifts (18.448–449). In the assembly of Book 19 Agamemnon offers again “all the gifts that brilliant Odysseus came yesterday and promised” (19.140–141), and Achilles tells him to give them, as is fitting, or keep them, and turns at once to the battle (19.146–148); Odysseus has them brought into the middle of the assembly (19.172–174). Young men carry out of Agamemnon's hut the seven tripods, twenty cauldrons and twelve horses, the seven women and Briseis the eighth, and Odysseus weighs out the ten talents of gold (19.238–248); Agamemnon swears by Zeus, Earth, the Sun and the Erinyes that he never laid a hand on Briseis (19.258–265), and the Myrmidons take everything to Achilles' ship (19.277–281). The spoil of Troy, the daughter and the seven cities are not mentioned again.

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates would not have the young taught to admire the gifts. Phoenix is not to be praised for advising Achilles to take gifts and help the Achaeans but not to give up his wrath without them, nor are we to believe that Achilles was so greedy as to take gifts from Agamemnon (*Republic* 3.390e; see [[plato-phoenix-and-the-gifts]]).
