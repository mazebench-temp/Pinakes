# σέλινον (Il. 2.776; Rhetoric 1413a13, 1413a28), σελίνου (Od. 5.72) and σέλινα (Rhetoric 1413a29).
grc: [σέλινον, σελίνου, σέλινα]
en: [parsley]
---

σέλινον is named twice in Homer. W. W. Merry notes that it may be parsley, “or, more likely, a kind of celery” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.72); the translation renders it “parsley”.

## In Homer

While Achilles keeps from the fighting, his Myrmidons amuse themselves on the shore with the discus, the javelin and the bow, “and the horses, each beside its chariot, / munching lotus and marsh-grown parsley, / stood idle” (*Iliad* 2.775–777; see [[lotus]], [[horses]] and [[myrmidons]]). ἐλεόθρεπτος, “marsh-grown”, is found only here.

In the Odyssey the parsley grows in the meadows around Calypso's cave: “And all around, soft meadows of violets and parsley / were in bloom” (*Odyssey* 5.72–73; see [[violet]], [[vine]] and [[calypsos-cave]]). Four springs flow beside the meadows (*Odyssey* 5.70–71), and Merry recalls on this place the marsh-grown parsley of the Iliad (on 5.72).

## Outside Homer

[[Aristotle]] quotes a poet's simile of the plant among the images that win approval when they are well made: “He carries his legs as curly as parsley” (*Rhetoric* 1413a13–14). The curled leaves of the plant are the likeness. He gives it again as a hyperbole, “You would have thought he had not legs but parsley, so curly were they” (*Rhetoric* 1413a28–29), to show that the hyperbole differs from the simile only in its wording, and that both are metaphors (*Rhetoric* 1413a15–29).
