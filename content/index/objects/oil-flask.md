## In Homer

The word occurs in one verse, said twice. When Nausicaa sets out for the washing, her mother puts food and wine on the wagon, “And she gave her liquid olive oil in a golden flask, / so that she might bathe and anoint herself, with her handmaids” (*Odyssey* 6.79–80; see [[bathing]] and [[provisions]]); and when Nausicaa has ordered her handmaids to bathe the stranger in the river, they lay clothes beside him “and gave him liquid olive oil in a golden flask” (*Odyssey* 6.215; see the translation's row χρυσέῃ ἐν ληκύθῳ ὑγρὸν ἔλαιον). The girls anoint themselves richly after their bath (*Odyssey* 6.96), and Odysseus, who sends the handmaids away, anoints himself, “for truly it is long since oil was on my skin” (*Odyssey* 6.220, 6.227). The flask is of gold, like the vessels of the houses of kings (see [[gold-and-silver]]). The oil of a house was kept in the storeroom (*Odyssey* 2.339; see [[the-storeroom-of-odysseus]] and [[olive]]).

## Outside Homer

In Plato Socrates reminds Hippias how he came to Olympia with nothing on his body that was not his own work, first the ring and the seal that he had engraved himself, then “a strigil and an oil flask, which you had made yourself”, the shoes he had cut, the cloak and tunic he had woven, and a girdle like the costly Persian ones (*Hippias Minor* 368b–c; see [[plato]]). The strigil and the flask are the gear of the bath and the wrestling ground.

The Suda calls λήκυθοι the vessels that hold oil, and says that the Attic writers called a λήκυθος of perfume an ἀλάβαστρον (λ 438). It quotes Aristophanes on the painter “who paints the oil flasks for the dead”, and explains that such things were painted at the tombs (λ 439).
