νηός (Attic νεώς) is a god's dwelling, from ναίω, “dwell”. It is easily confused with νηός, “of a ship”, from νηῦς ({{ref:Iliad 1.439}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.476}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.482}}).

[[Chryses]] opens his prayer by reminding Apollo of his service: “if ever I roofed a temple to your pleasure” ({{ref:Iliad 1.39}}). The roof completes a building, so the phrase may mean building the whole. The temple and the thigh-pieces he has burned are the priest's claim on the god (see [[prayer|prayer]] and [[priesthood|priesthood]]).

{{quote:9cf7ea6a-1e8e-5cb1-b32e-532a318030d6}}

Elsewhere in the Iliad temples stand on the citadel of Troy: Athena's, where the women lay a robe on the knees of her statue (Iliad 6.297–304), and Apollo's in Pergamus (Iliad 5.445–448).

## Outside Homer

Freestanding temples appear in Greece in the eighth century BCE, about the time the poems took shape.
