## In Homer

Hera, asked to bed on the open peak of Ida, objects that everything there is “in plain view”, and that if any of the gods saw them asleep and told the others, she could not go home for shame; let them go to the chamber that Hephaestus built (14.330–340; see [[nemesis]] and [[house]]). Zeus answers with a chamber of cloud:

{{quote:f932ba52-ccf0-5e73-946c-4939b6d327a5}}

Then the earth sends up grass and flowers beneath them, “and they drew over them a cloud, / fine and golden; and glistening drops of dew fell from it” (14.350–351; see [[lotus]], [[crocus]] and [[hyacinth]]). The cloud completes the deception, and Zeus provides it himself: Hera's pretended modesty has won them a hidden place on the open peak, and it is under the cloud that he falls asleep (14.352–353; see [[the-deception-of-zeus]] and [[hypnos]]).

**The Sun.** Zeus names the one watcher who sees everything: “not even the Sun could see through it to us, / whose light is the sharpest of all for seeing” (14.344–345; see [[helios]]). In the Odyssey it is the Sun who sees Ares and Aphrodite lying together in Hephaestus' house and goes to tell him (*Odyssey* 8.270–271, 8.302). Zeus' cloud keeps out even the Sun.

**Gold.** The cloud is golden, χρύσεον and χρυσείη (14.344, 14.351), like the things that belong to the gods (see [[gold-and-silver]]). Elsewhere in Homer golden clouds hide Ares, who sits on the peak of Olympus with the other gods, held back from the war by the will of Zeus (13.523–525), and Athena sets a golden cloud as a crown on the head of Achilles at the ditch (18.205–206; see [[the-beacon-fires]]). When Iris and Apollo come to Zeus on Gargarus in the next book, “a fragrant cloud” (θυόεν νέφος) is wreathed about him (15.152–153).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The gods use clouds and mist to hide. A god may stand beside a fighter “his shoulders wrapped in cloud” (5.186), as Apollo comes with a cloud about his shoulders and the aegis in his hands (15.307–308), and the gods who watch the battle of Book 20 sit with an unbreakable cloud about their shoulders (20.150). Zeus pours a thick mist around his horses when he comes to Ida (8.50). Gods also hide men: Aphrodite snatches Paris from the duel in thick mist (3.380–381). Once in the Odyssey a god hides love so: when Poseidon lies with Tyro, a dark wave arches over them, high as a mountain, and hides the god and the mortal woman (*Odyssey* 11.243–244).
