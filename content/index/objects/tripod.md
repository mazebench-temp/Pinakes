τρίπους, “three-footed”, is a cauldron standing on three legs, so that a fire could burn beneath it. Homer's tripods are of bronze, and they are counted among a hero's treasures beside gold, horses and women. Some are kept for use: a great tripod is set on the fire to heat water for washing the body of Patroclus, and the fire plays round its belly (18.344–348), and the same verses heat a bath for Odysseus in Alcinous' palace (Od. 8.434–437). Others are never put on the fire, ἄπυροι, “untouched by fire”, like the seven new tripods among Agamemnon's gifts to Achilles (9.122, 9.264).

As treasure, the tripod is a prize, a gift and a price. Agamemnon promises Teucer a gift of honor from the spoils of Troy, “a tripod, or two horses with their chariot, or a woman” (8.290–291; see [[geras]]); it is the first tripod of the Iliad. Achilles counts tripods, with cattle and horses, among the things that can be won, as a man's life cannot (9.406–409). Neleus sent four horses with their chariot to race for a tripod at the games in Elis (11.699–702). At Patroclus' funeral games the first prize of the chariot race is a woman and a tripod with ears, holding twenty-two measures, and the prize for the wrestling is a great tripod to stand on the fire, which the Achaeans valued at twelve oxen, against a woman valued at four (23.262–264, 23.702–705); in a simile racehorses run for “a tripod or a woman” (22.162–164). Polybus of Egyptian Thebes gave Menelaus two tripods (Od. 4.128–129), each of the Phaeacian lords gives Odysseus a great tripod and a cauldron (Od. 13.13–14), and Priam's ransom for Hector includes two gleaming tripods (24.233). Hephaestus makes twenty tripods on golden wheels, which go of themselves to the gods' assembly and come back, “a wonder to see” (18.373–377).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod won a tripod with ears for a song at the funeral games of Amphidamas at Chalcis, and dedicated it to the Muses of Helicon (*Works and Days* 654–659). Tripods were the commonest great offering in early Greek sanctuaries, and many bronze tripods of the Geometric period, the ninth and eighth centuries BC, have been found at Olympia and Delphi. After Plataea the Greeks dedicated at Delphi, from a tenth of the spoils, a golden tripod set on a bronze serpent with three heads (Herodotus 9.81).
