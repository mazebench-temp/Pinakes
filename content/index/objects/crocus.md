κρόκος is the crocus, and in later Greek also saffron, the yellow dye made from the flower. Homer names the flower once. When Zeus takes Hera in his arms on the peak of Gargarus, “under them the shining earth sent up fresh-sprung grass, and dewy lotus, and crocus, and hyacinth, thick and soft, which held them up off the ground” (14.347–349; see [[lotus]], [[hyacinth]] and [[earth]]). On it they lie down, and a golden cloud is drawn over them, from which glistening drops of dew fall (14.350–351; see [[the-golden-cloud]]). The bed is the earth's own work. Hera had said that she feared to lie with him on the open peaks of Ida, where some god might see them, and proposed the chamber that Hephaestus built for him; Zeus answered that he would wrap them in a golden cloud through which not even the Sun could see (14.330–345; see [[hera]], [[zeus]] and [[the-deception-of-zeus]]).

The flower's color is in an epithet. Dawn is κροκόπεπλος, “saffron-robed”, four times in the Iliad, each time at the dawn of a day on which the story turns (8.1, 19.1, 23.227, 24.695; see [[saffron-robed]] and [[eos]]).

## Outside Homer

In the *Hymn to Demeter* the crocus is among the flowers that Persephone was gathering with the daughters of Oceanus in a soft meadow, roses, crocus and violets, iris and hyacinth, when the earth opened and Hades carried her off; the narcissus that drew her hand had been grown by Earth as a snare, by the will of Zeus (*Hymn to Demeter* 5–18). Persephone tells it again to her mother, and there the narcissus grows “like a crocus” (425–430). In both poems the earth itself puts out the flowers for a union of the gods. In tragedy κρόκος is the dye: the chorus of [[aeschylus|Aeschylus]]' *Agamemnon* remembers Iphigenia at the altar pouring κρόκου βαφάς, “the dyes of saffron”, to the ground, her saffron-dyed robe (*Agamemnon* 239).
