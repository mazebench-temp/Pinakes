## The word

The πίθος is a jar for keeping stores, and Homer names it three times. In the storeroom of Odysseus, πίθοι οἴνοιο παλαιοῦ ἡδυπότοιο, “jars of old wine, sweet to drink”, stand “fitted in order against the wall” (ἑξείης ποτὶ τοῖχον ἀρηρότες, *Odyssey* 2.340–342; see [[the-storeroom-of-odysseus]] and [[wine]]). When Odysseus and Penelope tell each other their sorrows, she tells of the suitors who slaughtered cattle and sheep on her account, “and much wine was drawn from the jars” (πολλὸς δὲ πίθων ἠφύσσετο οἶνος, *Odyssey* 23.302–305; see [[the-suitors]]). The renderings of the twenty-third book are plain renderings, not the translation. And in Achilles' parable to Priam, “two jars stand on the floor of Zeus”, one of the evils he gives and one of the blessings (24.527–528; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]).

The jars stand (ἕστασαν, *Odyssey* 2.341), and the wine is drawn from them, ἀφύσσω, the verb with which Eurycleia draws off wine into two-handled jars for Telemachus' voyage (*Odyssey* 2.349, 2.379; see [[two-handled-jar]]).

## Outside Homer

Walter Leaf, on the jars of Zeus, compares the jar of evils that a woman opened in Hesiod, and finds that ἐν οὔδει, “on the floor”, recalls “the huge pointed jars of the Mykenaean age, which are literally stuck in the floor of the store-rooms” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.527). In the *Works and Days* the woman lifts the great lid from the jar, πίθου μέγα πῶμα, and scatters the evils among men; only Hope stays inside, under the lip of the jar, for she put the lid back before Hope could fly out (*Works and Days* 94–98; see [[hesiod]]). The same poem gives the farmer's jar its rules: drink your fill when the jar is first opened and when it is nearly done, but be sparing in the middle, for sparing at the bottom is poor (*Works and Days* 368–369); and among the days of the month, the fourth of the middle is the day to open a jar, a day holy above all (*Works and Days* 819–820).
