## The word

When a ship was built, the keel was laid first: a strong balk of timber, W. W. Merry thought, broader and flatter than the keel of a modern vessel, since Homeric ships were often run aground and hauled up and down the beach, where props pushed under the hull near the keel held them upright (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix I §2; *Iliad* 1.486). From its forward end rose the stem, which takes the shock when a ship is run ashore (Appendix I §3; see [[cutwater]]), and the mast was stepped into its solid wood, so that a mast snapped at its foot breaks off “at the keel” (*Odyssey* 12.422; Appendix I §12; see [[mast]]). Ahrens connected τρόπις with δρῦς, “oak”, and δόρυ, “timber”; Merry preferred τρέπω, “turn”, the keel being the center on which a vessel sways and rocks (Appendix I §2).

## In Homer

The Iliad never names a keel. In the Odyssey the word belongs to one story, told four times: the wreck of Odysseus' last ship after his companions had eaten the cattle of the Sun. Calypso tells Hermes that she saved him “when he was astride the keel” (*Odyssey* 5.130); Odysseus tells Arete, “but I took the keel of the curving ship in my arms / and was borne along for nine days” (*Odyssey* 7.252–253); in his tale to the Phaeacians the surge looses the sides of the ship from the keel, and he lashes keel and mast together and rides on them (*Odyssey* 12.420–425); and in the tale he tells Penelope in his disguise, the wave throws Odysseus ashore on the keel of his ship in the land of the Phaeacians (*Odyssey* 19.278–279, a plain rendering). That he could clasp it in his arms gives some idea of its size, Merry notes, for the keel was bare by then (Appendix I §2; see [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]] and [[calypso]]).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus describes the cargo boats of the Nile, built without ribs from short planks of acacia laid like bricks, whose single steering oar is passed through the keel (Herodotus 2.96.1–3; see [[herodotus]] and [[steering-oar]]). In Plato's *Laws* the Athenian, about to set out the patterns of lives according to the characters of souls, compares himself with a shipwright who, beginning his work, first sketches the shapes of his ships in their keels, and so, he says, he is truly laying down the keels of lives (*Laws* 7.803a; see [[plato]]).
