## Meaning

οὖρος, “boundary”, is the Ionic form of Attic ὅρος, the word of the later boundary stones (ὅροι) and of ὁρίζω, “mark a boundary”. The long first syllable is left by a lost consonant, an older ὅρϝος. Homer has two other words of the same sound: οὖρος, a fair wind (1.479), and οὖρος, a guardian, said of Nestor, “the guardian of the Achaeans” (8.80, 11.840). The neuter plural οὖρα is a measured stretch of ground: “the furrow-length / of mules” at the plow (10.351–352), and the cast of a discus (23.431).

## In Homer

The simile of Book 12 is set in a field held in common:

{{quote:b88bd160-b3e1-5e94-a41c-6476162f16c5}}

ἐπίξυνος, “common”, occurs only here. The two men hold μέτρα, measuring rods, and quarrel over a narrow strip, each claiming his equal share (περὶ ἴσης). So the battlements keep the Lycians and the Achaeans apart, and neither side can push the other back (12.417–424; see [[the-boundary-dispute]] and [[battlements]]). A few verses later a second simile from ordinary life, an honest working woman weighing wool, holds the battle level in the same way (12.433–436; see [[the-working-womans-scales]]).

In the battle of the gods Athena steps back and picks up “a stone lying on the plain, black, jagged and big, / which men of former times had set to be the boundary of a field” (οὖρον ἀρούρης), and with it strikes Ares on the neck and loosens his limbs (21.403–406; see [[athena]], [[ares]] and [[stone-throwing]]). The stone that earlier men set up to settle what belonged to whom becomes a goddess's weapon.

## Outside Homer

In Plato's *Laws* the first of the laws for farmers is given under Zeus of Boundaries (Διὸς ὁρίου): let no one move the boundary marks of the land, whether of a fellow citizen or, at the frontier, of a foreign neighbor; let a man rather try to move the greatest rock than a boundary stone, “a small stone that bounds friendship and enmity, sworn before the gods”; and whoever moves one is to be reported and brought to court (8.842e–843b).
