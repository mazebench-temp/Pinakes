θύον occurs once in Homer, beside the cedar on Calypso's hearth: “A great fire was burning on the hearth, and far off the scent / of cedar that splits easily and of thyine wood spread over the island / as they burned” (*Odyssey* 5.59–61; see [[cedar]], [[fire]] and [[calypsos-cave]]). The translation keeps the traditional English name, “thyine wood”.

W. W. Merry takes θύον for a tree with resinous wood, probably the arbor vitae (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.60; see the translation's note on 5.60). He holds that a remark of Athenaeus, that the fragrance of the cedar, the θύον and the cypress is a matter for the history of plants, settles that the word means a tree and not a compound like incense (Athenaeus 5.207e). Pliny, as Merry quotes him, identified Homer's thyon with the citrus, a tree he counts among the few furnishings of a more refined life; he said that Homer had it burned among the scents in the dwelling of Circe, by an error that Merry thinks a memory of Virgil's Circe, and he blamed those who took the word for a perfume, since the same verse names cedar and larch. Merry answers that the citrus gives no scent in burning, and that θύον is better taken as one of the resinous trees, pine, cypress or juniper (on 5.60).

The island has other scents. Its cypress is εὐώδης, “fragrant” (*Odyssey* 5.64; see [[trees]]), and Calypso clothes Odysseus in θυώδεα εἵματα, “fragrant garments”, before she sends him off (*Odyssey* 5.264; see [[fragrant]]).
