## Description

**The making.** Thetis asks Hephaestus for “a shield and a helmet, / and fine greaves fitted with ankle-pieces, / and a corselet” (18.458–460; see [[the-making-of-the-arms]]). He casts bronze, tin, gold and silver into the fire, sets the anvil on its block, and takes up hammer and tongs (18.474–477; see [[forge]]), and the shield comes first (πρώτιστα, 18.478), before the corselet, the helmet and the greaves (18.609–613). σάκος is Homer's word for the greatest shields (see [[shield]]), and “great and heavy”, μέγα τε στιβαρόν τε, is the phrase with which Paris, Patroclus and Achilles himself take up the shield when they arm (3.335, 16.136, 19.373; see [[arming-scene]]). The narrator repeats it when the shield is done (18.609).

**Rim, strap and layers.** The rim, ἄντυξ, is “threefold, gleaming” (τρίπλακα μαρμαρέην, 18.480), and from it hangs a silver strap, as from Agamemnon's shield (18.480; 11.38). Walter Leaf takes τρίπλαξ as a form of τριπλός, “threefold”, and the rim as a real part of the shield, perhaps three of the layers turned up or three metal bands, rather than the plaited ornament on the margins of early shields by which Löschcke explained it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.480; see the translation's note on 18.480). The shield has five layers, πτύχες (18.481), the word for the layers of hide in the shield of Ajax, which has seven and an eighth of bronze (7.220–223, 7.247; Leaf on 18.481; see [[the-shield-of-ajax]]).

**The designs.** “and on it / he made many intricate designs with his cunning skill” (18.481–482). δαίδαλα, “intricate designs”, and δαιδάλλων, “working it intricately” (18.479), belong to the family of words that gives Daedalus his name (see [[daedalus]] and [[intricately-worked]]). Each scene opens with ἐν, “on it”, and a verb of making: ἔτευξε, “made” (18.483), ποίησε, “made” (18.490, 18.573, 18.587), ἐτίθει, “set” (18.541, 18.550, 18.561, 18.607), and once ποίκιλλε, “inlaid” (18.590). The materials are named as the scenes go: gold for the two gods who lead the ambush, the plowland, the vineyard, the cattle and their herdsmen, and the dancers' knives (18.517, 18.549, 18.562, 18.574, 18.577, 18.598); silver for the vine poles and the straps of the knives (18.563, 18.598); tin for the vineyard's fence and for the cattle (18.565, 18.574); and κύανος, a dark blue, for the vineyard's trench, though it is not among the metals he casts into the fire (18.564; Leaf on 18.564; see [[kyanos]] and [[tin]]).

## The scenes

**The earth, the sky and the sea.**

{{quote:28b36646-3452-5c0b-bcd0-801e7ec51533}}

The sun is ἀκάμας, “tireless”, as on the evening when Hera sent it down unwilling (18.239, 18.484; see [[helios]]). τείρεα, “constellations”, occurs only here in Homer (Leaf on 18.485). The stars are those by which Odysseus steers from Calypso's island, where the Pleiades and late-setting Boötes are named and the three verses on the Bear return (*Odyssey* 5.272–275; Leaf on 18.486). For the stars see [[pleiades]], [[hyades]], [[orion]] and [[the-bear]], and for the moon [[moon]]; for the Bear that “alone has no share in the baths of Oceanus”, which Aristotle explains as a metaphor, see [[alone-without-a-share]].

**The city at peace.** In one of “two cities of mortal men, / fine ones” (see [[the-two-cities]]) there are weddings and banquets: brides are led by torchlight through the city to the bridal song, young men whirl in the dance to flutes and lyres, and the women stand at their doors and marvel (18.490–496; see [[marriage]]). The people are gathered in the place of assembly, where two men dispute the penalty for a man who has perished, and the elders rise in turn to give judgment, with two talents of gold for the straightest (18.497–508; see [[the-trial-on-the-shield]]).

**The city at war.** Two armies sit around the other city, divided whether to sack it or to divide in two all its wealth (18.509–512), the terms that Hector will think of offering Achilles (22.117–120; Leaf on 18.511). The besieged leave their wives, children and old men on the wall and go out to lie in ambush, led by Ares and Pallas Athena, golden and taller than the men (18.513–519; see [[ambush]]). Leaf calls the two gods “the only distinctively Hellenic touch in the shield” (on 18.516). At a river where the herds come to drink they kill the two herdsmen, who were playing their pipes and foresaw no trick, and cut off the cattle and the sheep; the besiegers ride to the din, and the two sides fight beside the river (18.520–534). Strife, Tumult and Doom are in the throng, and Doom drags a dead man by the feet, her cloak red with the blood of men (18.535–538; see [[strife]], [[tumult]] and [[ker-doom]]); the four verses stand almost word for word in the Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles* (*Shield* 156–159; see [[shield-of-heracles]]). The men “were in the throng and fought like living mortals, / and they dragged away the bodies of one another's dead” (18.539–540).

**The fields.** On a broad fallow, thrice plowed, the plowmen wheel their teams, and whenever they reach the end of the field a man puts a cup of honey-sweet wine into their hands (18.541–547; see [[the-plowing]]). Behind them the field grows dark:

{{quote:2404a644-f3fc-52c3-9ba4-11012ea588ca}}

Leaf took the darkening for some shading of the gold, by alloy or by glaze, and thought that the poet had an actual work in mind (on 18.548). On a king's domain hired laborers reap, binders tie the sheaves and boys carry the handfuls, while the king stands by the swath in silence, holding his scepter, glad at heart, and heralds make ready a feast under an oak (18.550–560; see [[the-kings-harvest]], [[kings]] and [[scepter]]). In a golden vineyard heavy with black grapes, fenced with tin, girls and young men carry the fruit in woven baskets, and a boy plays the lyre and sings the Linus song while the others follow, dancing (18.561–572; see [[the-vintage]] and [[the-linus-song]]).

**The herds.** Cattle of gold and tin hurry lowing from the byre to pasture beside a sounding river, with four golden herdsmen and nine dogs; two lions seize a bull from the front of the herd and gulp down its entrails and black blood, and the dogs stand close and bark but will not bite (18.573–586; see [[the-herd-and-the-lions]], [[cattle]] and [[lion]]). The lions are those of the poem's similes: in *Iliad* 17 a lion seizes the best cow of a herd and “gulps down her blood and all her entrails”, while dogs and herdsmen shout from far off and will not come near (17.61–67; see [[lion-among-cattle]]). Last of the countryside is a great pasture for white sheep in a lovely glen, with farmsteads, roofed huts and pens (18.587–589; see [[the-sheep-pasture]] and [[sheep]]).

**The dance.** Hephaestus inlays a dancing floor “like the one that once in wide Cnossus / Daedalus fashioned for Ariadne of the lovely tresses” (18.590–592; see [[the-dancing-floor-of-ariadne]]). On it young men and maidens dance with their hands on each other's wrists, the girls in fine linen and garlands, the young men in tunics glistening with oil, with golden knives on silver straps; now they run in a ring as lightly as a potter's wheel turns, and now in lines toward one another, while a great throng stands around, a singer plays the lyre, and two tumblers whirl in the midst (18.593–606; see [[dance]] and [[the-potters-wheel]]). Whether the singer belongs to the text is an old question (see the translation's note on 18.604–606 and [[the-singer-on-the-shield]]).

**Oceanus.**

“Okeanos surrounds the pictured as he surrounds the real world” (Leaf on 18.608; see [[oceanus]]).

## In the poem

**Made, not finished.** The shield is not described as a finished thing. It comes into being as Hephaestus makes it, with a verb of making at the head of each scene and the god's title, “the renowned god, lame in both legs”, returning near the end (18.587, 18.590; see [[lame-in-both-legs]]). Nor do the scenes stand still. Brides are led through the streets, the people cheer, the elders spring up to speak, armies march and fight, plowmen turn at the headland, cattle hurry to pasture and dogs bark (18.492–506, 18.516–540, 18.543–547, 18.575–586). There is sound: the bridal song, flutes and lyres, the herdsmen's pipes, the lowing of the cattle, and the Linus song (18.493–495, 18.526, 18.575, 18.570–572). Twice the narrator names the art in the life: the soldiers fight “like living mortals” (18.539), and the gold field looks plowed, “a wonder beyond all” (18.548–549).

**Wonder.** The armor was promised as a wonder. Hephaestus tells Thetis that her son will have armor “such that many a man / of the many men will marvel at it, whoever sees it” (18.466–467), and the armor he lost was “a wonder to see” (θαῦμα ἰδέσθαι, 18.83), as are the god's self-moving tripods (18.377; see [[thaumaston]]). When Thetis lays the new arms before Achilles at dawn they ring out, the Myrmidons tremble and none dares look at them, and Achilles delights in looking at the δαίδαλα, the intricate work (19.12–19).

**A world without names.** No hero of the war is on the shield, and no person or place is named but Daedalus, Ariadne and Cnossus, in the comparison for the dance; Leaf calls 18.591–592 “unique in the Shield for their local and mythological allusion” (on 18.590). Its gods are Ares and Athena, who lead the ambush, and the powers of battle, Strife, Tumult and Doom (18.516, 18.535). Yet much on it is the matter of the Iliad. The trial turns on the penalty for a killing, the custom that Ajax set before Achilles in Book 9 (9.632–636; see [[poine]]); the besiegers debate the division of a city's wealth (18.511; 22.117–120); the dead are dragged from the fight (18.537, 18.540), as Patroclus' body was fought over (see [[the-fight-for-the-body-of-patroclus]]); and the lions and the reapers are those of the poem's similes (17.61–67; 11.67–69; see [[the-reapers]]). Most of it is peace: weddings, a lawsuit, plowing, harvest, vintage, herding and a dance, beside a single siege.

**The night it is made.** The shield is made in the night after Patroclus' death, while the Achaeans lament over his body and the Trojans camp on the plain (18.314–315, 18.354–355; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]] and [[the-third-day-of-battle]]). Thetis asked for it for a son she called swift-fated (18.458), and Hephaestus wished that he could hide him from death as surely as he could give him armor (18.464–467; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Achilles takes up the shield after the greaves, the corselet and the sword, and its gleam shines far like the moon, and like a fire that sailors see burning high on a mountain (19.373–380). The verse that ends the simile (19.379) is the verse of the flame from Achilles' head at the ditch, “so from the head of Achilles the blaze reached the upper air” (18.214), with σάκεος, “shield”, in place of κεφαλῆς, “head”.

In the fighting the shield holds. Aeneas' spear does not break it, for the gold, the god's gift, holds the point (20.259–268), and the same verse is said of Asteropaeus' spear (21.164–165); in the river, though, the stream falls on the shield and pushes Achilles back (21.240–241). Four verses that give the five layers as metals, two of bronze, two of tin and one of gold, with the spear stopped in the gold, were athetized by Aristarchus (20.269–272; see the note on *Iliad* 20 and [[the-bronze-spear-stopped]]). Hector's spear strikes the middle of the shield and rebounds (22.290–291), and Achilles comes at him with the shield, fine and intricately worked, before his chest (22.313–314).

In the *Odyssey* the arms of Achilles, set as a prize by his mother, are judged between Ajax and Odysseus (*Odyssey* 11.543–551; see [[the-judgment-of-arms]]).

Other worked metal in Homer is smaller. Agamemnon's shield has ten circles of bronze and twenty bosses of tin, with a Gorgon, Terror and Fear (11.32–37). Heracles' golden baldric in the underworld shows bears, boars and lions, battles and slaughters (*Odyssey* 11.609–612), and Odysseus' golden brooch a hound holding a fawn, at which everyone marveled, how, though they were gold, the hound throttled the fawn and the fawn struggled to escape (*Odyssey* 19.226–231).

## Outside Homer

Walter Leaf reports that Zenodotus rejected the whole description of the shield (on 18.483); for its early readers, among them Agallis of Corcyra and Maximus of Tyre, who took the two cities for particular places, see [[title-of-book-18]]. The singer of 18.604–605, which editors since Wolf have printed on the authority of Athenaeus, is discussed under [[the-singer-on-the-shield]]. Later poets made shields on this model: the Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles*, which shares four verses with this one (see [[shield-of-heracles]]), and Virgil, whose Vulcan makes a shield for Aeneas at the request of Venus (*Aeneid* 8.370–453, 8.608–731; see [[virgil-shield-of-aeneas]]). Ovid's Ulysses speaks of the carvings of the shield in the contest for the arms (see [[ovid-the-carvings-of-the-shield]]). Pausanias lists the dance of Ariadne, carved in white stone, among the works of Daedalus at Cnossus (Pausanias 9.40.3; see [[the-dancing-floor-of-ariadne]] and [[pausanias-the-dance-of-ariadne]]).
