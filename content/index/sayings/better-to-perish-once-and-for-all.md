**The choice.** Ajax began this speech with the cry “Shame, Argives!” and the stakes, “either we perish / or we are saved” (15.502–503; see [[aidos]]); Hector is calling his men not to a dance but to fight (15.508; see [[not-to-a-dance-but-to-fight]]). He ends with a counsel and a maxim. The counsel is close combat, “to mingle our hands and our might with theirs hand to hand” (15.510; see [[menos]]); the maxim compares two deaths. ἕνα χρόνον, literally “at one time”, is a death that comes at once and settles the matter, “or to live”; στρεύγεσθαι, “to be squeezed out, drained away”, is a slow death by exhaustion. What makes the slow death worse is the enemy: “by worse men than we are” (ὑπʼ ἀνδράσι χειροτέροισιν, 15.513). Ajax counts the Trojans inferior even as they drive the Achaeans to their ships.

**Its kin.** The alternative of the first verse, ἢ ἀπολέσθαι … ἠὲ βιῶναι, closes the verse as it did in Nestor's words to Diomedes in the night of Book 10, when everything stood “on the edge of a razor”, whether grim destruction should come on the Achaeans, “or life” (ἠὲ βιῶναι, 10.173–174; see [[on-the-razors-edge]]). The second verse returns in the Odyssey. Eurylochus, urging the starving companions to eat the cattle of the Sun, would rather lose his life at once gulping at a wave “than be slowly drained away on a desert island” (ἢ δηθὰ στρεύγεσθαι ἐὼν ἐν νήσῳ ἐρήμῃ, *Odyssey* 12.350–351). The two verbs, ἀπολέσθαι and στρεύγεσθαι, set the same choice in battle and at sea: a quick end, even an end by drowning, is better than wasting away. Eurylochus' choice destroys the companions, who drown when Zeus wrecks their ship (*Odyssey* 12.403–419); Ajax holds out at the ships until Hector shears off his spear-point and the first ship takes fire (16.102–123).
