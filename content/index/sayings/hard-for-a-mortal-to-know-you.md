**The answer.** Athena has just told Odysseus that whoever got past him in tricks would have to be crafty and a cheat, “even if it were a god who met you” (*Odyssey* 13.291–292; see [[crafty-would-he-be]]). He answers with the same verb: it is hard for a mortal who meets her, ἀντιάσαντι, to know her (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 13.292, compares the two verses). No god could outwit him, and he cannot see through her. καὶ μάλʼ ἐπισταμένῳ, “even one who knows a great deal”, is himself, and σὲ γὰρ αὐτὴν παντὶ ἐΐσκεις gives the reason: αὐτήν is “yourself”, her person, as αὐτόν is the person of Odysseus whom she makes unknown (Monro on *Odyssey* 13.190, quoting this verse). ἐΐσκω is “make like”, the verb in which Odysseus likened Nausicaa to Artemis (*Odyssey* 6.152) and in which Alcinous refused to liken the stranger to a cheat (*Odyssey* 11.363). The goddess has met him in the book as a young herdsman and stands before him as a tall woman (*Odyssey* 13.221–225, 13.288–289; see [[shape-changing]]).

**Gods and mortals.** The verses state for the Odyssey a rule that its gods keep. Athena comes to Telemachus as Mentes and as Mentor, and to Odysseus on Scheria as a girl carrying a water jar (*Odyssey* 1.105, 2.268, 7.19–20; see [[in-the-likeness-of-mentor]]), and Telemachus does not see her at the door of the hut where his father does, “for the gods by no means appear in plain sight to all” (*Odyssey* 16.161, a plain rendering; see [[gods-are-hard-to-face]]). Between gods it is otherwise: Calypso knew Hermes at sight, “for the gods are not unknown to one another” (*Odyssey* 5.79; see [[the-gods-are-not-unknown-to-one-another]]). The form of the verse is that of Menelaus' maxim on Pharos, “for a god is hard for a mortal man to overpower” (ἀργαλέος … θεὸς βροτῷ ἀνδρὶ δαμῆναι, *Odyssey* 4.397; see [[a-god-is-hard-for-a-mortal-to-overpower]]); in the Iliad Ajax son of Oileus knows Poseidon by the movements of his feet and legs as he goes away, “and gods are easy to know” (13.71–72; see [[seeing-the-gods]]).

## Outside Homer

The Homeric *Hymn to Demeter* tells the same of a goddess in disguise. Grieving for her daughter, Demeter goes among the cities of men “marring her form for a long time, and no one of men / or of deep-girdled women knew her when they saw her”, and sits by the road at the Maiden's Well like an old woman; the daughters of Celeus come for water, “and they did not know her; for gods are hard for mortals to see” (οὐδʼ ἔγνον· χαλεποὶ δὲ θεοὶ θνητοῖσιν ὁρᾶσθαι, *Hymn to Demeter* 94–111; see [[hymn]] and [[demeter]]). When she reveals herself she changes her size and her form, “thrusting old age away” (*Hymn to Demeter* 275–276).
