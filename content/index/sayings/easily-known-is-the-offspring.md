**The son of a wise father.** Peisistratus has asked that the weeping stop until morning, granting tears to the dead as their only prize (*Odyssey* 4.190–202; see [[the-only-prize-of-miserable-mortals]] and [[peisistratus]]). Menelaus answers that he has said all that a wise man would say and do, even an older one, and gives the reason, τοίου γὰρ καὶ πατρός, “for your father too is such a man”: τοίου resumes πεπνυμένος, and the son speaks wisely because he is sprung from such a father (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.206). The maxim widens the thought from Nestor's son to any man whom Zeus favors, and Nestor is the example: he has been granted all his days to grow old in ease in his halls, and his sons to be men of good sense and the best with the spear (*Odyssey* 4.209–211; see [[nestor]], [[olbos]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). For λιπαρῶς γηρασκέμεν, “to grow old in ease”, Merry compares Teiresias' prophecy that death will come to Odysseus himself when he is worn out with sleek old age, γήραι ὕπο λιπαρῷ, with his people prosperous around him (*Odyssey* 11.134–137; on 4.210); Odysseus used to pray, Eurycleia says, that he might reach a sleek old age and raise a glorious son (*Odyssey* 19.367–368).

**At marriage and at birth.** ἐπικλώθω is the gods' spinning of a man's portion (see [[moira]]); at Pylos Telemachus used it of himself and his father, “But the gods have spun no such prosperity for me, / for my father and for me” (*Odyssey* 3.208–209). Merry renders γαμέοντί τε γεινομένῳ τε “at bridal and birth”, an order the metre requires, and suggests that Menelaus, speaking of a man's offspring, thinks first of his fortunate marriage and then adds that the same fortune has attended him from his birth; he rejects Eustathius' attempt to take γεινομένῳ as “begetting”, and quotes Hesiod's Fates, who give mortals at their birth to have good and evil (on 4.208; *Theogony* 218–219; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]).

**Easily known.** ῥεῖα δʼ ἀρίγνωτος opens Hector's maxim in the Iliad, “Easily known among men is the help that comes from Zeus” (*Iliad* 15.490; see [[the-help-of-zeus-is-easy-to-see]]): the god's favor shows itself, there in a battle, here in a son. Menelaus has said as much at the meal. The lineage of their parents is not lost in the two young men, “since base men would not beget such sons as you” (*Odyssey* 4.62–64; see [[genee]]); and when Telemachus refuses the horses he tells him, “You are of good blood, dear child, by the way you speak” (*Odyssey* 4.611). In the Ithacan assembly Athena, as Mentor, had said the other thing: “Few sons are like their father; / most are worse, and few are better than their father” (*Odyssey* 2.276–277; see [[few-sons-are-like-their-father]]).
