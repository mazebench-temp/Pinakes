**The verdict and the exception.** The soul of Agamemnon has told Odysseus how Aegisthus killed him at a feast “with my ruinous wife”, and how Clytemnestra killed Cassandra beside him and would not close his eyes (*Odyssey* 11.409–426; see [[agamemnon]], [[clytemnestra]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). From the one deed he draws a rule for all women: “So there is nothing more dreadful and more shameless than a woman / who puts such deeds as these into her heart”, and she “has poured shame on herself and on those to come hereafter, / the womenfolk, even on one who does good” (*Odyssey* 11.427–434). The counsel follows from it, τῷ, “wherefore”, the inference that all women are false; νῦν sets Odysseus' present case against the past, and γυναικί περ is “even your wife” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.441). He is never to be kind even to her, nor tell her “every word that you know well”, μῦθον meaning all that is in his mind, “but tell her one part, and let another part be hidden” (*Odyssey* 11.441–443; Merry on 11.442). Then he makes the exception at once: death will not come to Odysseus from his wife, for Penelope “has great good sense, and knows sound counsels in her heart” (*Odyssey* 11.444–446; see [[penelope]]). The second counsel and the maxim come after he has spoken of the son his wife would not let him see (*Odyssey* 11.447–456).

**The words.** πιστά is “trustworthiness”, a neuter plural used as an abstract noun, as ἶσα is “amends” (*Odyssey* 2.203–204) and φυκτά “escape” (*Odyssey* 8.299); Merry sets beside the sentiment Hesiod's verse, “whoever trusts a woman trusts thieves” (ὃς δὲ γυναικὶ πέποιθε, πέποιθʼ ὅ γε φηλήτῃσιν, *Works and Days* 375; Merry on 11.456; see [[hesiod]]). οὐκέτι is “no longer”, “any more”.

**Doubted verses.** A scholium in the Harleian manuscript says that “not even these were found in most copies, as conflicting with what lies before them”. It stands at 11.452, but Dindorf, La Roche and Nauck refer it to 11.454–456, and Merry brackets those verses: the note expresses the feeling that after such praise of Penelope Agamemnon cannot consistently tell Odysseus to be so suspicious of her (Merry's critical note on 11.452 and his note on 11.452; see the Odyssey translation's note on 11.454–456).

**What Odysseus does.** The counsel describes the rest of the poem. Odysseus is set ashore in Ithaca asleep and alone, and when Athena has told him where he is she observes that another man coming home from his wanderings would have been eager to see his children and his wife, “but it is not yet your pleasure to learn or to hear, / before you have made trial of your wife yourself” (*Odyssey* 13.333–336, a plain rendering), and he keeps himself hidden from Penelope until the suitors are dead. In the last book, when the soul of Agamemnon hears among the dead how they died, he sets her against his own wife: the fame of Penelope's excellence will never perish, and the immortals will make a gracious song of her for men, “not as the daughter of Tyndareus devised evil deeds”, whose song will be hateful and who will bring a harsh report on the womenfolk, “even on one who does good”, in the words of *Odyssey* 11.434 (*Odyssey* 24.192–202, a plain rendering; see [[husbands-and-wives]] and [[truth-and-deception]]).

## Outside Homer

For the verdict that comes before the counsel, that nothing is more dreadful than a woman who plots such deeds, Merry compares Hesiod, “none more chilling than a bad one” (τῆς δʼ αὖτε κακῆς οὐ ῥίγιον ἄλλο, *Works and Days* 703), Hippolytus' “by this it is plain that a woman is a great evil” (Euripides, *Hippolytus* 627), and Medea's words to herself, that women are by nature most helpless at noble deeds and “the most skillful contrivers of all evils” (Euripides, *Medea* 407–409; Merry on 11.427; see [[euripides]]). The chorus of the *Medea* answers the old singers at once: now it is men whose counsels are treacherous, and faith sworn by the gods no longer stands firm; honor is coming to the race of women, ill-sounding report will no longer hold them, and the Muses of the ancient singers will cease to hymn their faithlessness (*Medea* 410–422).
