**Between tears and stories.** The company at Sparta has wept: Menelaus for Odysseus, Telemachus for his father, Helen, and Peisistratus for his brother Antilochus (*Odyssey* 4.100–202; see [[tears]] and [[lament]]). Menelaus has turned them back to supper (*Odyssey* 4.212–215), and Helen has cast into the wine a drug “that brought forgetfulness of every ill”, so that whoever drank it would not let a tear fall for that day (*Odyssey* 4.219–226; see [[nepenthe]] and [[helen]]). Then she speaks, and the maxim stands between her greeting and her bidding, which the translation sets off with dashes. Merry reports Fäsi's view that ἀτάρ answers ἐσθλῶν, “good men indeed, but [even virtue has its trials, for] the god grants”, and Classen's, for whom the passage shows the parenthetical style of Homeric syntax; he himself takes ἀτάρ as anticipating the clause that gives the reason for the main one, ἦ τοι νῦν δαίνυσθε, “now feast”, as Andromache begins Ἕκτορ ἀτὰρ σύ μοί ἐσσι πατὴρ (*Iliad* 6.429) (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.236). What comes, good or evil, is Zeus' to give; so the company may feast and take delight in stories, ἐοικότα, “what is fitting”, things suited to that delight (Merry on 4.239). The stories she tells are of the war's evils, Odysseus' trial in Troy and her own blind folly (*Odyssey* 4.240–264; see [[ate]] and [[odysseus]]).

**For he can do all things.** The second half-verse comes again from Eumaeus, who gives his guest what there is, since the god will give one thing and let another be, as he wishes in his heart, for he can do all things (δύναται γὰρ ἅπαντα, *Odyssey* 14.444–445), and Hermes says of the moly that it is hard for mortal men to dig, but the gods can do all things (θεοὶ δέ τε πάντα δύνανται, *Odyssey* 10.305–306). Merry calls such sayings the theoretical view of the gods, and shows that the poem's facts do not bear it out: the gods' saving power is limited, and each god has his own measure of strength (on 4.379; see [[the-gods-know-all-things]] and [[not-even-the-gods-can-ward-off-death]]). At Sparta Menelaus will change Telemachus' gift, “for I can” (δύναμαι γάρ, *Odyssey* 4.612), and the phantom says of Athena that “she has the power” (δύναται γάρ, *Odyssey* 4.827).

**Good and evil from Zeus.** The thought runs through both poems. Achilles tells Priam of the two jars on the floor of Zeus, from which he gives some men a mixture of evil and good and others evils only (*Iliad* 24.527–533; see [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]). Telemachus tells his mother that the singers are not to blame, “but Zeus, I think, is to blame, who gives / to toiling men, to each, as he wills” (*Odyssey* 1.348–349; see [[who-is-to-blame]]), and Nausicaa tells the shipwrecked stranger that Zeus himself deals out prosperity to good men and bad, to each as he wishes (*Odyssey* 6.188–190; see [[olbos]]). The Muse gave Demodocus good and evil, ἀγαθόν τε κακόν τε: she took his eyes and gave him sweet song (*Odyssey* 8.63–64). In Ithaca Penelope says it of herself: “the Olympian has given me sorrows beyond / all the women who were born and raised with me” (*Odyssey* 4.722–723; see [[algos]] and [[zeus]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod gives the same office to the Fates, Clotho, Lachesis and Atropos, “who give to mortals at their birth to have good and evil” (ἀγαθόν τε κακόν τε, *Theogony* 218–219; compare 905–906; see [[hesiod]]); Merry quotes the verses on Menelaus' γεινομένῳ, “at his birth”, in the maxim on Nestor's sons (*Odyssey* 4.208; Merry on 4.208; see [[easily-known-is-the-offspring]]).
