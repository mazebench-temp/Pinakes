The truce has been broken and Menelaus wounded. Agamemnon names the pledges of the oath, the blood of lambs, the libations of unmixed wine and the right hands, and declares that none of them is in vain. The time of the punishment is uncertain; the punishment is not. αὐτίκα, “at once”, is set against ὀψέ, “late”, and the aorist ἀπέτισαν, “they pay”, states what always happens. The payment is the one the curse named when the wine was poured in Book 3: their brains poured out like wine, theirs and their children's, and their wives subdued by others (3.300–301; see [[poine]]). Agamemnon follows it with the prophecy that makes it particular: “a day will come when sacred Ilios will perish” (4.164–165; see [[a-day-will-come]]).

The narrator of Book 3 had already set the time: the son of Cronus “was not yet going to fulfill their prayer” (3.302). Delay belongs to the gods' justice in Homer. The portent at Aulis was “late in coming, late in fulfillment” (ὄψιμον ὀψιτέλεστον, 2.325), and a grudge, as Calchas said, is kept until it is fulfilled (1.82; see [[kotos]]). See [[oaths-and-their-breaking]] and [[prophecy]].

## Outside Homer

Solon made the same argument about the justice of Zeus: one man pays at once, another later, and if the guilty escape, the penalty comes all the same, and the innocent pay for them, their children or their descendants after them (fr. 13.25–32 West). Plutarch devoted a dialogue to the question why the gods punish late (*On the Delays of the Divine Vengeance*).
