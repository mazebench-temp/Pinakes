Nestor has told the assembly to yield to night and take supper, told the young men to keep watch beside the ditch, outside the wall, and told Agamemnon to feast the elders and follow the best counsel (9.65–75; see [[yield-to-night]], [[night-watch]] and [[boule]]). He ends with the reason. The enemy are burning many fires close by the ships, the fires the Trojans lit on the plain at the end of Book 8 (8.553–565), “who could rejoice at this?” Then the line: the night will decide. διαρραίω is to smash utterly, to break apart; elsewhere in the Iliad it is what the attacker is eager to do to the enemy (διαρραῖσαι μεμαῶτες, 2.473, 11.713, 11.733), and in the Odyssey what Telemachus fears the suitors will do to his whole house (*Odyssey* 2.48–49). σαόω is to keep safe. Nestor puts the army's whole fate on one night and two verbs.

**The same night.** The night of Book 9 runs on into Book 10, and Day 26 dawns only at 11.1. Odysseus puts the same choice to Achilles in the hut: “it is in doubt whether we save or lose the well-benched ships” (ἐν δοιῇ δὲ σαωσέμεν ἢ ἀπολέσθαι, 9.230–231). After the embassy has failed, Nestor wakes Diomedes and puts it again, in the image of a blade: “For now it stands for all of us on the edge of a razor, whether grim destruction comes on the Achaeans, or life” (10.173–174; see [[on-the-razors-edge]]). The night does not settle it. The embassy fails, the night raid of Book 10 brings back the horses of Rhesus, and the next day's fighting drives the Achaeans back to the ships.
