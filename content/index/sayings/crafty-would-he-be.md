**The maxim.** The young shepherd has told Odysseus that he is in Ithaca, and Odysseus has answered with a long lie, the story of a Cretan exile (*Odyssey* 13.237–286; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]] and [[truth-and-deception]]). The shepherd, who is Athena, answers it with a general rule: whoever should get past him in trickery would have to be crafty and a cheat. κερδαλέος, “crafty”, goes with πολυκερδής, “very crafty”, the word of his mind a few verses before, and with κέρδεα, “crafty ways”, in her next sentence (*Odyssey* 13.255, 13.297, 13.299; D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 13.291; see [[kerdos]]). ἐπίκλοπος, “a cheat”, is literally “thievish”: Hector throws the word at Achilles, “but you were a glib talker and a cheat with words” (22.281), and Alcinous had used it of the stranger to deny it, “this we do not judge you to be when we look at you, / a deceiver and a cheat” (*Odyssey* 11.363–364; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). παρέρχομαι, “get past”, is a word of contests, of the runner who passes another (*Odyssey* 8.230) and of the driver whom no one can pass once he has passed the others at the turning post (23.345; see [[cunning-not-strength]]); in the quarrel Achilles says to Agamemnon, “do not try to trick me. You will not outwit me” (κλέπτε νόῳ, ἐπεὶ οὐ παρελεύσεαι, 1.132). The last clause raises the stakes, “even if a god presented himself”, “even were it a god” (Monro on *Odyssey* 13.292): the god who meets him is the goddess who is speaking, and Odysseus turns the words back on her, “It is hard, goddess, for a mortal who meets you to know you” (*Odyssey* 13.312; see [[hard-for-a-mortal-to-know-you]]).

**The reproach.** The address that follows is admiring. σχέτλιε, “hard man”, is said in a friendly and admiring tone (Monro on *Odyssey* 13.293); ποικιλομῆτα, “of subtle wiles”, is the one vocative of that epithet in Homer (see [[of-subtle-wiles]]); and δόλων ἆτʼ, “insatiable of tricks”, is how the Trojan Socus hailed Odysseus in the Iliad, “Much-praised Odysseus, insatiable of tricks and toil” (11.430; see [[dolos]]). Even in his own land, she says, he would not leave off his “thievish words, which are dear to you from the ground up”. πεδόθεν is like *funditus*, though the notion is probably that of constancy, as in ἔμπεδος; the vulgate's παιδόθεν, “from childhood”, is “easily explained by itacism” (Monro on *Odyssey* 13.295; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 13.295).

**Two of a kind.** “But come, let us no longer talk like this, since we both know / crafty ways; for you are by far the best of all mortals / in counsel and in words, and I among all the gods / am famed for cunning and crafty ways” (*Odyssey* 13.296–299; see [[metis]] and [[athena]]). The rule about the man becomes a pairing of the man and the goddess, the best of mortals and the most famous of the gods in the same art. Then the reproach turns back on him: “yet you did not know / Pallas Athena, daughter of Zeus, who always / stand by you in all your toils and guard you” (*Odyssey* 13.299–301). He outdoes every man in tricks, and does not know the goddess who stands beside him.

## Outside Homer

Proclus quotes the goddess's words about herself. Commenting on Plato's description of Athena as a lover of war and a lover of wisdom (*Timaeus* 24c–d), he says that she is a lover of wisdom as the intellect of the maker of the world and as wisdom separate and immaterial, “and so she is called Metis among the gods; she herself, at any rate, says of herself” that she is “famed for cunning and crafty ways” (*On the Timaeus* I 168 Diehl, quoting *Odyssey* 13.299; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]] and [[metis]]). Aristotle gives the goddess's choice as an example of a good: what one of the wise or the good has preferred, “as Athena chose Odysseus” (*Rhetoric* 1363a16–18; see [[aristotle]]).
