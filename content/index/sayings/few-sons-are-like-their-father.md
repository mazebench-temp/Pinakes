Telemachus has prayed on the shore to the god who came to him the day before, and Athena comes to him in the likeness of Mentor (*Odyssey* 2.260–269; see [[athena]] and [[mentor]]). She speaks first of a condition. If his father's noble might has been instilled in him, his journey will not be in vain; if he is not the son of Odysseus and Penelope, she does not expect him to accomplish what he has in mind (*Odyssey* 2.270–275). The rule follows with γάρ, “for”: few sons equal their fathers, most fall short, few surpass them. Then she draws her conclusion. Since he will be no coward and no fool, and the counsel of Odysseus has not wholly failed him, there is hope that he will accomplish these things (*Odyssey* 2.278–280; see [[metis]] and [[noos]]). The rule is a measure, and she places him among the few. See [[fathers-and-sons]] and [[telemachus]].

**Fathers and sons in the Iliad.** The Iliad's speakers often look back to better men. Nestor tells the quarreling kings that he once kept company with better men than they are (1.260–272; see [[men-of-today]]). Agamemnon rebukes Diomedes with his father, Tydeus: “but the son / he fathered is worse than he in battle, though better in assembly” (4.399–400), and Athena says it again, “Tydeus has begotten a son little like himself” (5.800). Sthenelus answers with the opposite boast, “We boast that we are far better than our fathers” (4.405; see [[better-than-our-fathers]]), and Hector prays for his son, “may someone say one day, ‘He is far better than his father’” (6.479). Athena's rule has room for each of these verdicts: a few sons like their fathers, most of them worse, a few better.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod uses the same adjective, in the epic form ὁμοίιος, among the signs of the end of his age of iron: οὐδὲ πατὴρ παίδεσσιν ὁμοίιος οὐδέ τι παῖδες, the father will not be at one with his children, nor the children with him. Guest and host, comrade and comrade, brother and brother will be at odds, and children will dishonor their parents as they grow old (*Works and Days* 182–188). His races of men decline from the first: the second, of silver, was far worse than the golden (πολὺ χειρότερον), like it neither in body nor in mind (*Works and Days* 127–129).
