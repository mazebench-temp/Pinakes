**The invitation.** When the young Phaeacians have competed in the running, the wrestling, the jumping, the discus and the boxing (*Odyssey* 8.120–130; see [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[contests]]), Laodamas, the king's son and the best boxer, proposes to ask the stranger whether he knows some contest. His build is good, but he has been broken by many evils, “for I, for my part, say there is nothing else worse than the sea / to unstring a man” (*Odyssey* 8.132–139; see [[nothing-worse-than-the-sea]] and [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]]). Euryalus approves, and Laodamas goes to the middle and invites the stranger (*Odyssey* 8.140–151). ἔοικε, “it is fitting” that he should know contests, “This is the primitive praise of γυμναστική” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.146), and the maxim gives the reason. ὄφρα κʼ ἔῃσιν is “as long as he lives” (Merry on 8.147): the fame of the feet and hands is fame in a man's lifetime. The feet are the running, πόδες in the book's list of contests, and the hands the boxing, the wrestling and the throw (*Odyssey* 8.103; see the translation's table of key words). The invitation ends with kindness: let him make trial and scatter the sorrows from his heart, for his escort is ready (*Odyssey* 8.149–151; see [[pompe]]).

**The stranger's answer.** “Sorrows are on my mind far more than contests”, he says, since he sits in their assembly begging for his homecoming (*Odyssey* 8.153–157; see [[algos]] and [[nostos]]). It takes Euryalus' taunt, that he looks like a merchant captain and not like an athlete, to make him throw the discus beyond all the marks (*Odyssey* 8.158–198; see [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]]), and then he claims every contest “there are among men”, the bow and the spear above all, and fears only the running, since the sea has loosened his limbs (*Odyssey* 8.214–233). Alcinous' answer admits the limits of his people: “we are not flawless boxers, nor wrestlers, / but we run swiftly with our feet” (*Odyssey* 8.246–247; see [[different-gifts]]).

**Fame of the games.** Homer's fame is mostly the fame of war and of the dead, as Achilles' fame will be imperishable because he will die young (*Iliad* 9.410–416; see [[kleos]] and [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]). The games give fame too, and the old remember it: Nestor recalls the funeral games of Amarynceus, where no man was a match for him, in boxing, wrestling, the footrace and the spear (*Iliad* 23.629–637; see [[the-funeral-games-of-amarynceus]]). Only Laodamas makes the feet and hands the greatest fame a man can have in his life.

## Outside Homer

Euripides answers the claim in a fragment of his *Autolycus*. Of the countless evils in Greece none is worse than the race of athletes, and the Greeks are wrong to hold gatherings in their honor: “For who, by wrestling well, what swift-footed man, / or one who lifted the discus or struck a jaw well, / ever saved his fathers' city by winning a crown?” Wise and good men should be crowned with leaves, and the man who leads his city well, and the man who by words puts an end to battles and civil strife (fr. 282 Nauck; the verses quoted, 16–18, in a plain rendering; see [[euripides]]). The four contests he names are four of the five of the Phaeacian games.
