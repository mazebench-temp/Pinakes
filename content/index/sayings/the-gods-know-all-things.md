**Twice.** Menelaus says it to Eidothea when she asks why he lets himself be held on the island while his companions starve (*Odyssey* 4.371–381), and again, in the same verses, to Proteus when the old man has been caught and asks what he wants (*Odyssey* 4.462–470; 4.379–381 = 4.468–470). Each time the god answers. Eidothea tells him of her father, who “knows the depths / of the whole sea” and will tell him the way home and “whatever evil and good has been done in your halls” (*Odyssey* 4.385–393; see [[eidothea]]); Proteus tells him the rite the gods require, and the fates of Ajax, Agamemnon and Odysseus (*Odyssey* 4.472–569; see [[proteus]] and [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]).

**A courteous hyperbole?** Merry offers three readings: a courteous hyperbole, a magnifying of the gods' knowledge against human ignorance, or, better, “the theoretical view of the gods”, which goes with the gods' power to do all things (θεοὶ δέ τε πάντα δύνανται, *Odyssey* 10.306) and with Helen's maxim of Zeus, “for he can do all things” (*Odyssey* 4.237; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]). The poem's own story does not bear the belief out, he argues. Zeus, to whom Aphrodite went to ask marriage for the daughters of Pandareus, knows well all things, the good and ill fortune of mortal men, as Penelope says in her prayer to Artemis (*Odyssey* 20.73–76); but Proteus, who knows all the depths of the sea, is quite witless of the trick devised against him, and the gods ponder and search their hearts as men do (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.379). On Pharos the old man counts the men in their sealskins among his seals, “and had no thought / in his heart that there was a trick” (*Odyssey* 4.452–453; see [[dolos]] and [[a-god-is-hard-for-a-mortal-to-overpower]]).

**What the gods know.** In Menelaus' tale the divine speakers know in their own fields: the Old Man of the Sea is νημερτής, “unerring” (*Odyssey* 4.349, 4.384), and he knows the sea, the gods' anger and the returns; the phantom, sent by Athena, will not say whether Odysseus is alive or dead (*Odyssey* 4.836–837; see [[it-is-bad-to-speak-empty-wind]]). The Sirens make the same boast to Odysseus, that they know all that the Argives and Trojans suffered at Troy and all that happens on the earth (*Odyssey* 12.189–191). See [[truth-and-deception]] and [[theos]].

## Outside Homer

Aristotle's example of the argument from the more and the less turns the maxim around: “If not even the gods know everything, / hardly can men” (*Rhetoric* 2.23, 1397b12–13; see [[not-even-the-gods-know-everything]]).
