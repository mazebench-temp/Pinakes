**The levy.** The gifts of the Phaeacians are already many. In Book 8 Alcinous bade each of the twelve kings who rule with him bring the stranger a mantle, a tunic and a talent of gold (*Odyssey* 8.390–393), and Arete laid them in a chest (*Odyssey* 8.438–441). When the tale is over he asks for more, a great tripod and a cauldron ἀνδρακάς, “man by man”, from each of the lords who drink the wine of the elders in his halls (*Odyssey* 13.7–14; see [[tripod]], [[cauldron]] and [[kings]]). The cost is not to be theirs. τισόμεθα is “we will repay ourselves” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 13.15), by gathering the price from the people (ἀγειρόμενοι κατὰ δῆμον), and the maxim gives the reason. “So spoke Alcinous, and his word pleased them” (*Odyssey* 13.16).

**The word.** προικός is “as a free gift”, without such repayment. Monro explains it as originally a partitive genitive, “of his bounty”, used as an adverb like the Attic προῖκα, “gratis”, and takes the Homeric form of the noun to be πρόϊξ; from it comes προΐκτης, “a gift-man”, “one who lives on doles” (on *Odyssey* 13.15). The word belongs in the Odyssey to the beggar: the king's free gift becomes the beggar's free meal when Odysseus goes back to the threshold “to taste of the Achaeans at no cost”, and Antinous calls him προΐκτης, “how bold and shameless a beggar you are!” (*Odyssey* 17.413, 17.449, plain renderings; also 17.352). The man to whom the lords of Scheria give tripods as a free gift will ask the lords of Ithaca for bread. In the false tale he tells Penelope, the Cretan who entertained Odysseus fed his companions in the same way, with barley meal and wine, “gathering them from the people” (δημόθεν … ἀγείρας, *Odyssey* 19.194–198, a plain rendering).

## Outside Homer

The Suda glosses the word, “as a gift”, that is, freely, and adds that “the poet calls every gift so”; the nominative is προίξ (Suda π 2983). προΐκτης it explains as “beggar” (Suda π 2432).
