Achilles' horses stand apart from the battle and weep for their charioteer. Neither Automedon's whip nor his words will move them; they stand fixed like a pillar on a tomb, their heads bowed to the ground and their manes trailing in the dust (17.426–440; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[tomb]]). Zeus sees them and pities them, and shakes his head and speaks to his own heart, as he did when he saw Hector putting on Achilles' armor (17.200, 17.441–442; see [[pity]]).

His question is about a gift. The gods gave the horses to Peleus, a mortal, while they are “ageless and immortal” (17.443–444; see [[immortal-and-ageless]] and [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]]), and the only end Zeus can see in it is that they should have sorrows (ἄλγεʼ) among unhappy men (17.445; see [[algos]]). The saying gives the reason. ὀϊζυρός is “miserable”, from ὀϊζύς, “woe”, and the comparative ὀϊζυρώτερον is formed against the usual rule; Walter Leaf puts it down to the meter, since the regular form could not stand in a hexameter (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.446). “All the things that breathe and creep upon the earth” are the living creatures, and the horses are among them as long as they live among men.

Zeus' pity has its limits. He puts might into the horses so that they carry Automedon safe to the ships, and Hector will not ride behind them; but he will still give the Trojans glory to kill until the sun goes down (17.448–455; see [[kydos]]).

**The gods on men.** Gods and men in the Iliad speak of mortals in the same way. Apollo will not fight Poseidon for the sake of wretched mortals (βροτῶν … δειλῶν), who are like leaves, flourishing for a while and then wasting away (21.462–466), as Glaucus told Diomedes that the generations of men are like the generations of leaves (6.146–149; see [[the-generations-of-leaves]]). Achilles tells Priam that the gods have spun for wretched mortals a life of sorrow, while they themselves are free from care (24.525–526; see [[gods-and-mortals]]).

**In the Odyssey.** Odysseus, a beggar in his own house, warns the suitor Amphinomus in the same frame: the earth nourishes nothing feebler than man, οὐδὲν ἀκιδνότερον γαῖα τρέφει ἀνθρώποιο, “of all the things that breathe and creep upon the earth”; a man thinks he will never suffer harm while the gods give him strength, and when they bring sorrows on him he bears them unwillingly (*Odyssey* 18.130–135). Leaf compares the couplet.
