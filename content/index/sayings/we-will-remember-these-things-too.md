**What will be remembered.** The verse ends the first part of Odysseus' speech in the strait, after the smoke and the roar have made the companions drop their oars (*Odyssey* 12.201–212; see [[not-without-knowledge-of-evils]]). Its argument is his own record: the Cyclops' cave was no smaller evil, and “by my prowess and my counsel and my mind, / we escaped” (*Odyssey* 12.211–212; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]], [[arete]], [[boule]] and [[noos]]). τῶνδε, “these things”, are the dangers that threaten, the strait they are about to pass, not the sufferings they have already borne: the escape from the cave is a memory now, and so this escape will be (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.212, following Ameis). The promise is half kept. The ship comes through, but Scylla takes six men from it (*Odyssey* 12.245–259), and of all who hear the promise only Odysseus lives to remember, telling the tale to the Phaeacians (see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).

**The turn of phrase.** μνήσεσθαι ὀίω, “I think, will remember”, ends a verse of the Iliad too. Achilles, renouncing his anger, tells Agamemnon that their quarrel was the better thing for Hector and the Trojans, “but the Achaeans, / I think, will long remember my strife and yours” (*Iliad* 19.63–64; see [[eris]]). Penelope will say of the house of her marriage, which she must leave if she marries again, that she thinks she will remember it even in her dreams (μεμνήσεσθαι ὀΐομαι, *Odyssey* 19.581 = 21.79, a plain rendering). Achilles foresees a memory of shame, Penelope of loss; Odysseus promises one of rescue.

**Remembered toils.** For the thought that past dangers are pleasant to remember once one is safe, Merry compares a line of Euripides, which he cites from Macrobius (on 12.212). Aristotle quotes the same line, “It is sweet to remember toils when one is safe”, and sets beside it Eumaeus' words to the disguised Odysseus, “For a man takes delight even in his sorrows afterward, remembering” (*Rhetoric* 1370b4–6, quoting *Odyssey* 15.400–401; see [[sweet-to-remember-toils]] and [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]]). Odysseus' verse says nothing yet of delight: it promises only that there will be a later time from which to look back.

## Outside Homer

Virgil ends Aeneas' speech to his shipwrecked men on the Libyan shore with the verse: *revocate animos, maestumque timorem / mittite: forsan et haec olim meminisse iuvabit*, “call back your courage and put away sad fear: perhaps one day it will please you to remember even these things” (*Aeneid* 1.202–203, a plain rendering; see [[virgil]] and [[aeneas]]). Merry, after Ameis, notes that the imitation is slightly different in meaning: Homer means by τῶνδε the dangers that threaten, Virgil by *haec* the sufferings they are already enduring (Merry on 12.212). Virgil also adds the pleasure, *iuvabit*, which Homer leaves out.
