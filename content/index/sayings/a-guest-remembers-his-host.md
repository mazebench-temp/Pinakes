**The occasion.** Telemachus, sent home by Athena in the night, wakes Peisistratus and bids him yoke the horses (*Odyssey* 15.44–47). Peisistratus will not drive through the dark night: dawn will soon come, and they must wait until Menelaus brings gifts and sets them in the chariot “and sends us off, speaking comfortingly with gentle words” (*Odyssey* 15.49–53; see [[peisistratus]], [[gentle-words]], [[guest-gifts]] and [[pompe]]). The maxim gives his reason. What a guest takes home from a house is the gifts and the farewell, and by them he remembers his host for the rest of his life. Monro found in the promised words a reason for Menelaus' offer, a moment later, to take Telemachus on a tour through Hellas and Argos, which Telemachus was not likely to accept: “it may be that ancient manners required some such speech from the host—the μῦθοι ἀγανοί promised by Pisistratus” (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 15.80–85).

**Remembrance.** The poem's farewells are full of it. Menelaus offered Telemachus a goblet, so that he might pour libations to the gods with it “remembering me all your days” (ἐμέθεν μεμνημένος ἤματα πάντα, *Odyssey* 4.591–592; see [[libation]]); Helen gives him her robe as “a remembrance of Helen's hands” (μνῆμʼ, *Odyssey* 15.126; see [[robe]]); Nausicaa bids the stranger farewell “so that someday, when you are in your native land, / you may remember me” (*Odyssey* 8.461–462; see [[nausicaa]]); and Iphitus' bow, given at the beginning of a guest-friendship, lay in Odysseus' halls “as a remembrance of a dear guest-friend” (*Odyssey* 21.40–41, a plain rendering). A gift is a remembrance that can be kept, a κειμήλιον laid up in the house, “such as dear guest-friends give to guest-friends” (*Odyssey* 1.311–313; see [[possessions]] and [[xenia]]).

**The host wronged.** The second verse ends as Menelaus' prayer before his duel with Paris: “so that any man of those born hereafter may shudder / to do evil to a host who has shown him friendship” (3.353–354; see [[paris]] and [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]]). The host who prayed for vengeance on the guest who wronged him is the host whom Peisistratus expects his guest to remember. The duty runs both ways: Odysseus at the Phaeacian games will not contend with Laodamas, “who would fight with one who makes him welcome?” (*Odyssey* 8.208–211; see [[strife-with-ones-host]]).
