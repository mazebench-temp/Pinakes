**The stand.** Hector has seized the stern of Protesilaus' ship and calls for fire (15.704–725). Ajax, hard pressed by missiles, draws back a little from the deck “to the seven-foot bench” and stands there on the watch, keeping off with his spear every Trojan who brings fire, and shouting to the Danaans (15.727–732; see [[ajax]] and [[stern]]). The speech is his last in the book, and it reasons by elimination: no helpers behind, no stronger wall, no city fitted with towers and no people to turn the battle (15.735–738; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[polis]]). The army sits “in the plain of the close-armored Trojans”, “with our backs to the sea, far from our native land” (15.739–740). What remains is in their hands.

**The light.** φόως, “light”, is in the Iliad the light of rescue that a man brings to his side. Ajax himself, when the gods had left the battle, was the first to break a Trojan battalion, “and he brought light to his companions” (φόως δʼ ἑτάροισιν ἔθηκεν, 6.6); Agamemnon urges Teucer to shoot on, “and you may become a light to the Danaans” (8.282), and Nestor hopes the same of Patroclus (11.797), who repeats the words to Achilles in the next book (16.39). In this book the word has already been seen in its plain sense: when Athena thrusts the mist from the Achaeans' eyes, “a great light came to them on both sides” (15.668–669; see [[achlys]]). Now Ajax gives the light to no single hero. It lies “in our hands” (ἐν χερσί), in the fighting of all. Hector, a few verses before, had promised the Trojans the ships as Zeus' gift, “a day worth all the rest” (15.719); Ajax promises the Achaeans nothing but what they do.

**Not in softness.** μειλιχίη, “gentleness, softness”, a noun found only here in the Iliad, belongs with μειλίχιος, the adjective of soothing words (see [[soothing-words]]); in war it would mean holding back. The verse sets the two words against each other, light in the hands and no softness in war, and the next verse shows the meaning: “He spoke, and in his fury pressed on with his sharp spear” (15.742). Whoever brought fire to the ships Ajax stabbed with his long spear, twelve men in front of the ships hand to hand (15.743–746). In the next book Hector shears off his spear-point and the first ship takes fire (16.114–123).
