## The terms

Aristotle gathers the definition “from what has been said” (1449b23–24), and each phrase recalls an earlier distinction. The object is “an action that is serious” ([[spoudaios]]), and complete and of a certain [[megethos|magnitude]]; the medium is language “embellished” with rhythm and melody and song, the kinds of embellishment used separately in different parts (1449b28–31); the manner is enactment, “by people acting and not through narration” (see [[modes-of-imitation]]). The last clause is new, and it is the one that has never stopped being argued over: through [[pity]] and [[phobos]] tragedy accomplishes “the [[katharsis]] of such feelings”. Aristotle promised to explain catharsis, and does not do so in the surviving text.

The definition yields the six parts of tragedy (1449b31–1450a15; see [[six-parts-of-tragedy]]), and the next chapters take up its words one by one: completeness and magnitude in chapter 7, the unity of the action in chapter 8, pity and fear in chapters 9 and 13–14.
