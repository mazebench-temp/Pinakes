**Two men.** Poseidon, angry for his grandson Amphimachus, meets Idomeneus at the huts in the likeness of Thoas, the Aetolian, and asks where the Achaeans' threats have gone (13.206–220; see [[poseidon]], [[thoas]] and [[where-have-our-boasts-gone]]). Idomeneus answers that no man is to blame; it must be the pleasure of Zeus that the Achaeans perish here (13.222–230; see [[who-is-to-blame]]). The god curses the man who holds back from fighting on this day, and tells Idomeneus to take his armor and come: “we must hurry at this together, / if we may be of some use, though we are only two” (13.232–236). καὶ δύʼ ἐόντε, “though they are only two”, is what Asius said of the two Lapiths who held the gate against him in Book 12, refusing to give way “until they have either killed or been taken” (12.170–172; see [[wasps-at-the-roadside]]). Two men can hold a gate; two men can be of use.

**The maxim.** συμφερτή, “brought together, joined”, is found only here (see the translation's note on 13.237). The ἀρετή of very poor fighters, λυγροί, counts for something when it is combined (see [[arete]]). Poseidon has used the same adjective a hundred lines earlier, when he told the leaders that he would not quarrel with a man who held back from war “if he were a wretch” (λυγρὸς ἐών, 13.118–119; see [[aidos]]). Then the wretch was beneath reproach; now even wretches, joined, are worth something. The second verse turns the maxim to the speaker's pride: if poor men together count, “we two would know how to fight even against brave men” (13.238). The god does not count himself among the wretched.

**Numbers in the maxims.** The Iliad has several sayings about how men add up. Diomedes asks for a companion on the night mission, since “when two go together”, one sees the advantage before the other (10.224–226; see [[when-two-go-together]]); Sarpedon tells his Lycians that “the work of more men is better” (12.412; see [[the-work-of-many]]); and the two Ajaxes call on every man, outstanding, middling or worse, since “men are by no means all alike in war” (12.269–271; see [[not-all-alike-in-war]]). Poseidon's maxim is the counterpart of the last: men are not all alike, but even the worst of them count when they stand together. The pair the god proposes is himself and Idomeneus. He goes back into the toil of men, and Idomeneus finds his second in Meriones, his attendant, with whom he goes into the battle (13.239–329; see [[idomeneus]] and [[meriones]]).
