{{quote:12d5ee10-0100-5b64-8333-5449959307b7}}

Automedon, alone in Achilles' chariot, has taken Alcimedon as his driver and got down to fight, and Hector and Aeneas, with Chromius and Aretus, are coming to take the immortal horses (17.459–496; see [[automedon]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). He tells Alcimedon to keep the horses close behind him, calls the two Ajaxes and Menelaus to leave the body to the best men and keep off the pitiless day from the living, and ends with the half-verse and his own part in it: “for I too will throw, and all this will be the care of Zeus” (17.501–515). He throws, and his spear goes through Aretus' shield and belt into his belly (17.516–524).

**The verse.** ἀλλʼ ἤτοι μὲν ταῦτα θεῶν ἐν γούνασι κεῖται is Hector's too, when he first faces Achilles: he knows that Achilles is the better man, but whether he may take his life with his spear, weaker though he is, lies on the knees of the gods (20.434–437). In the Odyssey Athena, in the likeness of Mentes, says it of whether Odysseus will come home and take vengeance on the suitors (*Odyssey* 1.267–268); Eurymachus answers Telemachus with it, of who will be king in Ithaca (*Odyssey* 1.400–401); and Telemachus says it to Eumaeus of the suitors who are eating up his house (*Odyssey* 16.127–129). Each speaker has a hope or a fear, and none claims the outcome.

**The knees.** Walter Leaf calls the phrase very obscure, and thinks it can hardly be separated from the custom of *Iliad* 6, where the Trojan women lay their finest robe on the knees of Athena's seated image (6.92, 6.273, 6.303; see [[the-robe-for-athena]] and [[temple]]). A gift laid there passed for ever out of man's power into the god's, and the phrase would say the same of all that a man yields to divine government: Automedon means, in Leaf's words, “over the disposing of such matters I claim no longer any power” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.514). The knees are also what a suppliant clasps (see [[gounata]] and [[supplication]]).
