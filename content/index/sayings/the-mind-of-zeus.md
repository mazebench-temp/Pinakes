Diomedes has taken Nestor into his chariot and driven at Hector, and the Trojans would have been penned in their city like lambs, had not Zeus seen it. He thunders and sends a gleaming thunderbolt down in front of Diomedes' horses; they cower under the chariot, and the reins slip from Nestor's hands (8.130–137; see [[penned-like-lambs]], [[would-have-had-not]] and [[thunderbolt]]). The old man reads the sign at once, and his counsel has three parts. Turn the horses to flight, since “no help from Zeus” goes with them (see [[alke]]). Zeus grants glory to Hector “today”, and “afterward, if he wishes”, will give it to the Achaeans (see [[kydos]] and [[victory-shifts]]); the words σήμερον· ὕστερον αὖτε, “today; afterward”, are the ones in which Apollo and then Hector stopped the fighting for a day in Book 7 (7.30, 7.291). And no man can hold back the mind of Zeus. νόος is the mind with its intent (see [[noos]]), and εἰρύσσαιτο is “hold back, check”: what Zeus has in mind, no strength of a man can stop.

Diomedes grants that the counsel is “fair and right”, and fears only what Hector will say of him among the Trojans (8.146–150; see [[may-the-earth-gape]] and [[retreat]]). The reason Nestor gives, ἐπεὶ ἦ πολὺ φέρτερός ἐστι, “since he is far the stronger”, is given again by a god sixty lines later, when Poseidon refuses Hera's proposal that the gods who help the Danaans should hold off Zeus: “I would not wish that we should fight against Zeus, son of Cronus, we others, since he is far the stronger” (8.210–211; see [[poseidon]]). Hera herself had said it to Zeus in Book 4: “I accomplish nothing by grudging, since you are far the stronger” (4.56), and in Book 22 Priam says it of Achilles, begging Hector not to wait for him alone (22.38–40). The old man and the god yield to Zeus in the same half-line, and Zeus had told the gods at dawn what it means: “So far am I above the gods, and above men” (8.27; see [[the-golden-rope]]).

**Later.** The narrator states Nestor's rule when Patroclus forgets Achilles' warning and pursues the Trojans toward his death: the mind of Zeus is always stronger than men's; he puts even a brave man to flight and takes away his victory easily, and at times he himself rouses a man to fight (16.684–691). Hector says the same lines to Glaucus over the body of Patroclus, when Glaucus accuses him of fearing Ajax (17.170–178), and Aeneas tells Achilles that Zeus increases and diminishes men's prowess as he wishes (20.242–243; see [[arete]]). In the Odyssey the rule binds the gods as well. Hermes tells Calypso that no other god can evade the mind of aegis-bearing Zeus or make it come to nothing, and she repeats his words as she lets Odysseus go (*Odyssey* 5.103–104, 5.137–138).
