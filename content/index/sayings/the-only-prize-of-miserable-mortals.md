**Peisistratus' tears.** Menelaus' grief for Odysseus has roused in them all the longing for lament, and Peisistratus weeps too, remembering his brother Antilochus, whom the splendid son of shining Dawn had killed (*Odyssey* 4.183–189; see [[the-longing-for-lament]] and [[antilochus]]). Then he asks for an end to it. Merry supplies the thoughts the speech leaves out: be persuaded by me [to cease weeping], for I take no pleasure in weeping after supper, and besides, the morning will come [and serve for weeping]; not that I grudge it (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.193; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 4.194). νεμεσσῶμαι is “am displeased”, “grudge”, as when Telemachus tells his mother that he does not blame her for her anger, τὸ μὲν οὔ σε νεμεσσῶμαι κεχολῶσθαι (*Odyssey* 18.227; Merry on 4.195; see [[nemesis]]). He has his own reason for granting the tears: “For my brother too is dead, by no means the worst / of the Argives” (*Odyssey* 4.199–200).

**The prize.** γέρας is the share of honor (see [[geras]]), and the infinitives say what it is: to cut one's hair and to let a tear fall. Merry takes them as the epexegesis of γέρας, holds the two lines for a parenthesis, since καὶ γάρ follows directly on νεμεσσῶμαι, and for the custom compares the mourning for Patroclus and for Achilles (on 4.198). Achilles will not wash until he has laid Patroclus on the fire, heaped a mound, and cut his hair, in the words Peisistratus uses, κείρασθαί τε κόμην (*Iliad* 23.44–47); the Myrmidons cover the body with the hair they cut (*Iliad* 23.135–136), and in the Odyssey the shade of Agamemnon tells Achilles how the Danaans wept hot tears around his body and cut their hair (*Odyssey* 24.45–46). The Iliad's half-verse names the honors owed to the dead, “for that is the prize of the dead” (*Iliad* 16.457, 23.9; see [[the-prize-of-the-dead]]); Peisistratus' word is οἶον, “only”: tears and shorn hair are all the living can give (see [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[tears]]).

**The answer.** Menelaus grants it and praises the young man's sense as his father's (*Odyssey* 4.204–211; see [[easily-known-is-the-offspring]]): “So let us leave off the weeping that came before, / and let us remember supper again” (*Odyssey* 4.212–213). In the Iliad Odysseus gives an army's rule for the same thing, to bury the dead “after weeping for him a single day” and eat (*Iliad* 19.228–229; see [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]]). At Sparta Helen goes further, and mixes into the wine a drug against grief, so that no one who drank it would let a tear fall that day, “not even if his mother and his father lay dead” (*Odyssey* 4.219–226; see [[nepenthe]] and [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]).
