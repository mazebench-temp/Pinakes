**At the huts.** Meriones has broken his spear on Deiphobus' shield and gone back to the huts for another (13.156–168). Idomeneus, who has armed at his hut after Poseidon's rebuke, meets him near it and asks whether he is wounded or brings a message (13.246–253). He can take one of the Trojan spears that stand in Idomeneus' hut, “one or twenty”, taken from the men he has killed, “for I do not mean / to fight standing far off from hostile men” (13.259–265; see [[spear]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]). Meriones answers that he too has many spoils of the Trojans, though they are not near enough to take, and that he takes his stand among the foremost whenever the quarrel of war arises; some other Achaean may not notice him fighting, “but you yourself, I think, know it” (13.266–273; see [[meriones]]). Idomeneus' speech answers the half-spoken claim. He knows what Meriones is in prowess, and he will not let him praise himself (13.275).

**The test.** The ambush, λόχος, is the test because there is nothing to do in it but wait. The best men are chosen and sit hidden until the enemy comes (13.276–277, 13.285; see [[ambush]]), and fear has time to show in the body. Idomeneus describes it part by part. The coward's color changes this way and that; his heart cannot keep him sitting still, and he keeps shifting his crouch and sits back on both his feet; his heart pounds loudly against his chest “as he foresees the fates”, and his teeth chatter (13.279–283; see [[ker-doom]], [[kradie]] and [[thymos]]). The brave man's color does not change, and he is not much afraid once he has taken his place; he prays to close with the enemy quickly (13.284–286). μετοκλάζει, “keeps shifting his crouch”, is found only here, and how the coward shifts, from foot to foot or up and down, is debated (see the translation's note on 13.276–287). The prowess that shows itself is ἀρετή, and διαείδεται, “is seen clearly”, is the verb of Hector's boast in Book 8 that the next day Diomedes would learn his own prowess (ἣν ἀρετὴν διαείσεται, 8.535; see [[arete]]).

**Achilles' taunt.** The ambush was the test Achilles threw at Agamemnon in the quarrel: “never have you dared in your heart to arm for war with the army / or to go to ambush with the best of the Achaeans; that seems to you death” (κήρ, 1.226–228). Idomeneus describes the same company, “all the best of us” chosen for an ambush (13.276–277), and the man who goes with it and does not see death in it. The coward, by contrast, sits “foreseeing the fates” (κῆρας ὀϊομένῳ, 13.283).

**The coward and the brave man elsewhere.** The only ambush that the Iliad's own story tells at length is the night ambush of Book 10, and its prey shows the coward's body. Dolon, caught by Odysseus and Diomedes, “stood still, terrified, / stammering, and there was a chattering of teeth in his mouth, / pale with fear” (10.374–376; see [[dolon]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]). The Odyssey shows the brave man. In the wooden horse, which Odysseus calls “the close-packed ambush”, the other leaders of the Danaans wiped away tears and their limbs trembled, but Odysseus never saw Neoptolemus' fair skin turn pale or his cheeks wet; he kept begging to be let out, gripping his sword-hilt and his spear (*Odyssey* 11.523–532). In his false tale to Eumaeus, Odysseus says the same of himself: whenever he chose the best men for an ambush, his proud heart never foresaw death, and he leapt out first of all (*Odyssey* 14.216–221).

**Wounds in front.** Idomeneus ends with Meriones' wounds. If he were hit as he toiled, the missile would not fall on the back of his neck or on his back, “but it would meet your chest or your belly / as you pressed forward to the intimate talk of the champions” (13.288–291; see [[oaristys]]). A wound in the back is taken in flight (see [[retreat]]). Hector, facing Achilles, says the same of himself: “you will not fix your spear in my back as I flee, but drive it through my chest as I come on” (22.283–284).

**Enough.** Then he stops: “let us no longer talk like this, like children, / standing here, lest someone be overbearingly indignant” (13.292–293; see [[nemesis]] and [[nepios]]), and sends Meriones to the hut for a heavy spear. The two go into the war like Ares and his son Rout and choose the left of the army (13.295–329; see [[ares-and-rout]] and [[idomeneus]]). The test of courage in the poem is usually the front line, where a man must stand his ground (11.408–410; see [[the-cowards-leave-the-fighting]]); Idomeneus names the harder test of waiting, and the speech's place in the book answers the question it raises, for the two go straight from it into the fighting. See [[retreat]], [[alke]] and [[deos]].

## Outside Homer

The half-verse became a saying. The Suda quotes it twice “according to the poet”, both times without μάλιστα, as ἔνθʼ ἀρετὴ διαείδεται ἀνδρῶν: once under διαείδεται, which it glosses “is made known, is seen”, adding “or rather, where the greatness of prowess is tested” (δ 552), and once under φῶτα, “man”, where, after a phrase of Pindar's, “great danger does not take hold of a man without courage”, it says that the great man undertakes no small contest but goes in where others run away for cowardice, “where men's prowess is seen clearly, according to the poet” (φ 677). It glosses μετοκλάζει as μετακαθίζει, “shifts his seat” (μ 792).
