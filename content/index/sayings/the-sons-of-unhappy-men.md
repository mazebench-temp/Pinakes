Diomedes sees a man he has never seen before, who has come out in front of everyone to face him. He begins with a question, “Who are you, good sir, among mortal men?”, and before he asks whether the stranger is a god he gives a threat in the form of a general truth. δύστηνος is “wretched, unhappy”, and the unhappy ones are the fathers: whoever meets Diomedes will die, and his father will be left to grieve. The line looks past the fighter to his parents, as the obituaries of Book 5 did, where Diomedes killed the two sons of old Phaenops, who had no other son, and left their father lamentation and grief (5.152–158; see [[bereaved-parents]]). ἐμῷ μένει, “my might”, is the might Athena gave him in that book (see [[menos]]).

The speech that follows turns the threat into a question. If the stranger is a god, Diomedes will not fight him, and he tells the story of Lycurgus, who fought the gods and did not live long (6.128–141; see [[who-fights-the-gods]] and [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]]). If he is a mortal, “one of the mortals who eat the fruit of the field”, let him come nearer, “so that you may the sooner reach the bounds of destruction” (6.142–143). The meeting ends in friendship instead (see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]).

**In Achilles' mouth.** Both of Diomedes' threats return in Achilles' last day of fighting, and there they are carried out. He says the second to Hector when they first meet in Book 20, “Come nearer, so that you may the sooner reach the bounds of destruction” (20.429). He says the first to Asteropaeus, the Paeonian who stands up to him at the river: “Who and from where among men are you, who dared to come against me? It is the sons of unhappy men who meet my might” (21.150–151). Asteropaeus answers, like Glaucus, with his lineage, and dies (21.152–183; see [[genee]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]). Diomedes' challenge is made of the lines Achilles will use to kill.
