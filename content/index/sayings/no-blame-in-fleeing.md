**The proposal.** The wall is down and the Trojans are fighting at the sterns of the ships. Agamemnon concludes that it must be Zeus' pleasure “that the Achaeans should perish here, nameless, far from Argos”, and proposes to drag the nearest row of ships into the sea, anchor them out, and when the immortal night comes, if the Trojans hold off from war in it, launch the rest (14.65–79; see [[agamemnon]], [[ships]] and [[retreat]]). The maxim gives the reason in two verses. The first denies the shame: there is no νέμεσις, no cause for blame, in fleeing evil, “not even by night” (see [[nemesis]] and [[war-and-night]]). Its form is the elders' verdict on Helen, οὐ νέμεσις with an infinitive (3.156–157; see [[no-blame]]). The second gives a rule of prudence: βέλτερον, “better”, the man who flees and escapes than the man who is caught.

**The answer.** It is the third time Agamemnon has proposed flight: he did it to test the army (2.139–141) and in earnest at the night assembly (9.26–28), both times in the same verses, “let us flee with our ships to our dear native land” (see [[the-test-of-the-army]]). Odysseus answers with the formula of rebuke, “what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!” (14.83; see [[what-a-word]]), and with the soldier's view: such a word no man would let pass his lips who knew how to speak fitting words and bore a scepter, and if the ships are dragged down in the middle of the battle, the Achaeans will look away and hang back from the fighting, and the plan will destroy them (14.83–102; see [[odysseus]] and [[charme]]). Agamemnon yields at once (14.104–108).

**Flight and capture.** Odysseus had weighed the same two evils in Book 11, cut off and alone: “It is a great evil if I flee, / fearing the multitude; but it is worse if I am caught / alone” (11.404–406). He chose the third course, to stand, because “it is cowards who leave the fighting” (11.408; see [[the-cowards-leave-the-fighting]]). Agamemnon's maxim compares only flight and capture, and leaves out the stand. In the next book Ajax gives the opposite rule at the ships, also with βέλτερον: better to die once for all or to live than to be ground down slowly in the dread fighting (15.511–513).
