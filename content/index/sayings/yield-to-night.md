Hector and Ajax have thrown their spears and their stones, and they would have gone at each other with swords if the heralds had not come, “messengers of Zeus and of men” (7.273–276; see [[heralds]], [[idaeus]] and [[talthybius]]). The heralds hold out their scepters between the two, and Idaeus gives three reasons to stop: Zeus loves them both, both are spearmen, and night is coming on. πιθέσθαι is “to obey, to yield to”, the verb of persuasion and obedience (see [[peitho]]). Night is a power that even champions obey, and καί, “too”, sets it beside the other power that Idaeus has named, Zeus who loves them. Ajax answers that the man who made the challenge must speak first (7.284–286), and Hector takes up the herald's words:

{{quote:93b3a2ec-4b9b-554d-a5ff-a5de2e420d82}}

He adds a formula that Priam and Idaeus will repeat that evening and the next morning, when they ask for a truce, “until a god decides between us and gives victory to one side or the other” (7.291–292 = 7.377–378 = 7.396–397; see [[daimon]]), and then the herald's line. His proposal, “let us now cease from battle and combat for today; afterward we will fight again” (7.290–291), repeats almost word for word what Apollo proposed to Athena at the start of the book, when the gods planned the duel to stop the fighting “for today” (7.29–30; see [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]]). The day ends as the gods meant it to, and the men who end it do not know it.

**Elsewhere.** Other speakers yield to night in another verse, “but now let us yield to black night” (ἀλλʼ ἤτοι νῦν μὲν πειθώμεθα νυκτὶ μελαίνῃ), and go on to supper: Hector, when darkness has saved the Achaean ships (8.500–503), Nestor in the Achaean assembly (9.65–66), and in the Odyssey Eurylochus, persuading Odysseus to land for the night on the island of the Sun (*Odyssey* 12.291–292), with ruinous results. Night decides what men cannot. Agamemnon expects no pause in the war “unless night comes and parts the fury of men” (2.387), and in Book 18 Polydamas warns the Trojans that ambrosial night has stopped Achilles only for now (18.267–268). Even Zeus defers to her: he gave up his pursuit of Sleep, who had fled to Night, “for he shrank from doing what would displease swift Night” (14.258–261).
