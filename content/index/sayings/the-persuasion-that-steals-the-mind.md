**The verse.** The strap that Aphrodite gives Hera holds love, longing and intimate talk, and the verse that ends the list says what intimate talk is: πάρφασις, a persuasion that talks a person round (see [[aphrodites-band]], [[oaristys]] and [[peitho]]). The noun belongs with παράφημι, “talk over, persuade”. Its other form, παραίφασις, is the persuasion of a friend in Nestor's maxim, “Good is the persuasion of a companion” (11.793; see [[the-persuasion-of-a-friend]]), and Hector threatens Polydamas with death if he turns another man from the war by talking him over with words (παρφάμενος ἐπέεσσιν, 12.248–250). Here the persuasion is not a friend's. It “steals the mind” (ἔκλεψε νόον), the verb of theft: Agamemnon used it when he told Achilles not to try to trick him, κλέπτε νόῳ, for he would not outwit him or win him over (1.131–132; see [[noos]]). The aorist ἔκλεψε states what such persuasion always does, and πύκα περ φρονεόντων, “even of men who think shrewdly”, says on whom: shrewdness is no defense against it (see [[phrenes]]).

**In the story.** The verse is a description, and the book is its proof. Zeus, whose “shrewd mind” is named when Hera plans to pour sleep on it and when desire wraps it, is talked round by Hera's false story and her objections on Ida, and falls asleep (14.165, 14.294, 14.300–353; see [[the-deception-of-zeus]] and [[truth-and-deception]]).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle quotes the verse, with the participle in the singular (φρονέοντος, “of one who thinks shrewdly”), to show that desire is more unjust than anger. The angry man and anger itself are open, not scheming; desire is crafty, as Aphrodite is called “guile-weaving”, and as Homer says of her embroidered strap (*Nicomachean Ethics* 7.6, 1149b13–18; see [[aristotle]]).
