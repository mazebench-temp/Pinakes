## The saying

Since the poet is an imitator, like a painter, he must always imitate one of three things: things as they were or are, things as people say and think they are, or things as they ought to be (1460b8–11). So if a critic objects that something is not true, the answer may be that it is as it ought to be, as Sophocles said that he made people as they ought to be and Euripides as they are (see [[sophocles]] and [[euripides]]). If it is neither, the answer is that people say so, as with the stories of the gods (1460b35–1461a1; see [[xenophanes]]). The model should surpass reality, as Zeuxis painted people better than they are (1461b12–13; see [[zeuxis]] and [[painting-and-poetry]]). In Aristophanes' *Frogs* Euripides boasts of the same thing that Sophocles' saying implies, that he brought on stage “household matters, the things we use and live with” (959).
