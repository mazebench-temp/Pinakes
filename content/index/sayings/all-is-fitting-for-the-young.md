**The young man.** Everything, πάντα, is fitting (ἐπέοικεν, a perfect with the sense of a present) for a young man killed in battle, ἄρηϊ κταμένῳ, and torn by the sharp bronze: to lie there, κεῖσθαι, dead on the field (22.71–73; see [[death-in-battle]]). πάντα δὲ καλὰ θανόντι περ ὅττι φανήῃ, “all that is seen of him is fair, dead though he is”: whatever of the body lies bare is beautiful, torn as it is (see [[kalon]]). The poem shows it at once with Hector's own body. When he is dead the Achaeans run up and gaze at “the stature and admirable looks / of Hector”, and his head lying in the dust “once was handsome” (22.370–371, 22.402–403). But the young man's body is not left to lie: the Achaeans stab it, and Achilles drags it before the city, and has said that the dogs and the birds will tear it (22.371–375, 22.395–404, 22.335–336; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]).

**The old man.** Against him Priam sets himself, “last of all”, torn at his own doors by the dogs he reared in his halls to share his table and guard his doors (22.66–71; see [[dogs]]). The dogs “will lie in the doorway” (κείσοντʼ ἐν προθύροισι, 22.71), and the same verb, two verses later, lays the young man on the field. What they disfigure (αἰσχύνωσι, the verb of a disfigured corpse, as Iris warns Achilles that Patroclus' body may come back “disfigured”, 18.180; see [[aischros]]) is “the gray head and the gray beard / and the nakedness of an old man who is slain” (22.74–75): αἰδώς, “shame”, is here the body that shame covers, as when Odysseus threatens to strip Thersites of the clothes “which cover your nakedness” (2.262; see [[aidos]] and [[old-age-and-memory]]). τοῦτο δὴ οἴκτιστον, “this is the most pitiable thing” (22.76; see [[pity]]): δειλοῖσι βροτοῖσιν, “to wretched mortals”, ends the verse here and the star of late summer's “much fever to wretched mortals” a few verses before (22.31; see the translation's table of formulas).

**The argument.** The maxim closes Priam's plea (22.38–76; see [[priam]]). If Hector dies, the city falls, and what an old king then sees and suffers is worse than any young man's death: his sons killed, his daughters and his sons' wives dragged away, and his own body left to the dogs (22.59–76; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). The last appeal is to pity for the father, “And pity me too, unhappy as I am” (22.59). Hector does not answer; Priam tears his gray hair and does not persuade him (22.77–78), and Hector's own wish, when he knows that he must die, is to die “not … without a struggle and without glory” (22.304–305; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]] and [[hector]]).
