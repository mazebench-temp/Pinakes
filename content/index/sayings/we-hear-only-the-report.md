Two lines set gods and mortals on either side of a divide in knowledge. The Muses “are present” (πάρεστε) and “know” (ἴστε) all things. “We” hear only κλέος, the report that passes from mouth to mouth, and “know nothing” (οὐδέ τι ἴδμεν). ἴστε and ἴδμεν are forms of οἶδα, “I know”, which at root means “I have seen”: it shares its root with εἶδον, “I saw”, and Latin *video*. To know is to have been there and seen. The Muses were present at Troy; the singer and his audience were not.

κλέος is the word for what the singer passes on, and the word for fame (see [[kleos]]). Here it is set against knowledge: what has only been heard may be wrong. That is the reason for the invocation (see [[invocation-of-the-muses]] and [[muses]]). The Catalogue that follows, with its leaders, towns and numbers of ships, needs a witness, and only the Muses can supply one, “unless the Olympian Muses … were to remind me” (2.491–492; see [[ten-tongues]] and [[memory-and-the-muses]]).

In the Odyssey, Odysseus praises the singer Demodocus for telling the sufferings of the Achaeans “as if you had been present yourself, or had heard it from another” (ὥς τέ που ἢ αὐτὸς παρεὼν ἢ ἄλλου ἀκούσας, Odyssey 8.491).

## Outside Homer

The historians made the same distinction the basis of their method. Herodotus marks the point in his account of Egypt where his own sight, judgment and inquiry give way to what he heard from the Egyptians (2.99.1). Thucydides wrote of events at which he was present himself (οἷς τε αὐτὸς παρῆν) or which he had checked with others as accurately as he could (1.22.2). See [[thucydides]].
