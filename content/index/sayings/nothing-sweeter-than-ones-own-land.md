**The maxim.** Odysseus says it twice in the speech in which he gives his name. After the description of Ithaca, “rugged, but a good nurse of young men”, comes the first form, “and I, for my part, / can see nothing else sweeter than one's own land” (*Odyssey* 9.27–28); after Calypso and Circe, who held him back, each “longing for me to be her husband”, and “never persuaded the heart in my breast” (*Odyssey* 9.29–33), the maxim itself. ὥς, “so true is it that”, introduces a general sentiment drawn from the particular cases before it, as in Agamemnon's “so there is nothing more dreadful and more shameless than a woman” who plots such deeds (ὣς οὐκ αἰνότερον καὶ κύντερον, *Odyssey* 11.427; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 9.34). ἧς, “one's own”, is the possessive of the third person used of the speaker: a scholium remarks that he did not say “mine”, so that the saying might be more general (Merry on 9.28). Native land and parents go together, as the land of the fathers (πατρίς) and those who begot him (τοκῆες); the goddesses' houses are “rich” but in a foreign land (see [[dear-native-land]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).

**Homesickness.** The poem shows the longing before it lets Odysseus speak of it. At the start Athena tells Zeus that Calypso bewitches him “so that he may forget Ithaca; but Odysseus, / eager to see even the smoke leaping up / from his own land, longs to die” (*Odyssey* 1.57–59), with the same ἧς γαίης; to Calypso herself he says, “But even so I wish and yearn all my days / to come home and see the day of my homecoming” (*Odyssey* 5.219–220); and to Arete he tells her offer of immortality, “but she never persuaded the heart in my breast” (*Odyssey* 7.258), the verse he repeats here of both goddesses (see [[calypso]], [[immortal-and-ageless]] and [[nostos]]). When at last he stands on Ithaca and knows it, he rejoices, “rejoicing in his own land” (χαίρων ᾗ γαίῃ, with the same possessive), and kisses the grain-giving earth (*Odyssey* 13.353–354; see [[ithaca]]).

## Outside Homer

The contrary maxim had its own voice. Cicero, consoling the exile, argues that to be away from one's country is no evil for the wise, since the noblest philosophers spent their lives abroad, and that “the saying of Teucer can be fitted to every school”, *patria est, ubicumque est bene*, “one's country is wherever one is well off”; Socrates, asked what country he was from, answered that he was a citizen of the world (*Tusculan Disputations* 5.106–108; see [[cicero]]).
