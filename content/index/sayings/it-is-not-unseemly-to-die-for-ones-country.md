**The argument.** Hector has seen Teucer's bowstring break and has read the sign: Zeus is with the Trojans and diminishes the might of the Argives (15.484–493; see [[the-help-of-zeus-is-easy-to-see]]). He draws the order from it, to fight at the ships in a body, and turns at once to the death that fighting will cost. τεθνάτω, “let him die”, stands alone at the head of the verse, and the next words deny the shame: οὔ οἱ ἀεικὲς, “it is not unseemly”, the understatement of a man who means that such a death is honorable (see [[litotes]]). ἀμυνομένῳ περὶ πάτρης, “fighting in defense of his native land”, is the phrase of Hector's answer to Polydamas at the wall, “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (12.243; see [[one-omen-is-best]]). The reward is not the dead man's but his household's: his wife is safe, his children after him, “and his house and his allotment unharmed” (15.498; see [[the-city-and-the-household]] and [[house]]). κλῆρος, the lot, is here the land a man holds as his share (see [[lots]]).

**The other side.** Ajax answers on his side at once. He too calls for shame, but he can promise no household: if Hector takes the ships, the Achaeans will not walk home, and it is better to die once and for all than to be worn away beside the ships (15.502–513; see [[aidos]] and [[better-to-perish-once-and-for-all]]). At the end of the book he states the difference: there is no city near, fitted with towers, where they could defend themselves; they sit “far from our native land” with their backs to the sea (15.735–741; see [[the-light-is-in-our-hands]]). Nestor asks the Achaeans to remember their wives and children and parents, though “they are not here” (15.661–665). The Trojans fight in sight of what they defend.

**What the poem knows.** Hector's promise is the one he cannot keep for himself. In *Iliad* 6 he told Andromache that a day would come when Ilios would fall and she would be led away a slave, weaving at another woman's loom (6.447–465; see [[hector-and-andromache]]), and the Iliad ends with her lament for the husband, the city's “guardian, who kept the city itself / safe”, and for the child who will be hurled from the tower (24.729–738). After Hector's death the phrase is his father's: Priam asks Achilles for the body of the son he killed “as he fought in defense of his native land” (ἀμυνόμενον περὶ πάτρης, 24.500). Glaucus wishes the Trojans had the fearless spirit of men who fight for their native land (17.156–158).

## Outside Homer

The thought became the classic praise of a soldier's death. Its best-known form is Horace's, “It is sweet and fitting to die for one's country”, followed by the warning that death pursues even the man who flees (*Odes* 3.2.13–14). The orator Lycurgus, prosecuting Leocrates for leaving Athens after Chaeronea, quotes Hector's verses as those that taught the Athenians' ancestors to die for all Greece (*Against Leocrates* 102–104; see [[lycurgus-quotes-hector]]).
