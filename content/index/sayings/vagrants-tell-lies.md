**The saying.** ἀλῆται are wanderers, men without a home who go about begging, and κομιδή is “keep”, the care and food a man is given, as Odysseus tells the Phaeacians that on board the ship his keep was not unfailing (*Odyssey* 8.232–233; the translation's table of key words; see [[pseudos]]). ἄλλως, “at random”, is idly, to no purpose but their own. The wanderer's lie is a trade: news of a lost man for a meal and a garment. Alcinous, who believes the stranger at his table, names the same kind of man in order to deny that his guest is one of them, “a deceiver and a cheat, such as the many / whom the black earth feeds, men scattered far and wide, / fashioning lies out of things that no one could even see” (*Odyssey* 11.363–366; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]] and [[truth-and-deception]]).

**Turned on the speaker.** Eumaeus' rule is aimed at the man before him, who is a wanderer, wants keep, and is about to lie; and the stranger accepts the rule and claims to be the exception. He swears that his news is true, refuses any reward until it is fulfilled, and says that he hates as the gates of Hades the man “who, giving way to poverty, speaks deceits” (*Odyssey* 14.156–157; see [[hateful-as-the-gates-of-hades]], [[oath]] and [[euangelion]]). His news of Odysseus is the one true thing in a tale otherwise invented, and it is the one thing the swineherd will not believe (*Odyssey* 14.363–365; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle distinguishes liars as he distinguishes boasters: “one man delights in the falsehood itself, another reaches out for reputation or for gain”, and the man who pretends for the sake of silver, or what leads to silver, is the more unseemly; those who boast for gain claim things whose absence can escape detection, such as being a seer, a wise man or a physician (*Nicomachean Ethics* 4.7, 1127b9–20; see [[aristotle]]).
