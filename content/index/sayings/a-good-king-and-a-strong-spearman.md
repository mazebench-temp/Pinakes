Priam has asked Helen who the huge, handsome Achaean is who looks like a king (3.166–170). She answers with his name and titles, “the son of Atreus, wide-ruling Agamemnon”, and then with this line. ἀμφότερον, “both”, joins two excellences that the Iliad usually finds in different men. A βασιλεὺς ἀγαθός is good at ruling; a κρατερὸς αἰχμητής is strong with the spear. In Book 1 Nestor divided them between the two quarreling men: Achilles is strong (καρτερός), Agamemnon the greater (φέρτερος), because he rules over more (1.280–281; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). Helen gives Agamemnon both, and Priam answers by calling him blessed (3.182–183).

The praise comes from the enemy's side of the wall, and from a woman who then remembers that he was her brother-in-law, “if that was ever so” (3.180; see [[in-laws]]). See [[agamemnon]], [[basileus]] and [[kingship-and-authority]].

## Outside Homer

In Xenophon's *Memorabilia* Socrates asks a newly elected general why Homer praised Agamemnon with this line. His answer: a man is a strong spearman not if he fights well alone but if he makes the whole army do so, and a good king not if he manages his own life well but if he makes his people happy (3.2.2).
