**The argument.** Achilles has refused to eat until Patroclus is avenged, and would send the army out “fasting and unfed” (19.199–214; see [[fasting]]). Odysseus grants that Achilles is the better man with the spear, but claims to surpass him in understanding, “since I was born first and know more” (19.216–219), and answers with a maxim. κόρος is satiety, the point at which a desire is spent, and φύλοπις is “the din of battle”. Battle is a harvest gone wrong: the bronze lays the most straw (καλάμη, the stalks) on the ground, and the yield is the smallest. Walter Leaf, after Valeton and Lendrum, reads it as a labor of much toil and little reward, a reaping of stalks with little grain, for which soldiers must be kept up with food; he quotes Monro on ὀλίγιστος, “it is a harvest that is all cutting down, no storing up” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.221 and 19.223).

The harvest is over when Zeus “tips the scales” (κλίνῃσι τάλαντα): the battle is decided, as when Zeus held out his golden scales at midday in *Iliad* 8 and the fated day of the Achaeans sank (8.68–74; Leaf on 19.223; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). The last verse is the one the soldiers spoke in *Iliad* 4, of Zeus “who is the dispenser of war for men” (19.224 = 4.84; see [[dispenser-of-war]]). Leaf reports Lendrum's suggestion that ταμίης, a steward who deals out stores, has a point here: Zeus is a steward, but not of food, like the stewards beside the ships “dealing out the food” (19.44; Leaf on 19.224).

The conclusion follows at once. The Achaeans cannot mourn a corpse with the belly; the dead must be buried after a single day of weeping, and those who survive must remember drink and food (19.225–231; see [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]] and [[food-and-wine-are-might-and-courage]]).

**Two views of satiety.** Menelaus, standing over the body of Peisander, complained to Zeus that men have their fill of everything, of sleep and love, of song and dance, but the Trojans are insatiable of battle (13.631–639; see [[satiety-of-all-things]]). Odysseus uses the same two words, κόρος and φύλοπις, and says the opposite of men in general: they soon have their fill of it. Apart from these two places, the noun κόρος occurs in Homer only in Menelaus' words in the Odyssey, “for satiety of chilling lament comes quickly” (αἰψηρὸς δὲ κόρος κρυεροῖο γόοιο, *Odyssey* 4.103).

**Reaping.** The Iliad pictures killing as reaping. In a simile of Book 11 the Trojans and Achaeans cut each other down like reapers who drive their swaths toward one another through a rich man's field (11.67–71; see [[the-reapers]]), and on the Shield the reaping is a real harvest on a king's land (18.550–560; see [[the-kings-harvest]]). Odysseus takes the figure from the side of the yield, and finds it poor.
