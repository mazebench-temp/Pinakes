Meriones kills Phereclus, a craftsman skilled in every kind of intricate work, whom Athena loved above all others, and the obituary tells what he made: the ships in which Alexander sailed for Sparta and brought Helen home (5.59–68; see [[phereclus]] and [[the-abduction-of-helen]]). The ships are ἀρχέκακοι, “beginning evil”, a word found only here (see [[that-began-the-evil]]), and the verse unfolds it into a sentence: they became “an evil for all the Trojans and for himself”. “Himself” may be Phereclus, who dies here with a spear through his buttock into the bladder (5.65–68), or Alexander; the Greek allows both. Phereclus built them “since he knew nothing of the oracles of the gods” (θέσφατα, 5.64; see [[prophecy]]), and the poem does not say what the oracles were.

The Iliad marks other first steps in the same way. Menelaus accepts the duel of Book 3 because both armies have suffered “for the sake of my quarrel, and because Alexander began it” (ἕνεκʼ ἀρχῆς, 3.100; see [[arche-or-ate]]). Hector remembers the goods “that Alexander brought to Troy in his hollow ships, which was the beginning of the quarrel” (νείκεος ἀρχή, 22.114–116). And when Achilles calls Patroclus from his hut to find out who has been wounded, Patroclus “came out, the equal of Ares; and this was the beginning of evil for him” (κακοῦ δʼ ἄρα οἱ πέλεν ἀρχή, 11.603–604). The narrator sees the whole chain from its first link: the errand leads to Nestor's advice, the advice to Patroclus' going out in Achilles' armor, and that to his death. In the Odyssey the singer Demodocus tells of a quarrel between Odysseus and Achilles at a feast, which Agamemnon was glad to see, since it fulfilled an oracle of Apollo, “for then the beginning of woe was rolling on for the Trojans and the Danaans, through the plans of great Zeus” (*Odyssey* 8.75–82).

## Outside Homer

The historians took up the phrase. Herodotus says of the twenty ships that Athens sent to help the Ionians in their revolt against Persia: “these ships were the beginning of evils for Greeks and barbarians” (αὗται δὲ αἱ νέες ἀρχὴ κακῶν ἐγένοντο, 5.97.3; see [[herodotus]]). When the Spartan envoy Melesippus was sent away from Athens without a hearing at the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War, he said at the frontier: “this day will be the beginning of great evils for the Greeks” (Thucydides 2.12.3; see [[thucydides]]).
