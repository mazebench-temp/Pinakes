**The maxim.** Eumaeus speaks it of the suitors, who eat his master's swine, and he sets it between them and the raider who sails home with spoil: even such a man fears the gods' regard, and the suitors do not (*Odyssey* 14.80–92). δίκη is what is right, and αἴσιμα ἔργα, “righteous deeds”, are deeds in due measure (the translation's table of key words; see [[themis]] and [[aisa]]).

**The gods' regard.** ὄπις is the watch the gods keep on human transgression. It is usually θεῶν ὄπις, “the regard of the gods”; here and at *Odyssey* 14.88 the word stands alone with that meaning (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 14.82). In the Iliad's storm simile Zeus rains in anger on men who judge crooked ordinances in the assembly and drive out justice, “caring nothing for the regard of the gods” (*Iliad* 16.384–388; see [[the-storm-of-late-summer]]). In the Odyssey the phrase is used of the suitors again, who do not tremble at the regard of the gods (*Odyssey* 20.215), and of Heracles, who killed his guest Iphitus and respected neither the gods' regard nor the table (*Odyssey* 21.27–29; see [[xenia]]). The gods themselves, a suitor warns Antinous, go about the cities in the likeness of strangers, “watching over the arrogance and the lawfulness of men” (*Odyssey* 17.485–487, a plain rendering; see [[hybris]]).

**Fulfilled.** When the suitors lie dead, Odysseus forbids Eurycleia to raise the cry of triumph and gives the reason in the maxim's own words: “These men the fate of the gods has brought down, and their cruel deeds; / for they honored no one of men on earth, / neither base nor noble, whoever came to them” (*Odyssey* 22.413–415, a plain rendering; see [[the-return-of-odysseus]]). The cruel deeds the gods do not love were above all wrongs against strangers.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod teaches the same in the *Works and Days*. For those who care for evil violence and cruel deeds, σχέτλια ἔργα, far-seeing Zeus ordains punishment, and a whole city may suffer for one wicked man (*Works and Days* 238–247). The immortals are near among men and mark those who wear one another down with crooked judgments, θεῶν ὄπιν οὐκ ἀλέγοντες, “without regard for the gods”, and thirty thousand immortal guardians of Zeus go everywhere over the earth, clothed in mist, watching over judgments and cruel deeds (*Works and Days* 248–255; see [[hesiod]]).
