**The lesson.** Nestor begins with the facts. Zeus and Poseidon have taught his son horsemanship of every kind, and he knows well how to wheel around the turning posts, “but your horses / are the slowest at running”; the others' horses are swifter, but the men themselves do not know how to devise more than he does (23.306–312; see [[horses]]). Then the advice: “But come, my friend, put cunning into your heart, / cunning of every kind, so that the prizes do not slip past you” (23.313–314), and the maxim, three clauses each beginning with the same word, μήτι, “by cunning” (23.315–318; see [[metis]]). The woodcutter, δρυτόμος, “oak-cutter”, is better by cunning than by strength (βίηφι; see [[the-woodcutters]]). The helmsman steers a swift ship on the wine-dark sea though it is ἐρεχθομένην ἀνέμοισι, “buffeted by the winds”; Walter Leaf notes that ἐρέχθω is used elsewhere only in a metaphor, of a heart racked with grief, as Odysseus sits on Calypso's shore “rending his heart” (θυμὸν ἐρέχθων, *Odyssey* 5.83; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.317). The third clause is the point of the other two: μήτι δʼ ἡνίοχος περιγίγνεται ἡνιόχοιο, “by cunning one charioteer gets the better of another” (23.318). Woodcutter, helmsman and charioteer are men who work against a force greater than their own, the oak, the wind, the speed of other men's horses.

**The rule and the turn.** The maxim is followed by its application. The man who trusts in his horses and chariot wheels heedlessly wide, and his horses wander over the course; but “he who knows crafty ways, though he drives worse horses”, always watches the turning post and turns close, holds his horses at full stretch with the straps, and watches the man in front (23.319–325; see [[kerdos]]). Nestor shows him the post, the dry stump with a white stone on either side, and how to take it: lean a little to the left in the chariot, goad on the right-hand horse with a shout, let the left-hand horse press close to the post, and keep clear of the stone (23.326–343). If he passes the others at the post, no one will catch him (23.344–348).

**Cunning in the race.** Antilochus does not win at the turn but on the way back, where the road is narrow, by driving off the road beside Menelaus (23.415–437). He comes in second, “by crafty ways, not by speed” (23.515), and Menelaus calls it a trick and asks him to swear that he did not hamper his chariot by δόλος (23.585; see [[oath]] and [[antilochus]]). The narrow road recalls a story that Nestor told in *Iliad* 7: Lycurgus killed the mace-man Areithous “by guile, not by strength at all, / in a narrow road”, where his mace could not save him (7.142–143), and Antilochus means to slip past Menelaus στεινωπῷ ἐν ὁδῷ, “in the narrow road” (23.416), in the same words. When Antilochus yields the mare he turns his father's word against himself: a young man's mind is quicker, “and his counsel thin” (λεπτὴ δέ τε μῆτις, 23.590).

**Cunning and strength.** The pair runs through Homer. Odysseus tells Achilles that Achilles is stronger and better with the spear, but that he would surpass him in understanding, since he was born first and knows more (19.216–219); in the Odyssey the blinded Cyclops cries that Nobody is killing him “by guile and not by force” (δόλῳ οὐδὲ βίηφιν, *Odyssey* 9.408), and Athena tells Odysseus that she is famed among all the gods for cunning and crafty ways (μήτι τε κλέομαι καὶ κέρδεσιν, *Odyssey* 13.297–299). Later in the games the man of μῆτις holds his own against stronger or swifter men: Odysseus throws Ajax by striking the hollow of his knee, though the wrestling is judged a draw, and he beats the son of Oileus in the footrace with Athena's help (23.725–737, 23.768–779; see [[odysseus]]).

## Outside Homer

In the *Ion* [[plato|Plato]]'s Socrates has the rhapsode recite Nestor's advice on the turn (23.335–340) and asks whether a doctor or a charioteer would better judge if Homer speaks rightly in it. The charioteer, because he has the art; and each art, Socrates goes on, has its own work, so that what we know by the helmsman's art we shall not know by medicine, nor what we know by medicine by the carpenter's (*Ion* 537a–c; see [[turning-post]]). His arts are nearly those of Nestor's maxim, the helmsman's and the carpenter's beside the charioteer's, but he uses them to divide knowledge among the crafts, not to set cunning above strength.
