**The moment.** The Sirens are behind them when Odysseus sees smoke and a great wave and hears a roar; in their terror the companions let the oars fly from their hands, and the ship stops (*Odyssey* 12.201–205). He goes through the ship, standing beside each man, “with soothing words” (*Odyssey* 12.206–207; see [[soothing-words]]), and begins with what they already know. This evil is no greater than when the Cyclops penned them in his hollow cave, and from there too they escaped, by his prowess, counsel and mind (*Odyssey* 12.209–212; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]], [[arete]], [[boule]] and [[noos]]). Men who have come through one evil can come through the next: the saying is an argument from experience, and the experience is his. Then come the orders, to the rowers and to the helmsman (*Odyssey* 12.213–221). Of Scylla he says nothing, “lest my companions in fear should stop / rowing” (*Odyssey* 12.223–225; see [[silence]] and [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]).

**The words.** ἀδαήμων, “unknowing, without experience”, is from the root of δαῆναι, “learn, come to know”. The verse turns a boast of the Iliad. Ajax answers Hector's threats, “We are not without knowledge of battle at all” (οὔ τοί τι μάχης ἀδαήμονές εἰμεν, *Iliad* 13.811), and Tlepolemus taunts Sarpedon as “a man with no knowledge of battle” (*Iliad* 5.634). The warrior claims knowledge of battle; the seafarer, of evils.

## Outside Homer

Virgil gives the speech to Aeneas, who has lost most of his ships in the storm and stands with the survivors on the Libyan shore: *O socii—neque enim ignari sumus ante malorum— / O passi graviora, dabit deus his quoque finem. / Vos et Scyllaeam rabiem penitusque sonantis / accestis scopulos, vos et Cyclopea saxa / experti*, “O companions (for we are not without knowledge of evils before this), O you who have suffered heavier things, a god will give an end to these too. You have come near Scylla's rage and the rocks that sound from their depths, you have known the Cyclops' rocks” (*Aeneid* 1.198–202, a plain rendering; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.208, notes the imitation; see [[virgil]] and [[aeneas]]). Odysseus, before Scylla, names only the Cyclops; Aeneas, who has passed both, names Scylla and the Cyclops, and adds the god who will give an end. His speech closes with the line that renders Odysseus' “some day, I think, we will remember these things too” (*Aeneid* 1.203; see [[we-will-remember-these-things-too]]).
