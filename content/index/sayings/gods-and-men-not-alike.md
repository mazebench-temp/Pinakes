Diomedes has wounded Aphrodite and goes on after Aeneas, though he knows that Apollo is holding his hands over him: “he had no awe even of the great god” (5.433–434). Three times he springs, three times Apollo batters back his shield, and at the fourth charge the god speaks (see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]). Diomedes gives ground “a little way, avoiding the wrath of Apollo who shoots from afar” (5.443–444), and lives.

The warning answers the charge in its own terms. Diomedes has rushed on “like a god” (δαίμονι ἶσος, 5.438); Apollo tells him not to wish ἶσα φρονέειν, “to think equal things”, to the gods. φρονέω is to have something in mind, to set one's thoughts on it, and ἶσα φρονέειν is to aim at equality. The reason is a matter of kind. φῦλον is a race or stock (see [[phylon]]), and the two races are named by what divides them: the gods are ἀθάνατοι, “deathless”, and men are those who walk χαμαί, “on the ground”. χαμαί belongs with χθών, “earth”, and far back Latin *homo*, “man”, comes from the same root: the human being is the earthly one.

The same book gives the other side of the lesson. Diomedes has just wounded one god, with Athena's leave (5.130–132), and he will wound another at her side (5.855–859). Dione tells Aphrodite what becomes of the man who fights the gods (5.406–409; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]), and Ares, in his pain, calls the gods' sufferings the price of favors done for men (5.873–874). Apollo speaks from the side of the gods who are not wounded. See [[gods-and-mortals]], [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]] and [[apollo]].

Apollo says it again in the battle of the gods, when he refuses to fight Poseidon “for the sake of wretched mortals, who are like leaves”, now flourishing, now wasting away (21.462–467). The two scenes are one thought. Men are not the gods' equals, and they are not worth a quarrel between gods.

## Outside Homer

Pindar begins the sixth Nemean ode with an answer: “One is the race of men, one of gods; from one mother we both draw breath; but a power wholly apart divides us”. Yet men are something like the gods, he goes on, in greatness of mind or of body (*Nemean* 6.1–7). Elsewhere he gives Apollo's warning as advice: “do not seek to become Zeus … mortal things befit mortals” (*Isthmian* 5.14–16).
