**The prayer.** Flung back from the rock with the skin of his hands stripped away, Odysseus swims along outside the surf until he comes to the mouth of a fair-flowing river, where the place seems best to him, smooth, free of rocks and sheltered from the wind, and “he knew the god as he flowed forth, and prayed to him in his heart” (*Odyssey* 5.430–444; see [[the-river-of-scheria]] and [[rivers]]). Merry renders “he recognised him (sc. for a god) as he flowed forth”, and takes κατὰ θυμόν as “in his heart”, not, as the scholia suggest, because a spent swimmer has no breath left for words: Odysseus prays to Athena in his heart in the footrace too (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.444, comparing *Iliad* 23.769; see [[prayer]]). The prayer opens as Glaucus' prayer to Apollo opens, κλῦθι, ἄναξ, “Hear me, lord” (*Iliad* 16.514; see [[hear-me]]), and it names no god, “whoever you are”, for the river has no name in the poem. He comes “much prayed for” (πολύλλιστον; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 5.445), “fleeing out of the sea from the rebukes of Poseidon” (*Odyssey* 5.445–446; see [[poseidon]]). Then the maxim, and the claim it supports: “But pity me, lord; I claim to be your suppliant” (*Odyssey* 5.450; see [[supplication]] and [[pity]]).

**The maxim.** αἰδοῖος is one who is owed αἰδώς, respect (see [[aidos]]): Calypso greets Hermes as “respected and dear” (*Odyssey* 5.88), and Lycaon, begging Achilles for his life, says “I am as good as a revered suppliant to you” (ἱκέταο … αἰδοίοιο, *Iliad* 21.74–75). The claim is that the rule holds “even” among the immortals: the wanderer who comes to a god is owed what he is owed by men. ἀνδρῶν goes with ὅς τις, “whoever of men”, the relative clause standing as a noun (Merry on 5.448). Odysseus at once applies it to himself, “as I now / come to your stream and to your knees, after toiling much” (*Odyssey* 5.448–449): the knees are the suppliant's goal (see [[gounata]]), and he names them in words.

**The answer.** “So he spoke, and at once the god stopped his stream, and held back the wave, / and made a calm before him, and brought him safe / into the river's mouth” (*Odyssey* 5.451–453). Odysseus' knees and strong hands give way, and he lies breathless and speechless; when his spirit is gathered back, he unties the veil and lets it go into the river, which carries it back to Ino (*Odyssey* 5.453–462; see [[leucothea]]).

**Suppliants and strangers.** As the poem moves into the houses of men it repeats the rule there. Nausicaa tells her maids that all strangers and beggars are from Zeus (*Odyssey* 6.207–208); the Phaeacians pour libations to Zeus “who attends upon reverend suppliants” (ὅς θʼ ἱκέτῃσιν ἅμʼ αἰδοίοισιν ὀπηδεῖ, *Odyssey* 7.165, 7.181); and Odysseus bids the Cyclops respect the gods, for he and his men are his suppliants, and Zeus is the avenger of suppliants and strangers (*Odyssey* 9.269–271; see [[xenia]] and [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). In the Iliad Zeus sends Priam to Achilles with the assurance that “with great care he will spare a man who comes as a suppliant” (*Iliad* 24.157–158), and Achilles himself warns the old man not to stir his anger, lest he not spare even him, “suppliant though you are, and sin against the commands of Zeus” (*Iliad* 24.569–570).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod counts the man who does evil to a suppliant or a stranger among the wrongdoers with whom Zeus himself is angry and on whom he lays a harsh requital in the end, beside the man who goes up into his brother's bed, wrongs orphans or abuses his old father (*Works and Days* 327–334).
