**The warning.** Athena comes to Telemachus at Sparta, where he lies awake with cares for his father, and gives him two reasons to go home at once. The first is his mother's marriage: her father and her brothers are bidding her marry Eurymachus, who surpasses all the suitors in his gifts and has increased his bride-price, “beware lest against your will she carry some possession from the house” (*Odyssey* 15.16–19; see [[bride-price]], [[eurymachus]] and [[possessions]]). The maxim gives the reason for the fear. A woman's heart goes with her marriage: she wishes to increase the house of the man who marries her, and her first husband, once he is dead, and the children she had by him, she no longer remembers or asks about. The remedy follows, to entrust everything to the best of the slave women “until the gods show you a glorious wife” (*Odyssey* 15.24–26; see [[handmaids]] and [[marriage]]), and then the second reason, the ambush the suitors have laid in the strait (*Odyssey* 15.27–32; see [[ambush]]). Agamemnon's counsel in the underworld has the same order: first that there is nothing more dreadful than a woman who devises such deeds as Clytemnestra's, then, with the same turn, “And I will tell you something else”, a warning about the landing (*Odyssey* 11.427–434, 11.454–456; 15.27; see [[there-is-no-trusting-women]]).

**The words.** οἶκον ὀφέλλειν, “to increase the house”, takes up the word of Eurymachus' gifts, ἐξώφελλεν ἔεδνα, “has increased his bride-price” (*Odyssey* 15.18, 15.21; see the translation's table of key words): the wooer increases the gifts, the wife the house. The relative clause ὅς κεν ὀπυίῃ, “the man who marries her”, is unusual with κεν in a general statement; it might be defended because the wish to increase is felt as a future, but, Monro adds, it is of little use to discuss the syntax until the lost digamma of οἶκον has been restored (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 15.21). κουριδίοιο φίλοιο, “her dear wedded husband”, has ἀνδρός understood (Monro on *Odyssey* 15.22; see [[marriage]]). Monro compares the word in the beggar's consolation to Penelope, where the thought is the opposite of Athena's: any woman mourns when she has lost her wedded husband, “to whom she has borne children” (*Odyssey* 19.265–266, a plain rendering).

**Against the poem.** The warning is the goddess' means of hurrying a son home, and the poem does not bear it out. Agamemnon himself excepted Penelope from his counsel, “for she has great good sense, and knows sound counsels in her heart” (*Odyssey* 11.444–446). Penelope, when she resolves to set the contest of the bow for the suitors, thinks of the house of her marriage that she will leave, “which I think I shall remember some day even in my dreams” (*Odyssey* 19.570–581, a plain rendering). And the shade of Agamemnon answers the maxim in its own words at the end of the poem: “so well did she remember Odysseus, / her wedded husband”, ὡς εὖ μέμνητʼ … ἀνδρὸς κουριδίου, while Clytemnestra will bring harsh talk on the womenfolk, “even to one who does good” (*Odyssey* 24.194–202, a plain rendering; see [[husbands-and-wives]], [[penelope]] and [[the-talk-of-the-people]]).
