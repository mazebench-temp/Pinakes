**At the door.** Athena has led Odysseus through the city of the Phaeacians, hidden in her mist, with the warning that these men do not much put up with strangers, “nor do they greet with open arms and make welcome whoever comes from elsewhere” (*Odyssey* 7.32–33; see [[xenia]] and [[phaeacians]]). At the palace she tells him that he will find the kings whom Zeus has nurtured feasting at their feast, and bids him go in and not be afraid (*Odyssey* 7.49–51; see [[kings]]). Merry compares Tydeus, who came as a messenger to Thebes and found the Cadmeians feasting in the house of Eteocles: “There, though he was a stranger, horse-driving Tydeus / was not afraid, alone among the many Cadmeians” (4.385–388; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 7.49; see [[tydeus]]). The maxim gives the reason. θαρσαλέος is “bold”, the adjective of θάρσος, the daring that Homer's gods put into men (see [[tharsos]]), and τελέθει, “proves to be”, makes the sentence a general truth. The other half of the advice follows at once: he is to find the queen first, Arete (*Odyssey* 7.53–77; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[supplication]]).

**The second verse.** εἰ καί ποθεν ἄλλοθεν ἔλθοι, “even if he comes from some other place”, turns the maxim to the stranger, and its words answer Athena's warning: the Phaeacians do not welcome “whoever comes from elsewhere” (ὅς κʼ ἄλλοθεν ἔλθῃ, *Odyssey* 7.33), but the bold man fares better even so. Bekker and others reject the verse, and a scholium records a reading εἰ καὶ μάλα τηλόθεν, “even if from very far away”; Merry thinks that the addition spoils the general application of the gnome, which is far more sententious if it ends at ἀμείνων (Merry's critical note on *Odyssey* 7.52; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 7.52).

**Boldness and shame.** Merry sets beside it Alcinous' maxim at the end of the evening, “what is due is better in all things” (*Odyssey* 7.310; see [[what-is-due-is-better]]), and Penelope's word when the beggar will not come to her at once, κακὸς δʼ αἰδοῖος ἀλήτης, “it will never do for a mendicant to be shy”, in Merry's rendering (*Odyssey* 17.578; Merry on *Odyssey* 7.310; see [[aidos]]). The beggar's boldness can also be blamed: Antinous calls him “a bold and shameless beggar” (θαρσαλέος καὶ ἀναιδής … προΐκτης, *Odyssey* 17.449, a plain rendering), and Penelope calls the maid Melantho θαρσαλέη, κύον ἀδεές, “bold one, fearless dog” (*Odyssey* 19.91, a plain rendering; see [[dogs]]). The adverb is the suitors' grudging praise of a young man: they marvel at Telemachus “that he spoke so boldly” (*Odyssey* 1.381–382; again 18.410–411, 20.268–269), and Antinous says that the gods themselves are teaching him “to be a man of lofty speech and to speak boldly” (*Odyssey* 1.384–385).
