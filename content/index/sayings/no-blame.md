{{quote:476eb6c1-ae21-5073-8975-ce9354c33f2e}}

The old men of Troy, Priam's counselors, sit at the Scaean gates, done with war because of their age but still excellent speakers, like cicadas singing in a tree (3.146–153; see [[like-cicadas]] and [[trojan-elders]]). When Helen comes onto the tower they speak “softly” (ἦκα), to one another and not to her (3.155).

οὐ νέμεσις: there is no cause for [[nemesis|νέμεσις]], the indignation people feel at what is improper, in Trojans and Achaeans suffering so long for such a woman. The reason is her looks. She is “terribly” (αἰνῶς) like the immortal goddesses “to look on” (εἰς ὦπα). Homer never describes Helen's face; her beauty is given entirely by its effect on these old men, and the effect has fear in it.

{{quote:2c63f7c6-36e4-5f2c-bc2c-290ac8e7b707}}

The verdict has a second half. ἀλλὰ καὶ ὧς, “but even so”: let her go home, and not be left here as a grief (πῆμα) to us and to our children after us. The men who excuse the war want it ended, and they know what it will cost their children. Priam, who speaks next, goes further than his counselors and tells Helen that she is not to blame at all (3.164; see [[aitios]]). See [[beauty-and-its-price]] and [[who-is-to-blame]].

## Outside Homer

Valerius Maximus tells that the painter Zeuxis, when he had painted Helen, wrote under the picture the lines in which the old men say there is no blame in suffering so long for such a woman (3.7 ext. 3; see [[zeuxis-helen]]). G. E. Lessing made the passage his example of how poetry can show beauty, which it cannot describe part by part as painting can: Homer shows what Helen's beauty did to the old men who saw her (*Laokoon*, 1766, chapter 21; see [[lessing-helen]]).

In one of Lucian's *Dialogues of the Dead*, Hermes shows the Cynic Menippus Helen's skull. Menippus asks whether the thousand ships sailed for this, and Hermes answers with the elders' verdict: had he seen her alive, he too would have said there was no blame (see [[lucian-helens-skull]]). In Marlowe's *Doctor Faustus* the scholars who see Helen's shape pass over the stage say it again: “No marvel though the angry Greeks pursued / With ten years' war the rape of such a queen” (see [[marlowe-helen]]).
