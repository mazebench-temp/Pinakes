**The choice.** Achilles has just said that nothing is worth his life: cattle and sheep can be raided and tripods won, but a man's soul cannot come back once it has passed the barrier of his teeth (9.401–409; see [[psyche]]). Then he gives his reason. His mother, the goddess Thetis, has told him that “two different fates are carrying me toward the end of death” (9.411; see [[thetis]]). διχθάδιος is “twofold, split two ways”; the neuter plural is used when Nestor ponders, divided in his heart, which of two ways to go (14.20–21). κῆρες are the fates of death; Zeus sets two of them in his scales (8.70; see [[ker-doom]]). In θανάτοιο τέλος δέ the δέ is the suffix of direction, “toward the end of death”.

The two halves are built alike. Each begins with a condition, to stay and fight or to go home, and each says what is lost, in the same verb: ὤλετο μέν μοι νόστος, “my homecoming is lost”, and ὤλετό μοι κλέος ἐσθλόν, “my noble fame is lost” (9.413, 9.415; see [[nostos]] and [[kleos]]). The aorist speaks of the loss as already done. Fame is set against homecoming, and a long life (αἰών) against fame. The speech that follows draws the conclusion: he will sail home at dawn, and the others should sail too (9.417–420, 9.356–363).

**Imperishable fame.** κλέος ἄφθιτον occurs only here in Homer. ἄφθιτος, “unwithering, imperishable” (from φθίνω, “waste, perish”), is elsewhere in the Iliad a word for the things of the gods: Agamemnon's ancestral scepter, “imperishable forever” (2.46, 2.186), the golden rim of Hera's chariot wheel (5.724), Poseidon's golden house (13.22), the throne Hera promises Sleep (14.238), Hephaestus' starry house (18.370), the counsels of Zeus (24.88). Fame is the one thing a mortal can have of that kind. The Odyssey's nearest phrase is ἄσβεστον κλέος, “unquenchable fame” (Od. 4.584, 7.333). Adalbert Kuhn noticed that the Rigveda joins the same two words, *śrávas* and *ákṣitam*, “imperishable fame” (*Zeitschrift für vergleichende Sprachforschung* 2, 1853, 467), and the phrase has since been taken as a piece of inherited Indo-European poetry (Rüdiger Schmitt, *Dichtung und Dichtersprache in indogermanischer Zeit*, 1967). Others answer that in Homer it is not a fixed formula and may have been made afresh (Margalit Finkelberg, “Is κλέος ἄφθιτον a Homeric Formula?”, *Classical Quarterly* 36, 1986).

**A choice only here.** Elsewhere Achilles' fate is simply short. In Book 1 he says that his mother bore him “short-lived” (1.352), and she calls him ὠκύμορος, “swift-fated” (1.417; see [[the-short-life-of-achilles]] and [[death-of-achilles-foretold]]). In Book 18 she tells him that his death is ready straight after Hector's (18.95–96). Only in this speech is there a second road, and whether the choice is old in the story or shaped for this moment, to give force to his threat to go home, is debated. The Iliad has one other choice of the kind. The seer Polyidus of Corinth often told his son Euchenor that he would die of a painful sickness at home or be killed by the Trojans among the ships; Euchenor sailed for Troy, and Paris killed him (13.663–672).

**The choice made.** In Book 18, when Patroclus is dead, Achilles takes the road to Troy: “let me die at once”, since he will not go home to his dear native land, and “now let me win noble fame” (18.98, 18.101, 18.121), in the words of 9.415, κλέος ἐσθλόν. The Odyssey hears both sides. His shade tells Odysseus that he would rather be a hired laborer on earth, serving a man without land, than be lord of all the dead (Od. 11.488–491); in the last book the shade of Agamemnon tells him that he did not lose his name even in death, and that his noble fame will be among all men forever (Od. 24.93–94).

## Outside Homer

In Plato's *Symposium* Phaedrus tells the choice in a form that joins this passage to Book 18: Achilles learned from his mother that he would die if he killed Hector, and that if he did not, he would go home and die an old man; he chose to help Patroclus, and the gods honored him and sent him to the Isles of the Blessed (179e–180a). In the *Apology* Socrates cites the same choice in his own defense (28c–d; see [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]).
