{{quote:05ad25b3-c91b-563a-8e72-d7546b5fd4a1}}

Hector has found Paris in his chamber, polishing his armor while the people die around the walls, and rebuked him (6.325–331). Paris grants that the rebuke is fair, denies that he sat apart in anger, and says that Helen has already urged him back to the fight (6.333–338; see [[aisa]] and [[nemesis]]). The maxim is his reason for going: it will be better so, for victory does not stay with one man. ἐπαμείβομαι, “pass in turn from one to another”, comes only here in Homer in this sense. The book has just used the same verb, in the active, for the exchange of armor that Diomedes proposed (ἐπαμείψομεν, 6.230), and its simple form for the exchange Glaucus made, gold for bronze (ἄμειβε, 6.235). In Paris' mouth victory is a thing that changes hands.

The saying is his answer to his defeat. After the duel with Menelaus he told Helen the same: “This time Menelaus has won, with Athena's help; another time I will beat him; for we have gods on our side too” (3.439–440). A man who has lost can call the loss a turn of fortune, and Paris does it twice. See [[nike]], [[paris]] and [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]].

The thought is common in the poem, and not only in Paris' mouth. Menelaus says that “the bounds of victory are held among the immortal gods” (7.101–102; see [[the-bounds-of-victory]]), and in Book 18 Hector, refusing to retreat before Achilles, gives the soldier's form of it: “Enyalius is impartial, and he kills the man who would kill” (ξυνὸς Ἐνυάλιος, καί τε κτανέοντα κατέκτα, 18.309). Nestor tells Diomedes that Zeus gives glory to Hector today, and another day, if he wishes, will give it to them (8.141–143). The poem's formula for a battle turning is ἑτεραλκέα νίκην, “the victory that turns the battle” (7.26, 8.171, 16.362, 17.627). Hector's saying in Book 18 is his answer to Polydamas, who has advised the Trojans to go back inside the walls, and the narrator calls the Trojans who cheer him fools (18.285–313).
