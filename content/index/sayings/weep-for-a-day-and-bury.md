**The argument.** Achilles has said that nothing will go down his throat while his companion lies dead in his hut, and that he would send the army out unfed (19.205–214; see [[fasting]]). Odysseus first answers that men quickly have their fill of battle and must eat to bear it (19.221–224; see [[satiety-of-battle]]), and then turns to the dead. γαστέρι, “with the belly”, is meant to make fasting as a way of mourning ridiculous, as Walter Leaf reads it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.225). The dead are too many, “in rows, day after day”; if every death were mourned by fasting, no one would ever draw breath. πόνοιο, “toil”, is the toil of the fasting to Leaf and the toil of battle to Monro (Leaf on 19.226; see the translation's note on 19.225–229 and [[ponos]]). So the rule: bury the dead, “having a pitiless heart” (νηλέα θυμόν), after weeping a single day (ἐπʼ ἤματι, within a day's space, as at 10.48; Leaf on 19.229), and remember drink and food, so as to fight on (19.228–233). καταθάπτειν, “bury”, includes the burning of the body (Leaf on 19.228, comparing *Odyssey* 12.11–13; see [[cremation]]).

**The rule and the poem.** Achilles keeps the opposite rule. He has vowed to give Patroclus no burial until he has brought back Hector's armor and head (18.333–337), and he refuses food until sunset (19.303–308). The poem gives Patroclus his funeral only in Book 23. In Book 24 it is Achilles who tells the mourning Priam that even Niobe remembered food, and who holds back the war while the Trojans mourn Hector for nine days, bury him on the tenth and heap his mound on the eleventh (24.601–620, 24.656–670; see [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[lament]]). Odysseus' single day is the rule of an army in the field; the poem's last funeral is a city's.

## Outside Homer

Cicero quotes the passage in Latin verse in the *Tusculan Disputations* (3.65). Arguing that grief is a matter of opinion and will, not of nature, he asks about those whom circumstances themselves forbid to mourn: in Homer “the daily killings and the deaths of many bring a calming of grief”, and he gives four lines that render 19.226–229: *Namque nimis multos atque omni luce cadentis / Cernimus, ut nemo possit maerore vacare. / Quo magis est aequum tumulis mandare peremptos / Firmo animo et luctum lacrimis finire diurnis*, “For we see too many falling every day for anyone to be free of grief; all the more right, then, to give the dead to the tomb with a firm heart, and to end mourning with a day's tears.”
