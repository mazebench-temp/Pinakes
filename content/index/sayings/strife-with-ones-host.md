**The challenge and its exception.** The stranger's discus has flown beyond all the marks, and Athena, in the form of a man, has told him that none of the Phaeacians will reach it (*Odyssey* 8.186–198; see [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]]). Glad of a gentle companion in the gathering, he speaks “more lightly” and challenges them all, in boxing or wrestling or running, since they have angered him, all “except Laodamas himself” (*Odyssey* 8.199–207; see [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]]). τῶν δʼ ἄλλων, “of the rest”, looks forward to the exception (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.204). The reason: “For he is my host” (*Odyssey* 8.208). ξεῖνος here is the host, as ξεινοδόκος is in the next verses (see the translation's table of key words and [[xenia]]), and the question “who would fight with one who makes him welcome?” is in Merry's words “Who would care to strive with his entertainer?” (on 8.208).

**The maxim.** οὐτιδανός is “good for nothing”, “profitless”, whether to others or to one's own self; here it is the second, as the last words of the sentence explain: the man who strives with his host “cuts short all that is his own” (Merry on 8.209). κολούω is to dock, from κόλος, “docked, shortened” (Merry on 8.211). For ἔριδα ἀέθλων, “strife in contests”, Merry compares ἔριδος ἀγών in Sophocles' *Ajax* (on 8.210; see [[eris]]). δήμῳ ἐν ἀλλοδαπῷ, “in a foreign land”, names the stranger's case: away from his own people he has only what his host gives him, and Laodamas' father has promised him an escort home (*Odyssey* 8.26–45; see [[pompe]]). The form of the maxim is the Iliad's for the man who loves war among his own people: “Without clan, without law, without hearth is the man / who loves war among his own people” (*Iliad* 9.63–64; see [[without-clan-law-or-hearth]]). οὐτιδανός comes back in the Cyclops' cave, where Polyphemus curses the evils that “good-for-nothing Nobody” has brought him (*Odyssey* 9.460, a plain rendering; see [[no-one-is-nameless]]).

**Guest and host.** The host is the one who “makes him welcome” (φιλέοντι), and to strive with him is, in the stranger's words, to cut short one's own good. Menelaus prays in the Iliad that Paris may be punished, “so that any man of those born hereafter may shudder / to do evil to a host who has shown him friendship” (ξεινοδόκον, *Iliad* 3.351–354; see [[paris]]). The stranger who will not box with Laodamas keeps the rule of the host in a game. His challenge to the others is open: “But of the others I refuse no one, nor do I make light of any” (*Odyssey* 8.212), and the man who taunted him, Euryalus, will make amends with a sword before the day is out (*Odyssey* 8.396–415; see [[euryalus-the-phaeacian]] and [[amends-and-reconciliation]]).
