**The last question.** Alcinous ends his questions with the one he has been holding back: why does the stranger weep and mourn when he hears the fate of the Argives and of Ilios, which the gods wrought for men “so that there might be song even for those yet to be” (*Odyssey* 8.577–580; see [[song-and-fame]] and [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]])? He guesses the reason in the order of a man's attachments. First a kinsman by marriage, “a son-in-law or a father-in-law, who are / the dearest after one's own blood and kin” (*Odyssey* 8.581–583; see [[in-laws]] and [[genee]]); then a companion, “a man of kindly heart, / a good man” (*Odyssey* 8.584–585; see [[hetairos]]). The maxim gives the reason for the second guess. Merry renders κεχαρισμένα εἰδώς “with fond heart”, comparing Priam's words to Achilles, “you would show me a kindness” (κεχαρισμένα θείης, *Iliad* 24.661) (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.584; see the translation's table of key words). χερείων, “inferior”, is the word with which Calypso claims to be in no way inferior to Penelope in form or stature (*Odyssey* 5.211; Merry on 8.585). Some manuscripts repeat κεχαρισμένα in the last verse, for πεπνυμένα (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 8.586): the companion who is worth a brother is the one who has wise understanding, and not only a kind heart.

**The answer.** The guesses fall short of what the hearer knows. The song was of the stranger's own deed, the horse he brought into the citadel as a snare (*Odyssey* 8.492–495), and his answer is his name, given at the beginning of the next book: “I am Odysseus, son of Laertes” (*Odyssey* 9.19, a plain rendering; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). Earlier in the same speech Alcinous measured the stranger and the suppliant by a brother too (*Odyssey* 8.546–547; see [[as-good-as-a-brother]] and [[kasignetos]]).

**Companions and kin.** The Iliad's great companion is worth more than kin. Achilles honored Patroclus “above all my companions, / as my own life” (*Iliad* 18.81–82), and over his body he says that he could suffer nothing worse, “not even if I should learn of the death of my father”, or of his son (*Iliad* 19.321–327; see [[patroclus]] and [[achilles]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod gives the opposite counsel: “Do not make a comrade equal to a brother; / but if you do, do not be the first to wrong him” (μηδὲ κασιγνήτῳ ἶσον ποιεῖσθαι ἑταῖρον, *Works and Days* 707–708; see [[hesiod]]). Euripides' Orestes takes the side of Alcinous, when Pylades offers to hold him up and carry him through the city: “This is the saying: get companions, not kin alone; / for a man who is joined to you in his ways, though he is from outside the house, / is a better friend to have than ten thousand of your own blood” (Euripides, *Orestes* 804–806, a plain rendering; see [[euripides]]).
