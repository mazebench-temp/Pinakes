**The question it will not answer.** Athena has made a phantom in the likeness of Penelope's sister Iphthime and sent it to her as she sleeps, to make her cease from her weeping; it tells her that her son will come home, for Pallas Athena goes with him as his escort (*Odyssey* 4.795–829; see [[iphthime]] and [[eidolon]]). Penelope asks the other question: if you are a god, tell me also of that wretched man, “whether he still lives somewhere and looks on the light of the sun, / or is already dead and in the house of Hades” (*Odyssey* 4.831–834). The phantom will not tell it διηνεκέως, “from beginning to end”, and the maxim is its reason. ἀνεμώλια is made from ἄνεμος, “wind” (see [[anemolios]]), and the phantom goes off past the bolt of the doorpost “into the blasts of the winds” (*Odyssey* 4.838–839). Merry takes θεοῖό τε ἔκλυες αὐδῆς as “art sent here at the bidding of some god”, as Hermes, himself a god, might be sent by Zeus (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.831): the phantom tells what it was sent to tell, and no more (see [[onar]]).

**Again in the Underworld.** The shade of Agamemnon asks Odysseus whether he has heard of his son alive anywhere, at Orchomenus, at Pylos or at Sparta, and Odysseus answers with Proteus' words to Menelaus, “Son of Atreus, why do you ask me this?” (*Odyssey* 4.492), and with this verse: he does not know whether Orestes is alive or dead, and it is a bad thing to speak empty wind (*Odyssey* 11.457–464). Both times the one who asks wants news of the living or the dead, and both times the one who is asked will not guess (see [[truth-and-deception]]).
