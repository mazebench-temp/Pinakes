Aeneas has answered Achilles' taunts with the lineage of Dardanus (20.213–241), and the maxim that closes it answers what Achilles said before. Achilles reminded him that he once ran from him down the mountains of Ida and escaped to Lyrnessus, and that “Zeus and the other gods rescued you” (20.187–194); Walter Leaf takes Aeneas' line as an allusion to that sarcasm (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.242). If prowess is Zeus' to give and to take away, the flight was no shame, and the rescue was Zeus' as well. ὀφέλλω is “increase, make grow”, and μινύθω “make less” (see [[arete]]).

The thought is common in the poem. Hector told the Trojans that the help of Zeus is easy to see, both in those to whom he gives the higher glory “and in those whom he diminishes and will not defend, / as now he diminishes the might of the Argives” (15.490–493; see [[the-help-of-zeus-is-easy-to-see]]). The narrator says that the mind of Zeus is always stronger than men's: “he puts even a valiant man to flight and takes away victory / easily” (16.688–690; see [[the-mind-of-zeus]]). Agamemnon tells Achilles that his strength is a god's gift (1.178; see [[strength-is-a-gift]]). The reason Aeneas gives, “for he is the mightiest of all”, is Nestor's for giving way to Zeus, “since he is far the stronger” (8.144).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod opens the *Works and Days* with the same power of Zeus: “easily he makes strong, and easily he brings the strong low; easily he diminishes the conspicuous and increases the obscure” (ῥεῖα δʼ ἀρίζηλον μινύθει καὶ ἄδηλον ἀέξει, *Works and Days* 5–6; see [[hesiod]]). In the *Odyssey* Odysseus tells Telemachus that it is easy for the gods who hold the wide sky both to glorify a mortal man and to bring him low (*Odyssey* 16.211–212).
