summary: Sarpedon's speech to Glaucus before the Achaean wall, the Iliad's fullest account of why the best men fight. **The lion.** Zeus rouses his son against the Argives “like a lion against cattle with curving horns” (12.292–293), and as Sarpedon advances behind his shield the simile grows: a mountain-bred lion, long without meat, whose proud spirit bids him go into the close-built fold, will not be driven off without a try, though herdsmen guard the sheep with dogs and spears, “but either he springs on one and seizes it, or he himself / is hit among the foremost by a javelin from a swift hand” (12.299–306; see [[the-mountain-lion-and-the-fold]]). The simile states the speech's choice before the speech is made. “So then his spirit sent godlike Sarpedon / to rush at the wall” (12.307–308), and he turns to Glaucus, his cousin, a grandson of Bellerophon as he is (6.196–206), and with him the leader of the Lycians (2.876; see [[glaucus]] and [[sarpedon]]).

**What the honors are for.** The honors come first, and they are things that can be seen (see [[time]] and [[honor-and-dishonor]]). “A seat of honor, with meat, and with full cups” (12.311) is the verse with which Hector taunts Diomedes for the honors the Danaans gave him (8.161–163), and Agamemnon tells Idomeneus that his cup “always / stands full” at the feasts of the elders (4.262–263; see [[feast]]). All “look on us as gods” (12.312), as the Phaeacians look on their honored queen Arete, and on a man to whom a god has given grace in speaking (*Odyssey* 7.69–72, 8.169–173). The great domain by the Xanthus, “a fair one, of orchard and wheat-bearing plowland” (12.313–314), is the kind the Lycians marked out for Bellerophon (6.194–195; see [[bellerophon-in-lycia]] and [[xanthus-of-lycia]]) and the Aetolian elders promised Meleager (9.574–580). Then the duty: “So now we must take our stand among the foremost Lycians / and meet the blazing battle” (12.315–316). Agamemnon used nearly the same two verses to rebuke Menestheus and Odysseus, the first to be called to his feasts, for standing back (4.341–346). Sarpedon applies them to himself. The honors are paid in advance and earned in the front rank, so that one of the close-armored Lycians may say that their kings rule “not without glory”, for “there is strength in them too, / noble strength, since they fight among the foremost Lycians” (12.317–321; see [[kleos]] and [[tis-speech]]).

**What mortality decides.** The second argument goes deeper. If the two of them, once they had escaped this war, were to be “ageless and immortal forever”, Sarpedon would not fight among the foremost himself, nor send Glaucus into “the battle where men win glory” (12.322–325; see [[where-men-win-glory]]). Ageless and immortal is the condition of the gods: Zeus says it of the horses of Achilles, given to a mortal man (17.443–444), and Hector swore by his wish for it, “If only I were / immortal and ageless all my days” (8.538–541; see [[immortal-and-ageless]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]). Hector wishes to be what he cannot be. Sarpedon reasons from what he is: since “the fates of death stand over us, / countless, which no mortal can flee or escape” (12.326–327; see [[ker-doom]]), what is left to choose is how to meet them.

**Triumph given or taken.** “Let us go, and either hold out triumph to another, or another to us” (12.328). εὖχος ὀρέξαι, “hold out triumph”, is made like κῦδος ὀρέξαι, “hold out glory”, the phrase of a god's gift to men (12.174; see [[euchomai]] and [[kydos]]). Here men give it to one another, and the giver may be either one. The half-verse comes back when Idomeneus asks Meriones to go with him to the left of the battle, to see which of them will give the triumph and which take it (13.326–327), and when Hector, waiting for Achilles before the walls, would rather fight at once and see to which of them the Olympian will hold out triumph (22.129–130). Odysseus' rule for the man who would be best ends in the same place: he must stand his ground, “whether he is struck or strikes another” (11.408–410; see [[the-cowards-leave-the-fighting]]).

**Afterward.** “So he spoke, and Glaucus did not turn away, nor disobey” (12.329), and the two lead the Lycians against Menestheus' tower (12.330–332). Teucer's arrow hits Glaucus, who leaps back from the wall unseen, so that no Achaean should see him hit and boast over him; Sarpedon kills Alcmaon, pulls down a battlement and opens the wall, and Zeus wards off the fates from his son (12.387–407; see [[sarpedon]]). The speech's choice is decided in Book 16. Zeus, seeing Patroclus come against Sarpedon, wonders aloud to Hera whether to snatch his son alive out of the battle and set him down in Lycia; she answers that a mortal long since doomed cannot be freed from death, and he lets him die (16.431–461; see [[not-all-the-gods-approve]]). Sarpedon's last words are a charge to Glaucus, his “dear friend” (πέπον) as at the wall, to rouse the Lycian leaders and fight over his body (16.492–501).
