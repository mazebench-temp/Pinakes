Odysseus has finished the long offer, and Achilles begins with a declaration of method. He will speak ἀπηλεγέως, “without regard for anyone”, as he means it and as it will be done, so that the envoys need not sit beside him and murmur, “one from this side, one from that” (9.309–311). τρύζω, used only here in Homer, is the cooing of doves (see the translation's note on 9.311). Then the maxim: he hates like the gates of Hades the man ὅς χʼ ἕτερον μὲν κεύθῃ ἐνὶ φρεσίν, ἄλλο δὲ εἴπῃ, who hides one thing in his mind and says another (see [[phrenes]] and [[truth-and-deception]]).

**The gates of Hades.** The πύλαι Ἀΐδαο are the gates through which the dead go down: Tlepolemus promises Sarpedon that he will pass them (5.646), and the ghost of Patroclus asks to be buried so that he may (23.71). To hate a man like the gates of Hades is to hate him as one hates death. Achilles has not heard Agamemnon's last lines, which Odysseus left out of the offer: Hades “is implacable and untamable, and for that he is the most hateful to mortals of all the gods” (ἔχθιστος, 9.158–159). Achilles reaches for the same hateful god without knowing that the king has just used him against Achilles: an untamable man is as hateful as Hades (see [[hades]] and [[message-repeated]]).

**Whom does he mean?** The maxim may be aimed at Agamemnon, whom Achilles soon accuses of deceiving him (9.344, 9.375; see [[apate]]); at Odysseus, the master of guile, who has just repeated the offer without its demand; or at no one, as a statement of his own way of speaking. Readers have argued for each. Odysseus does not answer.

**In the Odyssey.** The line comes back once, in the mouth of Homer's great liar. Odysseus, disguised as a beggar in Eumaeus' hut, tells the swineherd that his master will come home, and before he swears to it he says: “hateful to me as the gates of Hades is the man who yields to poverty and tells deceitful tales” (*Odyssey* 14.156–157). Then he tells a long false tale of himself.

## Outside Homer

In Plato's *Hippias Minor* the sophist Hippias quotes the passage, “in the Prayers”, to show that Homer made Achilles “the simplest and truest” of men and Odysseus wily and false (364e–365b). His text differs from ours: for 9.310 it reads ὥσπερ δὴ κρανέω τε καὶ ὡς τελέεσθαι ὀίω, “as I shall accomplish it and as I think it will be fulfilled”, and it has no 9.311. Socrates turns the lines against their speaker: the man who told Odysseus he would sail at dawn (9.357–363) told Ajax that he would stay (9.650–655), and never sailed (369e–371e; see [[plato-hippias-minor]]).
