**Along the towers.** The Trojans, trusting the storm Zeus has sent from Ida, tear at the copings and battlements of the wall and lever out its buttresses, and the Danaans fence the battlements with oxhides and shoot from behind them (12.251–264; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[battlements]]). The two Ajaxes range “along the towers / in every direction, urging on the might of the Achaeans” (12.265–266; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[menos]]). Agamemnon had told the pair in Book 4 that he gave them no orders, “for you yourselves bid the people fight with all their might” (4.285–287), and here they do it. They rebuke whomever they see holding back from the battle, “one with soothing words, another with harsh words” (12.267–268; see [[neikos]]), as Agamemnon went along the ranks encouraging the eager and rebuking “those whom he saw holding back from hateful war” (4.232–241; see [[exhortation]]).

**Three kinds of men.** The address names three ranks: ἔξοχος, “outstanding”, μεσήεις, “middling”, a word found only here, and χερειότερος, “worse”, which the Iliad uses elsewhere only when Odysseus tells Thersites that “there is no mortal worse than you” (2.248; see the translation's note on 12.269–270). The maxim gives the reason for naming them all: men are by no means all alike in war. The armies of the Iliad are ordered on that principle. Nestor sets the brave behind the chariots and drives the cowards into the middle, “so that even an unwilling man would have to fight” (4.297–300; see [[battle-order]]), and Odysseus holds back a king or a man of note “with gentle words” but strikes a man of the people with the scepter (2.188–199; see [[the-army-and-its-leaders]]). The Ajaxes grant the difference and set it aside: “now there is work for all” (νῦν ἔπλετο ἔργον ἅπασι, 12.271). ἔργον is the work of fighting, the “great task” that appears before the Argives when the Lycians come on (12.416), and the work that Sarpedon says is better done by more men (12.412; see [[the-work-of-many]]).

**The man who shouts.** “Let no one turn back / toward the ships at the sound of the man who shouts at us” (12.272–273). ὁμοκλητήρ is one who shouts in command or threat, used once more of Diomedes shouting at his horses in the chariot race (23.450–452); here it is Hector (see the translation's note on 12.273 and [[hector]]). What the leaders do, the men are to do for one another: “press forward, and urge one another on” (12.274). The hope rests on Zeus, “the Olympian lord of the lightning”, that he may let them “thrust back the quarrel and drive the enemy to the city” (12.275–276; see [[zeus]]). He does not grant it that day. Before the book ends Hector breaks the gate, and the Danaans flee among the ships (12.453–471).

**The other half of the thought.** In the next book Poseidon rouses the two Ajaxes first, in the likeness of Calchas (13.43–58), and then, as Thoas, gives Idomeneus the other half of their maxim: men are not all alike, but joined together even very poor men have courage (συμφερτὴ δʼ ἀρετὴ πέλει ἀνδρῶν καὶ μάλα λυγρῶν, 13.237; see [[arete]]).
