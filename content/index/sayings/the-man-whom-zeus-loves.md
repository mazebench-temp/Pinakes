Nestor has told Agamemnon that he dishonored “the best of men, whom the immortals themselves honored” (9.110–111). Agamemnon grants it at once: he was blinded, and the proof is the battle. The maxim comes between the confession and the offer (see [[ate]]). ἀντί, “against, in place of”, sets one man in the scale against many people; the healer Machaon is called “worth many other men” (πολλῶν ἀντάξιος ἄλλων, 11.514), and Achilles will say that nothing is worth his life (ψυχῆς ἀντάξιον, 9.401; see [[psyche]]). Agamemnon does not name the man. τοῦτον, “this man”, is enough.

**Zeus' love and the king's.** In the quarrel Agamemnon told Achilles to run home if he liked: “I have others at my side who will honor me, and above all Zeus the counselor” (1.174–175). Odysseus said the same of kings in Book 2: “a king's honor comes from Zeus, and Zeus the counselor loves him” (2.197). Now the king admits that Zeus loves another man, and that his love has been paid in Achaean lives: “as now he has honored this man and beaten down the army of the Achaeans” (9.118). Thetis asked Zeus to give the Trojans the upper hand until the Achaeans honored her son (1.508–510), and Zeus promised (see [[plan-of-zeus]] and [[time]]). Agamemnon has understood the plan from its effects.

The admission is not repeated to Achilles. Odysseus reports the gifts and leaves out the king's words about himself (9.264–299; see [[message-repeated]]), and Achilles answers that Agamemnon is a man whose wits Zeus has taken away (9.377). Both men see Zeus in the defeat; neither hears the other say so.
