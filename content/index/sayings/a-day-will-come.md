Agamemnon has just seen Pandarus' arrow draw his brother's blood in the middle of a sworn truce. He holds Menelaus by the hand and declares that an oath is never in vain: if Zeus does not punish its breakers at once, he punishes them late (4.158–162; see [[fulfilled-late]]). Then comes the certainty, introduced by a line of its own, “For I know this well in my mind and in my heart” (4.163). The day will come, and Zeus will shake his dark aegis over the Trojans, “in resentment at this deceit” (4.166–168). ἔσσεται ἦμαρ, “there will be a day”, sets the end at an unnamed time, certain and not yet.

In Book 6 Hector says the same three lines to Andromache, and the same words change their meaning (6.447–449). Agamemnon speaks as the avenger, sure that the gods will punish the enemy; Hector speaks as the defender, sure that his city will fall, and says that he grieves less for Troy and his parents and brothers than for her, when some Achaean leads her away a captive (6.450–465). The poem gives the prophecy of Troy's end to both sides. See [[sack-of-troy-foretold]] and [[troy]].

## Outside Homer

In 146 BCE Scipio Aemilianus, watching Carthage burn, wept and spoke these lines, and when Polybius asked what he meant, he named Rome (Polybius 38.21–22; Appian, *Punica* 132; see [[scipio-at-carthage]]).
