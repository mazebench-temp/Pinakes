## Narrator and characters

Plato had drawn the same distinction to a different end. In the *Republic* Socrates distinguishes plain narrative, in which the poet speaks as himself, from imitation, in which he speaks as if he were someone else, and calls Homer a poet who uses both; he retells the opening of the Iliad, Chryses' plea to the Achaeans, as plain narrative without speeches to show the difference (3, 392c–394b; see [[return-of-chryseis]]). Aristotle turns the distinction into praise: the poet is an imitator only when his characters speak, and Homer, more than any other epic poet, lets them speak (see [[modes-of-imitation]] and [[homer]]). Nearly half of the Iliad is direct speech.
