{{quote:ed341eac-f8c1-57a3-ac02-70513c4d17b9}}

Nestor has just repeated the charge that Menoetius gave his son on the day he sent him from Phthia: Achilles is the better born and far the stronger, but Patroclus is the elder, and he must “speak a shrewd word to him well, and give him counsel, and direct him; and he will obey you, to his good” (11.786–789; see [[menoetius]] and [[peleus-sends-achilles-to-troy]]). “So the old man charged you, but you forget” is the reproach Odysseus made to Achilles about Peleus' charge (11.790 = 9.259; see [[kindliness-is-better]]). The maxim gives the reason a companion can do what envoys could not. παραίφασις, “persuasion, encouragement”, occurs only here and in the repetition in Book 15. It belongs to παράφημι, “talk round, advise”, whose participle stands at the start of the verse (παρειπών, “by persuading him”): Agamemnon turns his brother's mind “urging what was due” (αἴσιμα παρειπών, 6.62, 7.121), and Hera tells Zeus that she fears Thetis “has talked you round” (1.555; see [[peitho]]). The embassy of Book 9 came with gifts and with the claims of honor and friendship (see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]); Nestor sends the companion who is “dear to my heart”, as Achilles called him that afternoon (11.608; see [[hetairos]]), and leaves room for a god, σὺν δαίμονι, “with a god's help” (see [[daimon]]).

Nestor's own words do to Patroclus what he hopes Patroclus' words will do to Achilles: “So he spoke, and stirred the heart in his breast” (ὄρινε, 11.804; ὀρίναις, 11.792). Patroclus runs back along the ships, but stops to tend Eurypylus (11.805–848). When he leaves him in Book 15, seeing the Trojans storm the wall, he makes Nestor's words his own: he will hurry to Achilles to urge him to fight, and “who knows if, with a god's help”, he may stir his heart by persuading him; the persuasion of a companion is good (15.399–404; see [[patroclus]]).

In Book 16 he goes straight to Nestor's second plan, that Achilles send him out in his armor (16.36–45 ≈ 11.794–803), and the narrator calls him a fool, for he was begging for his own death (16.46–47; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). The companion persuades Achilles to send him, not to fight.
