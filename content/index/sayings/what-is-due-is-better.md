**Alcinous.** Odysseus has excused Nausicaa by his own fear of the king's anger, “for we tribes of men on the earth are quick to anger” (*Odyssey* 7.303–307; see [[quick-to-anger]]). The king answers that his heart is not such as to be angry μαψιδίως, “for no reason” (see the translation's table of key words), and closes with the maxim. Merry renders it “fair measure in all things is best”, and sets beside it the gnome of Athena at the palace door, “a bold man is better in all deeds” (*Odyssey* 7.51–52; see [[a-bold-man-is-better]]), Penelope's word on the beggar who will not come to her, κακὸς δʼ αἰδοῖος ἀλήτης, in his rendering “it will never do for a mendicant to be shy” (*Odyssey* 17.578), and a verse of Sophocles' *Antigone* (1327) (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 7.310). The king then shows the measure he speaks of: the wish that such a man might stay and marry his daughter, the promise that no Phaeacian will keep him against his will, and the escort for the next day (*Odyssey* 7.311–328; see [[nausicaas-wish]] and [[pompe]]).

**The word.** αἴσιμα, “what is due”, is the neuter plural of αἴσιμος, from αἶσα, a portion, and so the fair share, the measure that is fitting (see [[aisa]]). Agamemnon turns Menelaus from sparing the suppliant Adrastus, and later from fighting Hector, “urging what was due” (αἴσιμα παρειπών, 6.61–62, 7.120–121); it is a good thing, too, “when a messenger knows what is due” (15.207; see [[a-messenger-who-knows-what-is-due]]); and Mentor, in the assembly of Ithaca, would have no king know in his mind what is due, since no one remembers Odysseus (*Odyssey* 2.230–234 = 5.8–12). Alcinous' maxim is the same word, generalized: in all things, πάντα, the due measure is better than the excess.

**Menelaus.** The half-verse comes again when Menelaus lets Telemachus go home from Sparta. He will not keep him long, since he longs for his return; he is indignant with a host who loves beyond measure and hates beyond measure, “what is due is better in all things”; it is as bad to urge a guest to go who does not wish it as to hold back one who is eager, and one should welcome the stranger who is present and send him off when he wishes (*Odyssey* 15.68–74, a plain rendering; see [[xenia]]). In both places the maxim is spoken by a host to a guest who wants to go home, and in both it rules out excess: of anger in Alcinous, of welcome and of detaining in Menelaus.
