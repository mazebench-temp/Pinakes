The poet will name the leaders and the ships, but not the πληθύς, “the multitude” (2.488; see [[commoners]]). Even a body built for the task would fail. The list runs from the organs of speech to the source of strength: ten tongues, ten mouths, a voice that could not break (ἄρρηκτος), and a heart of bronze (χάλκεον … ἦτορ; see [[etor]]). Bronze is the metal of weapons, and the singer imagines himself with a warrior's endurance. It would not be enough. Only memory could do it, and memory is the gift of the Muses: “unless the Olympian Muses … were to remind me (μνησαίαθʼ) of all who came to Ilios” (2.491–492).

The disclaimer is a figure of speech, and it clears the ground for what the poet can do. “But I will tell the leaders of the ships, and all the ships” (2.493), he says, and for the next 266 lines he does, contingent by contingent (2.494–759; see [[catalogue-of-ships]]). The ten tongues measure a task beyond any singer, and the Catalogue is the part of it that a singer can perform with the Muses' help. See [[invocation-of-the-muses]], [[we-hear-only-the-report]] and [[memory-and-the-muses]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes the Muses the daughters of Mnemosyne, Memory (*Theogony* 53–54). Virgil raised the figure to a hundred tongues, a hundred mouths and a voice of iron (*Georgics* 2.43–44; *Aeneid* 6.625–627), and by the next century Persius could mock it as a habit of poets (*Satires* 5.1–2). See [[ten-tongues-in-virgil]].
