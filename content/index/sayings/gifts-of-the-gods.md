Hector has called Paris “best in looks” and a coward, and thrown his lyre, his hair and his looks in his face as “the gifts of Aphrodite” (3.39–55). Paris grants that the rebuke is fair (3.59) and answers only this: do not reproach me with the lovely gifts of golden Aphrodite. The maxim follows. The gifts of the gods are ἐρικυδέα (“glorious”; see [[kydos]]) and not ἀπόβλητα, “to be thrown away”; the gods give them “of themselves” (αὐτοί), and ἑκὼν δʼ οὐκ ἄν τις ἕλοιτο, “no man could take them by his own will”. A gift of the gods cannot be chosen, and so it cannot be refused either. See [[doron]].

The saying is true and self-serving at once. Homer's heroes regularly credit the gods with what they are. Agamemnon belittles Achilles' strength: “If you are very strong, it was a god, I think, who gave you that” (1.178; see [[strength-is-a-gift]]), and the Odyssey's Odysseus says that the gods do not give looks, sense and eloquence all to one man (*Odyssey* 8.167–177). Paris uses the same idea to defend beauty without courage. Then, in the next breath, he offers to fight Menelaus alone for Helen (3.67–75).

The gifts of the gods are not always good. Achilles tells Priam in the last book that two jars stand on the floor of Zeus' house, one of evil gifts and one of good, and that Zeus gives some men a mixture and some men only evils (24.527–533). Paris' gift from Aphrodite brought Helen, and the war.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod's Pandora is the gift of all the gods to men, named for it, and “a bane to men who eat bread” (*Works and Days* 80–82).
