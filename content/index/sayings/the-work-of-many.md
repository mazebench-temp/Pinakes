**At the battlement.** Sarpedon has killed Alcmaon and pulled down a battlement with his hands, so that the wall was laid bare, “and he made a path for many” (12.394–399). Teucer's arrow hits the strap of his shield, Zeus wards off the fates from his son, and Ajax's spear drives him back a little from the battlement; he does not draw back altogether, “since his heart hoped to win glory” (12.400–407; see [[sarpedon]] and [[kydos]]). His call to the Lycians opens with a rebuke, “why do you let go of your furious courage like this?” (12.409), in the words of Agamemnon's call to the Argives, “do not yet let go of your furious courage” (4.234; see [[alke]] and [[furious]]). Then he names the difficulty: “It is hard for me, mighty though I am, / to break through alone and make a path to the ships” (12.410–411). He has made a path for many; he cannot make one alone to the ships.

**The maxim.** πλεόνων δέ τι ἔργον ἄμεινον, “the work of more men is better”. ἔργον is the work of war, as in the two Ajaxes' call along the towers, “now there is work for all” (12.271; see [[not-all-alike-in-war]]). On the night mission Diomedes asked for a companion with another maxim about numbers, “when two go together”, one sees before the other (10.224–226; see [[when-two-go-together]]). Diomedes' second man brings wits; Sarpedon's many bring weight. ἐφομαρτεῖτε, “come on with me”, is a word the Iliad otherwise gives to drivers urging their horses, in the dual: Hector to his horses as he drives the Achaeans toward their ships (8.185–191), and Antilochus to his father's pair in the chariot race (23.414).

**The answer.** “So he spoke, and they, fearing their lord's rebuke, / bore down the harder around their lord, the counselor” (12.413–414). The first of these verses comes twice in the chariot race, of horses that fear their driver's shout and run the harder, Antilochus' and Menelaus' (23.417, 23.446). ὁμοκλή is a shout of command or menace (see the translation's note on 12.273 and [[the-army-and-its-leaders]]). The Lycians press on, and the Argives strengthen their battalions inside the wall. Neither side can drive the other back: the battlements part them as a line parts two men disputing a boundary in a common field, and the battle hangs as level as the scales of an honest working woman weighing wool for a meager wage (12.415–436; see [[the-boundary-dispute]] and [[the-working-womans-scales]]).

**One man after all.** The breach is made by one man. Zeus gives Hector the higher glory, and Hector lifts a stone that two men, the best of the people, could not easily heave from the ground onto a wagon, “such as mortals are now; but he wielded it easily even alone”, for Zeus made it light for him (12.437–450; see [[men-of-today]] and [[stone-throwing]]). What Sarpedon could not do μούνῳ, “alone” (12.411), Hector does καὶ οἶος, “even alone” (12.449), with a god's help. When the gate is down he calls the Trojans after him, and they pour over the wall and through the gates (12.462–471; see [[the-fight-at-the-wall]]).
