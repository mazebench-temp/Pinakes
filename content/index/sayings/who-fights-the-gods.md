Dione has told her daughter three stories of gods who suffered at the hands of men, and she ends with the man who has hurt her (see [[dione]] and [[mythological-paradigm]]). Her threat turns from the battlefield to the house. δηναιός, “long-lived”, is found only here in Homer, and so is παππάζω, a child's word, “call papa”. The man who fights the gods will not come home to his children's knees. She names the wife he will leave: let him take care that no one better than Aphrodite fights him, or Aegialeia, the wise daughter of Adrastus, will long wake her household with her wailing, “longing for her wedded husband, the best of the Achaeans” (5.410–415; see [[aegialeia]]).

Diomedes does not hear her, and in the next book he says the same thing himself. He asks Glaucus whether he is a god, because he would not fight the heavenly gods: “not even the son of Dryas, mighty Lycurgus, lived long”, who chased the nurses of Dionysus and was blinded by Zeus, “since he was hated by all the immortal gods” (6.128–141). His δὴν ἦν, “lived long”, is Dione's word in another form. The Odyssey has another example. Eurytus of Oechalia challenged Apollo at archery, and “great Eurytus died soon, and did not reach old age in his halls”, for Apollo killed him in anger (*Odyssey* 8.223–228).

The threat is not fulfilled. Diomedes wounds Ares too before the day is out (5.855–859), and in the Odyssey Nestor tells how his companions brought their ships safe home to Argos (*Odyssey* 3.180–182). Dione speaks as a mother consoling a child, and the poem lets her words stand as a threat and nothing more. The men who fought the gods and did not live long belong to the stories of earlier generations; the one who does it in the Iliad has a goddess at his side.
