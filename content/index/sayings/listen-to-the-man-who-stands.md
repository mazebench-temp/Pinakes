**The scene.** Achilles has renounced his wrath, and the Achaeans have cheered (19.74–75). Agamemnon, wounded in the arm by Coon in *Iliad* 11 and come last to the assembly (19.51–53; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]), answers “from where he sat, and not standing up in their midst” (19.77). In an assembly the speaker stood, and sat down when he had finished (see [[sat-down-stood-up]] and [[assembly-type-scene]]); Achilles has just stood up to speak (19.55). Agamemnon's first words are about listening: a man on his feet should be heard and not interrupted, since even a practiced speaker is hindered in the din of many men, “clear-voiced orator though he is” (19.79–82; see [[clear-voiced-orator]] and [[assembly]]). Walter Leaf takes the hesitant opening as a picture of a man who knows he is in the wrong and must follow a speaker who has won the crowd, mortified by the applause that greeted Achilles (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.80 and 19.85).

**The dispute.** Zenodotus did not have 19.77, and wrote 19.76 as “and lord Agamemnon stood up and spoke among them”; the Massaliot and Chian texts had the same verse and one more, of Agamemnon groaning over the wrath and in pain from his wound. Without 19.77 the maxim is plain: listen to the man who is on his feet. With it, the scholia, which ascribe the view to Aristarchus, made it an excuse: Agamemnon, unable to stand, will speak sitting rather than through another man's mouth. Leaf thinks this second reading perverse, notes that Agamemnon's wound was in the arm and no reason not to stand, takes ὑββάλλειν as “throw in a word”, so “interrupt”, and regards 19.77 as an early and mistaken explanation of 19.79–80 (Leaf on 19.76; see the translation's note on 19.76–84). The translation keeps 19.77 and gives the maxim its plain sense.
