Odysseus has taken Agamemnon's scepter and is turning the army back from the ships (see [[the-test-of-the-army]]). To kings and men of note he speaks gently. A man of the people he strikes with the scepter and tells to sit still and listen to his betters: he is unwarlike and weak, never “counted for anything in war or in council”, and “we Achaeans shall not all be kings here” (2.198–203). Then comes the maxim.

πολυκοιρανίη, “the rule of many lords”, is formed from πολύς, “many”, and [[koiranos|κοίρανος]], “ruler, lord”; Homer has the noun only here. The answer is one κοίρανος, one βασιλεύς ([[basileus]]), and the next line says where his authority comes from: the son of Cronus has given him “the scepter and the ordinances” (σκῆπτρόν τʼ ἠδὲ θέμιστας; see [[scepter]] and [[themis]]), “so that he may take counsel for them” ([[boule|βουλεύῃσι]]). Rule is a gift of Zeus, and its purpose is counsel for the people.

The maxim is spoken with the king's own staff in Odysseus' hand and a blow just given (see [[scepter]]). It is also spoken in a camp full of kings. Every leader in the Catalogue is a βασιλεύς, and the quarrel of Book 1 was about how far one of them could command another. Odysseus claims for Agamemnon the undivided authority that Achilles has just refused him (see [[kingship-and-authority]]). Immediately afterward the army comes back to its seats, and [[Thersites]] shows that not everyone accepts it.

## Outside Homer

Aristotle closes Book Λ of the *Metaphysics* with the line. Against thinkers who make the principles of the world many and unconnected, like a badly governed state, he says that the things that are do not want to be badly governed: “the rule of many is not good; let there be one ruler” (*Metaphysics* Λ 10, 1076a3–4; see [[aristotle-one-ruler]]). In the *Politics*, discussing a democracy in which the people rule together like a single monarch, he says it is unclear which rule of many Homer meant, that kind or rule by several men each on his own (*Politics* 4.4, 1292a10–13).

When kings visiting Rome argued about the nobility of their families at his table, the emperor Gaius (Caligula) cried out “Let there be one ruler, one king” in Greek, and was close to taking a diadem (Suetonius, *Caligula* 22.1).
