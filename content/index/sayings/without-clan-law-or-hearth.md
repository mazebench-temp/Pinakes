**The occasion.** Agamemnon has proposed flight, and Diomedes has answered him before the army: let the king go home if he likes; the rest will stay (9.32–49). Nestor praises the young man, says that no Achaean will fault his word, and adds that he has not yet reached the end of the matter (9.53–56). The maxim comes before the advice that ends the assembly. It is general, “that man” (ἐκεῖνος), and it is aimed at the danger of the moment: a quarrel among the Achaean leaders, while the enemy's fires burn by the ships (9.76–77). The quarrel of Book 1 is still unhealed, and a new one has just been spoken in the assembly (see [[the-army-and-its-leaders]]).

**Three bonds.** The three adjectives are formed with the negative prefix ἀ-, and set side by side without a conjunction (see [[privative-adjectives]]). ἀφρήτωρ is the man with no φρήτρη, the brotherhood or clan within a people; Nestor told Agamemnon in Book 2 to separate the men “by tribes and by clans”, “so that clan may help clan, and tribe tribe” (2.362–363). ἀθέμιστος is the man with no θέμιστες, the ordinances that come from Zeus (see [[themis]]). ἀνέστιος is the man with no hearth (ἑστίη), no house of his own; in the Odyssey the hearth of Odysseus is sworn by with Zeus and the table of hospitality (*Odyssey* 14.158–159). ἀφρήτωρ and ἀνέστιος occur only here in Homer. Together the words describe an outcast, the man who has made himself a stranger to his kin, his laws and his home.

**War among his own people.** πόλεμος ἐπιδήμιος is war “at home”, among one's own δῆμος (ἐπιδήμιος, “at home”, of Odysseus in *Odyssey* 1.194, 1.233). ὀκρυόεις, “chilling”, is the word Helen uses of herself when she calls herself a dog (6.344). Nestor names no one, and the translation keeps the phrase general, “war among his own people” (see the translation's note on 9.63–64). The man who loves strife among his own is the opposite of the leader the night needs, and Nestor goes on at once to the watch, the supper and the council (9.65–78; see [[yield-to-night]] and [[this-night-will-shatter-or-save]]).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle quotes the line in the *Politics*. Man is by nature an animal of the city, and the man who is without a city by nature, and not by chance, is either a poor creature or more than a man, “like the one reviled by Homer, ‘without clan, without law, without hearth’”; such a man is at the same time a lover of war, like an isolated piece in a board game (*Politics* 1.2, 1253a1–7; see [[aristotle-man-without-a-city]]).
