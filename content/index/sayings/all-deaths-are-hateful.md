**The argument.** Eurylochus speaks when Odysseus has gone up the island to pray and the gods have poured sleep on his eyelids (*Odyssey* 12.333–338). For a month the South Wind has kept the ship on the island; when the bread and wine were gone, the companions roamed about for fish and birds with curved hooks, “and hunger wore at their bellies” (*Odyssey* 12.325–332; see [[gaster]] and [[fasting]]). His maxim compares deaths. θάνατοι, in the plural, are “forms of death”, what Homer elsewhere calls the κῆρες, the fates of death (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.341, comparing *Iliad* 2.302 and 12.326–327; see [[thanatos]] and [[ker-doom]]). All of them are hateful, and death by hunger is the most pitiable. From the rule he draws the plan: drive off the best of the Sun's cattle and sacrifice them to the immortals, build the Sun a rich temple in Ithaca, and, if the god is still angry, “I would rather lose my life once and for all with one gulp of the wave / than be slowly drained away on a desolate island” (*Odyssey* 12.343–351; see [[better-to-perish-once-and-for-all]]). The speech begins and ends with a choice of deaths: the slowest is the worst, and the quickest is the best.

**The verse and its kin.** δειλοῖσι βροτοῖσι, “to wretched mortals”, ends verses of general truth, and the nearest is Priam's. Begging Hector to come inside the wall, he says that all is fitting for a young man killed in battle, but when dogs disfigure the gray head and the gray beard of an old man who is slain, “this is the most pitiable thing that comes to wretched mortals” (τοῦτο δὴ οἴκτιστον πέλεται δειλοῖσι βροτοῖσιν, *Iliad* 22.71–76; see [[all-is-fitting-for-the-young]] and [[pity]]). Priam measures deaths by what is done to the body, Eurylochus by how slowly it comes. The end of the second verse, θανέειν καὶ πότμον ἐπισπεῖν, “to die and meet one's fate”, is a phrase of the Odyssey's other judgments on deaths: Menelaus is not fated “to die and meet your fate in horse-pasturing Argos” (*Odyssey* 4.562), and Odysseus in the storm wishes that he had died and met his fate at Troy, where he would have had his funeral rites and his fame, instead of the wretched death at sea that seemed fated for him (*Odyssey* 5.308–312; see [[three-times-blessed]]). The death by drowning that Eurylochus prefers to hunger is the death without honor that Odysseus feared. Sarpedon draws another conclusion from the many deaths: “since in any case the fates of death stand over us, / countless, which no mortal can flee or escape, / let us go, and either hold out triumph to another, or another to us” (*Iliad* 12.326–328; see [[sarpedons-speech]]).

**What follows.** The companions applaud, kill the cattle and feast for six days, and the gods show them signs, the hides crawling and the meat lowing on the spits (*Odyssey* 12.352–398; see [[teras]]). On the seventh day they put out to sea, Zeus strikes the ship with his thunderbolt, and they are carried on the waves “like sea crows around the black ship”, until “the god took away their homecoming” (*Odyssey* 12.399–419; see [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). The man who preferred the wave to hunger dies in the wave.

## Outside Homer

Plato quotes the second verse. In the third book of the *Republic* Socrates asks whether it is fit for a young man to hear, for the sake of his self-mastery, the wisest of men saying that the finest thing of all is a table laden with bread and meat and the wine-pourer going round with the cups (*Odyssey* 9.8–10), “or this: ‘to die of hunger is the most pitiable, and to meet one's fate’” (λιμῷ δʼ οἴκτιστον θανέειν καὶ πότμον ἐπισπεῖν, *Republic* 390a–b, a plain rendering; see [[plato]] and [[the-praise-of-the-feast-in-later-writers]]). He gives the verse without its speaker, beside Odysseus' praise of the feast, among the things the young should not hear.
