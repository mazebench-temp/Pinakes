**The lament.** Briseis, brought back to Achilles' huts among Agamemnon's gifts, sees Patroclus “torn by the sharp bronze” and throws herself around him (19.282–285; see [[briseis]] and [[lament]]). She begins with the turn of her own going and coming: she left him alive and finds him dead. Then the turn becomes the rule of her life, “so evil follows evil for me always”, and she counts the evils: the husband her parents gave her, whom she saw killed before the city, and her three brothers, “who all met their day of destruction” (19.291–294). It was Achilles who killed her husband and sacked the city of divine Mynes (19.295–296; see [[the-sack-of-lyrnessus]] and [[mynes]]). Patroclus was the exception. He would not let her weep, and promised to make her Achilles' wedded wife; “therefore I weep for you without end, dead, who were always kind” (19.295–300; see [[marriage]] and [[captive-women]]).

**The words.** δέχεται, “follows”, is used without an object, “takes up from, succeeds”. Walter Leaf reports that the lexica give no other instance of the sense in Greek except a verse of Hesiod (below), and allows ὥς to be either Monro's introduction of a reflection or an exclamation, “how!” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.290). The phrase sets the same noun in two cases, κακὸν ἐκ κακοῦ (see [[polyptoton]]). Leaf compares the end of Ajax' long struggle at the ships, “and everywhere evil was piled on evil” (κακὸν κακῷ ἐστήρικτο, 16.111), and, in another sense, the goatherd Melanthius' insult to Eumaeus and the beggar, κακὸς κακὸν ἡγηλάζει, “the vile one leads the vile” (*Odyssey* 17.217; see [[like-to-like]]).

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod the verb describes the punishment of a god who swears falsely by the water of the Styx. He lies breathless for a full year, and comes near no ambrosia and nectar; and when he has finished that sickness, “another trial, harder still, takes up from the one before” (ἄλλος γʼ ἐξ ἄλλου δέχεται χαλεπώτερος ἄεθλος): for nine years he is cut off from the councils and feasts of the gods (*Theogony* 793–804; see [[oath]] and [[styx]]).
