{{quote:c687b0f3-a0ae-5cfd-8a55-a1bda23e67e2}}

The Argives have driven the Trojans back after Odysseus kills Democoon, and Apollo, looking down from Pergamus, is indignant (4.505–508). He gives the Trojans two reasons to stand. The first is the plainest fact of war: the enemy's flesh can be cut, “since their flesh is not stone or iron to withstand the flesh-cutting bronze when they are struck” (4.510–511). The second is that Achilles, the one man they have reason to fear, is not fighting (4.512–513; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]).

The comparison works both ways. Stone and iron are the images of what does not give: in the Odyssey Eurycleia promises to keep a secret “like hard stone or iron” (*Odyssey* 19.494), and Hector, Hecuba and Achilles each call a heart “of iron” that can do or bear what others cannot (22.357, 24.205, 24.521). Men are not made of either, and a god reminds them of it to send them back into the fighting. See [[gods-and-mortals]], [[apollo]] and [[the-first-battle]].
