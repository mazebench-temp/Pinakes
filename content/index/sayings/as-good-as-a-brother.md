**The host's reason.** Alcinous has noticed for the second time that his guest weeps at Demodocus' song, and he has the singer stop: the song does not give delight to all of them, and the stranger has not ceased from lamentation since it began (*Odyssey* 8.532–541; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]] and [[demodocus]]). Let them take delight alike, “hosts and guest” (ξεινοδόκοι καὶ ξεῖνος, *Odyssey* 8.543; see [[xenia]]), since all has been made ready for the sake of the revered stranger, “the escort and the gifts of friendship” (*Odyssey* 8.544–545; see [[pompe]] and [[guest-gifts]]). The maxim gives the ground of it. ἀντί is “as good as”, as Lycaon says to Achilles, “I am as good as a revered suppliant to you” (ἀντί τοί εἰμʼ ἱκέταο … αἰδοίοιο, *Iliad* 21.75; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]), and as Aristarchus rightly explained the Amazons ἀντιάνειραι as “equal to men”, not “opposed to men” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.546). The Iliad reckons worth with the same word: “Worth many / people is the man whom Zeus loves in his heart” (ἀντί νυ πολλῶν / λαῶν, *Iliad* 9.116–117; see [[the-man-whom-zeus-loves]]).

**A little wisdom.** The second verse limits the rule to a man who has “even a little hold on wisdom”. Merry gives the common rendering of ἐπιψαύῃ, “who has even but a little range with his wits”, comparing Diomedes' boast that his arrow kills “even if it only grazes” (εἴ κʼ ὀλίγον περ ἐπαύρῃ, *Iliad* 11.391), and allows also “has got close to wisdom”, with πραπίδεσσι as the thing touched; ἐπιψαύω is found nowhere else in Homer (Merry on 8.547). The man who does not hold the stranger as a brother has not even a little sense, as the man who strives with his host is “witless indeed and a nobody” (*Odyssey* 8.209; see [[strife-with-ones-host]]).

**Brother, stranger and suppliant.** The maxim measures the guest by the nearest kin (see [[kasignetos]]). The same speech ends with a second measure of the kind: kin by marriage are “the dearest after one's own blood and kin”, and a companion with wise understanding is “in no way inferior to a brother” (*Odyssey* 8.581–586; see [[in-laws]] and [[a-companion-no-worse-than-a-brother]]). The stranger and the suppliant stand together as Nausicaa's “strangers and beggars” do (*Odyssey* 6.207–208; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]]), and as they do in the libation to Zeus, “who attends on revered suppliants” (*Odyssey* 7.165, 7.181; see [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]] and [[supplication]]). Odysseus came into the hall as a suppliant at the queen's knees (*Odyssey* 7.142–152); at the end of the second day he is the guest of the house, with gifts laid up for him in a chest.

**The request.** τῷ is “wherefore”: because he is treated with such brotherly kindness (Merry on 8.548), the stranger should not hide “with crafty thoughts” what he is asked, but tell his name, his land, his people and his city (*Odyssey* 8.548–556; see [[kerdos]] and [[no-one-is-nameless]]). He gives his name at the beginning of the next book, “I am Odysseus, son of Laertes” (*Odyssey* 9.19, a plain rendering; see [[odysseus]]).
