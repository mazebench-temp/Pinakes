Zeus has asked whether to snatch Sarpedon alive out of the battle, and Hera has warned him that every god with a son at Troy would want the same (16.431–449; see [[not-all-the-gods-approve]]). Her counsel puts a burial in place of a rescue. Let him fall at the hands of Patroclus, and when his soul and his life have left him, let Death and Sleep carry him home to Lycia, where his brothers and kinsmen “will give him burial / with a tomb and a pillar; for that is the prize of the dead” (16.456–457). Zeus gives the order to Apollo in the same words, and Apollo carries it out (16.666–683; see [[sarpedon]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]).

**The share of the dead.** A γέρας is a prize of honor, the share set apart for a man as the mark of his standing (see [[geras]]). In *Iliad* 1 it was a captive woman, and the poem's quarrel began over it (1.118–120); here it is what remains to a man who can hold nothing more. The verb ταρχύω, “give burial”, is used only here and in Hector's challenge, where he promises that if he kills his man the Achaeans may bury him and heap up a mound beside the Hellespont, which men will point to long after (7.84–91; see [[tomb]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). The mound (τύμβος) and the pillar (στήλη) on it are what the living see: Paris shoots at Diomedes “leaning against a pillar on the man-made tomb / of Ilus” (11.371–372).

**The laments.** The half-verse comes once more in the Iliad and twice in the Odyssey, and each time the prize is a rite. Achilles keeps the Myrmidons from unyoking their horses so that they may drive near and weep for Patroclus, “for that is the prize of the dead” (ὃ γὰρ γέρας ἐστὶ θανόντων, 23.9). In the Underworld the shade of Amphimedon tells Agamemnon that the suitors' bodies lie uncared for in Odysseus' halls, since their kin do not yet know, who would wash the black blood from their wounds and lay them out and lament them (*Odyssey* 24.186–190). Laertes, not yet knowing his son, imagines Odysseus dead far away, unwept by his mother and father, and unmourned by his wife, who did not close his eyes (*Odyssey* 24.290–296). Burial, lament, washing and the closing of the eyes are the dead man's share, and to be denied them is the fear that runs through the Iliad from its first lines (1.4–5; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]).

**The prize of the old.** With one word changed the half-verse belongs to the old. Nestor, too old to fight, will command the horsemen with counsel and words, “for that is the prize of the old” (4.323), and Achilles says it of the envoys who must carry his answer (9.422; see [[prize-of-the-old]]). The old and the dead each have their due.
