**The parable.** Achilles has raised Priam from his knees and bids him sit, and let their sorrows lie quiet, “for there is no profit in chilling lament; / for so the gods have spun the thread for wretched mortals, / to live in grief; but they themselves are free from care” (24.522–526; see [[achilles]], [[priam]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]). The jars give the reason. They hold the gifts of Zeus, δώρων οἷα δίδωσι, the one of evils and the other of ἑάων, “blessings” (24.528; see [[doron]] and [[zeus]]). The man to whom Zeus gives a mixture meets now with evil and now with good; the man to whom he gives only from the grievous one, he makes λωβητός, “a thing of scorn”, and κακὴ βούβρωστις, “evil, ravening hunger”, drives him over the shining earth, honored by neither gods nor men (24.529–533). No man receives from the good jar alone: Walter Leaf takes the unmixed good to be the gods' own, who are “free from care” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.527).

**Two jars or three.** The verses can be read in two ways. In one, both jars hold evils, and the jar of blessings is a third, ἕτερος, “the other”, being set against the first two taken as a pair; this agrees with what follows, since a man then receives at best a mixture, and may receive no good at all, and with Pindar's saying, as Leaf quotes it, that the immortals give mortals two evils for every good (*Pythian* 3.81; see [[pindar-the-jars-of-zeus]]). In the other, which the translation follows, there are two jars, one of evils and one of blessings. Leaf prefers it, on the authority of Plato and the construction of the sentence (on 24.527; see the translation's note on 24.527–528). ἐν οὔδει, “on the floor”, recalls to him the huge pointed jars of the Mycenaean age that stood sunk in the floors of the storerooms; and he calls the image less an allegory than a survival of what may once have been taken for a real explanation of where sorrow and joy come from (on 24.527). ἑάων is “a most obscure word”, found elsewhere only in the gods' title δωτῆρες ἑάων, “givers of good things” (*Odyssey* 8.325; Hesiod, *Theogony* 633), and of Hermes, δῶτορ ἑάων (*Odyssey* 8.335); Leaf leaves it “among the unsolved problems of the language” (on 24.528). βούβρωστις, rendered “ravening hunger”, was taken by the scholia for a gadfly that drives a man over the earth, and by others for famine; Leaf adds that a goddess Boubrostis was worshipped at Smyrna (on 24.532; see the translation's note on 24.532).

**In the poem.** The parable answers Priam's claim to be the most pitiable of men (24.503–506; see [[supplication]]), and Achilles applies it at once. Peleus had every gift of the gods, wealth, rule and a goddess for a wife, but on him too “the god laid evil”, a single son doomed to an untimely death; Priam was blessed once in wealth and sons, and now has war around his city (24.534–548; see [[peleus]]). Neither has the unmixed evils of the wanderer, and both have their share: “Bear up” (24.549). Others in Homer say the like of Zeus' giving. Paris tells Hector that the glorious gifts of the gods are not to be cast aside (3.65; see [[gifts-of-the-gods]]); Agamemnon says that at their birth Zeus laid heavy misery on them (10.70–71); and in the Odyssey Helen says that Zeus gives good and evil now to one man and now to another, for he can do all things, and Nausicaa tells Odysseus that Zeus deals out happiness to good and bad as he wills, and that what he has given must be endured (*Odyssey* 4.236–237, 6.188–190; see [[gods-and-mortals]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells of another jar of evils: before Pandora, men lived free of evils and of hard toil and sickness, until the woman took the great lid off the jar and scattered them, and only Hope stayed inside, under its lip (*Works and Days* 90–98; see [[hesiod]]). Leaf compares the story (on 24.527).

Plato's Socrates names the jars first among the things the poets of his city must not say of the gods: a god, being good, is the cause of good things only. He quotes the verses with a second line that is not in our text, κηρῶν ἔμπλειοι, ὁ μὲν ἐσθλῶν, αὐτὰρ ὃ δειλῶν, “full of fates, the one of good, the other of bad”, and gives the rest in prose: to whom Zeus gives a mixture of both, he meets now with evil, now with good, but to whom he gives the one kind unmixed, “him evil, ravening hunger drives over the shining earth”; nor may it be said that Zeus is the steward of good and evil for us (*Republic* 2.379c–e; see [[plato-broken-oaths]] and [[plato]]). Leaf calls the form of 24.528 in Plato a rare instance of large deviation from the vulgate in his quotations (on 24.527).
