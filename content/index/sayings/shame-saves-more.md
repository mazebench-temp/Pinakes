The Trojans have rallied under Hector, Ares and Apollo, and the Achaeans stand like clouds on a windless mountain (5.519–527). Agamemnon goes through the throng and gives the whole art of the exhortation in four lines (see [[exhortation]]). First the command: “be men” (ἀνέρες ἔστε), and “take a valiant heart”. Then the means: “feel shame before one another” (ἀλλήλους τʼ αἰδεῖσθε). The αἰδώς he asks for is not a private feeling. Each man is to feel it before the others, who can see whether he stands or runs (see [[aidos]]). Then two reasons, set against each other in two verses that begin with the same kind of word: αἰδομένων, “when men feel shame”, and φευγόντων, “when men flee”. The first is practical: shame holds the line together, and a line that holds loses fewer men. The second is heroic: flight wins neither κλέος, fame (see [[kleos]]), nor ἀλκή, courage (see [[alke]]).

The claim that shame saves lives is not a paradox. In Homer the man who turns his back is the man most easily killed: Agamemnon's first victim in this book was the leader of the Halizones, speared in the back “as the man was the first to turn” (5.40), and Menelaus kills the hunter Scamandrius “in the back as he fled before him” (5.56). Agamemnon himself then kills Deicoon, a man in the front rank, with a spear through his shield (5.533–540).

Telamonian Ajax speaks the same lines in the battle at the ships, with a different first verse: “Friends, be men, and put shame in your hearts” (αἰδῶ θέσθʼ ἐνὶ θυμῷ, 15.561–564). The one-word form of the appeal is αἰδώς, “Shame!”, with which Hera, in the likeness of Stentor, rallies the Argives at the end of this book (5.787; see [[aidos]]).

## Outside Homer

Tyrtaeus, the Spartan poet of the seventh century BC, puts the same argument in elegiacs: those who dare to stand beside one another and go forward to close combat die in fewer numbers and save the people behind them, but when men run, all their worth is lost (fragment 11.11–14 West). Aristotle places the courage of citizens, which comes from shame (διʼ αἰδῶ) and the fear of reproach, next to true courage, and quotes Diomedes and Hector as examples (*Nicomachean Ethics* 3.8, 1116a17–29).
