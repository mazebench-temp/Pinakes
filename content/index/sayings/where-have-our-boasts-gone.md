Hector has penned the Achaeans between the ditch and their ships, and Hera puts it into Agamemnon's mind to rouse them (8.213–219). He takes his stand on Odysseus' ship, in the middle of the line, from which a shout carries to both ends, and shouts (8.220–227; see [[agamemnon]]). His first line is the one Hera shouted to the Achaeans in Book 5 in the likeness of Stentor, “Shame, Argives, wretched disgraces, admirable in looks!” (5.787; see [[aidos]] and [[elenchos]]). The question follows. εὐχωλή is a vow, a cry of triumph or a boast (see [[euchomai]]), and these are the claims the army made “when we said that we were the best” (see [[aristos]]). Agamemnon includes himself in that “we” before he turns to “you”, and he calls the boasters κενεαυχέες, “empty boasters”, a word found only here in Homer (see [[empty-boasting]]). The measure is exact: each man was to stand against a hundred or two hundred Trojans, and now all of them together are not worth one man.

The boasts were made at a feast, over the meat of oxen and mixing bowls brimming with wine. The poem tells of no stay on Lemnos on the way to Troy beyond this verse; Lemnos is where Philoctetes was left with his wound (2.721–723), and the island whose ships bring wine to the camp (7.467–471; see [[lemnos]] and the translation's note on 8.230). The feast is where honor is given, and the battle is where it is repaid. Agamemnon reminds Menestheus and Odysseus in Book 4 that they are the first to be called to his feasts, and now they would gladly watch ten columns fight in front of them (4.341–348). Hector taunts Diomedes in this book that the Danaans honored him “with a seat of honor, with meat, and with full cups” and will now dishonor him (8.161–163), and Sarpedon names the same honors as the reason why he and Glaucus must fight in the front line (12.310–321; see [[time]] and [[feast]]).

**Elsewhere.** “Where have they gone?” is a form of rebuke in the Iliad, put to a man whose strength or words have failed him. Aeneas asks Pandarus, “where are your bow and your feathered arrows and your fame?” (5.171–172), and Sarpedon asks, “Hector, where has the might gone that you used to have?” (5.472). Poseidon, in the likeness of Thoas, asks Idomeneus where the threats have gone that the sons of the Achaeans made against the Trojans (13.219–220), and Apollo, in the likeness of Lycaon, asks Aeneas where the threats are that he made to the Trojan kings over the wine, that he would fight Achilles face to face (20.83–85). Aeneas' threats, like the Achaeans' boasts on Lemnos, were made at the wine.
