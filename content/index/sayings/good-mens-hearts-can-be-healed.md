**The grievance.** Poseidon has come among the Achaean leaders in Calchas' likeness and rebuked them for their slackness (13.95–110; see [[aidos]] and [[poseidon]]). The Trojans, who used to be like deer, now fight by the ships “through the baseness of their leader and the slackness of the army, / who in their strife with him are not willing to defend / the swift-sailing ships” (13.108–110). The leader is Agamemnon, and the army's strife with him is Achilles' quarrel spreading through the camp (see the translation's note on 13.108–114). Poseidon grants the grievance in full, with the verb Calchas used in Book 1 when he named Agamemnon's offense against Apollo's priest (ἠτίμησʼ, 1.94): even if Agamemnon is truly and wholly to blame, “because he dishonored the swift-footed son of Peleus” (ἀπητίμησε, 13.113; see [[who-is-to-blame]] and [[time]]), “for us at least there is no way to hold back from war” (13.114).

**The cure.** ἀλλʼ ἀκεώμεθα θᾶσσον, “let us rather heal it quickly”. What is to be healed is not said: the quarrel of 13.109–113, or the slackness of 13.108 and 13.121 (see the translation's note on 13.115). ἀκέομαι is the verb of healing a wound, as Leto and Artemis heal Aeneas in Apollo's temple (5.448) and Glaucus prays Apollo to heal his arm (16.523), and of setting a thing right; ἀκεστός, “curable”, is found only here. The maxim gives the reason for haste: “the hearts of good men can be healed”. φρένες are the seat of thought and feeling (see [[phrenes]]), and the ἐσθλοί, “the good”, are the noble and brave, the men Poseidon calls “the best men in the army” a moment later (13.117; see [[aristos]]). Their grievance, however just, need not last. The maxim praises those it rebukes, and it leaves them no excuse.

**Turned.** In Book 15 the maxim comes back to the god who spoke it. Zeus, awake, sends Iris to order Poseidon out of the battle, and Poseidon refuses, claiming equal honor (15.174–199). Iris asks him to reconsider: “will you turn your heart a little? The minds of the good can be turned” (στρεπταὶ μέν τε φρένες ἐσθλῶν, 15.203), and reminds him that the Erinyes always follow the elder (15.204). He yields, saying that she has spoken rightly (15.206–211; see [[nemesis]]), but with a threat: if Zeus spares Troy against the will of the gods who favor the Achaeans, there will be “incurable anger” between them (ἀνήκεστος χόλος, 15.212–217). ἀνήκεστος, “incurable”, is formed from the same root as ἀκεστός with the negative prefix. The two verses end alike, φρένες ἐσθλῶν, and make the same claim, that a noble mind can change. Phoenix put it more boldly to Achilles in Book 9: “even the gods themselves can be turned” (9.496–497; see [[even-the-gods-can-be-turned]]).

**What follows.** The cure Poseidon asks for comes at once. He ends his rebuke with the news that Hector has broken the gates and the long bar, and the battalions take their stand around the two Ajaxes, shield pressed on shield, so close that their helmets touch when they nod (13.123–135; see [[battle-order]]). The quarrel itself is not healed until Book 19.
