Euphorbus, who struck Patroclus first, has told Menelaus to leave the body and the bloody spoils or lose his life (17.12–17; see [[euphorbus]]). Menelaus answers with a reminder. The sons of Panthous are prouder than leopard, lion or boar, and Euphorbus' brother Hyperenor, who scorned him, did not go home on his own feet (17.19–28; see [[hyperenor]]). Then comes the warning, and the proverb closes it. ῥεχθέν is the passive participle of ῥέζω, “do”: “a thing done”. νήπιος is the child, or the fool who does not see what is coming (see [[nepios]]). Walter Leaf gives the thought as “be wise before you have come to harm; even a fool is wise after the event”, and suggests that the verb παθέειν, “suffer”, earlier in the verse called up the proverb, with ῥεχθέν in place of the παθών of Hesiod's form (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.32).

Euphorbus is not persuaded (17.33), and he learns as the proverb says. His spear bends back on Menelaus' shield, and Menelaus' spear goes through his neck (17.43–50; see [[menelaus]]).

**Again in Book 20.** Achilles says the same three verses to Aeneas, who has come out to meet him: let him go back into the crowd and not stand against Achilles, before he suffers harm (20.196–198 = 17.30–32; see [[commoners]]). Aeneas answers that Achilles should not hope to frighten him with words, as if he were a child (νηπύτιον ὥς, 20.200–202), and only Poseidon saves him from Achilles' sword (20.288–340).

**Related sayings.** Odysseus warned Achilles that there is no cure for an evil once it is done, ῥεχθέντος κακοῦ (9.249; see [[no-cure-once-done]]). Ajax, later in this book, turns another saying about fools against his own side: “even one who is an utter fool / would know that father Zeus himself is helping the Trojans” (17.629–630; see [[even-a-fool-can-see]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod uses the proverb in a warning to his brother Perses: Justice wins over Outrage when she comes to the end, παθὼν δέ τε νήπιος ἔγνω, “and the fool learns it by suffering” (*Works and Days* 217–218; see [[hesiod]]). In Plato's *Symposium* Alcibiades warns Agathon not to be deceived by Socrates but to learn from what others have suffered, and not, “as the proverb says, like a fool, learn by suffering” (κατὰ τὴν παροιμίαν ὥσπερ νήπιον παθόντα γνῶναι, *Symposium* 222b).
