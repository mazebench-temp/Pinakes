**The question it answers.** Three times Odysseus springs toward the soul of his mother, and three times “she flew from my hands like a shadow or a dream” (*Odyssey* 11.206–207). He asks her why she will not stay, so that even in the house of Hades they might throw their arms around each other and take their fill of lament, or whether this is “some phantom that noble Persephone / has urged on toward me, so that I may groan and mourn still more” (*Odyssey* 11.210–214; see [[anticleia]], [[persephone]] and [[eidolon]]). The answer turns the fear of a trick into a rule. Nothing is being kept from him: what he cannot hold is what every man becomes.

**What death does.** The answer is a short account of a death and a cremation. The ἶνες, the “sinews”, no longer hold flesh and bones together, ἔχουσιν meaning “hold together”, as the midriff “holds” the liver (*Odyssey* 9.301; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.219). The “strong might of the blazing fire” overcomes the body as soon as the θυμός, the spirit, has left the white bones, and the ψυχή, the soul, flies away like a dream: πεπότηται, a perfect with the force of a continuing state, “hovers to and fro” (Merry on 11.222; see [[thymos]], [[psyche]], [[cremation]] and [[dream]]). The body goes to the fire and the soul to Hades; the soul keeps the likeness of the living, but nothing that can be grasped.

**The word.** δίκη here is not justice but the way a kind of being is, what Merry renders “the way with mortals” (on 11.218; see [[themis]]). The Odyssey uses the turn for other kinds as well: Penelope says of Odysseus that he never did or said anything excessive to anyone among the people, “though that is the way of divine kings” (*Odyssey* 4.690–691), and later books speak of the way of slaves, who are always afraid when their masters are young (*Odyssey* 14.59–61), of the gods, whose presence makes a hall shine as if with fire (*Odyssey* 19.36–43), and of old men, who sleep soft after their bath and their meal (*Odyssey* 24.254–255; plain renderings). The verse reads ὅτε τίς κε θάνῃσιν, “when one of them dies”, against the common ὅτε κέν τε θάνωσι; Merry prefers the singular, as the reading of good manuscripts and because it suggests the particular instance of Anticleia herself (his note and critical note on 11.218; see the Odyssey translation's note on 11.218, 11.221).

**In the Iliad.** The Iliad teaches the same lesson to a living man. The soul of Patroclus comes to Achilles in his sleep, asks to be buried “as quickly as may be, so that I may pass the gates of Hades”, and says that “never again / shall I come back from Hades, once you have given me my portion of fire” (*Iliad* 23.71–76). Achilles reaches out, “but did not take hold of it; and the soul went beneath the earth like smoke, / gibbering”, and he cries that “even in the house of Hades there is some / soul and phantom, but there is no mind in it at all” (*Iliad* 23.99–104; see [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]]). What Achilles learns by trying, Odysseus is told by one of the dead. See [[the-land-of-the-dead]].

## Outside Homer

Virgil gives Aeneas the three vain embraces twice, in the same verses, with the shade of his wife Creusa as Troy falls and with his father Anchises in the underworld: *ter conatus ibi collo dare bracchia circum; / ter frustra comprensa manus effugit imago, / par levibus ventis volucrique simillima somno*, “three times he tried to put his arms around her neck there; three times the image, grasped in vain, fled from his hands, like the light winds and most like a winged dream” (*Aeneid* 2.792–794 = 6.700–702; Merry on 11.206 cites the first; see [[virgil]]). The dream of the simile has come from Anticleia's answer into the scene of the embrace itself.
