Nestor has told Patroclus how, as a young man whom his father would not let arm, he killed Mulius, took fifty chariots, and drove the Epeians to Buprasium, so that the Pylians gave thanks “to Zeus among the gods, and to Nestor among men” (11.670–761; see [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). He sums it up in three words, ὣς ἔον, “such was I”, and takes up the last word of the thanksgiving, “among men” (μετʼ ἀνδράσιν, ἀνδρῶν). Between them comes the doubt: εἴ ποτʼ ἔον γε, “if that was ever so”. The old man who remembers every name and every day of the raid speaks of the young man as of someone gone (see [[old-age-and-memory]] and [[nestor]]). Then he turns to Achilles, whose prowess will profit no one but himself (11.762–764; see [[arete]]).

**If ever he was.** The clause, in its third-person form εἴ ποτʼ ἔην γε, is a formula, and every time it speaks of something lost. Helen, naming Agamemnon for Priam, says that he was her brother-in-law, “dog-faced as I am, if that was ever so” (3.180; see [[in-laws]] and [[dog-face]]). Priam, told that his son's body is unharmed, says that his son, “if ever he was”, never forgot the gods (24.425–428). In the Odyssey Telemachus tells a stranger that his father is Odysseus, “if ever he was; but now he has perished” (*Odyssey* 15.267–268); Penelope says there is no master in the house now “such as Odysseus was among men, if ever he was” (*Odyssey* 19.314–316), in nearly the words of Nestor's verse; and Laertes asks the disguised Odysseus about his son, “if ever he was” (*Odyssey* 24.288–290). The others say it of a tie that no longer holds or of a man they have lost. Nestor says it of himself.

**So I was once.** At the funeral games of Book 23 Achilles gives Nestor a prize without a contest, and Nestor answers with another story of his youth that begins with the same wish, “If only I were young like that, and my strength were steadfast” (23.629 ≈ 11.670, 7.157): at the games for Amarynceus at Buprasium he won every contest but the chariot race, which the twin sons of Actor took from him (23.630–642; see [[the-moliones]]). He ends with ὥς ποτʼ ἔον, “so I was once”, and leaves such work to younger men (23.643–645).
