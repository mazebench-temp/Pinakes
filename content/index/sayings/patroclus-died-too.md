**The answer to a plea.** Lycaon has pleaded the bread he ate at Achilles' side, the price he fetched and the ransom he paid, his mother and his brother, and that he is not of the same womb as Hector (21.74–96; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[the-death-of-lycaon]]). Achilles answers none of it. Before Patroclus died he spared Trojans and sold them; now no one whom a god throws into his hands before Ilios shall escape death, “and above all of the sons of Priam” (21.99–105). The two verses of the maxim give the reason. Lycaon must die because Patroclus has died, and Patroclus was “far better” than he (see [[patroclus]] and [[thanatos]]).

**Friend.** φίλος, “friend” (21.106), has been read both ways. The scholia heard mockery of Lycaon's claim to have eaten at Achilles' table; Walter Leaf hears a sudden turn in Achilles' mood to real pity for his victim (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.106; see [[philos]] and [[pity]]). Some ancient readers put a stop after θάνε and made φίλος its subject, so that Achilles says that his friend has died; Leaf mentions the reading only to set it aside (on 21.106).

**Achilles too.** καί, “too”, carries the speech: Lycaon must die too, Patroclus died too, and Achilles too will die.

{{quote:5b3d36f5-397b-5381-bd7f-e4703861bb82}}

He offers himself as the measure. If a man so handsome and so tall, the son of a brave father and of a goddess, must die, Lycaon cannot complain. He reasoned in the same way about himself when he chose to go back to the war after Patroclus' death: “For not even mighty Heracles escaped his doom, / who was the dearest of all to lord Zeus, son of Cronus” (18.117–118), and “So I too, if indeed a like fate is prepared for me, / shall lie still when I am dead” (18.120–121; see [[heracles]]). The verse-end θάνατος καὶ μοῖρα κραταιή, “death and mighty fate” (21.110), is the one in which the dying Patroclus foretold Hector's death, “but already / death and mighty fate stand close beside you” (16.852–853), and Thetis will tell Achilles the same of himself in the last book (24.131–132; see [[mighty-fate]] and [[moira]]).

**The hour and the weapon.** ἔσσεται, “there will come” (21.111), opens Agamemnon's and Hector's prophecy of Troy's fall, “a day will come when sacred Ilios will perish” (4.164, 6.448; see [[a-day-will-come]]), and Leaf compares the construction (on 21.111). δείλη, “evening”, is found only here in Homer (see the translation's note on 21.111), and here ἠώς is the morning, the time of day (see [[eos]]). The hour is left open, and so is the weapon, “either with a spear or with an arrow from the string” (21.113). In the river Achilles will remember his mother's word that he would die “by the swift arrows of Apollo” under the wall (21.277–278), and the dying Hector will name Paris and Apollo at the Scaean gates (22.359–360; see [[death-of-achilles-foretold]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]).

**The end of the plea.** “So he spoke, and the other's knees and dear heart were loosened where he was” (21.114). Lycaon lets go of the spear he has been holding and sits with both hands spread, and Achilles kills him with the sword (21.115–119).

## Outside Homer

The line was remembered on its own. Walter Leaf calls it famous, and reports Plutarch's story of Callisthenes and Lucretius' adaptation of it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.107). For Plutarch's account of the words that Callisthenes repeated at Alexander's court, see [[callisthenes-quotes-achilles]]. Lucretius gives the thought to the reader who fears death: good Ancus has left the light, “who was better than you, wretch, in many things”, and kings, Homer, Democritus and Epicurus himself have died; “will you then hesitate, and resent dying?” (*qui melior multis quam tu fuit, improbe, rebus … tu vero dubitabis et indignabere obire?*, *On the Nature of Things* 3.1024–1045; see [[lucretius-on-the-great-dead]]).
