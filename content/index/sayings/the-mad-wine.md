**Anger and wine.** The second verse is the Iliad's verse on anger with its last word changed. Achilles wished that strife might perish from among gods and men, “and anger, which sets even a man of much wisdom to rage” (ὅς τʼ ἐφέηκε πολύφρονά περ χαλεπῆναι, *Iliad* 18.108; see [[sweeter-than-dripping-honey]] and [[of-much-wisdom]]); here wine sets him μάλʼ ἀεῖσαι, “to sing”. Both overrule the wise man. ἠλεός is “mad” as the cause of madness, as Dionysus is the “mad god”, μαινόμενος (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 14.464, citing *Iliad* 6.132; see [[dionysus]]). The wine's gifts rise by steps, from song to soft laughter and the dance, and end with the word that should not have been spoken; for προέηκεν, “brings out”, the reading of Aristarchus and the manuscripts, Aristophanes read παρέθηκεν, probably in the Attic sense “put into his mind” (Monro's critical note on *Odyssey* 14.466).

**The excuse.** The maxim is the speaker's apology for what follows. εὐξάμενος ἐρέω is “I will say with a wish”, give utterance to a wish (Monro on *Odyssey* 14.463): the wish to be young again, as on the night of the ambush beneath Troy (see [[if-only-i-were-young]]). ἀνέκραγον, “spoke out”, has a colloquial stamp (Monro on *Odyssey* 14.467). The word better left unsaid is a request for a cloak, which the story only hints at (see [[the-tale-of-the-cloak]]).

**Wine elsewhere in the Odyssey.** The poem knows the wine that harms. Antinous tells the beggar that honey-sweet wine wounds him, and that wine drove the Centaur Eurytion to blind folly in the house of Peirithous (*Odyssey* 21.293–304), and the beggar fears that a maid may say that he weeps “my wits heavy with wine” (*Odyssey* 19.122, a plain rendering; see [[wine]]). The excuse in Eumaeus' hut is the drinker's own, but the poet has told us that the speaker is testing his host (*Odyssey* 14.459–461).

## Outside Homer

Proclus uses the half-verse against Porphyry. Arguing that the gods know what is not fixed in a manner of their own, he rejects Porphyry's view that what is not fixed is not fixed for the gods either: “for he uttered this, which is better left unsaid” (*On the Timaeus* I 352 Diehl; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). Later Greek writers describe the wine's ladder as Homer does. Plato's Athenian holds that drinking is the cheapest and safest test of a man's character, and that every drinker is lifted up above himself, glad, and full of outspokenness (*Laws* 1.649d–650b, 2.671b; see [[plato]]); and the *Problems* ascribed to Aristotle say that wine drunk by degrees makes the sober and silent more talkative, then eloquent and bold, then headstrong, insolent and mad, and they quote the Odyssey's line on tears and wine (*Problems* 30.1, 953a–b; see [[aristotle]]).
