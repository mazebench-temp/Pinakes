**Calypso knows Hermes.** Hermes has crossed the sea like a gull and stood gazing at the wood, the vine and the springs around the cave; then he goes in, and Calypso “did not fail to know him” when she looked him in the face (*Odyssey* 5.75–78; ἄντην goes with ἰδοῦσα, Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.77; see [[hermes]], [[calypso]] and [[calypsos-cave]]). The maxim gives the reason. The scholia, as Merry reports them, explain that she knew him not because she had seen him before but by a divine power (on 5.79), and he has not been a frequent guest: “Before now you have not come often,” she says (*Odyssey* 5.88). Merry takes the second verse, “not even if one lives in a house far away”, as a description of Calypso's own case, alone on her distant island (on 5.79, referring to *Odyssey* 5.100; see [[ogygia]]), and Hermes says as much when he gives his errand: he came only because Zeus bade him, for “who of his own will would speed across so much salt water, / boundless?” (*Odyssey* 5.99–101). For οὐδʼ εἴ τις Aristarchus read οὐδʼ ἥ τις (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 5.80). Hermes' first words put the two of them side by side: “You, a goddess, ask me, a god, why I have come” (θεὰ θεόν, *Odyssey* 5.97; see [[polyptoton]]).

**What mortals see.** Men do not know the gods so easily. The gods of the poems come among men in borrowed likenesses, and a man knows one, when he does, by a sign. Achilles turns and knows Pallas Athena at once (*Iliad* 1.199–200); Helen knows Aphrodite when she notices her beautiful neck, her breasts and her flashing eyes (*Iliad* 3.396–398); and Ajax son of Oileus is the first to know Poseidon, who has come in the likeness of Calchas, by the movements of his feet and legs as he goes away, “and gods are easy to know” (*Iliad* 13.66–72; see [[seeing-the-gods]]). Athena takes the mist from Diomedes' eyes “so that you may clearly tell god from man” (*Iliad* 5.127–128; see [[achlys]]), and Apollo mocks Achilles, a mortal pursuing an immortal, who has “not even yet” recognized that he is a god (*Iliad* 22.8–10). In the Odyssey, Odysseus tells Athena that it is hard for a mortal who meets her to know her, however skilled he is, since she makes herself like anything (*Odyssey* 13.312–313), and Telemachus does not see her at the door of the hut where his father and the dogs do, “for the gods do not appear in plain sight to all” (*Odyssey* 16.161; see [[gods-are-hard-to-face]]). Between gods there is no such difficulty: Calypso has only to look. See [[gods-and-mortals]] and [[theos]].
