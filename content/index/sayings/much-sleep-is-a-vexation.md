**The saying.** The stranger has asked how the swineherd came to Ithaca: was his city sacked, or was he taken alone with the flocks by men in ships and sold (*Odyssey* 15.381–388; see [[eumaeus]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). Eumaeus answers first with an invitation to a long night. The guest is to listen in silence and drink his wine; the nights are endless, and there is time both to sleep and to listen for those who take delight in it; he need not lie down “before it is time”, πρὶν ὥρη, with ἔῃ understood (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 15.394). The maxim closes the argument: sleep is good in its measure, and too much of it is itself a vexation. Then the others are sent out to sleep, so that they may follow the swine at dawn, and the two men stay in the hut to drink and feast and take delight in each other's sorrows (*Odyssey* 15.395–401; see [[delight-in-remembered-sorrows]] and [[silence]]).

**The other night.** The saying answers the dispute about the hour at the Phaeacian court. There Alcinous told Odysseus, who had said that it was time to sleep, “This night is very long, endless; and it is not yet time / to sleep in the hall” (*Odyssey* 11.373–374), and Odysseus granted that there is a time for many words and a time also for sleep (*Odyssey* 11.379; see [[a-time-for-many-words]]). The swineherd's νύκτες ἀθέσφατοι, “endless nights”, are the king's νὺξ … ἀθέσφατος (see the translation's table of key words), and now it is Odysseus who listens. ἀνίη, “vexation”, is trouble that wears a man down: the Phaeacians will send their guest home “without toil and vexation” (*Odyssey* 7.192), and Scylla is “a vexation past remedy” (*Odyssey* 12.223). On the night before the slaughter Athena uses the same turn against the opposite excess, when Odysseus lies awake: “But let sleep take you too; it is a vexation, too, to stand guard, / keeping watch all night long” (ἀνίη καὶ τὸ φυλάσσειν, *Odyssey* 20.52–53, a plain rendering; see [[hypnos]] and [[athena]]).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Athenian stranger, ordering the day of his citizens, says the same without naming Homer: much sleep suits by nature neither our bodies nor our souls nor the actions that concern them, for a man asleep is worth nothing, no more than one who is not alive, and whoever cares most for life and thought stays awake as long as he can, keeping only what is useful for health, which is not much once it has become a habit (*Laws* 7.808b–c; see [[plato]]).
