Phoenix begins his plea with his own place in Achilles' life. When Peleus sent his son from Phthia to Agamemnon (see [[peleus-sends-achilles-to-troy]]), he sent Phoenix with him, because the boy was νήπιος, “a child”, who knew nothing of war, “common to all”, nor of assemblies, “where men become distinguished” (ἀριπρεπέες, 9.440–441). The two are named as fields of glory alike: battle, and once the assembly too, is called the place “where men win glory” (see [[where-men-win-glory]]). Phoenix was to teach him both, and the line names the finished man: μύθων ῥητήρ, a speaker of words, and πρηκτήρ ἔργων, a doer of deeds. ῥητήρ occurs only here in Homer, and πρηκτήρ elsewhere only of traders, the men who do business at sea, in Euryalus' sneer at Odysseus (*Odyssey* 8.161–162; see the translation's note on 9.443). The pairing of word and deed runs through the poem (see [[word-or-deed]]).

The ideal is stated by a teacher to his pupil at the start of the longest speech in the Iliad (9.434–605), and the occasion tests it. Phoenix's words are meant to bring Achilles back to the deeds; Achilles has already told Odysseus that he will join Agamemnon “in no counsel, nor in any deed” (9.374; see [[boule]]).

**Elsewhere.** Homer's men are measured in both fields, and seldom excel in both. Nestor told the quarreling kings that they were “first among the Danaans in counsel, and first in battle” (1.258). Agamemnon told Diomedes that he was worse than his father in battle, “though better in assembly” (4.400), and on this night Nestor praises him for both (9.53–54). Polydamas tells Hector that a god gives one man the works of war and puts a good mind in another (13.726–734). Achilles himself, in his grief for Patroclus, calls himself such as no other of the Achaeans in war, “but in assembly others too are better” (18.105–106). See [[phoenix]] and [[peleus]].

## Outside Homer

Cicero, in *On the Orator*, cites the line for the time when the same teachers taught men how to act and how to speak: Phoenix says that Peleus gave him to the young Achilles as a companion for the war, “to make him an orator of words and a doer of deeds” (*De oratore* 3.57; see [[phoenix-the-model-teacher]]).
