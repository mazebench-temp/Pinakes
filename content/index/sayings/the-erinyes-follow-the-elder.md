**Two claims.** Zeus' order to Poseidon rests on two grounds, strength and birth: he says that he is “far stronger … and the elder by birth” (βίῃ πολὺ φέρτερος … καὶ γενεῇ πρότερος, 15.165–166, repeated by Iris at 15.181–182; see [[genee]]). The narrator gave the same reason in Book 13 for Poseidon's helping the Achaeans only in secret: the two were of one race and one father's line, “but Zeus was born first and knew more” (13.355; see [[poseidon]]). Poseidon answers with a third ground, the lot. The three sons of Cronus divided the world by lot, “and each has his share of honor”, so that he is Zeus' equal in honor (ὁμότιμον, 15.186–193; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]] and [[time]]). He does not dispute Zeus' strength. He says Zeus should keep his threats for his children (15.194–199).

**The elder's right.** Iris answers the claim of equality with the claim of birth. The Erinyes are the powers who punish wrongs done to those who have a claim on a man by blood: Phoenix's father called on “the hateful Erinyes” against his son (9.454), Althaea's curse on Meleager was heard by “the Erinys who walks in darkness” (9.571), and Athena tells Ares, struck down, that so he may pay his mother's Erinyes in full, since he has deserted the Achaeans (21.412–414; see [[erinyes]] and [[curses]]). Here they take the side of the elder brother. ἕπονται, “follow”, is the verb of an escort: the Erinyes go with the elder as his followers and avengers, and a younger brother who wrongs him has them to fear. The maxim is said as common knowledge, οἶσθʼ ὡς, “you know how”, and Poseidon accepts it with a word of praise for the messenger (15.206–207; see [[a-messenger-who-knows-what-is-due]]). It is the only place in Homer where the Erinyes defend an elder brother.

**The other order.** Homer's Zeus is the eldest son of Cronus. In Hesiod he is the youngest: Rhea bore Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hades, the Earth-shaker and last Zeus, “the youngest of her children”, whom she bore in secret in Crete (ὁπλότατον παίδων, *Theogony* 453–458, 477–480; see [[hesiod]]). Hesiod's Zeus becomes king when the gods, having settled their contest for honors with the Titans by force, urge him to rule at Earth's advice, and it is he who then apportions their honors among them (*Theogony* 881–885); Homer's Poseidon holds his share by the lot (15.189–193). Hesiod's Erinyes are born of the blood that fell to Earth when Cronus maimed his father Ouranos (*Theogony* 178–185): from the first they belong to a wrong done to a parent by his son.
