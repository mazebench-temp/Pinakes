Idaeus has told the Achaean assembly that Paris will give back all the possessions he brought from Argos, and more from his own, but not Helen (7.385–397). The assembly falls silent, as it did before Hector's challenge (7.398 = 7.92; see [[silence]]), and Diomedes speaks “at last”, as Menelaus stood up “at last” that morning (ὀψὲ δὲ δή, 7.94, 7.399). His answer refuses the whole offer, the goods as well as the woman, and gives a reason anyone can see. γνωτόν is “known, plain”; νήπιος is the child or the fool who does not see what is in front of him (see [[nepios]]). The offer itself is the sign: a city that sends back its plunder is losing. The Achaeans shout their approval, “admiring the word of Diomedes” (7.403–404), and Agamemnon tells Idaeus that the Achaeans' answer is his own (7.406–407; see [[mythos]] and [[diomedes]]).

The bounds of destruction, ὀλέθρου πείρατα, are “made fast” (ἐφῆπται) on the Trojans like a rope or a net (see [[the-bounds-of-victory]]). Polydamas uses the phrase of the Achaeans when he thinks their end is near (12.79), and the Odyssey of the suitors when Odysseus reveals himself (*Odyssey* 22.33, 22.41). Diomedes is right about the end of the war and wrong about the next day: at noon of the next day of battle Zeus weighs the fates of the two armies, and the Achaeans' day of doom sinks (8.69–74).

**The fool.** Ajax speaks the same half-line in Book 17, and turns it round: “even one who is an utter fool would know that father Zeus himself is helping the Trojans” (17.629–630). The poem has a related proverb, “once a thing is done, even a fool knows it” (ῥεχθὲν δέ τε νήπιος ἔγνω), which Menelaus says to Euphorbus and Achilles to Aeneas, warning each to get out of the way before he learns it by suffering (17.32, 20.198). Hesiod's form of it is “the fool learns by suffering” (παθὼν δέ τε νήπιος ἔγνω, *Works and Days* 218; see [[hesiod]]).
