## Gift and madness

Plato had made poetry a kind of possession: poets compose not by art but inspired and out of their minds (*Ion* 533e–534b), and whoever comes to the doors of poetry without the madness of the Muses, trusting to art, will fail, the poetry of the sane eclipsed by that of the mad (*Phaedrus* 245a). Aristotle names the mad beside the gifted, but grounds both in the same capacity, to feel the emotions one portrays and so to convey them; the gifted can mold themselves to any part, the mad are carried out of themselves. Some editors read “of the gifted rather than of the mad”. The Aristotelian *Problems* asks why all who have been outstanding in philosophy, statecraft, poetry or the arts were melancholic (30.1, 953a10–12), and Horace mocks Democritus for shutting the sane poets out of Helicon (*Ars Poetica* 295–297; see [[horace]]).
