Apollo has sent Aeneas against Achilles, and Hera calls on Poseidon and Athena: either they turn Aeneas back, or one of them stands by Achilles and gives him great power, “so that he may know that the best of the immortals love him” (20.115–124). Then comes the warning. The danger she fears is not that Achilles will be beaten but that he will be frightened, and the maxim says why. χαλεπός is “hard to endure”, dangerous, and the infinitive is added loosely, “hard in their appearing openly”, as Hephaestus says of Zeus, “for the Olympian is hard to stand against” (1.589; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.131). ἐναργής is a god seen “in plain sight”, in his own form (see the translation's row on ἐναργής).

**In the Odyssey.** The word is used there of gods who show themselves. Athena stands at the door of Eumaeus' hut, and Odysseus and the dogs see her but Telemachus does not, “for the gods do not appear in plain sight to all” (οὐ γάρ πω πάντεσσι θεοὶ φαίνονται ἐναργεῖς, *Odyssey* 16.161); Alcinous says that the gods have always appeared plainly to the Phaeacians when they offered hecatombs, and feasted beside them (*Odyssey* 7.201–203); and Nestor says that Athena came to him in plain sight at the feast of the god (ἐναργής, *Odyssey* 3.420).

**In the event.** Poseidon talks Hera out of fighting, and no god appears to Achilles in his own form in this book. Poseidon pours a mist over his eyes and takes Aeneas away, and Achilles sees only the work, “a great wonder” (20.321–344); it is Hector who hears the voice of a god, and is afraid (20.375–380; see [[seeing-the-gods]] and [[achlys]]).

## Outside Homer

Walter Leaf compares Aeneas' words to Dido in Virgil, when Mercury has brought him Jupiter's command: “I myself saw the god in clear light entering the walls, and drank in his voice with these ears” (ipse deum manifesto in lumine vidi / intrantem muros vocemque his auribus hausi, *Aeneid* 4.358–359; Leaf on 20.131).
