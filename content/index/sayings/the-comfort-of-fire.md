Idaeus has brought the Achaeans two proposals from Troy. Paris will give back the possessions he took, but not Helen; and the Trojans ask for a truce to burn their dead (7.385–397). Diomedes answers the first for the army, and the Achaeans shout their approval (7.400–404; see [[even-a-fool-can-see]]). Agamemnon adopts that answer and grants the second, and the maxim is his reason. φειδώ is sparing, stinting, holding back what one could give; the noun occurs nowhere else in the Iliad. μειλίσσω, “soothe, soften”, is also found only here in the Iliad; in the Odyssey it is used of softening a hard truth out of pity (*Odyssey* 3.96, 4.326). The fire is a kindness done to the dead, and it should come quickly (ὦκα). The ghost of Patroclus will beg Achilles to bury him “as quickly as possible”, so that he may pass the gates of Hades (23.71; see [[cremation]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). Agamemnon then makes the truce an oath, calls Zeus to witness, and lifts his scepter to all the gods (7.411–412; see [[oath]] and [[scepter]]).

The rule is spoken by an enemy about the enemy's dead, and it holds on both sides of the war. Hector made the return of the loser's body a term of his challenge, so that the Trojans could give him his portion of fire if he fell (7.76–86), and at dawn both armies burn their dead under the truce (7.421–432; see [[the-truce-for-the-dead]]). The poem's last books test it. Hector, dying, asks Achilles for fire in the words of his challenge (22.342–343 = 7.79–80), and Achilles refuses: the dogs and the birds will share out all of him (22.345–354). Only in the last book does Achilles give the body back and hold back the war while Troy burns its dead son, as Agamemnon holds it back here (24.656–670).
