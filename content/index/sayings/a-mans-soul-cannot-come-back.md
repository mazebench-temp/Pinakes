**Goods and a life.** Achilles has refused the gifts, and every other treasure he can name: not the wealth Ilios had in peacetime, nor all that the stone threshold of Apollo's temple at Pytho holds (9.401–405). The reason is a lesson in exchange. The four lines set two kinds of property against the one thing that is not property. Cattle and sheep are ληϊστοί, to be had by raiding; tripods and horses are κτητοί, to be acquired. The ψυχή is οὔτε λεϊστή οὔθʼ ἑλετή, neither to be raided nor to be caught, once it has crossed ἕρκος ὀδόντων, “the barrier of the teeth”, as the breath leaves at death (see [[psyche]]). The teeth are a fence, ἕρκος, and the soul has escaped like an animal from a pen that no one can drive back. The phrase is used elsewhere of the word that escapes a speaker's lips (see [[what-a-word]]).

The gifts are measured in the first kind of wealth: tripods, horses, women won in raids, cities with men “rich in flocks and rich in cattle” (9.122–156). The life that would be spent in earning them belongs to the other. In Book 1 Achilles counted the shortness of his life as a claim to honor (1.352–356); here he counts it as a reason why no honor is worth the cost, and goes on at once to the two fates his mother told him of (9.410–416; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]).

**Elsewhere.** The poem makes the comparison again when Hector runs from Achilles around the walls. The two were not running for a beast for sacrifice or an oxhide, the prizes of a footrace, “but for the life of Hector” (περὶ ψυχῆς, 22.159–161). In the Odyssey Achilles' shade tells Odysseus that he would rather work the land for hire for a poor man than be lord of all the dead (*Odyssey* 11.488–491), the same valuation from the other side of death.
