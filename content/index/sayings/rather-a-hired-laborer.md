**The consolation refused.** Odysseus has come to consult Teiresias and has not yet set foot on his own land; he contrasts his troubles with the fortune of Achilles: “no man before was more blessed than you, nor will be hereafter. / For before, while you lived, we honored you equally with the gods, / we Argives, and now again you are mighty among the dead, / being here; so do not grieve at all that you are dead, Achilles” (*Odyssey* 11.482–486; see [[achilles]], [[three-times-blessed]] and [[time]]). Achilles answers at once, “Do not speak comfortingly to me of death” (*Odyssey* 11.488): θάνατόν γε catches up impatiently the word θανών of Odysseus' last line, and παραυδάω, “speak comfortingly”, is the word with which Penelope will refuse her housekeeper's comfort (*Odyssey* 18.178; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.488). Being lord among the dead is what Odysseus has offered him as honor; he sets beside it the lowest life he can name on earth.

**The lowest life.** ἐπάρουρος is literally “on the soil”, a serf bound to the land, and θητευέμεν, “serve for hire”, describes his condition: the θῆτες are named beside the slaves, δμῶες, in the Odyssey (θῆτές τε δμῶές τε, “hired men and slaves”, *Odyssey* 4.644), and working for hire seems to be what marks the difference between them; in the Solonian constitution at Athens the θῆτες were the hired laborers (Merry on 11.489; see the Odyssey translation's table of key words, ἐπάρουρος, θητεύω, ἄκληρος, and its note on 11.489, where a variant reads πάρουρος). The man he would serve is ἄκληρος, “who had no land of his own”, and the words that follow, “and no great livelihood”, explain it (Merry on 11.490). The only hired service the Iliad tells is that of two gods, Poseidon and Apollo, who “served for hire for a year / for a fixed wage” under Laomedon, and were cheated of it (*Iliad* 21.444–445; see [[misthos]], [[slavery-and-freedom]] and [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]]).

**The other choice.** In the Iliad Achilles knew of two fates, a lost homecoming with imperishable fame or a long life without it, and chose to stay and die (*Iliad* 9.410–416; see [[the-two-fates-of-achilles]]); what he would not trade then for all the goods Agamemnon offered was his life, which “cannot come back again” once it has left him (*Iliad* 9.401–409; see [[a-mans-soul-cannot-come-back]]). The dead man prices the life he lost above all that the fame has brought him. Yet the scene does not end on that price: he asks for his son and his father, and when Odysseus tells him of Neoptolemus' deeds, the soul goes ranging with long strides across the meadow of asphodel, “glad because I said that his son was preeminent” (*Odyssey* 11.492–540; see [[neoptolemus]] and [[the-land-of-the-dead]]).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates begins with these verses his list of what must be struck out of poetry about Hades, so that the guardians may not fear death more than slavery: “We shall wipe out, then, beginning from this verse, all such things” (*Republic* 3.386c; Merry on 11.490; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]). He quotes them again of the prisoner who has been dragged out of the cave into the light and comes back to the shadows: he would not envy those honored and powerful among the prisoners, but would have suffered “Homer's” wish, and would much rather be on the soil and serve for hire another man with no land of his own, and suffer anything, than hold their opinions and live their life (*Republic* 7.516d; see [[plato]]). The first time the verses are a fear to be removed; the second time they are a judgment Plato makes his own.
