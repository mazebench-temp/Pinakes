**The terms refused.** When Achilles and Hector meet at last, Hector speaks first. He will flee no more, but let them call upon the gods, “for they will be the best / witnesses and overseers of agreements”: if Zeus grants him the victory he will not defile Achilles, but strip his armor and give his body back to the Achaeans, “and you do the same” (22.254–259; see [[hector]], [[oath]] and [[the-death-of-hector]]). They are the terms of his challenge in Book 7, when he called Zeus to witness that the victor would give back the other's body (7.76–86; see [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]). Achilles' first words throw them out: “Hector, accursed one, do not talk to me of agreements” (22.261). συνημοσύνας, “agreements”, are the ἁρμονίαι of Hector's appeal to the gods (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 22.261; see the translation's note on 22.261), and ἄλαστε, “accursed one”, is said of a man only here (see the translation's row for ἄλαστος).

**Lions and men, wolves and lambs.** The two images are of creatures that can make no compact with each other. Men swear oaths to men; a lion and the men who hunt it have nothing to swear, and wolves and lambs “mean evil to one another through and through” (22.264; see [[lion]], [[wolves]] and [[lambs]]). In the similes wolves fall upon lambs scattered by a careless shepherd and tear them apart, “since their hearts have no courage” (16.352–355; see [[like-wolves]]). ὁμόφρονα θυμόν, “hearts at one” (22.263), is found only here in Homer; the *Odyssey* has its noun and verb for husband and wife who keep house “with one mind” (ὁμοφροσύνη, ὁμοφρονέοντε, *Odyssey* 6.181–184).

The comparison does not say which of the two men is the beast, but the poem has given the part to Achilles. When he went out against Aeneas he rushed on “like a lion, / a ravager, whom men are eager to kill, / gathered together, a whole people” (20.164–166; see [[wounded-lion]]), and in the last book Apollo will tell the gods that Achilles knows savage things like a lion, that he has destroyed pity and has no shame (λέων δʼ ὣς ἄγρια οἶδεν, 24.41–45; see [[like-a-lion]] and [[pity]]).

**Until one has fallen.** There will be no oaths “before one or the other has fallen / and glutted Ares with blood, the warrior with the oxhide shield” (22.266–267; see [[ares]] and [[with-the-oxhide-shield]]). Diomedes told Pandarus the same in Book 5, after Pandarus' spear had failed to wound him: “You missed; you did not hit me. But I do not think you two / will stop before one or the other of you has fallen / and glutted Ares with blood” (5.287–289). The narrator gave the verse to Achilles' own longing on this day, when the gods went down to the battle and he wished above all to meet Hector, “for it was with his blood above all that his heart bade him / glut Ares, the warrior with the oxhide shield” (20.76–78). When Achilles' spear has flown over him, Hector answers with Diomedes' word, “You missed!” (ἤμβροτες, 22.279; 5.287).

**Friendship and oaths.** “so it is not possible for you and me to be friends, nor will there be / oaths between us two” (22.265–266). φιλήμεναι, “to be friends”, and ὅρκια, “oaths”, are the two halves of a truce in the poem's language. In Book 3 the armies were to settle the war, “making friendship and swearing a trusty truce” (φιλότητα καὶ ὅρκια πιστὰ ταμόντες, 3.73; again 3.94, 3.256, 3.323; see [[philotes]] and [[the-oaths-sworn]]), and when Pandarus' arrow broke the truce, Agamemnon said that the Trojans had “trampled on the trusty oaths” (4.157; see [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]). Hector's duel with Ajax ended in gifts, so that men would say that the two “parted in friendship, reconciled” (7.301–302; see [[parted-in-friendship]]). Earlier on this day Achilles received trusty oaths from Agamemnon, sworn before the army with Zeus, Earth, the Sun and the Erinyes as witnesses (ὅρκια πιστά, 19.191; 19.257–265; see [[oath-formulas]]); Hector gets none.

**Remember your prowess.** Achilles goes on: “Remember all your prowess; now you must indeed / be a spearman and a bold warrior” (22.268–269). Sarpedon, dying, charged Glaucus in the same verses (16.492–493), and Diomedes once said that the Achaeans “used to marvel at brilliant Hector / for being a spearman and a bold warrior” (5.601–602). Achilles leaves Hector no hope of it: “there is no escape for you any longer, but at once Pallas Athena / will bring you down by my spear; and now you will pay back all at once / the sorrows of my companions, whom you killed raging with your spear” (22.270–272; see [[athena]] and [[hetairos]]).

## In the poem

**The refusal kept.** The answer holds after the kill. Hector, dying, asks once more for his body, and his last two verses are those of his challenge in Book 7 (22.342–343 = 7.79–80); Achilles answers that no ransom, not even Hector's weight in gold, will keep the dogs from his head (22.338–354; see [[apoina]] and [[the-dragging-of-hector]]). In the last book the gods' command brings him to give the body back to Priam for a ransom (24.137–140), and he grants the old man a truce for the funeral, holding back the war for as long as Priam asks, and takes him by the right hand at the wrist so that he may not be afraid (24.656–672; see [[truce]]). The man who would make no agreement with Hector makes one with his father.

**Athena's oaths.** Here Athena stands by Achilles, and in the likeness of Deiphobus, “in form and in tireless voice”, she deceives Hector into the fight (22.227, 22.247; see [[truth-and-deception]]). The *Odyssey* ends with the same goddess making peace. Odysseus obeys her, in the words said of Achilles here, “So spoke Athena, and he obeyed, and rejoiced in his heart” (22.224 = *Odyssey* 24.545), and Pallas Athena sets oaths between the two sides, in the likeness of Mentor in form and voice (*Odyssey* 24.546–548).
