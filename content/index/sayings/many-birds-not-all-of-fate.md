Zeus has sent two eagles over the assembly of Ithaca, and Halitherses, who alone of his age knew how to read birds, has read them: Odysseus is near, and plants slaughter and doom for the suitors (*Odyssey* 2.146–176; see [[the-omen-of-the-eagles]], [[halitherses]] and [[divination]]). Eurymachus answers with a general truth. Birds are many, and not every one is ἐναίσιμος, “of fate”, a bird that carries a god's meaning; the adjective is the narrator's word for Halitherses' gift, “speaking words of fate” (ἐναίσιμα μυθήσασθαι, *Odyssey* 2.159; see [[aisa]] and [[birds]]). Then he puts his own reading in its place: “But Odysseus / has perished far away” (*Odyssey* 2.182–183). The maxim lets him charge the seer with interpreting for gain, “waiting for a gift for your own house” (*Odyssey* 2.186), and ends in a threat of a fine and the refusal of the suitors, “nor do we heed the oracle” (*Odyssey* 2.192–202). Telemachus had said the same the evening before, of the prophets his mother consults (*Odyssey* 1.415–416; see [[theopropion]]).

**Hector and Polydamas.** The Iliad has the same scorn of a reader of birds. When Polydamas reads an eagle at the Achaean wall as a warning, Hector answers that he does not care whether birds fly to the right or to the left, and that “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (12.237–243; see [[one-omen-is-best]]). Both men answer a seer with their own will, and in both poems the seer is right: the Trojans do not come back from the ships in good order, and the suitors die in the hall as Halitherses foretold. In the last assembly of the Odyssey he tells the people that it came about because they would not listen to him (*Odyssey* 24.451–462).
