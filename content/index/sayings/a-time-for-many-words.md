**Whose time.** The saying settles a small dispute about the hour. Odysseus broke off his list of the women he saw among the dead, for “before that even the immortal night would wane. But it is time / to sleep”, either at the ship or in the palace (ἀλλὰ καὶ ὥρη / εὕδειν, *Odyssey* 11.330–332). Alcinous answers him with his own words turned around: “This night is very long, endless; and it is not yet time / to sleep in the hall; so go on telling me of wondrous doings”, and he could hold out till dawn (οὐδέ πω ὥρη / εὕδειν, *Odyssey* 11.373–376; see [[alcinous]]). To Odysseus, eager for bed, the night seemed far spent; Alcinous, eager to hear more, would have it early still, and says playfully that a vast part of the night is left. That is the simple meaning, better than Eustathius' refinement that the season was autumn or later; Alcinous seems to parody what Odysseus had said (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.373). Odysseus grants both: the words and the sleep each have their time.

**Where the weight falls.** The second half carries the stress. The verse that follows, “but if you still long to listen”, shows that the emphasis lies on “a time also for sleep”: Odysseus thinks that now is the time for sleep, but he is not unwilling to make a sacrifice (Merry on 11.379). He will not begrudge telling of things “more pitiable even than these”, the sorrows of his companions who escaped the war and perished in their homecoming “by the will of an evil woman” (*Odyssey* 11.380–384; see [[mythos]], [[hypnos]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).

**The hour of sleep.** ὥρη with a genitive or an infinitive, “it is time for”, closes many evenings in the Odyssey. At Pylos the disguised Athena bids the company pour libations “and then think of bed; for it is the hour” (τοῖο γὰρ ὥρη, *Odyssey* 3.334); in the later books it is time for supper (*Odyssey* 14.407; 21.428) and soon time for pleasant rest (*Odyssey* 19.510). The nearest thought is the swineherd Eumaeus' to his guest: these nights are endless, there is a time to sleep and a time for those who enjoy it to listen, nor should he lie down before it is time, “too much sleep is a weariness too” (*Odyssey* 15.392–394, plain renderings), and the two men go on to take their pleasure in each other's sorrows (*Odyssey* 15.398–401). The Iliad's evenings end otherwise, with the herald's “it is good to yield to night too” (*Iliad* 7.282; see [[yield-to-night]] and [[satiety-of-all-things]]).
