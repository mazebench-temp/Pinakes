Artemis has taunted her brother for yielding the victory to Poseidon, and Hera, “the revered wife of Zeus, in anger” (21.479), answers for him (21.470–480; see [[artemis]], [[hera]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). τοξοφόρῳ περ ἐούσῃ, “bow-bearer though you are”: the bow does not make her a match for Hera (see [[bow-bearer]]). γυναιξί, “to women”, is the emphatic word, as Walter Leaf puts it: her bow was given her for use against women, not against goddesses (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.483). Zeus made her so, and gave her leave to kill whichever of them she wishes: the deaths of women are her province, as in Homer the deaths of men are Apollo's. She killed Laodameia, Sarpedon's mother, in her anger (6.205), and Andromache's mother in her father's halls (6.428); Achilles wishes she had killed Briseis with an arrow on the day he took her (19.59–60). In the *Odyssey* Odysseus asks his mother's shade whether Artemis came with her gentle arrows and killed her (*Odyssey* 11.171–173), and Penelope prays to Artemis for death (*Odyssey* 18.202–204, 20.61–63). Leaf takes the lion here for a figure of death, and Artemis for a goddess of death to women; the masculine λέοντα is strange of a goddess, he notes, but the feminine is not found in Homer, and λέων may be taken as of either gender (on 21.483; see [[lion]]).

The second half of the taunt sends her back to the hunt: “Truly it is better to slay wild beasts on the mountains / and wild deer than to fight in force with those stronger than you” (21.485–486; see [[wild]] and [[deer]]). The *Hymn to Aphrodite* gives Artemis the same delight, the bow and the killing of wild beasts on the mountains (τόξα καὶ οὔρεσι θῆρας ἐναίρειν, *Hymn to Aphrodite* 18). Hera goes on, “But if you wish to learn of war”, in the words with which Glaucus offered Diomedes his lineage (21.487; 6.150; see [[genealogy-in-battle]]), and breaks off; the beating supplies the rest (21.489–492; see [[the-dove-and-the-hawk]]).
