**The blessing.** Odysseus has asked the girl for very little, to be shown the city and given a rag to throw around him (*Odyssey* 6.178–179), and he ends his plea with what a suppliant can give in return, a wish: “may the gods give you all that you have in your mind” (*Odyssey* 6.180; see [[supplication]] and [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]]). He guesses well what that is. The goddess has told Nausicaa in a dream that her marriage is near, and she has been ashamed to name it to her father (*Odyssey* 6.25–35, 6.66; see [[marriage]] and [[aidos]]). The wish is the first of the book's thoughts of a husband for her: she will say to her handmaids, “Would that such a man might be called my husband” (*Odyssey* 6.244), and she fears what the town will say if she is seen with him (*Odyssey* 6.273–288; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]]).

**The words.** ὁμοφροσύνη, “oneness of mind”, and ὁμοφρονέω, “be of one mind”, are words of the Odyssey. Telemachus tells Peisistratus, as they drive back from Sparta, that the journey they have made together will set them still more in one mind (ὁμοφροσύνῃσιν, *Odyssey* 15.196–198, a plain rendering), and the blinded Cyclops wishes that his ram could think as he does and speak (ὁμοφρονέοις, *Odyssey* 9.456). The Iliad has only the adjective, of the wolves and lambs that have no hearts at one (ὁμόφρονα θυμόν, *Iliad* 22.263; see [[no-oaths-between-lions-and-men]]). The νοήματα in which husband and wife are at one are their thoughts and purposes (see [[noos]]), and οἶκον ἔχητον, “they keep house”, is said in the dual: the house is held by the two together.

**Grief to enemies, joy to friends.** The accusatives πόλλʼ ἄλγεα, “many sorrows”, and χάρματα, “joy”, stand in apposition to the whole clause, the construction of Hector's reproach to Paris, “a joy to your enemies, and a shame to yourself” (δυσμενέσιν μὲν χάρμα, *Iliad* 3.51; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.184; see [[algos]]). The last words, μάλιστα δέ τʼ ἔκλυον αὐτοί, “and they themselves know it best”, are hard. Merry follows the scholium, “they themselves feel the good of it”, comparing the man in whom Zeus puts a good mind, “and he himself knows it best” (*Iliad* 13.732–734); he rejects Lobeck's “they praise each other and hear themselves praised”, and records Nauck's verdict, *verba vitiosa* (Merry on 6.185; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 6.185).

**In the poem.** The man who speaks the blessing is on his way home to a wife of his own, whom he has just preferred to a goddess (*Odyssey* 5.215–220; see [[penelope]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]). The Phaeacian king will soon wish him for a son-in-law, “being such as you are, and thinking the thoughts I think” (*Odyssey* 7.311–315, a plain rendering; see [[alcinous]]).

## Outside Homer

Euripides' Nurse says the same of Medea and Jason before their quarrel: she went along with her husband in all things, “and this is the greatest safety, when a wife does not stand apart from her husband” (*Medea* 13–15), a passage Merry reports as Lowe's comparison (on 6.182).

The third book of the *Economics* ascribed to Aristotle, which survives only in Latin, takes Odysseus' words to Nausicaa as a lesson for husbands and wives (Rose fr. 184; see [[nausicaa]]).
