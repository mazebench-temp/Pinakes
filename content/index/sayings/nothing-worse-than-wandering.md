**The thanks.** Eumaeus has refused to let his guest go down to the city to beg among the suitors, whose servants are not such as he: let him stay, for no one in the hut is vexed by him, and when Telemachus comes he will clothe him and send him where he wishes (*Odyssey* 15.325–339; see [[eumaeus]] and [[welcome-the-guest-send-him-on]]). Odysseus answers with the wish with which he thanked the swineherd for the chine the night before, “Would that you, Eumaeus, might be as dear to father Zeus / as you are to me” (*Odyssey* 15.341 = 14.440), and gives a new reason, “since you have given me rest from wandering and dread misery”. The maxim follows from that word. ἄλη, “wandering”, comes back in the third verse with “bane and sorrow”, and between the two stands the cause: men suffer such things “for the sake of the ruinous belly”.

**The words.** πλαγκτοσύνη, “roaming”, is found only here (see the translation's table of key words); it is formed from πλάζομαι, the verb of the man of many turns in the proem, “who was driven / far astray” (*Odyssey* 1.1–2), and of the swineherd himself, whom the stranger has just pitied as a child “driven so far from your native land and your parents” (ἀπεπλάγχθης, *Odyssey* 15.382). The belly is οὐλομένη, a word that always means “miserable” or “accursed”, as Schulze showed, and not “destructive”; in this context it has a slightly mock-heroic effect (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 15.344). The translation keeps the epithet of the wrath, “ruinous” (*Iliad* 1.2; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 15.344, and [[gaster]]). Some manuscripts omit *Odyssey* 15.345 (Monro's critical note on *Odyssey* 15.345).

**The beggar's belly.** The disguised Odysseus says the like whenever he must account for himself. To Alcinous he excused his hunger, since there is nothing more shameless than the hateful belly, which bids a man remember it even in grief (*Odyssey* 7.215–221); in Ithaca he will say that there is no hiding the ruinous belly, “which gives many evils to men”, for whose sake ships are fitted out against enemies (*Odyssey* 17.286–289, a plain rendering), that Antinous struck him because of it (*Odyssey* 17.473–474, a plain rendering), and that his belly, “that evil-worker”, urges him into the fight with Irus (*Odyssey* 18.53–54, a plain rendering; see [[odysseus]]). The saying in the hut is the gentlest of these: it is spoken in thanks, to the man who has stopped the wandering for a night.

## Outside Homer

Euripides' Jocasta asks her exiled son what it is to be deprived of one's native land, whether it is a great evil, and Polynices answers, “The greatest; greater in fact than in word”; asked how he fed himself before his marriage, he says that sometimes he had enough for the day, and then he did not (*Phoenician Women* 388–389, 400–401, a plain rendering; see [[euripides]]). The scene is a parallel, not a quotation: the exile's evils, like the beggar's, begin with the want of a living.
