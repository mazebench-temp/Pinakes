Achilles speaks over the body of Patroclus, among the Myrmidons, on the night after the fight for the body (18.314–323). The word he gave Menoetius in his halls was “vain”: he had told him that he would bring his son back to Opus “when he had sacked Ilios and won his share of the spoil” (18.324–327; see [[menoetius]], [[opus]] and [[nostos]]). The maxim takes his own failure into a general truth, and the next verse gives the reason: “for it is fated that both of us redden the same earth / here in Troy” (18.329–330; see [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]).

The book has already shown Zeus fulfilling a plan. Thetis tells Achilles that what he prayed for in *Iliad* 1 has been fulfilled by Zeus, the Achaeans penned among the sterns, and he answers, “My mother, these things the Olympian has indeed fulfilled for me; / but what pleasure have I in them” (18.74–80; see [[aekelios]]). When he sent Patroclus out he prayed for two things, and Zeus “granted him one thing, and refused him the other”: the war was driven from the ships, but Patroclus did not come back (16.249–252). Nestor said the same of Hector's hopes before the night raid, that Zeus “will not fulfill for Hector all the plans / that he now hopes for” (10.104–105), and the next book gives the other side of the thought when Agamemnon excuses his folly: a god brings all things to their end (θεὸς διὰ πάντα τελευτᾷ, 19.90).
