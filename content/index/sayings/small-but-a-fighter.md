Athena finds Diomedes beside his chariot, cooling the wound Pandarus gave him; the sweat under the broad strap of his shield is wearing him down, and his arm is tired (5.793–798). She lays her hand on the yoke of his horses and begins with his father. The μέν … ἀλλά of the line sets body against spirit: μικρός, “small”, in δέμας, “build”, but a μαχητής, “fighter”. The size of Homer's heroes is seldom given, and when it is, it is usually great. μικρός occurs only here in the Iliad. The Catalogue says something similar of the lesser Ajax, “he was small, and wore a linen corselet, but with the spear he surpassed all the Panhellenes and Achaeans” (2.529–530).

Athena goes on with the story of Tydeus at Thebes. Even when she would not let him fight, but told him to feast at his ease in the halls, “he, with the strong spirit he always had”, challenged the young Cadmeians and beat them all (5.802–808; see [[tydeus-at-thebes]]). Then comes the charge: either weariness has sunk into Diomedes' limbs, or fear holds him, “then you are no offspring of Tydeus, the wise-hearted son of Oeneus” (5.811–813). She repeats Agamemnon's verdict of Book 4, that Tydeus fathered a son worse than himself in battle (4.399–400; see [[better-than-our-fathers]]), and her first line says it outright: “Tydeus has begotten a son little like himself” (5.800).

The rebuke is a spur, and it is also unjust, as Agamemnon's was. Diomedes answers that he is not afraid but obedient: she forbade him to fight any god but Aphrodite, and he has recognized Ares in the battle (5.815–824). Athena releases him from her own command, and they drive at Ares together (5.825–863). See [[fathers-and-sons]], [[gennaios]], [[tydeus]] and [[diomedes]].

## Outside Homer

Statius made Tydeus' smallness part of his character in the *Thebaid*. When Tydeus and Polynices fight at night in the porch of Adrastus' palace, “a greater courage reigned in his small body” (*maior in exiguo regnabat corpore virtus*, *Thebaid* 1.417; see [[statius]] and [[statius-tydeus]]).
