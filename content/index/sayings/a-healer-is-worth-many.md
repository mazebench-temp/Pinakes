Paris has hit Machaon in the right shoulder with a three-barbed arrow while he was “the best in the fighting” (ἀριστεύοντα), and the Achaeans are afraid that if the battle turns the Trojans may take him (11.505–509). Idomeneus asks Nestor to take him up in his chariot and drive to the ships, and gives his reason as a maxim. ἀντάξιος, “worth as much as”, sets one man in the scale against many (πολλῶν … ἄλλων), as Agamemnon did in his admission to the elders, “Worth many people is the man whom Zeus loves” (ἀντί νυ πολλῶν, 9.116–117; see [[the-man-whom-zeus-loves]]), and as Achilles did when he said that nothing was worth his life (ψυχῆς ἀντάξιον, 9.401; see [[psyche]]). The second verse says in what a healer's worth lies: cutting out arrows and spreading on soothing drugs, as Machaon sucked the blood from Menelaus' wound and spread on the drugs that Chiron had given his father (4.217–219; see [[healing]] and [[machaon]]). The man whose skill is the cure of arrow wounds has been hit by an arrow. Nestor does not answer; he mounts his chariot at once (11.516–520).

The rescue of the healer starts the rest of the book. Achilles sees Nestor's mares carry a wounded man past his ship and sends Patroclus to learn who it is (11.597–615; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). At the end of the book the maxim is put to the test. Eurypylus, shot in the thigh, finds no healer free: of the two, “one, I think, with a wound, in the huts, himself in need of a noble healer, lies there”, and the other is fighting on the plain (11.833–836; see [[podalirius]]). So he asks Patroclus to do a healer's work in nearly the words of Idomeneus' second verse, to cut the arrow out and spread soothing drugs on the wound (11.829–830), drugs that Patroclus learned from Achilles, and Achilles from Chiron (11.831–832; see [[chiron]] and [[eurypylus]]). Patroclus does it (11.844–848).

## Outside Homer

When Alcibiades bursts drunk into Agathon's party, the doctor Eryximachus asks what they are to do, whether they are simply to drink like thirsty men, without talk or song. Alcibiades answers that they must obey him, “for a healer is a man worth many others”, and tells him to give whatever order he likes (Plato, *Symposium* 214a–b; see [[plato]]).
