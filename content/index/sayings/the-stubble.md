**The figure.** καλάμη is the stalk of grain, what is left standing or lying when the ear has been reaped. Homer has the word once more, in Odysseus' picture of battle as a harvest in which men soon have their fill of fighting, “in which the bronze strews the most straw on the ground, / but the harvest is smallest” (*Iliad* 19.221–224; see [[satiety-of-battle]] and [[the-reapers]]). In the Odyssey the stubble is a man. The speaker has just named the excellence that once won him a wife of a wealthy family, a man “no worthless man / nor one who fled from war” (*Odyssey* 14.212–213; see [[arete]]), and what is left of him is what the reaper leaves; but from the stubble one may still judge what the crop was. γιγνώσκειν, “tell what it was”, has its object understood, and the boast that follows, of the daring that Ares and Athena gave him, is the crop (*Odyssey* 14.216–221; see [[the-brave-man-in-ambush]]).

**A true figure in a false tale.** The man who uses the figure has been made old by Athena, who withered his skin, took the fair hair from his head and dimmed his eyes (*Odyssey* 13.429–438; see [[odysseus-changed-into-a-beggar]]). The Cretan's past is invented, but the stubble is real enough, and what it hides is the man himself (see [[old-age-and-memory]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle, explaining why urbane sayings please, says that learning easily is naturally pleasant, that rare words are unknown and standard words familiar, and that metaphor teaches most: “When the poet calls old age ‘stubble’, he gives us learning and knowledge through the genus, for both have lost their bloom” (*Rhetoric* 3.10, 1410b14–15; see [[metaphora]], [[asteion]] and [[aristotle]]). He reads the stubble as old age, and the genus is the faded thing, ἀπηνθηκότα, “past their bloom”, that holds old age and the stalk together.
