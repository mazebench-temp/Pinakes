Going through the ranks, Agamemnon speaks to the men he sees eager for battle. His reason for fighting is the broken oath. The Trojans are liars (ψευδέσσι; with a different accent, ψεύδεσσι, “lies”, and both readings are ancient), because an oath is a word about the future and they have made it false. Zeus, the witness of oaths, will not stand by them (see [[pseudos]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]). The rest of the speech gives the curse of Book 3 its content: the breakers' flesh for the vultures, their wives and children for the Achaean ships (3.300–301, 4.237–239).

The maxim is Agamemnon's, and it holds in the poem, in its way. The audience has seen Zeus agree that the Trojans should be made to break the oath (4.64–72), and the Trojans will win for a long time before they lose. But Pandarus dies in the next book (5.290–296), in Book 7 Antenor tells the Trojans that they are fighting “having played false (ψευσάμενοι) with the trusty oaths”, and that nothing good will come of it (7.351–353), and the city falls in the end. See [[fulfilled-late]] and [[a-day-will-come]].
