**The rule.** Telemachus has asked to be sent home, and Menelaus answers with a judgment on hosts before he gives his consent: he is indignant with a host who loves beyond measure and hates beyond measure, “what is due is better in all things” (*Odyssey* 15.68–71; see [[what-is-due-is-better]]). The next two verses explain the opposition. A host may fail a guest in two ways, by pressing him to go when he would stay, and by keeping him when he is eager to go; too much kindness, shown in keeping a guest against his will, is as bad as sending him away before he wishes (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 15.70). The last verse gives the rule in two halves, each the remedy for one fault. φιλεῖν, “make welcome”, is a host's word for receiving a guest (see the translation's table of key words, ἔξοχα φιλέω), and πέμπειν, “send off”, is the escort a host owes a guest who must go (see [[pompe]] and [[xenia]]).

**A gnomic verse.** Aristonicus reports that the verse was not found in many copies, and Monro brackets it: “the gnomic line 74 is clearly an addition, as such a line is apt to be” (Monro on *Odyssey* 15.70, and his critical note on *Odyssey* 15.74; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 15.63, 15.74, 15.139). Murray prints it, and without it the speech still makes its point in *Odyssey* 15.72–73. Monro also reports J. B. Bury's objection to what follows, that Menelaus, “who has himself been dwelling on the duty of speeding the parting guest”, then offers to take Telemachus on a tour through Hellas and Argos (Monro on *Odyssey* 15.80–85; see [[hellas]]).

**Hosts and the departing guest.** The verbs are those of the poem's own detentions. κατερύκω, “hold back”, is what Calypso does to Odysseus (*Odyssey* 1.55), what the disguised Athena asked Telemachus not to do when he offered her a bath and a gift (*Odyssey* 1.315), and what Nestor did when Telemachus would have slept at the ship (*Odyssey* 3.345). The good hosts of the poem let go in the same terms: Alcinous promises that “against your will no one will keep you / of the Phaeacians” (*Odyssey* 7.315–316; see [[alcinous]]), and Circe, asked to keep her promise, tells Odysseus and his men to stay no longer in her house against their will (*Odyssey* 10.483–489). Menelaus himself pressed Telemachus to wait eleven or twelve days, and was refused (*Odyssey* 4.587–599).

## Outside Homer

Two sayings outside Homer give the same measure, without naming him. Among Hesiod's precepts is “do not be called either a man of many guests or a man of none” (μηδὲ πολύξεινον μηδʼ ἄξεινον καλέεσθαι, *Works and Days* 715, a plain rendering; see [[hesiod]]), and Aristotle, asking whether one should make as many friends as possible, applies the words, “aptly said” of hospitality, to friendship: neither friendless nor a man of too many friends (*Nicomachean Ethics* 9.10, 1170b20–23; see [[aristotle]]).
