**The answer Iris will not carry.** Zeus has sent Iris to order Poseidon out of the battle, “into the shining sea” or among the gods, since Zeus is “far stronger … and the elder by birth” (15.158–167); she has delivered the order in his words (15.174–183; see [[message-repeated]]). Poseidon refuses. Brave as Zeus is, he “has spoken arrogantly”: the three sons of Cronus drew lots for the sea, the darkness and the sky, the earth is common to all, and Poseidon is Zeus' equal in honor; let Zeus rebuke his own sons and daughters (15.184–199; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]] and [[time]]). Iris does not take the answer back as it stands. “Am I really to carry it in just this way … / this word to Zeus, so harsh and stern, / or will you turn it somewhat?” (15.201–203; see [[mythos]]).

**Turning.** μεταστρέφω is to turn something about, and Zeus had used it of Poseidon a hundred and fifty verses before: if only Hera thought as he did, Poseidon “would quickly turn his mind to follow your heart and mine” (μεταστρέψειε νόον, 15.52). Nestor had hoped in the same words that Achilles might turn his heart from its anger (μεταστρέψῃ φίλον ἦτορ, 10.107). The maxim gives the reason in the adjective of the same root: στρεπτός, “turnable, pliant”, is said of the φρένες, the mind (see [[phrenes]]), of the ἐσθλοί, the good and noble. To change one's mind is the mark of a noble man, not of a weak one, and Iris leaves Poseidon a way to yield without loss. The next verse adds the pressure: “You know how the Erinyes always follow the elder” (15.204; see [[the-erinyes-follow-the-elder]]).

**Poseidon yields.** He grants that the word is “fair and right” (κατὰ μοῖραν, 15.206; see [[moira]]) and praises the messenger who knows what is due (15.207; see [[a-messenger-who-knows-what-is-due]]). Then he gives way, “for all my indignation” (15.211; see [[nemesis]]), with a threat: if Zeus spares Troy against the will of the gods who favor the Achaeans, “there will be incurable anger” between them (15.212–217; see [[cholos]]). He plunges into the sea, “and the Achaean heroes missed him” (15.219; see [[pothe]] and [[poseidon]]). Zeus calls the outcome “far better both for me and for himself” (15.226), since otherwise the matter “would not have been settled without sweat” (15.228).

**Its kin.** In *Iliad* 13 Poseidon himself, in Calchas' likeness, told the Achaean leaders that “the hearts of good men can be healed” (ἀκεσταί τοι φρένες ἐσθλῶν, 13.115; see [[good-mens-hearts-can-be-healed]]). The two half-verses end alike, and now the god who healed others' grievance with a maxim is turned by one. Phoenix had argued to Achilles from the gods themselves: “even the gods themselves can be turned”, by sacrifice, vows and libation (στρεπτοὶ δέ τε καὶ θεοὶ αὐτοί, 9.497; see [[even-the-gods-can-be-turned]]); Achilles was not turned. Aeneas, before he fights Achilles, uses the adjective of speech itself: “the tongue of mortals is a twisting thing” (στρεπτὴ δὲ γλῶσσʼ, 20.248).
