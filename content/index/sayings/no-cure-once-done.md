**Too late.** Odysseus has described Hector raging by the ships, praying for dawn so that he can burn them (9.236–246). His appeal to Achilles begins with time. “Late though it is” (καὶ ὀψέ περ, 9.247), Achilles can still save the Achaeans; afterward the grief will be his own (αὐτῷ τοι μετόπισθʼ ἄχος, 9.249), and no remedy will help. μῆχος, “means, remedy”, is rare: Nestor used it in Book 2 of the army's endless quarrels, for which no remedy could be found (2.342). ἄκος is a healer's cure. ῥεχθέντος κακοῦ, “an evil once done”, is the core of the maxim: what is done cannot be undone (see [[foresight]]).

Phoenix ends the night's pleading with the same argument in his own terms: it would be worse to defend the ships when they are already burning (9.601–602). Both envoys foresee what happens. Achilles waits until the fire is at the ships, sends Patroclus, and loses him; when the news comes, “a black cloud of grief covered him” (ἄχεος νεφέλη, 18.22), and he wishes to die at once, since he was not there to save his friend (18.98–100; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]).

**The fool knows it once it is done.** The Iliad has a second proverb for the same truth, ῥεχθὲν δέ τε νήπιος ἔγνω, “a fool knows a thing once it is done”. Menelaus says it to Euphorbus, and Achilles to Aeneas, each warning a man to retreat before he comes to harm (17.32, 20.198; see [[nepios]]). The participle is the one Odysseus uses here.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod gives the proverb in another form, of the man who learns too late that Justice wins over outrage in the end: παθὼν δέ τε νήπιος ἔγνω, “the fool learns it by suffering” (*Works and Days* 218).
