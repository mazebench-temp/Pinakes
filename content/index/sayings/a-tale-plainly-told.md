**Where the tale stops.** Odysseus began his tale by asking what he should tell first and what last, since the heavenly gods had given him many sorrows (*Odyssey* 9.14–15), and he ends where his story among the Phaeacians had begun. On the evening before, asked by Arete who he was and where he got his clothes, he had said that it was hard to tell his sorrows from beginning to end, and told only how he came from Calypso's island (*Odyssey* 7.241–297; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]). Now the long tale reaches Ogygia in nearly the same verses, “From there for nine days I was borne along, and on the tenth night / the gods brought me to the island of Ogygia” (*Odyssey* 12.447–448, after 7.253–254; see [[ogygia]] and [[calypso]]), and he will not tell the rest again. τί τοι τάδε μυθολογεύω; “But why do I tell you this tale?”: here Odysseus turns and addresses Alcinous directly, and χθιζός, “yesterday”, refers back to that answer (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 12.450–451; see [[alcinous]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).

**The words.** μυθολογεύω, “tell a tale”, a verb of [[mythos|μῦθος]], is found only in these two verses (*Odyssey* 12.450, 12.453). ἀριζήλως, “plainly, clearly”, is the adverb of ἀρίζηλος, which the Iliad uses of what is plain for all to see or hear: the serpent at Aulis, which the god made a sign for all to see, the flashes of the lightning and the rays of the star, Ares and Athena on the Shield, and the voice of the trumpet and of Achilles ringing out clear (*Iliad* 2.318, 13.244, 22.27, 18.519, 18.219–221). Merry joins αὖτις with μυθολογεύειν and ἀριζήλως with εἰρημένα: “to tell over again what has been plainly told” (on 12.453). A thing told clearly once needs no second telling.

**A poem of repetitions.** The teller who will not repeat himself speaks in a poem that repeats freely, and in a book that does so more than most. Circe's instructions for the Sirens return in Odysseus' words to his companions and again in the narrative of the passage (*Odyssey* 12.50–54, 12.160–164, 12.178–179; see [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]]); her warning about the cattle repeats Teiresias' verses (*Odyssey* 12.137–141 = 11.110–114; see [[the-prophecy-of-teiresias]]); and the arrival at Ogygia repeats the verses of *Odyssey* 7.253–255. The ancient critics noticed the last: the scholia report that eight verses of Odysseus' answer to Arete were athetized because the story is told here (see the Odyssey translation's note on 7.251–258). The saying names a teller's distaste, not the poet's rule.

## Outside Homer

Proclus borrows the verse in his commentary on the *Republic*. Coming to the passage of souls into the bodies of animals, he says that he has made the most exact examination of the question in what he wrote on the palinode of the *Phaedrus*, and thinks it superfluous “to tell over again here too what has been plainly told there” (αὖτις ἀριζήλως εἰρημένα ἐν ἐκείνοις μυθολογεύειν), and he sets out only the conclusions (Proclus, *On the Republic*, Kroll II, p. 309, a plain rendering; see [[proclus]]). He does not name Homer: the verse has become a learned formula for declining to repeat oneself.
