**The praise.** Odysseus ends the story he tells Arete with the girl on the shore. He saw her handmaids playing, and she among them “like the goddesses”; he came to her as a suppliant, “and she did not fall short of good sense” (*Odyssey* 7.290–292; see [[nausicaa]] and [[supplication]]). νοήματος … ἐσθλοῦ is “good sense”, a word of the νόος family (see [[noos]]), and ἤμβροτεν, “fell short”, stands for ἥμαρτεν, the aorist of ἁμαρτάνω, “miss” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 7.292). The maxim gives the measure of the praise: good sense is what one would not expect of a young person who meets a stranger. ἀντιάσαντα, “on meeting you”, carries a sense of suddenness or emergency, as in Nausicaa's own promise to the “much-tried suppliant who has come our way” (*Odyssey* 6.193; Merry on *Odyssey* 6.193). The sequel is the girl's bread and wine, the bath in the river and the clothes that Arete has recognized (*Odyssey* 7.295–297; see [[xenia]] and [[clothing]]).

**A commonplace.** ἀφραδέω, “be thoughtless”, belongs with ἀφραδής, “senseless”, the word for the suitors in Ithaca (ἀφραδέων, *Odyssey* 2.282; see the translation's table of key words). Merry thought that the verse “seems to come in awkwardly”: ἀντιάσαντα ends the line and the sense in Nausicaa's verse (*Odyssey* 6.193 = 14.511) and in *Iliad* 10.551, so that ἐρξέμεν may have been added here to avoid an elliptical construction, and a commonplace used to fill out the line (Merry's critical note on *Odyssey* 7.294; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 7.294). ἐρξέμεν, “to do”, is probably an aorist (Merry on *Odyssey* 7.294).

**Young and old.** The Iliad has the same thought in other words. Menelaus asks that old Priam come to swear the oaths, since “The minds of younger men are always flighty; / but in whatever an old man takes part, he looks both before and after” (3.108–109; see [[before-and-after]]), and Antilochus, making amends to Menelaus after the chariot race, pleads his youth: “You know what the transgressions of a young man are like; / for his mind is quicker, and his counsel thin” (23.589–590; see [[metis]]). Odysseus turns the commonplace into a compliment: the girl showed the sense that the young are said to lack. Alcinous will still find fault with her, for not bringing the stranger home with her handmaids, and Odysseus will take the fault on himself (*Odyssey* 7.298–307; see [[quick-to-anger]]).
