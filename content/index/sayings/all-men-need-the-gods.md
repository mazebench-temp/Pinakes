**The cup.** Telemachus and Athena come upon the Pylians as they sacrifice black bulls to Poseidon on the shore (*Odyssey* 3.4–9; see [[the-sacrifice-at-pylos]]). Nestor's son Peisistratus, “of an age” with Telemachus (*Odyssey* 3.49; see [[peisistratus]]), takes both strangers by the hand, seats them on fleeces beside his father and his brother, gives them portions of the entrails and pours wine into a golden cup (*Odyssey* 3.36–41; see [[cup]] and [[libation]]). He gives it first to the stranger who looks older, bidding her pray to Poseidon, “for it is his feast you have come upon in coming here” (*Odyssey* 3.44), and then to give it to the younger, who prays to the immortals as well. The maxim is the reason for the second cup. Merry renders εὔχεσθαι here “a worshipper”: the young stranger is a man who prays, as all men are (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.48; see [[euchomai]] and [[prayer]]).

**The just young man.** The order of the giving pleases the goddess: “and Athena rejoiced in the wise and just man, / because he gave the golden goblet to her first” (*Odyssey* 3.52–53). Merry takes δίκαιος as “proper”, one who observes the common usage of men, and joins it to πεπνυμένος, as if knowing how men act and think were the same as doing what is fitting (on 3.52; see [[themis]] and [[wise]]). Then the goddess in disguise prays to Poseidon for Nestor, his sons and the Pylians, and for her own and Telemachus' return, “and she herself was bringing it all to pass” (*Odyssey* 3.55–62): as a seeming mortal she prays to a higher power, as a goddess she answers the prayer herself (Merry on 3.62; see [[athena]] and [[poseidon]]).

## Outside Homer

Nitzsch compared the opening of Aratus' *Phaenomena*, πάντη δὲ Διὸς κεχρήμεθα πάντες, “everywhere we all have need of Zeus” (*Phaenomena* 4), as Merry reports (on 3.48).
