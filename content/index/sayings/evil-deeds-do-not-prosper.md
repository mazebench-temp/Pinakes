**The gods in the doorway.** Hephaestus has called the gods to come and see “deeds that are laughable, and hard to bear” (*Odyssey* 8.307), and they gather to the bronze-floored house, Poseidon, Hermes and Apollo, while the goddesses stay at home for shame (*Odyssey* 8.321–324; see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]] and [[olympians]]). Standing in the doorway, they break into unquenchable laughter as they see the craft of Hephaestus, τέχνας, the snare of bonds he has poured round the bed (*Odyssey* 8.325–327; see [[laughter-of-the-gods]] and [[unquenchable-laughter]]). Then comes the saying, introduced with the verse that gives the talk of a crowd, “And this is what a man would say, looking at his neighbor”, with which the Iliad gives the army's verdict on Odysseus' beating of Thersites (*Odyssey* 8.328 = *Iliad* 2.271; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.328; see [[tis-speech]]). The gods judge the case as men in an assembly would (see [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]]).

**The proverb and the case.** The verse holds two sayings, and the next three verses apply them. ἀρετᾷ is “prosper” (Merry on 8.329), the verb used once more in Homer, of the people who prosper under a just king (ἀρετῶσι, *Odyssey* 19.114); the bad deed is Ares' theft of another god's wife, who “shamed the bed and couch / of lord Hephaestus” (*Odyssey* 8.269–270). κιχάνει τοι βραδὺς ὠκύν, “the slow overtakes the swift”, is a general truth, and Ares and Hephaestus are its instance: “Hephaestus, slow as he is, has caught Ares, / though he is the swiftest of the gods”, and he has caught him “lame as he is, by his craft” (*Odyssey* 8.330–332). Ares is the type of speed, whose name the Iliad gives its fast fighters, “the equal of swift Ares” (*Iliad* 13.295, 16.784; see [[equal-of-ares]] and [[ares]]). Hephaestus is lame from birth, so that his mother wished to hide him away (*Iliad* 18.395–397; see [[lame-in-both-legs]] and [[hephaestus]]), and he has just complained that Aphrodite loves Ares “because he is handsome and sound of foot, while I / was born a weakling” (*Odyssey* 8.310–311). The word for “sound of foot”, ἀρτίπος, is used elsewhere in Homer only of Blind Folly, who outruns the lame Prayers over all the earth while they come after her and heal the harm (*Iliad* 9.502–507; see [[litai]]). The lame god does what the Prayers do not: he catches the swift one. Nestor's lesson to Antilochus, whose horses are “the slowest at running”, is the same thought turned to a race: “by cunning one charioteer gets the better of another” (*Iliad* 23.310–318; see [[cunning-not-strength]]).

**The adulterer's fine.** τό is “wherefore” (Merry on 8.332): because he is caught, Ares “owes the adulterer's fine”, μοιχάγρια, a word found only here in Homer (see the translation's table of key words). It is formed like ζῳάγρια, “the price for one's life” (see [[zoagria]]): what is owed by an adulterer taken in the act. Hephaestus has said what he means to have: the bonds will hold the lovers “until her father gives me back the whole bride-price” (*Odyssey* 8.317–319; see [[bride-price]] and [[zeus]]). A scholium makes Ares' gifts to Aphrodite at the beginning of the song a μοιχάγρια, which Merry calls a strange anticipation of the story; the gifts were given to win her consent (Merry on 8.269). When Poseidon promises that Ares will pay all that is due, Hephaestus answers with another saying (*Odyssey* 8.344–356; see [[pledges-for-the-worthless]] and [[poseidon]]).

**Justice among the gods.** The saying is the moral of men's tales, spoken by gods about a god, and in laughter. The thought that a wrong is caught in the end is Agamemnon's when the oath is broken: if Zeus has not fulfilled it at once, “he fulfills it in the end, even late” (*Iliad* 4.160–161; see [[fulfilled-late]]). At the first council of the Odyssey Zeus tells the gods of a mortal adulterer: Aegisthus married Agamemnon's wedded wife and killed him on his return, though Hermes had been sent to warn him, “and now he has paid back all at once” (*Odyssey* 1.35–43; see [[aegisthus]]). In the song the end comes quickly, by a trap, and the price is a fine; once Poseidon has made himself surety for it, the adulterer goes off free to Thrace (*Odyssey* 8.359–361; see [[aphrodite]]).
