**The ambush for the old man.** Eidothea has told Menelaus of the unerring Old Man of the Sea, immortal Proteus of Egypt, who knows the depths of the whole sea and will tell him the way home, if he can lie in ambush and catch him (*Odyssey* 4.383–393; see [[proteus]] and [[eidothea]]). Menelaus asks her to devise the ambush herself: αὐτή is emphatic, “do thou thyself contrive it” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.395), lest the god see him first or know beforehand and avoid him. The maxim gives the reason. Merry notes the personal construction, “a god is hard to overpower”, as in Hephaestus' warning to Hera, “for the Olympian is hard to stand against” (ἀργαλέος γὰρ Ὀλύμπιος ἀντιφέρεσθαι, *Iliad* 1.589), and of the Achaean ditch, “it is very hard to cross” (*Iliad* 12.63), where elsewhere the neuter ἀργαλέον stands with an infinitive (*Odyssey* 2.244, 7.241; on 4.397).

**Overpowered all the same.** The god is caught by the help the maxim asks for. Eidothea brings four freshly flayed sealskins, “for she was devising a trick against her father”, lays the men in hollows in the sand under them, and puts ambrosia under their noses against the stench (*Odyssey* 4.435–446; see [[dolos]]). Proteus counts the men first among his seals, “and had no thought / in his heart that there was a trick” (*Odyssey* 4.452–453; see [[the-gods-know-all-things]]). Then it is strength: they hold him “with enduring heart” as he becomes a lion, a serpent, a leopard, a boar, water and a tree (*Odyssey* 4.454–459), until he grows weary and asks, “Which of the gods, son of Atreus, helped you to devise this plan, / so that you might lie in ambush and take me against my will?” (*Odyssey* 4.462–463). He takes it for granted that no mortal planned it alone.

**Gods and men.** The Iliad says the same of the gods in battle. Hera fears that Achilles will be afraid if a god meets him, “for gods are hard to face when they appear in plain sight” (*Iliad* 20.131; see [[gods-are-hard-to-face]]), and Dione warns that the man who fights the immortals does not live long (*Iliad* 5.407; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]). Menelaus' own fault was against the gods: they held him in Egypt “since I had not offered them perfect hecatombs” (*Odyssey* 4.351–352; see [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]).
