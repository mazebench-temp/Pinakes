**The day.** ἦμαρ ὀρφανικόν, “the day of orphanhood” (see the translation's table of key words), is one of the days by which Homer names a condition of life: the day of freedom that the conqueror takes away from the women of a sacked city and the day of slavery that follows it (6.455, 6.463), the fated day of death (8.72), the evil day that a defender wards off (see [[eleutheros]], [[doulios]] and [[evil-day]]). ὀρφανικός is the child left without a father; Andromache begged Hector not to make his child an orphan and his wife a widow (6.432), and Diomedes says of the man his spear strikes that “his children are orphans” (11.393–394; see [[chere]]). παναφῆλιξ, found only here, joins πᾶς, “all”, ἀπό, “away from”, and ἧλιξ, “of the same age”: the child is cut off from all his age-mates.

**Always.** The verbs are in the present, τίθησι, “leaves”, and in the aorist of what always happens, ἐδίηνε, “wets”, ἐστυφέλιξε, “drives”: what Andromache tells is the lot of every orphan. He hangs his head in everything (ὑπεμνήμυκε, a perfect of ἠμύω, “droop”, found only here), and his cheeks are wet with tears, δεδάκρυνται δὲ παρειαί, the half-verse of Theoclymenus' vision of the doomed suitors in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 20.353; see [[tears]]). In his need he goes up to his father's companions, the men with whom his father ate and fought (see [[hetairos]]), and pulls at a cloak or a tunic (see [[cloak]] and [[tunic]]). Someone pities him and holds out a cup for a moment (see [[pity]]): it “wets his lips, but does not wet his palate”, ὑπερῴη, the roof of the mouth, found in Homer only here in this sense (see the translation's table of key words).

**The feast.** The boy “whose parents are both alive”, ἀμφιθαλής, “flourishing on both sides”, found only here, drives him from the feast (ἐκ δαιτύος, also found only here), striking him with his hands and rebuking him with abusive words (22.496–497; see [[oneidos]]): “Off with you! Your father does not feast among us” (22.498). ἔρρʼ, “Off with you!”, is the word with which Hector dismissed Diomedes as a “wretched doll” (8.164). A son sat at the feast as his father's son, and with the father gone the place is gone (see [[feast]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).

## In the poem

Andromache speaks it over Hector, dragged and dead, of their son. The war may spare the child, “For even if he escapes the tearful war of the Achaeans, / always there will be toil and sorrows for him / hereafter; for others will take away his lands” (22.487–489), and the picture of any orphan follows. At its end she comes back to her own son, “And in tears the boy goes up to his widowed mother, / Astyanax” (22.499–500), and to the life he had on his father's knees (22.500–507; see [[astyanax]], [[andromache]] and [[childhood]]). The fear is the one she spoke at the Scaean gates while Hector lived (6.407–432; see [[hector-and-andromache]]). In her last lament she foresees worse: slavery with her, or death thrown from the tower (24.732–739; see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]).
