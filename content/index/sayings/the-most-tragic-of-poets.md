## The verdict

Aristotle defends Euripides against critics who blamed him for ending many of his plays in misfortune: the change from good fortune to bad is the right one for the finest tragedy, and on the stage such plays, if they are well done, appear the most tragic (see [[the-best-tragic-plot]] and [[euripides]]). The praise is qualified, “even if he does not manage other things well”, and elsewhere the *Poetics* faults Euripides for the needless baseness of Menelaus in the *Orestes*, the inconsistency of Iphigenia, the ending of the *Medea* by the machine, and his handling of the chorus (1454a28–33, 1454b1–2, 1456a25–29).
