## The art of lying

The marvelous is pleasant, and everyone adds something when telling a story, to please; Homer above all has taught the others how to tell lies as they should, by false inference. People think that if B follows from A, then when B is true A is true as well; so if the premise is false but something true would follow from it, the poet should add the true thing, and the audience infers the false (1460a17–25; see [[paralogismos]] and [[thaumaston]]). The example is from the Bath (see [[the-bath]]). By the usual interpretation, it is the scene in which the disguised Odysseus describes to Penelope the cloak and the brooch that Odysseus wore twenty years before, and she, knowing the description true, concludes that the stranger really did entertain her husband in Crete (*Odyssey* 19.215–250). The Odyssey says it of him in the same book: “he made the many lies he told like the truth” (*Odyssey* 19.203). The thought is as old as Hesiod's Muses, who “know how to tell many lies like the truth, and know, when we wish, how to sing what is true” (*Theogony* 27–28; see [[hesiod]] and [[pseudos]]).
