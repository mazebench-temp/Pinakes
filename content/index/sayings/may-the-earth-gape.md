Agamemnon, over his wounded brother, has imagined the worst: Menelaus dead, the army gone home, Helen left to the Trojans. He ends with the words of an enemy. “One of the overweening Trojans”, leaping on Menelaus' tomb, will mock the king who led his army to Troy in vain and went home with empty ships. The speech inside the speech is a [[tis-speech]], what “someone will say”, and it is the voice of the shame that a Homeric hero fears more than death. The wish that follows is for the earth to open and hide him before he hears it. See [[elenchos]] and [[achos]].

The wish recurs in the same shape. Diomedes, urged by Nestor to flee Zeus' thunderbolt, speaks of the same “dread grief” (αἰνὸν ἄχος) that Agamemnon felt (4.169, 8.147), imagines Hector boasting that the son of Tydeus ran from him, and ends in Agamemnon's words: “then may the wide earth gape for me” (8.147–150). Hector wishes it of Paris: “There and then may the earth gape for him” (6.281–282). And when the Achaeans are driven back over the body of Patroclus, they say to one another that it would be better if the black earth gaped for them all than to let the Trojans drag the body away (17.415–419). In each case the imagined words of others are worse than the earth closing over the one who hears them. See [[wide-earth]] and [[words-and-violence]].
