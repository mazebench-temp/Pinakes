Eurycleia fears for him: he is an only and beloved son, his father has perished far away, and the suitors will devise his death as soon as he is gone (*Odyssey* 2.363–370; see [[eurycleia]] and [[dolos]]). Telemachus answers with a god. He does not say which. The god is the stranger of the day before, whom he took for a god as she flew away (*Odyssey* 1.319–323, 1.420), and who has just promised him a ship in the likeness of Mentor (*Odyssey* 2.267–295; see [[athena]] and [[boule]]). Then he binds his nurse with an oath (see [[oath]]).

**A god behind it.** The phrase gives a god as the cause of what a mortal could not do alone. In the Iliad Pandarus, not sure whether the man who rages among the Trojans is a god, says that if it is the son of Tydeus, “he does not rage like this without a god, but someone / of the immortals stands near him, his shoulders wrapped in cloud” (5.185–186; see [[diomedes]] and [[seeing-the-gods]]). In the Odyssey the seer Theoclymenus tells Telemachus, in the same words, that not without a god did the bird fly on his right (οὔ τοι ἄνευ θεοῦ ἔπτατο δεξιὸς ὄρνις, *Odyssey* 15.531); Eurymachus turns it into a jest at the beggar whose bald head shines like a torch, οὐκ ἀθεεί, “not without a god”, has this man come to the house of Odysseus (*Odyssey* 18.351–355); and in the last assembly the herald Medon tells the Ithacans that Odysseus did not do his deeds against the will of the gods, since he saw a god beside him, in every way like Mentor (*Odyssey* 24.443–449).
