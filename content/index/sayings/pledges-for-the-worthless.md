**The promise.** Laughter rose among the gods at Hermes' answer to Apollo, “But laughter did not hold Poseidon”, and he keeps begging Hephaestus to set Ares free (*Odyssey* 8.339–345; see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]] and [[laughter-of-the-gods]]). Merry explains his part: Poseidon is the most venerable of the gods of Olympus next to Zeus, and besides he was uncle to Ares (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.344; see [[poseidon]] and [[ares]]). He promises that Ares himself “will pay all that is due among the immortal gods” (*Odyssey* 8.347–348), “all just requital in public presence of all the gods”, as Merry renders it (on 8.347): the debt of the adulterer's fine that the gods in the doorway have just named (*Odyssey* 8.332; see [[evil-deeds-do-not-prosper]]).

**The saying.** Hephaestus refuses with a maxim, and its sense turns on δειλός, “wretched”. Nitzsch held that in Homer the word must mean “poor, weak”, and followed one explanation in the scholia: the pledges a weak man gets are weak too, since such a man cannot pursue the wrongs done to him; δειλῶν would then be Hephaestus himself, “Even the surety that a weak man gets is but a weak security to hold in hand”. Merry prefers the other explanation of the scholia, that pledges given for the bad are bad themselves, since the faith pledged for such a man cannot be kept, because the Iliad joins δειλός with οὐτιδανός, “a nobody” (*Iliad* 1.293), and he renders: “Even surety for a sorry fellow is a sorry thing to hold in hand” (Merry on 8.351; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 8.351). The translation follows him. ἐγγύαι are the pledges, and the middle ἐγγυάασθαι is the act of the one who takes them, as Merry quotes Eustathius: the giver pledges (ἐγγυᾷ), the taker accepts the pledge (ἐγγυᾶται). The sorry fellow is Ares, who may slip away: “How could I bind you among the immortal gods, / if Ares should go off, escaping both the debt and the bonds?” (*Odyssey* 8.352–353). δέοιμι means “put you in chains”, and must not be diluted to “keep a hold on you” (Merry on 8.351): the surety would take the debtor's place in the bonds. A scholium reports that Aristarchus wrote εὐθύνοιμι, “call you to account”, which La Roche takes as his explanation and not his reading (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 8.352–353).

**The answer.** Poseidon removes the objection by taking the debt upon himself: “even if Ares should escape the debt / and go off in flight, I myself will pay you this” (*Odyssey* 8.355–356). Hephaestus yields: “It is not possible, nor would it be fitting, to refuse your word”, where οὐκ ἔστʼ οὐδὲ ἔοικε is in Merry's Latin *non licet neque decet* (*Odyssey* 8.358; Merry on 8.358). The bonds are loosed, and the lovers go off, Ares to Thrace and Aphrodite to Cyprus (*Odyssey* 8.359–363; see [[hephaestus]] and [[aphrodite]]). The song does not say whether the fine was paid. The language of the scene is the language of men's dealings: a debt (χρέος, *Odyssey* 8.353), a bride-price to be given back (*Odyssey* 8.318; see [[bride-price]]), a fine, and a surety.

## Outside Homer

In Plato's *Charmides* Critias takes “Know yourself”, written at the temple of Delphi, for the god's greeting to those who enter, and says that the men who set up the later inscriptions, “Nothing too much” and “A pledge, and ruin is near” (Ἐγγύη πάρα δʼ ἄτη), mistook it for a piece of advice and added useful advice of their own (*Charmides* 165a; see [[know-yourself]] and [[nothing-too-much]]). The verb was also the word of betrothal. When Cleisthenes of Sicyon chooses Megacles for his daughter Agariste, he “pledges” her to him “according to the laws of the Athenians”, and when Megacles says that he accepts the pledge (ἐγγυᾶσθαι, the middle again), the marriage is settled (Herodotus 6.130.2).
