**What a god cannot do.** The goddess, in the likeness of Mentor, has just told Telemachus that a god, if he wished, could easily save a man even from far away (*Odyssey* 3.231; see [[a-god-can-save-a-man-from-afar]]). The second maxim sets the bound of the first. θάνατος is ὁμοίιος, “common to all”, as the Iliad's old age is “the common lot” (4.315; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.236; see [[common-to-all]]), and when the fate of death takes a man down, μοῖρʼ ὀλοὴ καθέλῃσι τανηλεγέος θανάτοιο, the verse of Penelope's shroud (*Odyssey* 2.100), no god can ward it off (see [[thanatos]], [[moira]] and [[that-lays-men-low]]). Telemachus takes the words for a sentence on his father: there is no true homecoming for him, “but already / the immortals have devised for him death and black doom” (*Odyssey* 3.241–242; see [[ker-doom]] and [[nostos]]).

**A man they love.** φίλῳ ἀνδρί is the gods' own word for the men they cannot save in the Iliad. Zeus sees Sarpedon, “dearest of men to me”, fated to fall at the hands of Patroclus, and asks whether he should snatch him out of the battle; Hera answers, “A man who is mortal, long since ordained to his portion, / do you wish to set free again from grim-sounding death? / Do it; but not all of us other gods approve”, and Zeus sheds bloody raindrops for his son and lets him die (16.433–461; see [[sarpedon]] and [[not-all-the-gods-approve]]). When Achilles chases Hector round the wall, Zeus grieves for “a man dear to me” and asks the gods whether they shall save him from death, and Athena answers in Hera's words (22.168–181; see [[hector]]). Achilles, choosing to die, says the same of Heracles: “For not even mighty Heracles escaped his doom, / who was the dearest of all to lord Zeus, son of Cronus” (18.117–118; see [[heracles]]). The gods could rescue these men from one danger, as they rescue Paris and Hector in the Iliad; they cannot rescue them from the death that is their portion.
