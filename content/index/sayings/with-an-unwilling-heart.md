Hera has refused Zeus' proposal of peace. Zeus asks what Priam has done to her, and gives way: “Do as you wish” (4.37). He sets a condition, that she must never hinder him when he wants to destroy a city she loves, and gives his reason: “for I have granted you this willingly, though with an unwilling heart” (ἑκὼν ἀέκοντί γε θυμῷ, 4.43). Then he says what he is giving up: of all the cities under the sun, sacred Ilios was the most honored in his heart (4.44–49).

The phrase sets the will against the heart. ἑκών is the act, done freely and without compulsion; ἀέκων θυμός is the feeling that does not consent to it. Zeus chooses to give Troy up, and the choice costs him. The same pair of words, set against each other, makes other hard choices in Homer. Ajax, arming to fight Hector, says that no man will drive him back by force, one willing against one unwilling (ἑκὼν ἀέκοντα, 7.197), and Odysseus lies with Calypso at night, “unwilling beside the willing” (παρʼ οὐκ ἐθέλων ἐθελούσῃ, *Odyssey* 5.155).
