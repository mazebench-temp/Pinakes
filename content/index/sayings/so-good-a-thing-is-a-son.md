**The son who pays the killer back.** Nestor knows nothing of Odysseus, only what he has heard sitting in his halls: who came home safely, and how Agamemnon came home and Aegisthus devised a grim destruction for him (*Odyssey* 3.184–194; see [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). “But truly that man paid for it miserably” (*Odyssey* 3.195; see [[poine]] and [[aegisthus]]): the son is the one who makes the killer pay. Merry prefers ὡς ἀγαθόν, “How good a thing it is!”, to the accent ὥς, and takes λιπέσθαι as “should remain behind”, as in the Iliad's old father who “had begotten no other son to leave as heir to his possessions” (5.154); Bothe's paraphrase, he reports, makes the man blessed who leaves behind not only wealth but a son to guard it and avert ruin (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.196). The third line is Athena's of Orestes in *Odyssey* 1, “scheming Aegisthus, who had killed his famous father” (*Odyssey* 1.300; see [[orestes]] and [[kleos]]).

**You too.** Nestor ends with the verses with which Athena ended her own example: “You too, my friend—for I see that you are very handsome and tall— / be valiant, so that some one of those born hereafter may speak well of you” (*Odyssey* 3.199–200, as *Odyssey* 1.301–302; see [[born-hereafter]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). Telemachus grants the example and refuses it for himself: Orestes took vengeance, and the Achaeans will carry his fame wide, but “the gods have spun no such prosperity for me, / for my father and for me” (*Odyssey* 3.203–209; see [[olbos]]).

**The fatherless child.** The Iliad gives the other side of the maxim in the old father whose two sons Diomedes killed, with no other son to leave, so that “distant kinsmen divided his wealth among them” (5.152–158), and in Andromache's orphan: “The day of orphanhood leaves a child without friends of his own age” (22.490; see [[the-day-of-orphanhood]]).

## Outside Homer

At their father's tomb in Aeschylus' *Libation Bearers*, the children pray to Agamemnon not to wipe out the seed of Pelops' house, “for children are the saving voices of a dead man's fame” (παῖδες γὰρ ἀνδρὶ κληδόνες σωτήριοι / θανόντι), holding him up as corks hold up a net (*Libation Bearers* 503–507).
