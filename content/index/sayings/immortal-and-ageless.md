Hector has told the Trojans to camp on the plain and keep fires burning all night, and he ends with the next day. Diomedes will learn his own prowess if he stands up to Hector's spear; he will lie wounded among the foremost, with many companions around him, when the sun rises (8.532–538; see [[arete]] and [[diomedes]]). Then comes the wish. Its form is an oath of a kind the poem uses for strong assurance: two clauses joined by ὡς … ὡς, “as surely as”, in which the speaker wishes for something he cannot have, to vouch for the thing he is sure of. Hephaestus promises Thetis armor for Achilles in the same way, wishing that he could hide her son from death “as surely as” the armor will be a wonder to men (18.464–467), and Odysseus shouts to the blinded Cyclops that he wishes he could send him to Hades as surely as not even Poseidon will heal his eye (*Odyssey* 9.523–525).

What Hector wishes for is the condition of the gods. “Immortal and ageless” is the formula for it. The aegis is “ageless and immortal” (2.447); Zeus asks the immortal horses of Achilles, weeping for Patroclus, why the gods gave them to a mortal man, “but you are ageless and immortal” (17.443–445); and in the Odyssey Calypso offers to make Odysseus “immortal and ageless all his days”, and he refuses (*Odyssey* 5.135–136, 7.256–258). The second half of the wish is honor, “as Athena and Apollo are honored”: the two children of Zeus who are named with him when the poem's speakers wish for the impossible, “Father Zeus and Athena and Apollo” (2.371, 4.288, 7.132, 16.97; see [[time]]).

What Hector vouches for, that the day will bring evil to the Argives, comes partly true. The day that follows is the worst of the war for the Achaeans, and Diomedes is wounded among the foremost, though by an arrow of Paris and not by Hector's spear (11.369–400); Diomedes' spear has already stunned Hector himself (11.349–360). On that day the wish returns, when Ajax has mocked Hector before the ships: Hector wishes that he were the son of aegis-bearing Zeus and that queen Hera had borne him, and honored as Athena and Apollo are honored, “as surely as this day brings evil to the Argives” (13.825–828). What he wishes for cannot come true. Sarpedon gives the poem's answer before the wall: if he and Glaucus, escaping this war, would be ageless and immortal forever, he would not fight in the front himself; but since the fates of death stand over men in thousands, which no mortal can escape, let them go and win glory or give it to another (12.322–328; see [[gods-and-mortals]] and [[ker-doom]]). Hector wishes to be a god. Sarpedon fights because he is not one.

## Outside Homer

The Homeric Hymns tell the wish granted and spoiled. Zeus made Ganymede “immortal and ageless like the gods”, but when Dawn asked Zeus to make Tithonus immortal, she forgot to ask for youth, and he grew old forever (*Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* 202–238). Demeter would have made the child Demophon “ageless and immortal” if his mother had not cried out and broken the spell (*Homeric Hymn to Demeter* 231–262). Pindar gives the warning: “Do not, dear soul, strive for immortal life, but use to the full the means that are at hand” (*Pythian* 3.61–62).
