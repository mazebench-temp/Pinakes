{{quote:7e48128d-5964-5456-a8ff-16c710799c78}}

Night has parted the two champions, and Hector has agreed to stop (7.288–298; see [[yield-to-night]]). He ends his speech with a proposal and a reason. The gifts are to be “glorious” (περικλυτά), and their purpose is what men will say: “so that this is what a man will say, of Achaeans and Trojans alike”. It is the form of the speech he imagined at the start of the day, when a passer-by would read the tomb of the man he killed (7.87–91; see [[tis-speech]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). Then he looked for fame from a death; now he looks for it from a reconciliation.

The two lines are built as a pair, ἠμέν … ἠδʼ αὖτε, “both … and then”: the fight in “heart-devouring strife” (see [[eris]]) and the parting in φιλότης (see [[philotes]]). ἀρθμέω, “join in friendship”, is found only here in Homer; the adjective ἄρθμιος, “in league”, is used of allies in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 16.427). διέτμαγεν, “they parted”, is the verb of two who separate after an agreement, as Zeus and Thetis do (1.531), and Athena and Odysseus (*Odyssey* 13.439). The gifts follow at once. Hector gives a silver-studded sword with its sheath and its strap, and Ajax gives “a belt bright with purple” (7.303–305; see [[sword]] and [[belt]]), the gift Oeneus once gave Bellerophon (6.219).

The scene answers the meeting of Glaucus and Diomedes in Book 6. There two enemies found that their grandfathers had been guest-friends and exchanged armor “so that these men too may know” (6.230–231; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]] and [[guest-gifts]]). Hector and Ajax have no such bond. Their friendship is made by the fight itself, and it binds them no further than the day. When they meet again in Book 14, Hector's spear strikes where two straps cross on Ajax's chest, one of his shield and one of his silver-studded sword, and the straps save him (14.402–406); the poem does not say whether the sword is Hector's gift. In Book 16 Hector shears off the point of Ajax's spear with a great sword, and Ajax retreats, knowing the work of the gods (16.114–122). See [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]].

## Outside Homer

Sophocles made the gifts deadly to both men. His Ajax, before his death, calls the sword “a gift from Hector, most hateful of enemies”, since which he has had nothing good from the Argives, and quotes the proverb that “the gifts of enemies are no gifts, and bring no profit” (*Ajax* 661–665); he falls on it. Teucer, over his body, sets the two gifts side by side: Hector was dragged to his death by the belt that Ajax gave him, and Ajax died on Hector's sword. In the Iliad Achilles binds Hector to his chariot with thongs of oxhide (22.396–398). See [[sophocles]].
