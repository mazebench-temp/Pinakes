The curse comes in the middle of Menelaus' rebuke. He has called the silent Achaeans “Achaean women, Achaean men no longer” and said that it will be a disgrace if none of them faces Hector (7.96–98; see [[achaean-women-no-longer-men]] and [[lobe]]); after it he says that he will arm himself, and leaves the outcome to the gods (7.101–102; see [[the-bounds-of-victory]]). γένοισθε is an optative of wish: may you become water and earth. What water and earth make, in the curse, is a body with the life gone out of it. The words that follow describe how the men sit now: ἀκήριοι, “spiritless”, “without heart” (κῆρ), or “lifeless”, as the word is used of a man struck down by an arrow and of withering leaves (11.392, 21.466; see the translation's note on 7.100), and ἀκλεές, “without glory”, the negative of κλέος (see [[kleos]]). The men who will not risk their lives are like the dead already, and without the fame that is the dead man's reward.

The Iliad has a thought like it near its end. Apollo, protesting to the gods against Achilles' treatment of Hector's body, says that in his fury he “does outrage to the senseless earth” (κωφὴν γαῖαν, 24.54): the dead body is earth, and cannot feel what is done to it (see [[honoring-the-dead]]). The poem's other curses ask the earth to open and hide the man who speaks (see [[may-the-earth-gape]]), or wish that a man had never been born (see [[never-born]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod's Zeus orders Hephaestus to mix earth with water to make the first woman (γαῖαν ὕδει φύρειν, *Works and Days* 60–61; see [[hesiod]]), and Xenophanes of Colophon taught that “we are all born from earth and water” (fragment B33 DK). Read beside them, Menelaus' curse wishes the men back into the stuff of which they were made; whether Homer's first audience heard it so is uncertain.
