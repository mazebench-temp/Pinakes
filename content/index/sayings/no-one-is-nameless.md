**The question of the name.** The first night in the palace the queen asked, “Who are you among men, and from where?” (*Odyssey* 7.238; see [[who-are-you-among-men]]), and the stranger told his journey from Ogygia but not his name. At the end of the second day Alcinous, who has twice seen him weep at the singer's songs, asks it plainly. A stranger is as good as a brother to a man of sense, and so the stranger should not hide “with crafty thoughts” what he is asked (*Odyssey* 8.546–549; see [[as-good-as-a-brother]]): let him tell the name by which “they called you there, your mother and your father, / and the others who live in the city and round about it” (*Odyssey* 8.550–551). κεῖθι, “there, yonder”, is a general way of naming a home of which Alcinous does not yet know the name (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.550). The maxim follows. πάμπαν goes with οὔ τις, “no one at all” (Merry on 8.552; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 8.552), base or noble, since parents give every child its name at birth: ἐπὶ πᾶσι τίθενται, they “set it upon all” (Merry on 8.244, citing this verse). Then comes the second question, his land, his people and his city, so that the ships may carry him there (*Odyssey* 8.555–556; see [[phaeacians]]).

**Nameless.** ἀνώνυμος is found only here in Homer. Its kin νώνυμνος, “nameless”, is what an army fears in the Iliad, to “perish here, nameless, far from Argos” (*Iliad* 12.70, 13.227, 14.70), and Athena tells Telemachus that “The gods have not made your lineage nameless for the time to come, / since Penelope bore such a son as you” (*Odyssey* 1.222–223). The name the parents give at birth is one thing, the name that men repeat afterward another (see [[kleos]]).

**The answer.** The stranger gives his name at the beginning of his tale, “I am Odysseus, son of Laertes, known to all men for my wiles, and my fame reaches the sky” (*Odyssey* 9.19–20, a plain rendering; see [[odysseus]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]), as the fame of the lay of his quarrel with Achilles had reached the wide sky at the beginning of the book (*Odyssey* 8.74; see [[oime]]). In the tale he gives the Cyclops another name, in the very words of Alcinous' question: “Nobody is my name; Nobody they call me, / my mother and my father and all my companions” (Οὖτις ἐμοί γʼ ὄνομα· Οὖτιν δέ με κικλήσκουσι / μήτηρ ἠδὲ πατὴρ ἠδʼ ἄλλοι πάντες ἑταῖροι, *Odyssey* 9.366–367, a plain rendering; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). The blinded Cyclops curses “the evils that good-for-nothing Nobody brought me” (οὐτιδανὸς … Οὖτις, *Odyssey* 9.460, a plain rendering). His own name was found by his grandfather: when the nurse Eurycleia set the newborn child on Autolycus' knees and asked him to find a name, he told his son-in-law and his daughter, “give him the name I say” (τίθεσθʼ ὄνομʼ, with the maxim's verb), and named him Odysseus (*Odyssey* 19.399–409, a plain rendering; see [[autolycus]] and [[eurycleia]]).
