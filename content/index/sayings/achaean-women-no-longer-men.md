{{quote:c85422e7-fb25-5294-9d07-199abffb1184}}

The words are Thersites', in the assembly of Book 2. He has railed at Agamemnon's greed, and he turns on the army for putting up with it: ὦ πέπονες, κάκʼ ἐλέγχεα, “weaklings, wretched disgraces”, and then the half-line that makes the men women (see [[my-dear]] and [[elenchos]]). Ἀχαιΐδες is the ordinary word for the women of Achaea, as when Achilles says that there are many Achaean women in Hellas and Phthia whom he could marry (9.395); here it is turned on the men. Thersites' conclusion is to go home and leave Agamemnon to his prizes (see [[thersites]] and [[odysseus-and-thersites]]).

Menelaus says it again in Book 7, when Hector's challenge has been met with silence:

{{quote:0d8831f4-1695-5e50-bdc7-38d9c1166d00}}

He puts ἀπειλητῆρες, “braggarts”, in place of Thersites' “weaklings”: men who make threats and do not carry them out. The word occurs only here in Homer. In the next book Agamemnon shames the army with the same charge, asking where their boasts have gone, the boasts they made at Lemnos over meat and brimming bowls of wine, that each of them would stand against a hundred or two hundred Trojans, when now they are not a match for one, Hector (8.228–235). The half-line serves opposite ends. Thersites uses it to send the army home, Menelaus to send it into the fight. The worst man at Troy and the man for whom the war is fought say the same words.

The men of the Iliad measure courage against women. Hector tells Ajax not to test him “as if I were some feeble boy or a woman, who does not know the works of war” (7.235–236; see [[the-works-of-war]]), and mocks the retreating Diomedes, “you are no better than a woman” (8.163). See [[women-and-war]], [[shame-saves-more]] and [[praise-and-blame]].

## Outside Homer

Virgil gave the insult to an Italian. Numanus Remulus mocks the Trojans besieged in their camp as effeminate Phrygians, and ends: *o vere Phrygiae, neque enim Phryges*, “truly Phrygian women, for you are not Phrygian men” (*Aeneid* 9.617). Ascanius answers him with an arrow, his first kill in war. See [[virgil]] and [[virgil-numanus]].
