**The rebuke.** Hector has gone along the front looking for Deiphobus, Helenus, Adamas and Asius, and has found them dead or wounded (13.758–764). He finds Paris on the left, encouraging his companions, and turns on him with the abuse of Book 3, “Evil Paris, best in looks, mad for women, seducer” (13.769 = 3.39), and the question of where the others are: “now sheer destruction is sure for you” (13.770–773; see [[hector]], [[paris]] and [[where-have-our-boasts-gone]]). In Book 3 Paris began his answer “Hector, since you rebuke me fairly and not beyond what is fair” (3.59). Here he begins in the same way and refuses the rebuke.

**The answer.** “Hector, since your heart is set on blaming one who is not to blame” (13.775; see [[who-is-to-blame]] and [[aitios]]). He may hold back from war at some other time, but not now, “since my mother did not bear me wholly without courage either” (13.776–777; see [[alke]]). Since Hector roused the battle at the ships they have been fighting without rest, and the companions he asks about are dead; Deiphobus and Helenus have gone away wounded in the hand, and Zeus kept off death from them (13.778–783). Then he puts himself under Hector's command and promises courage with a limit: “I say we will not fall short / in courage, as far as our strength allows” (ὅση δύναμίς γε πάρεστι, 13.785–786). The maxim follows: πὰρ δύναμιν δʼ οὐκ ἔστι καὶ ἐσσύμενον πολεμίζειν.

**Strength and eagerness.** δύναμις, “power, strength”, is a rare word in the Iliad. Teucer, urged on by Agamemnon though he is eager already, answers that he does not stop “as far as I have strength” (ὅση δύναμίς γε πάρεστι, 8.293–294), and Achilles, cheated of Hector by Apollo, says that he would take vengeance on the god “if I had the power” (22.20). πὰρ δύναμιν, “beyond one's strength”, is found only here. ἐσσύμενον, “eager, rushing”, is the participle that Book 13 uses of Hector himself: the two Ajaxes might hold him back from the ships “for all his haste” (ἐσσύμενόν περ, 13.57), and the Ajaxes and Teucer will give him his fill of war “for all his eagerness” (καὶ ἐσσύμενον πολέμοιο, 13.315). Hector's eagerness is the poem's measure of zeal, and Paris answers it with a limit that no zeal can pass.

**It pleases.** The maxim is a modest one, and it works. “So saying, the hero persuaded his brother's mind” (13.788; see [[peitho]]), and the two go together where the battle is thickest (13.789–794). Paris' answer is the last thing in the book that Hector accepts before he leads the Trojans on; Ajax's challenge and the eagle on the right come next (13.802–832).

**In the Odyssey.** When Odysseus, after the killing of the suitors, asks Telemachus to consider how they will escape the vengeance of the dead men's kin, Telemachus answers that his father's counsel is best and ends with Paris' two verses: “we will follow with you eagerly, and I say we will not / fall short in courage, as far as our strength allows” (*Odyssey* 23.127–128 = 13.785–786). The maxim that follows them here is not in the Odyssey's text.
