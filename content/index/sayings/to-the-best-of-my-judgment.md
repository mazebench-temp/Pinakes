summary: The clause of the Athenian jurors' oath that bound them, where the laws were silent, to judge by their best judgment; Aristotle shows speakers arguing from it both for and against the written law.
aliases: [the jurors' oath, the most just judgment]
source: The jurors' oath (Aristotle, Rhetoric 1.15, 1375a29–30, 1375b16–17, 1376a19)

Athenian jurors swore once a year to vote according to the laws and decrees, and on matters for which there were no laws, by “the most just judgment” (γνώμῃ τῇ δικαιοτάτῃ), a clause the orators remind them of (Demosthenes 39.40). The full text of the oath given in Demosthenes' *Against Timocrates* (24.149–151) is generally thought to be a later reconstruction. Aristotle quotes the clause as “the best judgment”, and in the *Politics* he speaks of magistrates trained by the law to decide “by the most just judgment” what the law leaves undetermined (3.16, 1287a25–27). Whether the clause let jurors set aside the letter of the law, or only guided them where it was silent, is disputed; the *Rhetoric* shows speakers arguing it both ways (see [[epieikes]] and [[oath]]).
