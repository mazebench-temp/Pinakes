{{quote:e11a5ff3-2647-523f-8a92-c6c75fdc877b}}

Agamemnon has wished that old age had taken some other man and left Nestor among the young (4.313–316). Nestor wishes it too, and names the day: when he killed Ereuthalion, the story he tells in full in Book 7 (7.132–156; see [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]). Then he accepts his lot with a maxim: “But the gods never give men all things at once. If I was a young man then, now old age is upon me.” Youth and wisdom are both gifts of the gods, and they are given in turn, not together. The old man keeps what age leaves him, counsel and words, “for that is the prize of the old” (4.323; see [[prize-of-the-old]]).

The same idea answers other boasts in the poem. Polydamas tells Hector, who wants to excel in counsel because a god has given him excellence in war: “you will never be able to take everything at once (ἅμα πάντα) for yourself. To one man a god has given the works of war, to another the dance, to another the lyre and song, and in another's breast far-seeing Zeus puts a good mind” (13.726–734). In the *Odyssey* Odysseus tells the young Euryalus that the gods do not give their gifts to all men, looks and sense and eloquence together (*Odyssey* 8.167–177). The gods' gifts are divided among men, and in Nestor's case across a lifetime. See [[gifts-of-the-gods]], [[strength-is-a-gift]] and [[old-age-and-memory]].
