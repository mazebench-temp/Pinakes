{{quote:2e70ac6c-dcf8-5871-a307-2d5aae40bb35}}

The line closes Aeneas' account of speech: there are insults enough for both men to speak, the tongue of mortals turns every way, and wide is the pasture of words (20.246–249; see [[the-tongue-of-mortals]]). Walter Leaf reports Heyne's remark that the line has no connecting word and looks like an independent gnomic tag (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.250). Achilles has given Aeneas taunts, and Aeneas has given them back: Achilles mocked his hope of Priam's honor and reminded him of his flight, and Aeneas answered that he too knew “how to speak both taunts and evil words” (20.178–202; see [[kertomia]]). The exchange could go on without end, and Aeneas turns to the spears (20.251–258; see [[words-and-violence]] and [[praise-and-blame]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod gives the rule among his counsels on dealing with others: “the best treasure among men is a sparing tongue, and most grace goes with one that keeps measure; if you speak ill, you may soon hear worse yourself” (εἰ δὲ κακὸν εἴποις, τάχα κʼ αὐτὸς μεῖζον ἀκούσαις, *Works and Days* 719–721; Leaf on 20.250; see [[hesiod]]).
