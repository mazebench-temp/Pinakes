{{quote:754e9ee5-7609-52ab-bad2-e3a2ac1757a2}}

Diomedes has been told to choose his companion, the best man and not the most kingly (10.234–239). He chooses Odysseus, and gives his reasons before them all: “how then could I forget divine Odysseus, whose heart and proud spirit are eager above all others in every toil, and Pallas Athena loves him? If he goes with me, even out of blazing fire we would both come home, since he is wise beyond all others” (10.243–247). It is high praise, spoken to Odysseus' face before the assembled chiefs (see [[praise-and-blame]]).

Odysseus answers in three short clauses. The first refuses praise and blame together. The simple verb αἰνέω, “praise”, is rare in the Iliad; besides this line it occurs only when Antilochus, at the funeral games, tells Achilles to give Eumelus another prize “so that the Achaeans may praise you” (23.552). νεικέω is the poem's ordinary word for rebuke (see [[neikos]]), and Nestor used it of Diomedes a few lines before, waking him with his heel and rebuking him to his face (10.158). Odysseus will take neither. The second clause gives the reason: “you are saying this among Argives who know it” (εἰδόσι). What everyone knows does not need saying. Antilochus, speaking “among the Argives” at the games, begins in the same terms: “I will tell you, friends, who all know it” (εἰδόσιν ὔμμʼ ἐρέω πᾶσιν, 23.786–787). The third clause ends the talk: “But let us go; for the night is far spent, and dawn is near” (10.251; see [[war-and-night]]).

The refusal suits the man. Antenor remembered Odysseus in Book 3 standing with his eyes on the ground like a man without sense until he began to speak (3.216–224; see [[odysseus]]). He is praised anyway. When he comes back with the horses of Rhesus, Nestor greets him, “Come, tell me, much-praised Odysseus, great glory of the Achaeans” (πολύαινʼ Ὀδυσεῦ, 10.544), the verse with which Agamemnon greeted him on his return from Achilles' hut (9.673; see [[much-praised]] and [[great-glory-of-the-achaeans]]). πολύαινος is usually taken with αἶνος, “praise”, the noun of the verb Odysseus refused, though αἶνος can also be a tale, and some read “of many tales” (see the translation's note on 9.673). The same address is used in hostility by the Trojan Socus, who calls him “much-praised Odysseus, insatiable of tricks and toil” (11.430), and by the Sirens, who use it to draw him to his death (*Odyssey* 12.184).
