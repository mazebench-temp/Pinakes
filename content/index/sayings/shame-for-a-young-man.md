**No more need of shame.** The goddess, in the likeness of Mentor, opens with the word he will answer: “Telemachus, you have no more need of shame, not even a little” (*Odyssey* 3.14). Merry glosses αἰδοῦς there as “shyness” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.14; see [[aidos]]). The young man gives the rule that holds him back. The αἰδώς he feels is the respect owed to age: the questioner is νέος, the man questioned γεραίτερος, “older” (see [[geron]]), and in the construction the noun is drawn into the accusative by the infinitive, νέον ἄνδρα … ἐξερέεσθαι (Merry on 3.24). Athena answers that some things he will think of himself, and in others a god will give him counsel (*Odyssey* 3.26–28; see [[some-things-you-will-think-of-yourself]]).

**Young and old.** The rule is the Iliad's too. Antilochus, Nestor's son, asks Menelaus to bear with him after the chariot race, “for I am much younger / than you”, and “You know what the transgressions of a young man are like; / for his mind is quicker, and his counsel thin” (23.587–590; see [[antilochus]]), and Menelaus says that “The minds of younger men are always flighty” (3.108). At Pylos the young man's speech is the surprise: Nestor finds his words so like his father's that “you would not say / that a younger man would speak so like him” (*Odyssey* 3.124–125; see [[fathers-and-sons]] and [[mythos]]). That night Athena claims the older man's place among Telemachus' companions: “For I claim to be the only older man among them; / the others are younger men who follow out of friendship” (*Odyssey* 3.362–363; see [[mentor]]).

## Outside Homer

For the construction Merry compares Cassandra's words in Aeschylus, προτοῦ μὲν αἰδὼς ἦν ἐμοὶ λέγειν τάδε, “before this, there was shame in me to speak of these things” (*Agamemnon* 1203), and Agamemnon's πολλὴ γὰρ αἰδὼς δωματοφθορεῖν ποσίν, “for it is great shame to ruin the house with my feet”, as he takes off his shoes to walk on the purple cloths (*Agamemnon* 948; on 3.24).
