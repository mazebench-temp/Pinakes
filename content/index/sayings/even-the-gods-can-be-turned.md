**The argument.** Phoenix has told Achilles how he reared him, and now asks him to “tame your great spirit” (9.496; see [[thymos]]). His reason runs from the greater to the less. The gods have more ἀρετή, τιμή and βίη, prowess, honor and strength, than any man (see [[arete]]), and yet men turn them aside (παρατρωπῶσι) with burnt offerings, gentle vows, libation and the savor of the fat, “whenever someone oversteps and does wrong” (9.499–501; see [[sacrifice]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). If the gods can be turned by a wrongdoer's gifts, so can Achilles. στρεπτός, “turnable, pliant”, is formed from στρέφω, to turn. The Prayers follow at once (9.502–512; see [[litai]]), and later Phoenix asks that no god “turn you that way” (τρέψειε, 9.601), toward Meleager's choice.

**Elsewhere in Homer.** The thought recurs. When Iris brings Poseidon Zeus' order to leave the battle, she asks whether he will change his answer: “the minds of the noble can be turned” (στρεπταὶ μέν τε φρένες ἐσθλῶν), and the Erinyes always follow the elder (15.201–204). Poseidon yields (15.211). Poseidon himself tells the Achaeans that “the minds of the noble can be healed” (ἀκεσταί τοι φρένες ἐσθλῶν, 13.115). Aeneas, facing Achilles, calls the tongue of mortals “a twisting thing” (στρεπτή, 20.248). The Odyssey gives the other side. Nestor tells Telemachus how Agamemnon stayed behind at Troy to appease Athena's anger with hecatombs, “the fool, not knowing that she would not be persuaded; for the mind of the gods who are forever is not quickly turned” (*Odyssey* 3.143–147). In the Iliad itself Athena refuses the Trojan women's prayer and robe (6.311).

## Outside Homer

In Plato's *Republic* Adeimantus says that begging priests and seers, who promise to cure a man's wrongs with sacrifices and spells, call Homer to witness that the gods can be led by men, and quotes 9.497 and 9.499–501. His text reads λιστοί, “to be won by entreaty”, for στρεπτοί, and leaves out 9.498, the line about the gods' greater prowess, honor and strength (*Republic* 2.364b–e; see [[plato-phoenix-and-the-gifts]]).
