**The exchange.** In the fight over the body of Sarpedon, Aeneas throws at Meriones and misses, and the spear quivers in the ground behind him (16.608–615). He taunts him: “Meriones, dancer though you are, my spear / would soon have stopped you for good, if I had hit you” (16.617–618; see [[meriones]], [[aeneas]] and [[not-to-a-dance-but-to-fight]]). Meriones answers in kind: Aeneas too is mortal, and if he were hit he would give Meriones triumph and his soul to Hades (16.620–625). Patroclus cuts the exchange short. Insults will not make the Trojans give ground from the body; the earth will first hold many a man (16.627–629; see [[oneidos]] and [[fighting-over-the-body]]). Then the maxim, and its conclusion: “so there is no need to heap up words, but to fight”. He leads the way, and Meriones follows (16.632).

**Hands and council.** τέλος is the end in which a thing is decided, the word of “the end of death” (see [[the-end-of-death]]). The verse gives each kind of contest its own place: war is decided by the hands, words in the council (βουλή, see [[boule]]). It is the pairing on which a Homeric hero's distinction rests, the assembly “where men win glory” and the war (1.490–491), which Phoenix was sent to teach Achilles, “to be a speaker of words and a doer of deeds” (9.443; see [[speaker-of-words-doer-of-deeds]] and [[word-or-deed]]). Patroclus does not despise words; he puts them out of place. In the battle at the ships Ajax said the same of the light of rescue, “so in our hands lies the light, not in softness in war” (ἐν χερσὶ φόως, 15.741; see [[the-light-is-in-our-hands]]).

Meriones has been checked for talking before. When he met Idomeneus at the huts in Book 13 and spoke of his own courage, Idomeneus answered, “I know what you are in prowess; why do you need to tell of it?” (13.275), and broke off: “But come, let us no longer talk like this, like children, / standing here, lest someone be overbearingly indignant” (13.292–293). Aeneas says the same verse to Achilles before they fight (20.244). The battlefield has room for taunts and boasts, and the Iliad gives them in full, but the man who stops them is the one who goes back to the fighting (see [[words-and-violence]]).

## Outside Homer

The Suda, under Ἄρης, says that the word properly means iron and also war, and derives it from ῥῶ, “say”, with the privative α, “for in war there is need not of words but of deeds”; it quotes the verse as Homer's witness (Suda α 3852; see [[ares]]).
