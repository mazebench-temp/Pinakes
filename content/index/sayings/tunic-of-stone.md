The last words of Hector's rebuke are a grim figure. ἕσσο is a past tense of ἕννυμι, “put on, clothe oneself”, the verb for dressing, and the tunic ([[tunic|χιτών]]) is a man's inner garment. The stones would have covered Paris as closely as his clothes. The phrase is usually understood as death by stoning, the punishment a whole people inflicts on one who has brought ruin on it; some have taken it as the cairn heaped over a dead man. The first reading suits the context: the Trojans are “great cowards” (δειδήμονες) for not doing it. The book ends with the same people's judgment in another form. None of them would have hidden Paris from Menelaus, “for he was hateful to them all, like black doom” (3.454).

The joke has an edge. Paris came out wearing a leopard skin on his shoulders (3.17), and he will soon arm in fine bronze (3.328–338). Hector offers him different clothes. See [[never-born]] and [[who-is-to-blame]].

## Outside Homer

Stoning remained a communal punishment in Greece. In 479, when the councillor Lycidas proposed at Salamis that the Athenians accept the Persian terms, the Athenians stoned him, and their women stoned his wife and children (Herodotus 9.5). In Sophocles' *Antigone*, Creon's edict threatens whoever buries Polynices with death by public stoning (δημόλευστον, 35–36).
