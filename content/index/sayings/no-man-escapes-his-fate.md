Hector has given the child back to Andromache and seen her laughing through her tears. He strokes her with his hand and speaks to her for the last time in the poem. The comfort comes in two steps. First, a promise that is not quite a promise: no one will send him to Hades “beyond my portion” (ὑπὲρ αἶσαν; see [[aisa]]). Then the reason, a rule for all men. μοῖρα is the portion allotted to each man (see [[moira]]); πεφυγμένον ἔμμεναι, “to have escaped”, is a perfect, a thing that has never once been done. “Once he has been born” (ἐπὴν τὰ πρῶτα γένηται) fixes the portion at birth, where Homer's speakers picture it spun like a thread (20.127–128, 24.209–210; *Odyssey* 7.196–198).

οὐ κακὸν οὐδὲ μὲν ἐσθλόν, “no coward and no brave man”, is the heart of the answer to Andromache. She began by telling him that his own might would destroy him (6.407), and begged him to stay on the tower. Hector answers that courage does not hasten a man's death and cowardice does not put it off: staying would not save him, and going will not kill him before his day. Then he sends her home to her loom, for “war will be the concern of men” (6.490–493; see [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]]).

The thought runs through the Iliad in other mouths and moods. Achilles, refusing Agamemnon's gifts, says it in bitterness: “the coward and the brave are held in a single honor; the idle man dies just the same as the man who has done much” (9.319–320). Sarpedon makes it the ground of heroism: if the two of them could live forever, ageless and immortal, he would not fight in the front ranks, but since the fates of death stand over men in thousands, “which no mortal can flee or escape”, let them go and win glory or give it to another (12.322–328). In the Odyssey Athena, in the likeness of Mentor, says that not even the gods can keep death, common to all, from a man they love (*Odyssey* 3.236–238). When Hector's own hour comes, he sees it in the same terms: “now my fate has caught me” (νῦν αὖτέ με μοῖρα κιχάνει, 22.303). ## Outside Homer

Callinus of Ephesus urges young men to fight for land, children and wife in the same spirit: death will come when the Fates spin it, “for it is not fated that a man escape death, not even if his race is from immortal ancestors” (fragment 1.8–9, 12–13 West). When Croesus reproached Apollo for his defeat, the Pythia answered that “not even a god can escape the portion that is fated” (Herodotus 1.91).
