**Shame and counsel.** The goddess, in the likeness of Mentor, has told Telemachus that he has no more need of shame, and sent him straight to Nestor to learn “what counsel he keeps hidden in his breast” (*Odyssey* 3.14–20; see [[aidos]] and [[metis]]). He answers that he does not know how to greet the old man: “I am not yet practiced at all in shrewd words, / and there is shame for a young man in questioning an older one” (*Odyssey* 3.23–24; see [[shame-for-a-young-man]] and [[mythos]]). She divides the work between his own mind and a god. νοήσεις is the young man's own thought (see [[noos]] and [[phrenes]]); ὑποθήσεται, “will give you counsel”, is the verb of advice, ὑποτίθημι, and the adviser is a δαίμων, a god not named (see [[daimon]]).

**Not against the will of the gods.** Her reason is a litotes, οὐ γὰρ ὀίω / οὔ σε θεῶν ἀέκητι γενέσθαι τε τραφέμεν τε. Merry takes the second negative as belonging closely to the familiar phrase θεῶν ἀέκητι, “not under the disfavour of heaven” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.27), and τραφέμεν as an intransitive aorist, “grew up” (on 3.28). The son had the same confidence the evening before, when he told his nurse, “Take heart, nurse, since this plan is not without a god” (*Odyssey* 2.372; see [[not-without-a-god]]).

**His own thought and the god's.** The book shows both at work. Telemachus answers Nestor “taking heart; for Athena herself had put daring in his mind” (*Odyssey* 3.76; see [[tharsos]]), and his words are so like his father's that the old man is amazed (*Odyssey* 3.123–125; see [[sebas]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). Merry sets the line beside Odysseus' account of the Cyclops driving all his flocks into the cave that evening, ἤ τι ὀισάμενος, ἢ καὶ θεὸς ὣς ἐκέλευσεν, “whether from some thought of his own, or because a god so bade him” (*Odyssey* 9.339), and takes the god of such phrases as Homer's name for an impulse a man cannot account for (on 3.214; see [[divine-intervention]]).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Athenian quotes the three verses for the young citizens of his *Laws*: they too must think that what has been said is enough, and that the god and the daemon will suggest the rest (*Laws* 7.803e–804a; see [[plato]]). Cicero, waiting at Formiae for Caesar, wished for Homer's Minerva in Mentor's likeness to put Telemachus' question to her, and in a later letter gave himself her answer in its Greek (*Letters to Atticus* 9.8.2, 9.15.4; see [[cicero]]). Proclus counts the second verse among Homer's words for the daemon who governs the impulses within us (*On the Republic* II 298 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]).
