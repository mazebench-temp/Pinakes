**Over the moly.** Hermes has pulled the plant from the ground and shown Odysseus its nature: black at the root, with a flower like milk, and called by the gods by a name of their own. Digging it is hard for mortal men, and the god has just done it (ἐκ γαίης ἐρύσας, *Odyssey* 10.303); the maxim gives the reason (see [[moly]], [[hermes]] and [[language-of-gods-and-men]]). Merry thought the moly most likely a creation of the poet's fancy (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.305; see the Odyssey translation's note on 10.305).

**Knowing or doing.** The reading is uncertain. The best manuscripts give ἴσασι, “the gods know all things”, and a scholium (Schol. H.) alters it to δύνανται, which Murray prints. Merry finds it hard to decide: ἴσασι may be a reminiscence of Menelaus' verse to Eidothea, θεοὶ δέ τε πάντα ἴσασιν (*Odyssey* 4.379 = 4.468), and δύνανται a gloss suggested by χαλεπόν, “hard”, in the verse before (Merry's critical note on 10.306; see the Odyssey translation's note on 10.306 and [[the-gods-know-all-things]]).

**Of Zeus and of Athena.** The poem gives the same power to single gods. Helen says that Zeus gives good and evil, now to one and now to another, “for he can do all things” (δύναται γὰρ ἅπαντα, *Odyssey* 4.236–237; see [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]), and Eumaeus, giving his guest what there is, says it of the god who will give one thing and let another be, as he wishes in his heart (*Odyssey* 14.444–445). The phantom of Iphthime tells Penelope that Athena goes with her son as his escort, “for she has the power” (δύναται γάρ, *Odyssey* 4.826–827). Odysseus tells Telemachus that his changed looks are Athena's work, who made him such as she wishes, “for she can” (δύναται γάρ, *Odyssey* 16.207–208), and Penelope tells her nurse that the gods have made her mad, the gods “who can make even the very wise foolish” (οἵ τε δύνανται, *Odyssey* 23.11–12, plain renderings).

**The limits.** The poem does not let the claim stand without limit. Athena herself tells Telemachus that “death, which is common to all, not even the gods / can ward off from a man they love” (*Odyssey* 3.236–238; see [[not-even-the-gods-can-ward-off-death]]). Merry, on Menelaus' verse, sets this one beside it as “the theoretical view of the gods”, an article of belief that the facts of the poem do not illustrate: the gods' saving power is limited, Poseidon himself cannot heal his blinded son (*Odyssey* 9.525), and each god seems to have his own measure of strength, just as different men have (on 4.379). The Iliad has the same verse-end for the river's strength against Achilles: the wave kept overtaking him, “nimble though he was; for the gods are stronger than men” (θεοὶ δέ τε φέρτεροι ἀνδρῶν, *Iliad* 21.263–264; see [[gods-and-men-not-alike]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]).
