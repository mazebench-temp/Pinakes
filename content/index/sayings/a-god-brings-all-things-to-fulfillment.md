**The plea.** Agamemnon speaks from his seat, after Achilles has renounced his wrath (19.76–84; see [[listen-to-the-man-who-stands]]). He does not deny the act. He took the prize himself, αὐτός (19.89; see [[geras]]), but the cause lay outside him: Zeus and Fate and the Erinys cast ἄτη, “blind folly”, into his mind in the assembly (19.86–88; see [[ate]], [[moira]] and [[erinyes]]). The maxim makes the case general. θεός here is divine power, Walter Leaf notes, not Blind Folly herself, and διὰ … τελευτᾷ means “brings to their issue”; there were ancient variants, θεούς, and Hellanicus' θεόσδια, “god-given” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.90; see the translation's note on 19.90). The speech goes on to prove the maxim on the highest example: Blind Folly, the eldest daughter of Zeus, blinded Zeus himself on the day Heracles was to be born, and he hurled her from heaven to the works of men (19.91–133).

**Who is to blame.** The question runs through the poem (see [[who-is-to-blame]] and [[aitios]]). Priam told Helen, “it is the gods who are to blame” (3.164), and the narrator says from the first lines that “the plan of Zeus was being fulfilled” (1.5; see [[plan-of-zeus]]). In *Iliad* 19 the plea is taken up by others. Achilles answers it with his own: “Father Zeus, truly great are the blind follies you give to men”, and “Zeus, it seems, / wanted death to come upon many of the Achaeans” (19.270–274). Achilles' horse speaks it last: “nor are we / to blame, but a great god and mighty Fate” (19.409–410). In the Odyssey Zeus complains of the habit: mortals blame the gods for their evils, when by their own recklessness they suffer beyond their portion (*Odyssey* 1.32–34; see [[atasthalie]]). The plea does not excuse Agamemnon from paying. He goes on at once to offer the gifts (19.137–144; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). See also [[zeus-does-not-fulfill-all-plans]] and [[fulfilled-late]].
