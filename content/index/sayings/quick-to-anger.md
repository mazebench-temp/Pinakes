**The blame.** Alcinous has heard the stranger's story and finds one fault in his daughter: “this, at least, she did not devise rightly, / my child, in that she did not bring you with her handmaids / to our house; and it was to her first that you came as a suppliant” (*Odyssey* 7.299–301; see [[nausicaa]] and [[alcinous]]). The last clause gives the reason why she was wrong: a stranger was regarded as the suppliant, with all the rights of the word, of the one with whom he first established relations, as Arete will say of him, “he is my guest” (*Odyssey* 11.338, a plain rendering; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 7.301 and 7.53; see [[supplication]] and [[xenia]]).

**The answer.** Odysseus takes the fault on himself. She did bid him follow with her handmaids, but he would not, δείσας αἰσχυνόμενός τε, “out of fear and shame”, lest the king's heart too be indignant when he saw it (*Odyssey* 7.303–306). Merry notes the aorist and the present participle together, the first for the sudden fear that came over him, the second for the abiding condition of modesty, as the heralds come to Achilles “in fear and awe of the king” (ταρβήσαντε καὶ αἰδομένω, 1.331; Merry on *Odyssey* 7.305; see [[deos]], [[aischros]] and [[aidos]]). Part of this is true: Nausicaa told him to walk with the handmaids behind the wagon while they crossed the fields (*Odyssey* 6.259–261). But it was she who told him to wait in Athena's grove and come into the city alone, and her reason was the people's talk (*Odyssey* 6.262–299; see [[the-talk-of-the-people]]). Eustathius, as Merry reports him, characterizes the account “with charming simplicity”: “see how Odysseus lies openly; which the wise man would do at the right moment” (Merry on *Odyssey* 7.305; see [[odysseus]]).

**The maxim.** The last verse turns his fear into a rule for all mankind. δύσζηλος, found only here in Homer, is “quick to anger”, “touchy” (Merry on *Odyssey* 7.307; see the translation's table of key words). The masculine δύσζηλοι with the neuter φῦλʼ ἀνθρώπων, “the tribes of men”, agrees with the sense and not with the noun, as Nitzsch, whom Merry reports, shows from Hesiod's φῦλα γυναικῶν … οὐ σύμφοροι, the tribes of women who are no companions of poverty (*Theogony* 591–593), where there is a variant σύμφορα (Merry on *Odyssey* 7.307; see [[phylon]]). The king answers the maxim with another: his heart is not such as to be angry for no reason, and “what is due is better in all things” (*Odyssey* 7.309–310; see [[what-is-due-is-better]] and [[cholos]]).
