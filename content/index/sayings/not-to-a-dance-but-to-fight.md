**The answer to Hector.** The two leaders exhort their men in turn. Hector has told the Trojans that Zeus is with them and that whoever dies dies for his native land (15.486–499; see [[it-is-not-unseemly-to-die-for-ones-country]]). Ajax, “on the other side”, tells the Argives what that speech means for them (15.501). He makes them listen to the enemy: “Do you not hear him urging on his whole army, / Hector, who is eager to set fire to the ships?” (15.506–507). Hector's call is a call to fight, and the only answer is to meet it “hand to hand” (15.509–510; see [[better-to-perish-once-and-for-all]] and [[exhortation]]).

**Dance and war.** χορός is both the dance and the dancing floor (see [[dance]]), the place of festivals and of display, and the Iliad sets it against war. Aphrodite tells Helen that Paris, saved from Menelaus, looks as if he were going to a dance, or had just stopped dancing and sat down, not as if he had come from fighting a man (3.392–394; see [[paris]]). Menelaus, standing over Peisander, says that men would rather have their fill of sleep and love, “of sweet song and of flawless dancing”, than of war, but the Trojans are insatiable of battle (13.636–639; see [[satiety-of-all-things]]), and Polydamas counts dancing among the gifts a god gives one man and not another (13.731; see [[different-gifts]]). The words of the dance can also be turned into war. Hector told Ajax at their duel that he knew how, in close fighting, “to dance for destructive Ares” (μέλπεσθαι Ἄρηϊ, 7.241; see [[single-combat]]); Aeneas mocks Meriones, who dodged his spear, as a dancer (ὀρχηστήν περ ἐόντα, 16.617), and Priam calls the sons he has left “liars and dancers” (24.261).

**The point.** Ajax does not mock the Trojans as dancers. He warns his own men that the enemy is not playing. The litotes of the verse, “not to a dance”, keeps the image of the festival in view only to deny it, as Hector's οὔ οἱ ἀεικές, “it is not unseemly”, denied the shame of death (15.496; see [[litotes]]).
