**The rule on the shore.** The handmaids have fled at the sight of a naked man crusted with brine, and Nausicaa calls them back: “Stand still, my handmaids: where are you fleeing at the sight of a man?” (*Odyssey* 6.199). No enemy comes to the Phaeacians, she says, for they are very dear to the immortals and live far apart in the sea, the farthest of men (*Odyssey* 6.200–205; see [[phaeacians]] and [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]]). The man is a wanderer, and the maxim gives the reason why he must be cared for; the orders follow from it, food and drink and a bath in the river where there is shelter from the wind (*Odyssey* 6.209–210; see [[xenia]] and [[bathing]]). She has just told him himself that he will not lack clothing, “nor anything else / that is fitting for a much-tried suppliant who has come our way” (*Odyssey* 6.192–193; see [[supplication]]).

**From Zeus.** πρὸς Διός is literally “from Zeus”, sent by him, and so under his guidance and protection, as the judges of the Iliad guard “the ordinances that come from Zeus” (θέμιστας / πρὸς Διὸς εἰρύαται, *Iliad* 1.238–239; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.207; see [[themis]]). Merry sets beside it the libation the Phaeacians will pour to Zeus, who attends upon reverend suppliants (*Odyssey* 7.165). Zeus is ξείνιος, “god of guests”, whose harsh wrath, Menelaus says, the Trojans did not fear when they carried off his wedded wife after she had welcomed them (*Iliad* 13.623–627), and Odysseus will name him so to the Cyclops: Zeus is the avenger of suppliants and strangers, the god of guests, who attends upon reverend strangers (*Odyssey* 9.269–271, a plain rendering; see [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]] and [[zeus]]). The beggar stands beside the stranger: πτωχός, “beggar”, is found only in the Odyssey.

**Small but dear.** The second half of the verse names the gift. The scholium explains the pair of adjectives: small to the one who gives, dear to the one who receives, since want counts even a little dear (Merry on 6.208; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 6.208). Achilles used the same pair for his share of the spoils, which he takes back to his ships when he is worn out with fighting, “something small but dear” (ὀλίγον τε φίλον τε, *Iliad* 1.167; see [[geras]]). δόσις, “a giving”, is Nestor's word for the gift promised to the spy, “and he will have a fine gift” (*Iliad* 10.213; see [[doron]]). Merry compares the blind Oedipus at the opening of Sophocles' *Oedipus at Colonus*, who asks for little and is given less, and finds it enough (on 6.208, citing *Oedipus at Colonus* 5).

**At the swineherd's hut.** The verses come back when Odysseus, disguised as an old beggar, thanks Eumaeus for his welcome. The swineherd answers that it is not right for him to dishonor a stranger, not even if one worse than he should come, “for they are all from Zeus, strangers and beggars; and a gift is small but dear, ours”, since that is the way of slaves (*Odyssey* 14.56–59, a plain rendering). In Eumaeus' mouth the stranger is a beggar indeed, and the gift a slave's. In the hall the suitors will not keep the rule: Antinous throws a footstool at the beggar and strikes him on the right shoulder (*Odyssey* 17.462–463; see [[the-suitors]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod counts the man who does evil to a suppliant or a stranger among the wrongdoers with whom Zeus himself is angry, and on whom he lays a harsh requital in the end (*Works and Days* 327–334).
