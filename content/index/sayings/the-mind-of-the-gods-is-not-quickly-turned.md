**The quarrel.** Nestor tells how Zeus devised a grim homecoming for the Argives after the sack of Troy, “since in no way discerning or just / were they all”, and how many of them met an evil fate “through the deadly wrath” of Athena, who set strife between the two sons of Atreus (*Odyssey* 3.130–136; see [[the-departure-of-the-greeks]], [[menis]], [[eris]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]). Merry takes the wrath as aimed first at the Locrian Ajax for his outrage on Cassandra, and at the chiefs who left it unpunished (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.135). The brothers called the army to an assembly at sunset, against all order, when the men came heavy with wine; Menelaus bade them think of their homecoming, but Agamemnon wished to hold back the army and offer holy hecatombs, “so that he might heal the terrible anger of Athena” (*Odyssey* 3.137–145; see [[agamemnon]], [[menelaus]], [[hecatomb]] and [[cholos]]).

**The fool.** Nestor calls him νήπιος, the word for a man who acts without knowing what the gods have in store (see [[nepios]]). The verse is *Iliad* 20.466, where the one who will not be persuaded is Achilles, and Tros, son of Alastor, comes in vain to clasp his knees (see the translation's note on 3.146); Merry renders it “that she had no thought of complying” (on 3.146). In the Iliad the next line gives the reason in Achilles' temper, “for he was no sweet-tempered man, nor gentle-hearted” (*Iliad* 20.467); here the reason is a maxim about the gods. The brothers answered each other with harsh words, the army was divided in its counsel and spent the night pondering harsh things, “for Zeus was preparing a bane of evil for us”, and at dawn half of it sailed away and half stayed with Agamemnon (*Odyssey* 3.148–158; see [[nostos]] and [[the-returns-of-the-achaeans]]).

**Turned and not turned.** Phoenix argued to Achilles the other way: “even the gods themselves can be turned”, and men turn them aside with burnt offerings, vows, libation and the savor of the fat, “whenever someone oversteps and does wrong” (*Iliad* 9.497–501; see [[even-the-gods-can-be-turned]] and [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]]). Nestor does not say that the gods cannot be turned, only that they are not turned quickly, αἶψα. In the Iliad Athena herself refuses the Trojan women's robe and the twelve cows they promise: “So she spoke in prayer, but Pallas Athena shook her head” (*Iliad* 6.311; see [[athena]] and [[noos]]).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Athenian argues at length that the gods cannot be won over (παραιτητοί) by wrongdoers who bring them gifts, and that they are “wholly not to be won over” against justice (*Laws* 10.905d–907b); in the *Republic* the begging priests and seers who sell absolution call Phoenix's lines to witness for the opposite view (*Republic* 2.364d–e; see [[even-the-gods-can-be-turned]]).

The Suda quotes the two verses under παραιτησάμενος, "begging off", of a man who "gave back the gold, so as to beg off the god's anger with it", and reads the first without the negative, νήπιος, οὐδὲ τὸ ᾔδει, ὃ πείσεσθαι ἔμελλεν, so that πείσεσθαι comes from πάσχω, "suffer": "the fool, he did not even know what he was going to suffer" (Suda π 507).
