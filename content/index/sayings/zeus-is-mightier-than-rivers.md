**Over the river's grandson.** Asteropaeus, asked who he was, had answered with his lineage from the broad-flowing Axius, “who begot Pelegon, famed for his spear; and he, they say, / was my father” (21.157–160; see [[asteropaeus]], [[axius]] and [[pelegon]]). When he has killed him, Achilles stands on his chest and answers the lineage with his own (21.182–189; see [[genealogy-in-battle]]). “Lie there as you are. It is hard for you to strive with the children of the mighty son of Cronus, / even though you are sprung from a river” (21.184–185). Asteropaeus had his descent by report; Achilles sets “you say” against “I claim”: “You say that you are of the lineage of a broad-flowing river, / but I claim to be of the lineage of great Zeus” (21.186–187; φῆσθα, εὔχομαι; see [[euchomai]] and [[genee]]). His own line goes up in two verses, Peleus, Aeacus, Zeus: “The man who begot me is lord over many Myrmidons, / Peleus, son of Aeacus; and Aeacus was the son of Zeus” (21.188–189; see [[peleus]], [[aeacus]] and [[myrmidons]]). Then the maxim, which makes the fight a contest of ancestors: as Zeus is to rivers, so are the children of Zeus to a river's children.

**The words.** παισίν, “the children” (21.185), is plural, Walter Leaf notes, because the statement is general (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.185). τῶ, “So”, may mean “therefore”, or, written as a relative, “by so much”, with a second clause answering it, “by so much is the lineage of Zeus mightier”; and ποταμοῖο, “a river's”, is said for a river's offspring (on 21.190, 21.191; see the translation's note on 21.184–191). ἁλιμυρήεις, “that murmur to the sea”, is found again only in the Odyssey (see [[murmuring-to-the-sea]]), of the river at whose mouth Odysseus, come ashore, gives the goddess's veil back to the stream (on 21.190; *Odyssey* 5.459–460).

**There is no fighting with Zeus.** Achilles turns the maxim on the river beside them and on all waters:

{{quote:926be7aa-29dc-5ebb-ad73-c9d63a16740f}}

The great river “here beside you” would seem to be the Scamander, at whose bank they fight, though Leaf notes that the context points rather to the Axius (on 21.192; see [[scamander]]). Achelous, the great river of Greece, is named only here in Homer, and Oceanus is the source of all rivers, of the whole sea and of all springs (see [[achelous]], [[oceanus]] and [[rivers]]). Yet even Oceanus fears the thunderbolt and the thunder of Zeus (see [[thunderbolt]] and [[zeus]]).

**The river answers.** The boast is said in the river's hearing, and the river at once takes up the fight. In the likeness of a man the Scamander calls to Achilles from an eddy to stop the slaughter, then rises against him, and Achilles, about to drown, cries to Zeus that no god will save him from a river (21.211–283; see [[achilles-and-the-river]]). The river is no stranger to Zeus' lineage: he is “the eddying Xanthus, whom immortal Zeus begot” (21.2 = 14.434), and his wave is the wave “of the river fallen from Zeus” (21.268; see [[fallen-from-zeus]]). He is beaten not by Zeus but by the fire of Hephaestus, sent by Hera (21.328–382; see [[hephaestus]]).

**Lineage and might.** Other men of the poem claim Zeus as their ancestor in battle. Idomeneus, over the body of Alcathous, tells Deiphobus to see “what kind of offspring of Zeus” he is: Zeus fathered Minos, Minos Deucalion, and Deucalion him (13.449–452; see [[idomeneus]]). Tlepolemus tells Sarpedon, the son of Zeus, that men lie who call him so, so far is he below those who were born of Zeus in former times (5.633–637; see [[tlepolemus]] and [[sarpedon]]). Aeneas, after his own lineage from Zeus, says that it is Zeus who gives and takes away the prowess of men (20.241–243; see [[zeus-increases-and-diminishes]]). Achilles' maxim is the boldest of these claims: the ancestor decides the fight.
