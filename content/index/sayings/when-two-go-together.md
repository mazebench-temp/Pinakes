{{quote:bbb8fa8d-b569-5d7b-8d4f-fd125756025f}}

Nestor has asked whether any man will trust his daring heart and go among the Trojans, and has been met with silence (10.204–218; see [[silence]]). Diomedes breaks it. His heart and his proud spirit urge him to go, “but if some other man would go with me, there would be more comfort, and more boldness” (10.220–223; see [[tharsos]]). The maxim gives the reason. σύν … ἐρχομένω is a dual, “two going together”, and πρὸ ὃ τοῦ is “the one before the other”: of two men, one sees first. What he sees is κέρδος, the advantage, the way that profits (see [[kerdos]]), and the verb is νοέω, to notice and understand at once (see [[noos]]). The second half turns to the man alone, and its syntax breaks off as if the speaker changed his mind in the middle: “but one man alone, even if he sees it, his mind is slower, and his counsel thin” (see the translation's note on 10.224–226). βράσσων, “slower”, a comparative of βραδύς, is found only here. The last half-verse, λεπτὴ δέ τε μῆτις, recurs once, when Antilochus asks Menelaus to bear with him after the chariot race: a young man's “mind is quicker, but his counsel thin” (23.587–590; see [[metis]]). The man alone and the young man are short of the same thing.

The maxim decides the choice that follows. Many are willing to go (10.227–232), and Agamemnon tells Diomedes to take the best man, not the most kingly (10.234–239). Diomedes chooses Odysseus, “since he is wise beyond all others” (ἐπεὶ περίοιδε νοῆσαι, 10.247), the one who will see first. The night bears him out. Odysseus sees Dolon coming and plans the ambush, sees Rhesus first and points him out, and drives off the horses while Diomedes kills (10.339–348, 10.476–481, 10.498–502). The two go out together and come back together (see [[odysseus-and-diomedes]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]).

## Outside Homer

The verse was much quoted. In Plato's *Protagoras* Socrates gives it as the reason for questioning Protagoras: Homer was right, “for all of us human beings are somehow better provided for every deed and word and thought”, and a man who sees something alone goes round at once looking for someone to show it to (*Protagoras* 348c–d). In the *Symposium* he turns it into a pun. Taking Aristodemus uninvited to Agathon's dinner, he says that “two going together” will plan “on the way” (πρὸ ὁδοῦ, for Homer's πρὸ ὃ τοῦ) what to say; then he falls behind, lost in thought, and sends his companion on ahead (*Symposium* 174d; see [[plato-menelaus-uninvited]]). The *Second Alcibiades*, handed down under Plato's name, has “two of us considering together” (σύν τε δύο σκεπτομένω, 140a; see [[plato]]).

Aristotle quotes it twice. Friends, he writes, help those in their prime to noble actions, “when two go together”, for they are more able both to think and to act (*Nicomachean Ethics* 8.1, 1155a15). In the *Politics*, against the rule of one man, he argues that two good men are better than one: “this is the 'when two go together', and Agamemnon's prayer, 'would that I had ten such counselors'” (*Politics* 3.16, 1287b13–15; Iliad 2.372; see [[aristotle]]).

Cicero, writing to Atticus in 49 BCE after Pompey had left Italy, wished that his friend had been well enough to be with him: “we would have been together; counsel at least would not have been lacking; σύν τε δύʼ ἐρχομένω” (*Letters to Atticus* 9.6.6). Earlier in the same letter he had described his own distress in Agamemnon's words from the same book, “my heart is not steady, but I am distraught” (10.93–94; *Letters to Atticus* 9.6.4; see [[cicero]]).
