Nestor has a second plan in case Patroclus cannot persuade Achilles to fight: if Achilles is shunning some oracle, let him send Patroclus out with the Myrmidons, and give him his armor (11.794–800; see [[theopropion]] and [[the-persuasion-of-a-friend]]). The likeness is meant to win the Achaeans a pause. ἀνάπνευσις, “a drawing of breath”, occurs only in this verse, which the Iliad uses three times; it is the noun of ἀναπνέω, the verb of the line before (ἀναπνεύσωσι, 11.800), which the battle books use of men who get their breath back. When Odysseus and Diomedes turned on the Trojans, the Achaeans “were glad to take breath as they fled from brilliant Hector” (ἀνέπνεον, 11.327), and Paris, having shot Diomedes, wishes he had killed him: “Then the Trojans too would have had a breathing space from misery” (11.382).

The maxim can be read in two ways (see the translation's note on 11.796–803). Taken as rendered, a breathing space in war is short, and the Achaeans can hope for no more than a pause. Taken the other way, ὀλίγη, “little”, makes it a reason to seize even a short one: even a little breathing space counts for much in war. Either way the old man asks for no victory, only time.

**Repeated.** Patroclus puts the plan to Achilles in Book 16 nearly word for word, with himself in the place of “you” (16.36–45; see [[the-death-of-patroclus]]). Achilles grants it, and the verse comes true in both its senses. When the Trojans see Patroclus in the armor they think Achilles has cast off his wrath, and each man looks for a way to escape; the Danaans drive the fire from the ships and τυτθὸν ἀνέπνευσαν, “drew breath for a little”, but πολέμου δʼ οὐ γίγνετʼ ἐρωή, “there was no rest from war” (16.278–302). The third time the verse is a goddess's. After Patroclus is dead and the armor lost, Iris tells Achilles to go to the trench as he is, without armor, and show himself, so that the Trojans, afraid of him, may hold off and the Achaeans draw breath, and she ends with the same verse (18.197–201). What Nestor hoped to win with Achilles' armor on another man, Achilles wins by showing himself. See [[patroclus]] and [[achilles]].
