**The king who did no wrong.** Medon has overheard the suitors' plan, and Penelope meets him at the threshold: have the suitors sent him to tell the slave women to prepare their feast? May this be their last meal (*Odyssey* 4.677–685; see [[medon-the-herald]] and [[the-suitors]]). They waste the substance of Telemachus, and never heard from their fathers what Odysseus was among them: he did nothing excessive to anyone and said nothing excessive among the people, “though that is the way of divine kings: / one man among mortals they hate, another they love” (*Odyssey* 4.686–692; see [[kingship-and-authority]] and [[themis]]). Merry renders the clause “as is indeed the common way with kings”, and puts the weight on the hatred: the ordinary king, while he may befriend one man, is pretty sure to spite another (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.690). ἀτάσθαλον, “reckless”, is to him “cruel”, parallel to the ἐξαίσιόν τι of *Odyssey* 4.690 (on 4.693; see [[atasthalie]]). Against such a king stand “your spirit and your shameful deeds”, and the maxim draws the conclusion. εὐεργέων is the neuter genitive plural of εὐεργής, “good deeds”, as again in *Odyssey* 22.319 (Merry on 4.695), and χάρις is the thanks that a service earns (see [[charis]]).

**The same complaint.** Mentor told the Ithacan assembly that no king should be gentle and kind any longer, “since no one remembers divine Odysseus / among the people he was lord over, and he was kind, like a father” (*Odyssey* 2.233–234), and Achilles told the envoys of Agamemnon that “there was, it seems, no thanks / for fighting against the enemy without rest, forever” (*Iliad* 9.316–317; Glaucus says it to Hector, 17.147–148). In *Odyssey* 22 the suitors' soothsayer Leodes clasps Odysseus' knees: he never said or did anything reckless to the women in the halls, and tried to stop the others; yet he will lie dead among them, since there is no thanks afterward for good deeds, in the verse of Penelope's maxim (*Odyssey* 22.310–319). Odysseus kills him (*Odyssey* 22.320–329).
