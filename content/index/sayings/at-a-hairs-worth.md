**The verdict.** Achilles has just said that Zeus has taken away Agamemnon's wits (9.377). The next line answers the whole offer in two clauses. The first takes up Odysseus' own words: if the son of Atreus is hateful to you, “himself and his gifts”, pity the rest (9.300–301). Yes, Achilles says, ἐχθρὰ δέ μοι τοῦ δῶρα, “his gifts are hateful to me” (see [[doron]]). The second turns the language of the offer against its maker. The embassy has promised that the seven cities and the army will honor Achilles “like a god”; τίω, “honor”, is the verb of those promises, and Achilles uses it to set Agamemnon's worth at almost nothing (see [[time]]). αἶσα is a portion or measure (see [[aisa]]), and ἐν καρὸς αἴσῃ is “at the measure of a κάρ”.

**κάρ.** The word occurs nowhere else in Homer. It has been explained from κείρω, “cut, shear”, as a hair or a shaving, something too small to cut, and the translation renders it so (see the translation's note on 9.378). Ancient readers also took it as Κάρ, “a Carian”: in the classical period Carians were known as hired soldiers, and Plato's Socrates twice speaks of running a risk “on a Carian”, on a man whose loss would cost little (*Laches* 187b; *Euthydemus* 285c). Either way the phrase is an insult of measure, and it comes from the man who complains that the brave and the coward are held in one honor (9.319).

What follows is the measure turned the other way: not ten or twenty times as much, not the wealth of Orchomenus or of Thebes in Egypt, not gifts as many as the sand and the dust would persuade him (9.379–387; see [[the-sand-and-the-dust]]). The king who is worth a hair could not pay enough.

## Outside Homer

Sophocles' Ajax, before his death, calls on the proverb that “the gifts of enemies are no gifts, and bring no profit” (ἐχθρῶν ἄδωρα δῶρα κοὐκ ὀνήσιμα, *Ajax* 664–665), of the sword that Hector gave him (see [[sophocles-sword-and-belt]]).
