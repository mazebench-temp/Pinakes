**A trainer's eye.** When the young men have competed, Laodamas proposes to ask the stranger whether he knows some contest, and looks him over as a trainer would: thighs and shins, “both his hands above”, χεῖρες ὕπερθεν, a fixed description of the arms as the upper limbs (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.135), the massive neck and the great strength; he lacks nothing of youth, “but he has been broken by many evils” (*Odyssey* 8.133–137; see [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]] and [[the-games-of-the-phaeacians]]). The maxim gives the reason. συγχεῦαι, “to unstring”, “weaken”, is a word generally used of the mind, as Achilles tells Phoenix, “do not confuse my heart with weeping and grieving” (*Iliad* 9.612), and here of the body (Merry on 8.139; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 8.139). The sea breaks the strong man as well as the weak: εἰ καὶ μάλα καρτερὸς εἴη, “however strong he may be”.

**What the sea did.** The poem has shown it. When Odysseus reached the river's mouth on Scheria after two days and nights in the water, he let his knees sink and his strong hands, “for his dear heart was overcome by the sea” (*Odyssey* 5.453–457; see [[the-river-of-scheria]]). In Book 8 he says it himself. He has endured “the wars of men and the grievous waves” (*Odyssey* 8.182–183), and he fears that someone may outrun him, “for I have been too shamefully overcome / among many waves”, ἐδαμάσθην, the verb of Book 5 (*Odyssey* 8.230–233; see [[sea]]). Laodamas means the maxim kindly, and ends his invitation with the hope that the stranger may scatter the sorrows from his heart (*Odyssey* 8.149; see [[no-greater-fame]]).

**Nothing worse.** The turn of the phrase, “nothing else worse”, is Achilles' over Patroclus, “for I could suffer nothing worse” (οὐ μὲν γάρ τι κακώτερον ἄλλο πάθοιμι, *Iliad* 19.321), and the beggar's to Eumaeus, that for mortals there is nothing worse than wandering (πλαγκτοσύνης δʼ οὐκ ἔστι κακώτερον ἄλλο βροτοῖσιν, *Odyssey* 15.343, a plain rendering). The man the Phaeacians look at has known both the sea and the wandering.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod does not praise spring sailing, “for it does not please my heart”; men sail through ignorance, since wealth is life to wretched mortals, “and it is a terrible thing to die among the waves” (δεινὸν δʼ ἐστὶ θανεῖν μετὰ κύμασιν, *Works and Days* 682–687, a plain rendering; see [[hesiod]]). Livius Andronicus' Latin *Odyssey* kept the verses: *namque nullum peius macerat humanum quamde mare saevum; vires cui sunt magnae topper confringent importunae undae*, “for nothing wears a man down worse than the savage sea; one whose strength is great, the cruel waves will quickly break” (Livius Andronicus, *Odussia*, the fragment of *Odyssey* 8.138–139, as The Latin Library prints it; a plain rendering). Merry notes that he renders συγχεῦαι by *macerare*, “wear down” (on 8.139).
