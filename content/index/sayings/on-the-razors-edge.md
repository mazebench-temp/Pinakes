Nestor has woken Diomedes with his heel, and Diomedes has protested: the old man never rests, and younger men could do the waking (10.164–167). Nestor grants that all he has said is “fair and right” (κατὰ μοῖραν, 10.169; see [[moira]]); he has sons and people who could go round. “But a very great need has overpowered the Achaeans” (10.172), and the image gives its measure. ξυρόν, the razor, and ἀκμή, its edge, are both found only in this verse in Homer. The verb ἵσταται, “stands”, gives the army's fate a footing as narrow as a blade, and the next verse names the two ways it can fall: “whether grim destruction comes on the Achaeans, or life” (ἢ … ἠὲ βιῶναι). Ajax puts the same choice at the same line-end when the Trojans are at the ships: “better once and for all to die or to live” (ἢ ἀπολέσθαι ἕνα χρόνον ἠὲ βιῶναι, 15.511).

The thought is the one Nestor spoke earlier the same night, “this night will either shatter the army or save it” (9.78; see [[this-night-will-shatter-or-save]]), and that Odysseus put to Achilles, “it is in doubt whether we save or lose the well-benched ships” (9.230–231). What was said in the assembly and in Achilles' hut is now said to one man just woken from sleep, as the reason for waking him. Nestor ends the speech with an old man's request, “for you are younger, if you have pity on me” (10.176; see [[pity]]), and Diomedes puts on his lion skin and goes (10.177–179). The night that follows is the night of the raid (see [[war-and-night]] and [[the-doloneia]]).

## Outside Homer

The phrase became proverbial. In Herodotus, Dionysius of Phocaea tells the Ionians gathered at Lade, before their sea battle with the Persians, “our affairs stand on the razor's edge, men of Ionia, whether to be free or slaves, and slaves as runaways” (ἐπὶ ξυροῦ γὰρ ἀκμῆς ἔχεται ἡμῖν τὰ πρήγματα, *Histories* 6.11.2). He keeps Nestor's two outcomes as well as his image. The Ionians obeyed his drills for seven days, then gave up the toil, and the battle was lost (*Histories* 6.12–15; see [[herodotus]]).

The tragedians use the image of a blade as well as of a danger. In Aeschylus' *Libation Bearers* a servant says that Clytemnestra's neck, it seems, is now “near the razor”, about to fall under the stroke of justice (*Libation Bearers* 883–884); in Euripides' *Heracles* the hero, back from Hades, asks his children, “had you come so near the razor's edge?” (*Heracles* 630). The Suda glosses ἐπὶ ξυροῦ as “in danger, at the very height of the matter”, quotes ἐπὶ ξυροῦ ἀκμῆς as “the saying”, and explains the edge as “the sharpest point”, from the fineness of iron blades (Suda ε 2498, ξ 162).
