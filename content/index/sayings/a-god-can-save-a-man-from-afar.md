**Not even if the gods so willed.** Nestor has wished that Athena might love Telemachus as she cared for Odysseus at Troy, “for never yet have I seen the gods show love so openly” (*Odyssey* 3.218–224; see [[athena]] and [[philos]]). Telemachus cannot believe it: “Not for me, / for all my hoping, would these things happen, not even if the gods so willed” (*Odyssey* 3.227–228). The goddess answers with the formula of rebuke, “what a word has escaped the barrier of your teeth!” (*Odyssey* 3.230; see [[what-a-word]]), and with a god who does will: θεός γʼ ἐθέλων.

**From far away.** Merry reports Nitzsch's argument that τηλόθεν cannot go with θεός, as if a god could help from a distance, in the sense of Aeschylus' “a god hears even from afar” (*Eumenides* 297) or of Glaucus' prayer to Apollo, “you can hear from everywhere / a man in sorrow” (16.515–516): the question is not of hearing but of helping, the Homeric gods do not help without being present, and Telemachus' doubt is whether a god could bring his father home after so long an absence from some unknown place. The word goes with ἄνδρα, a man far from home, or, as Nitzsch preferred, with σαώσαι, “can bring a man safe home even from a great distance” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.231).

**Rather late than at the hearth.** Athena adds what she herself would choose: to toil through many sorrows and then see the day of homecoming, rather than come home and perish at the hearth, “as Agamemnon / perished by the guile of Aegisthus and of his own wife” (*Odyssey* 3.232–235; see [[nostos]], [[day-of-homecoming]] and [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). Merry puts the stress on ἄλγεα πολλὰ μογήσας: better to go through one's portion of misery before reaching home than to get home at once and be murdered there (on 3.232). It is the course the poem gives Odysseus, who comes home in the twentieth year, after many sorrows, and does not perish at his hearth. Then she names the limit: “But death, which is common to all, not even the gods / can ward off from a man they love” (*Odyssey* 3.236–237; see [[not-even-the-gods-can-ward-off-death]]).

**As a god can.** The Iliad's gods save men with the same ease, ῥεῖα μάλʼ ὥς τε θεός, “very easily, as a god can”: Aphrodite snatches Paris from Menelaus (3.380–382), and Apollo snatches Hector from Achilles (20.443–444; see [[divine-intervention]]). Athena's ῥεῖα claims that ease for a rescue across the sea.
