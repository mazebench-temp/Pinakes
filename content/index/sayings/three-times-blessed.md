**In the storm.** Poseidon, coming back from the Ethiopians, sees the raft from the mountains of the Solymi, gathers the clouds and rouses the four winds (*Odyssey* 5.282–296; see [[poseidon]]). Odysseus' knees and heart are loosened, and he speaks to his own heart (*Odyssey* 5.297–298; see [[loosened-his-limbs]]). He fears that the goddess spoke the truth, that on the sea he would fill up the measure of sorrows before he reached home (*Odyssey* 5.300–302, from Calypso's words, *Odyssey* 5.206–207; see [[calypso]]); he lays the storm on Zeus, as men commonly do, not knowing it is Poseidon's work (*Odyssey* 5.303–304; the scholia, in Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.304; see [[who-is-to-blame]]); and “Now sheer destruction is sure for me” (*Odyssey* 5.305), where Merry hears irony in σῶς, as one says “safe to be destroyed” (on 5.305). Then comes the cry. τρὶς μάκαρες is a felicitation raised by counting, “three times blessed”, and raised again, “and four times”.

**A death at Troy.** The Danaans who perished “in wide Troy” are blessed because they died in war, “doing a favor to the sons of Atreus” (*Odyssey* 5.306–307; see [[danaans]], [[atreidae]] and [[charis]]), the words with which Achilles tells Phoenix not to trouble his heart “to do a favor to the hero son of Atreus” (*Iliad* 9.612–613). Odysseus would have died with them on the day the Trojans hurled their spears at him around the dead son of Peleus (*Odyssey* 5.308–310). Merry describes the scene as Ajax carrying off the body while Odysseus kept the Trojans at bay, and refers to the shade of Agamemnon's account in the underworld (on 5.309; *Odyssey* 24.36–42; see [[the-fight-over-the-body-of-achilles]] and [[achilles]]). What such a death would have given him is said in a verse: τῷ κʼ ἔλαχον κτερέων, καί μευ κλέος ἦγον Ἀχαιοί, “Then I would have had my funeral rites, and the Achaeans would have spread my fame” (*Odyssey* 5.311; “or brought back the report of me”, Merry on 5.311; see [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[kleos]]). A man who drowns has neither.

**The same reckoning.** Telemachus makes it for his father in *Odyssey* 1: if he had been brought down among his companions at Troy, the Achaeans would have made him a tomb, and he would have won great fame for his son, “But now the Harpies have snatched him away without report” (*Odyssey* 1.236–241; Eumaeus says it in nearly the same verses, *Odyssey* 14.367–371; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). The shade of Achilles says it to the shade of Agamemnon, who died at home: if he had met his death at Troy, the Achaeans would have made him a tomb, but now it was fated that he be caught by a most pitiful death (*Odyssey* 24.30–34). Odysseus' last verse is Achilles' own in the Iliad, when the Scamander has him: he wishes that Hector had killed him, “then a brave man would have been the killer, and he would have slain a brave man. / But now it was fated that I be caught by a wretched death, / trapped in a great river, like a swineherd boy” (*Iliad* 21.279–282; *Odyssey* 5.312 = *Iliad* 21.281; see [[achilles-and-the-river]] and [[the-swineherd-boy]]). Both men, held by the water, set a death in battle above the death they are about to die. εἵμαρτο is “had been fated”, Merry notes, even before this voyage began (on 5.312; see [[moira]]).

**Blessed.** The felicitation comes back twice. Odysseus' first speech to Nausicaa calls her father and mother “thrice blessed”, and her brothers, in such a daughter, and her husband-to-be most blessed of all (*Odyssey* 6.154–159). In the underworld the shade of Agamemnon calls Achilles ὄλβιε, “blessed”, because he died in Troy far from Argos, with the best of the Trojans and the Achaeans killed around him in the fight for his body; he was washed and wept for, and “did not lose his name even in death” (*Odyssey* 24.36–46, 24.93–94). Odysseus will not die at sea: the wave breaks on him as he speaks (*Odyssey* 5.313), and Ino comes with her veil (see [[leucothea]]).

## Outside Homer

Virgil gives Aeneas the cry in the first storm of the *Aeneid*. His limbs are loosened with cold (*solvuntur frigore membra*), as Odysseus' knees and heart were loosened, and stretching his hands to the stars he cries *O terque quaterque beati*, “O three and four times blessed”, those whose lot it was to die before their fathers' eyes beneath the high walls of Troy, and wishes that he could have fallen on the plains of Ilium by the hand of Diomedes, bravest of the Danaans (*Aeneid* 1.92–98; see [[virgils-storm]] and [[virgils-diomedes]]). Merry compares κατʼ ἄκρης, the wave that strikes Odysseus “from above” (*Odyssey* 5.313), with Virgil's *ingens a vertice pontus in puppim ferit* in the same storm (on 5.313, citing *Aeneid* 1.114).
