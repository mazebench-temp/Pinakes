Agamemnon ends his long rebuke of Diomedes, the story of Tydeus at Thebes, with a judgment of father and son:

{{quote:724d0ccc-7e1b-5263-a605-e7685a4c8303}}

Diomedes says nothing. His companion Sthenelus, whose father Capaneus also died at Thebes, answers for both of them:

{{quote:962da432-0690-53a3-9676-5c0e0b672ce4}}

The sons of the Seven, the Epigonoi, took the city the fathers could not, with a smaller army against a stronger wall, trusting in the portents of the gods and the help of Zeus; the fathers “perished through their own recklessness” (see [[atasthalie]] and [[the-epigonoi]]). Sthenelus uses εὐχόμεθα, “we claim, we boast” (see [[euchomai]]), and he makes the claim at the fathers' expense.

Diomedes does not endorse it. He tells Sthenelus to sit in silence, since the king has the right to urge the army on, and answers the rebuke by going to fight (4.411–421). In the next book Athena tells him the same thing Agamemnon did, “Tydeus has begotten a son little like himself” (5.800), and in Book 14 he claims his father's name with pride, “the son of a good father, Tydeus, whom the heaped earth covers at Thebes” (14.113–114). The Iliad's usual verdict runs the other way from Sthenelus'. Athena, in the likeness of Mentor, tells Telemachus that “few sons are like their fathers; most are worse, and few are better” (*Odyssey* 2.276–277). Only a father can wish it otherwise: Hector prays that someone may say of his son, “he is far better than his father” (6.479). See [[fathers-and-sons]], [[sthenelus]], [[diomedes]] and [[tydeus]].
