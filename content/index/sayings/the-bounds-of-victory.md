Hector has challenged the best of the Achaeans, and they sit in shamed silence (7.92–93; see [[aidos]]). Menelaus stands up, rebukes them as “Achaean women” and curses them (7.96–100; see [[achaean-women-no-longer-men]] and [[water-and-earth]]), and ends with these two lines before he puts on his armor (7.103). The maxim is his reason for daring. The narrator says at once that Hector “was far the mightier” (7.105), and Agamemnon holds his brother back (7.104–122; see [[menelaus]]). If the outcome lies with the gods, a weaker man may still fight, and the man for whose sake the war is fought is the one who offers to.

**The bounds.** πείρατα (from πεῖραρ, related to πέρας, “end”) are ends or limits, and so the outcome of a thing. The word also means the ends of a rope: in the Odyssey πείρατα are the ropes that bind Odysseus to the mast (*Odyssey* 12.51, 12.162, 12.179), and in the Iliad Zeus and Poseidon knot together and stretch over both armies “the rope (πεῖραρ) of mighty strife and of war common to all, unbreakable and not to be loosed, which loosened the knees of many” (13.358–360). Some take the bounds of victory as its ropes, held above by the gods; R. B. Onians argued that πείρατα in such phrases are bonds (*The Origins of European Thought*, 1951). The same book gives the other πείρατα of war: Diomedes says that “the bounds of destruction are already made fast upon the Trojans” (ὀλέθρου πείρατʼ ἐφῆπται, 7.402; see [[even-a-fool-can-see]]), a phrase Polydamas uses of the Achaeans (12.79) and the Odyssey of the suitors (*Odyssey* 22.33, 22.41), and Diomedes and Achilles threaten a man with reaching “the bounds of destruction” (6.143, 20.429; see [[the-sons-of-unhappy-men]]).

**The gods decide.** The thought is common in the poem. Hector ends the duel “until a god decides between us and gives victory to one side or the other” (7.291–292; see [[yield-to-night]]). Automedon and Hector say, in a formula the Odyssey also knows, that “these things lie on the knees of the gods” (θεῶν ἐν γούνασι κεῖται, 17.514, 20.435; *Odyssey* 1.267, 1.400, 16.129), and Zeus weighs the fates of the two armies and of Hector and Achilles in his golden scales (8.69–74, 22.209–213). Paris gives the thought a turn of his own, “victory shifts from man to man” (6.339; see [[victory-shifts]]). See [[nike]] and [[divine-intervention]].
