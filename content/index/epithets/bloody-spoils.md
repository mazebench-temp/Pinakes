## Meaning

ἔναρα (“spoils,” the arms taken from a man killed in battle) + βροτόεις (“bloody,” from βρότος, “gore, clotted blood”). The noun gives the verbs ἐναρίζω and ἐξεναρίζω, “strip, slay and strip,” which run through the killings at the start of Book 6 (6.20, 6.30, 6.36; [[stripping-the-dead]]). βροτόεις is found only in the Iliad, and always of spoils: eight times in this formula, and once of ἀνδράγρια (“spoils taken from men”), when the poet asks the Muses which of the Achaeans was the first to win bloody spoils (Il. 14.508–510).

## Use

Hector ends his prayer for his son with the triumph of a warrior's return:

{{quote:203de985-14df-5cd6-b168-e655a58743e4}}

The spoils are the sign that the son has killed his man, and the mother's joy is their reward ([[fathers-and-sons]], [[better-than-our-fathers]]). The formula follows Hector through the poem. He boasts that he will kill Diomedes and carry off his bloody spoils (Il. 8.534), and orders the Trojans to leave the bloody spoils and rush the ships (Il. 15.347). In Book 22 Athena, in the likeness of Deiphobus, urges him to stand and fight, “to see whether Achilles will kill us both and carry off the bloody spoils to the hollow ships” (Il. 22.243–246). The spoils Hector wishes for his son are, in the end, his own ([[hector]]).
