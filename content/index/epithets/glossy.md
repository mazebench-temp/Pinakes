## Meaning

σιγαλόεις means “glossy, shining”; its origin is uncertain. In the Iliad it describes reins, in the line-end formula ἡνία σιγαλόεντα (5.226, 5.328; Il. 8.116, 8.137, 11.128, 17.479), and fine cloth: the glossy garments that the Trojan women once washed at the springs (Il. 22.153–155), and the bright headdress that Andromache throws from her head when she sees Hector dragged (Il. 22.468). In the Odyssey it is used of rugs and coverings, of Calypso's shining chair, and of the upper rooms of Odysseus' house (for example Od. 5.86, 16.449). Reins were of leather, and they could be decorated: Mydon's are “white with ivory” (5.583).

## Use

The reins pass from hand to hand in Book 5. Aeneas offers them to Pandarus, “But come now, take the whip and the glossy reins” (5.226), and Pandarus refuses, since the horses will run better under the driver they know (5.230–234; [[chariot]]). Later Sthenelus, having taken Aeneas' horses, “mounted his own chariot and took the glossy reins” (5.328; [[strong-hoofed]]). In Book 8 Nestor takes the glossy reins in Diomedes' chariot, and they escape his hands as Zeus' thunderbolt strikes before the horses (Il. 8.116, 8.137).
