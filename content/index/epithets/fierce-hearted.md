## Meaning

κρατερόφρων = κρατερός (“strong, mighty”; [[krateros]]) + φρήν (“mind, heart”; [[phrenes]]): “strong of heart, fierce in spirit.” Homer uses it five times. In the Iliad it describes the beast that the sheepdogs hear coming through the woods (10.184) and Heracles, whom Alcmene bore to Zeus (14.323–324). In the Odyssey Menelaus uses it of the absent Odysseus, in whose bed the suitors want to lie, “weaklings as they are,” like a hind that lays her fawns in a lion's lair (Od. 4.333–340), and Telemachus repeats his words to Penelope (Od. 17.124–131); Leda bore Tyndareus two fierce-hearted sons, Castor and Polydeuces (Od. 11.298–300). Compare [[karterothymos]] (καρτερόθυμος) and [[stout-hearted]].

## Use

{{quote:014b4795-e0fe-5b19-afde-91b73482c078}}

The sentries at the ditch keep a sleepless watch, like dogs round the sheep in a fold that hear a fierce-hearted beast coming down through the woods and over the mountains ([[the-watchdogs]], [[dogs]], [[sheep]], [[night-watch]]). The beast is not named. The din of men and dogs rises against it, and sleep is lost to them, as it is to the sentries, who face toward the plain listening for the Trojans. The dangerous animal of the simile is the enemy whom the watch cannot see; the next beasts in the book are the two Achaeans themselves, who set out “like two lions through the black night” (10.297; [[two-lions]], [[black-night]]).
