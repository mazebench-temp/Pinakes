## Meaning

δῖος goes back to *diw-yo-*, “of the bright sky,” from the same root as Ζεύς (genitive Διός) and related to Latin *dīvus* (“divine”). From “heavenly” it came to mean “bright, excellent, noble.”

It is one of the most general epithets of praise. Goddesses are δῖα θεάων (“brilliant among goddesses”), the sea is δῖα ([[shining-sea]], {{ref:Iliad 1.141}}), and in the Odyssey the swineherd Eumaeus is δῖος ὑφορβός. It says so little about any particular bearer that it can be given even to Clytemnestra, δῖα Κλυταιμνήστρη (Od. 3.266).

The form must be told apart from Διός, the genitive of Zeus, which differs from it only in accent. The concordance here matches δῖος with its accent for that reason.

## Use

Achilles enters the poem as δῖος Ἀχιλλεύς, set against Agamemnon in the last line of the proem:

{{quote:2bffc4f0-a5a0-5770-842d-f3576baeeade}}

The line balances the two men, each with his honor: the king by patronymic and rank ([[son-of-atreus]], [[lord-of-men]]), the hero by a personal epithet. δῖος Ἀχιλλεύς fills the last two feet of the verse, and it returns in the same place when Achilles answers Agamemnon ({{ref:Iliad 1.121}}, with [[podarkes]]) and when he breaks in on him ({{ref:Iliad 1.292}}).

At {{ref:Iliad 1.145}} Agamemnon names “brilliant Odysseus” among the men who might command the ship to Chryse. δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς is as common a formula for Odysseus as δῖος Ἀχιλλεύς is for Achilles; in the narrative of the voyage itself Odysseus is instead [[of-many-wiles]] (πολύμητις).
