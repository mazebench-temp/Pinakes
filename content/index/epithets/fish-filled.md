## Meaning

ἰχθυόεις = ἰχθύς (“fish”) + the suffix -όεις (“full of”): “full of fish.” It is an epithet of the open sea, πόντος, most often in the line-end formula πόντον ἐπʼ ἰχθυόεντα, “over the fish-filled sea,” in the Iliad once in a simile (19.378) and in the Odyssey of storm-driven voyages (Od. 4.516, 5.420, 9.83, 23.317), and in the question Menelaus asks the sea-nymph Eidothea and her father Proteus, and Odysseus asks Circe: how shall I go home “over the fish-filled sea” (Od. 4.381, 4.390, 4.424, 4.470, 10.540). The Iliad also gives it to the Hellespont (9.360) and to the river Hyllus in Lydia, near the lake where the Trojan ally Iphition was born (20.390–392); the Odyssey speaks of “the fish-filled paths” of the sea (Od. 3.177).

The fish are in the word, not on the heroes' tables. In Homer men fish with bent hooks only when hunger drives them, as Menelaus' companions do on Pharos and Odysseus' on the island of the Sun (Od. 4.368–369, 12.330–332). Patroclus' joke over the dead Cebriones turns on the same sea: a man who dives so nimbly from a chariot could feed many by diving for oysters “on the fish-filled sea” (16.745–748).

## Use

{{quote:63cd51ff-19a6-5536-9421-f310d712a415}}

Book 9 uses the epithet twice, at the beginning and at the turning point of the night. In the opening simile two winds, the North Wind and the West Wind, blow from Thrace and stir up the fish-filled sea, as fear tears the hearts of the Achaeans (9.4–8; [[two-winds-from-thrace]], [[boreas]], [[zephyrus]], [[thrace]]). In his answer to Odysseus, Achilles names the same water as his road home: if Odysseus cares to look, he will see “very early, sailing on the fish-filled Hellespont, my ships, and in them men eager to row” (9.359–361; [[hellespont]]). The sea that the storm troubles in the simile is the sea over which the best of the Achaeans threatens to leave.
