## Meaning

πολυκλήϊς = πολύς (“many”) + κληΐς (“bar, bolt”). On a ship the κληΐδες are the rowing positions: either the thole-pins to which the oars were tied or the benches where the rowers sat. The sense is disputed, but either way the compound means a ship with many oars. πολύζυγος = πολύς + ζυγόν (“yoke; crossbeam”), here the thwart on which a rower sits: “with many thwarts.” It occurs only once in Homer.

The translation renders both “many-benched.” The adjectives say what the other ship epithets of Book 2 do not: that the ships are driven by men at the oars. Compare [[well-benched]] (ἐΰσσελμος), and for the ships' look, [[black-ships]], [[hollow-ships]], [[curving]].

## Use

Each use in Book 2 comes in a speech about going home. Agamemnon tells the council his plan: he will test the army and “order them to flee with their many-benched ships” ({{ref:Iliad 2.74}}). Athena reproaches Odysseus with the result: “you will flee, tumbling into your many-benched ships” ({{ref:Iliad 2.175}}). Odysseus then excuses the army's longing:

{{quote:30559a09-8947-53a2-ab46-2365f413ce80}}

A man penned in by winter storms chafes beside his ship after a single month; the army has waited nine years. The many benches are the means of escape, and the rhetoric of Book 2 keeps the ships in view as the thing the army wants.
