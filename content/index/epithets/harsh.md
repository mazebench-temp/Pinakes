## Meaning

δυσ- (“ill, hard”) + the root of ἄλγος (“pain”): Walter Leaf refers the word, like τανηλεγής (8.70), to ἄλγος, with the ε developed as in ἀλεγεινός, “grievous” ([[grievous]]), and finds it the same in sense as δυσηχής, “grim-sounding” ([[grim-sounding]]) (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.154). The translation keeps the three apart (see its table of key words).

Homer has the word twice. In the Iliad it is war, when the gods of both sides sit apart on the wall of Heracles and on Callicolone: “So they sat on either side, devising / their plans; but to begin harsh war / both sides were loath, though Zeus, sitting on high, bade them” (20.153–155; [[polemos]]). In the Odyssey it is death, which the suitors' soothsayer Leodes, clasping Odysseus' knees, will not escape (*Odyssey* 22.310–325).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod uses it of the painful bonds from which the Hundred-Handers came back to the light (*Theogony* 650–653), and of the frosts that are harsh on the earth when the North Wind blows (*Works and Days* 504–506). In the Hymn to Apollo the god tells the dying serpent that neither Typhoeus nor the Chimaera will keep harsh death from her (*Homeric Hymn to Apollo* 367–368).
