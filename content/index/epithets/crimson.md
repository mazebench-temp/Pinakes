## Meaning

φοινικόεις is formed from φοῖνιξ, the costly red or purple-red dye, with which a woman stains ivory in a simile of Book 4 (4.141; [[ivory-stained-with-purple]], [[purple]]), and the suffix -όεις (“full of”): “crimson.” The same φοῖνιξ is the name of the Phoenicians, the traders of the Odyssey, and of the date palm (Od. 6.163). In Homer the adjective describes cloaks three times and the welts of wrestlers once: Nestor's cloak on the night of the raid (10.133); the red welts that rise “crimson with blood” on the sides and shoulders of Ajax and Odysseus as they wrestle (23.716–717); and in the Odyssey the crimson cloak that Thoas throws off to run to the ships in the beggar's tale of a night ambush under Troy (Od. 14.499–501), and the one that Telemachus takes off before he sets up the axes for the contest of the bow (Od. 21.118). Compare [[blood-red]] (δαφοινός) and [[red]].

## Use

{{quote:8a953499-8276-5e06-8207-313312dd9caf}}

Nestor dresses in nearly the verses with which Agamemnon dressed at the start of the book, tunic and sandals (10.21–22, 10.131–132; [[sleek]], [[dressing-scene]]), and where Agamemnon then took a lion's skin, Nestor fastens a crimson cloak with a brooch, “double and wide, with a thick woolly nap upon it” ([[double]], [[cloak]]). Agamemnon, Menelaus and Diomedes put on the skins of beasts; the old man goes out in a fine cloak, with his spear ([[strong-spear]], [[sharp]]). In Odysseus' night story in the Odyssey a crimson cloak is the thing a man leaves behind when he runs through the dark to the ships (Od. 14.499–502). See [[nestor]].
