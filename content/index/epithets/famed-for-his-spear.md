## Meaning

δουρί, dative of δόρυ (“spear”) + κλυτός (“famous,” literally “heard of,” from κλύω, “hear”): “famous with the spear.” κλυτός is from the same root as κλέος, fame as what is heard and told of a man ([[kleos]]). The spear is the chief weapon of the Iliad's heroes, and a man's reputation rests on it. Homer uses the phrase ten times, of Idomeneus, Tlepolemus, Meges, Diomedes, Odysseus and others. The compounds have the same English: δουρικλυτός, in the Iliad of Idomeneus, Odysseus, Automedon, Meriones and Achilles (5.45, 11.396, 16.472, 16.619, 21.233), and in the Odyssey only of Peiraeus, Telemachus' companion (*Odyssey* 15.544, 17.71); δουρικλειτός, above all of Menelaus at the end of the verse, δουρικλειτὸς Μενέλαος (5.55, 5.578, 10.230; *Odyssey* 15.52, 17.147). See [[menelaus]] and [[peiraeus-son-of-clytius]].

The phrase comes at the entry's opening and again at its close ({{ref:Iliad 2.650}}), and a few lines later the same half-line gives the command of the Rhodians to Tlepolemus ({{ref:Iliad 2.657}}). δουρὶ κλυτὸς ἡγεμόνευεν (“famed for his spear, led them”) is a ready-made unit of the Catalogue, into which any leader with a name of the right length can be set.

## Outside Homer

The Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* gives Menelaus the Odyssey's compound: Helen “bore Hermione to Menelaus, famed for his spear,” ἣ τέκεθʼ Ἑρμιόνην δουρικλειτῷ Μενελάῳ (fr. 70 Evelyn-White, a plain rendering; see [[hesiod]] and [[hermione-daughter-of-helen]]).
