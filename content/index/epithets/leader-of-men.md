## Meaning

ὄρχαμος (“leader, chief”) + ἀνδρῶν (“of men”). The origin of ὄρχαμος is uncertain; it has been connected with ἄρχω (“lead, begin”) but the forms do not fit easily. Unlike [[lord-of-men]] (ἄναξ ἀνδρῶν), it is not a royal title. In the Iliad it is used twice of Asius (Il. 2.837, 12.110) and once of Achilles (Il. 6.99). The Odyssey gives it to Nestor's son Pisistratus, to Odysseus' companion Polites, and most often to the swineherd Eumaeus and the cowherd Philoetius, who are “leaders” of the herdsmen. In address the title takes another form, ὄρχαμε λαῶν, “leader of the people,” which the Iliad gives to Agamemnon in Odysseus' rebuke (Il. 14.102), to Menelaus in Euphorbus' challenge (Il. 17.12), to the dead Patroclus in Briseis' lament (Il. 19.289) and to Achilles in the words of the river (Il. 21.221). In the Odyssey it closes the verse of address to Menelaus six times (Od. 4.156, 4.291, 4.316, 15.64, 15.87, 15.167), and Circe uses it once to Odysseus (Od. 10.538).

## Use

{{quote:de37ec23-05bd-545e-a892-0dbe5fdafe56}}

The Trojan Catalogue names Asius twice in two lines, the second time taking up the end of the first (Ἄσιος Ὑρτακίδης), and adds his great tawny horses. In Book 12 he refuses to leave his chariot and drives it at the Achaean wall, and the narrator foretells that he will not come back from the ships (Il. 12.110–115). The title in Book 2 prepares a leader whose pride in his horses brings him down.
