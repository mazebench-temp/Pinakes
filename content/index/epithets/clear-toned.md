## Meaning

λιγύς (feminine λίγεια) describes a sound that is clear, high and carrying. Homer uses it of the wind (λιγὺς οὖρος, Od. 3.176), of the orator whose voice is heard ([[clear-voiced-orator]], λιγὺς ἀγορητής), of the Muse (Μοῦσα λίγεια, “the clear-voiced Muse,” Od. 24.62), and in compounds of the heralds' voices and the whistling wind ([[clear-voiced]], [[shrill]]). Of an instrument it is the lyre's: φόρμιγξ λίγεια is the lyre of a singer, in the Iliad of Achilles (9.186) and of the boy who sings the Linus song among the grape-pickers on the shield (18.569–572), and in the Odyssey the lyre of Demodocus and Phemius (Od. 8.67, 8.105, 8.254, 8.261, 8.537, 22.332, 23.133). See [[lyre]].

## Use

{{quote:c34c5e0a-194e-5ca3-ae31-707e13b059eb}}

The envoys find Achilles “delighting his heart with a clear-toned lyre,” beautiful, finely wrought ([[intricately-worked]]), with a silver crossbar, taken from the spoils of Eetion's city ([[eetion]], [[sack-of-thebe]]), and singing the famous deeds of men ([[kleos]]). The epithet is the one the Odyssey gives the lyre of its professional singers. Out of the fighting, the best of the Achaeans plays the part of a bard, and his song is of the kind of fame that he will win only by going back to war.
