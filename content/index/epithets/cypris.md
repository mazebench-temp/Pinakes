## Meaning

Κύπρις is formed from Κύπρος, “Cyprus”: “the Cyprian,” the goddess of the island. In the Odyssey, after the song of Ares and Aphrodite, she goes to Cyprus, to Paphos, where she has a precinct and a fragrant altar (Od. 8.362–363). Unlike [[pallas]] and [[phoebus]], the name is never joined to the goddess's first name: Κύπρις stands in place of Ἀφροδίτη. It occurs in Homer only in Book 5 of the Iliad (5.330, 5.422, 5.458, 5.760, 5.883) and never in the Odyssey. Everywhere else the poems call her Aphrodite, with her epithets [[golden]], [[laughter-loving]] and [[daughter-of-zeus]].

## Use

{{quote:d730882e-0979-54dd-a999-05bb83d05903}}

The narrator gives her the name as Diomedes goes after her. The Cyprian is set apart from the goddesses who rule over war, Athena and Enyo ([[enyo]], [[sacker-of-cities]]), and the pitiless bronze reaches her hand ([[pitiless-bronze]], [[diomedes-wounds-aphrodite]]).

The other four uses are in the speech of gods, and none is spoken to her. Athena mocks her before Zeus: Cypris must have been urging some Achaean woman to follow the Trojans, and scratched her slender hand on a golden brooch (5.421–425; [[well-robed]]). Apollo tells Ares how Diomedes “came close to Cypris and wounded her hand at the wrist” (5.458), and Ares repeats the verse in his complaint to Zeus (5.883). Hera, asking Zeus for leave to strike Ares, says that “Cypris and Apollo, god of the silver bow, take their pleasure” in his rampage (5.760–761; [[god-of-the-silver-bow]]). The name belongs to talk about her, and in this book the talk is of the goddess who was wounded.

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod's Theogony the goddess grows in the foam of the sea, comes first to Cythera and then to Cyprus, and takes two of her names from the islands: Κυθέρεια from Cythera, and Κυπρογενής, “Cyprus-born,” because she was born in Cyprus (*Theogony* 188–199). The Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite opens with Aphrodite, “the Cyprian” (Hymn 5.1–2), and takes her to her fragrant temple at Paphos (Hymn 5.58–59).
