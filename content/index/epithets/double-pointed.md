## Meaning

ἀμφίγυος joins ἀμφί, “on both sides, at both ends,” to γυῖον, “limb”: “with a limb at either end.” It is explained as a spear with a point at the head and a spike at the butt, the σαυρωτήρ, on which a spear could be planted upright in the ground, as the spears of Diomedes' companions stand while they sleep (10.152–153); the translation follows this sense. Others take it as “pliant at both ends” (see the translation's note on 13.147). The same root is in ἀμφιγυήεις, Hephaestus' title, whose sense is disputed in a similar way ([[lame-in-both-legs]]).

Homer uses the word nine times, always in the dative plural, and all but once at the end of the verse (15.386). Six times it closes the formula of close fighting, ξίφεσίν τε καὶ ἔγχεσιν ἀμφιγύοισιν, “with swords and double-pointed spears”: the Achaeans push Hector back here, the fighting at the wall and the ships goes on with it (14.26, 15.278), it is fought around Sarpedon and around Patroclus (16.637, 17.731), and in the Odyssey Odysseus and his son fight with it against the suitors' kinsmen (Od. 24.527). The ship of the suitors comes home laden with shields and double-pointed spears (Od. 16.474).

## Use

{{quote:173580e2-2a01-5926-b2c1-bbaeed86e5af}}

Hector comes down on the close ranks like a rolling boulder and stops against them, “and the sons of the Achaeans, facing him, stabbing at him with swords and double-pointed spears, pushed him away from them; and he gave ground, reeling” (13.146–148; [[the-rolling-boulder]], [[sons-of-the-achaeans]], [[battle-order]], [[hector]]). It is the first use of the formula. The whole verse returns twice: as the Danaans follow the Trojans in a body before they see Hector risen again (15.277–278), and in the fight over Patroclus (17.731). See [[spear]].
