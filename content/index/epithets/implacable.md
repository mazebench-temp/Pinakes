## Meaning

ἀμείλιχος = ἀ- (“not”) + μείλιχος (“gentle, soothing”), from the family of μειλίσσω (“soothe, appease”): “not to be soothed.” The words of conciliation belong to the same family: μειλίχιοι μῦθοι, “soothing words” ([[soothing-words]]), and μείλια, the gifts a father sends with his daughter (9.147). Homer uses ἀμείλιχος three times in the Iliad, always of powers of death or of a man who will not be appeased: of Hades (9.158), of the heart of the Erinys who hears Althaea's curse (9.572; [[erinyes]]), and in Andromache's lament of the “implacable lord” for whom her son may one day toil as a slave, if no Achaean throws him from the wall; his father, she says, “was not gentle” in battle (οὐ … μείλιχος, 24.732–739). In the Homeric Hymn to Athena the goddess has “an implacable heart” (*Hymn* 28.2). See [[privative-adjectives]].

## Use

{{quote:07e672bc-3b87-50b2-8055-78166e3440b0}}

Agamemnon closes his offer with a demand. Achilles is to be tamed: Hades is implacable and untamable ([[untamable]]), and for that reason the most hateful of the gods to mortals. The implication is plain: a man who will not be appeased makes himself like the god of death. Nestor had asked for gifts and words: “with kindly gifts and with soothing words” (9.113), and Agamemnon answers with gifts and with a word that the envoys do not carry: Odysseus leaves these lines out of his repetition (9.260–299; [[message-repeated]]). In Phoenix's story the Erinys who walks in darkness, “she of the implacable heart,” hears the mother's curse on her son (9.571–572; [[who-walks-in-darkness]]). See [[hades]].
