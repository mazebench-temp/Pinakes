## Meaning

Λυκηγενής = a first element + -γενής (“born”). It occurs only in two lines of Book 4, Athena's instruction and Pandarus' prayer (4.101, 4.119), and its first element can be read in three ways:

- Λυκίη, Lycia: “born in Lycia.” Apollo has close ties to Lycia in the Iliad. The Lycian Glaucus prays to him as a god who may be “in the rich land of Lycia or in Troy” (Il. 16.514–515), and Apollo has the body of Sarpedon carried home to Lycia (Il. 16.666–683).
- λύκος, “wolf”: “wolf-born.” A Greek tale told that Leto came to Delos in the shape of a she-wolf (see Outside Homer).
- a root for light, seen in ἀμφιλύκη, “half-light” (Il. 7.433): “born of light.”

The translation takes the first.

## Use

{{quote:62046cc7-8f34-5a58-853b-51bbda20bdcd}}

Athena, in the likeness of the Trojan Laodocus, tells Pandarus to pray to the Lycian-born god and to vow a hecatomb of firstborn lambs when he comes home to Zeleia, and he prays in the same words as he draws (4.119–121; [[famed-for-his-bow]], [[zeleia]]). Apollo is Pandarus' god, the one who “himself had given his bow” to him ({{ref:Iliad 2.827}}; [[the-bow-of-pandarus]]). The Catalogue calls Pandarus' men from Zeleia Trojans ({{ref:Iliad 2.824-827}}), but in Book 5 Pandarus says that Apollo roused him when he set out “from Lycia” (Il. 5.105), and Agamemnon, not knowing who shot, calls the archer “one of the Trojans or Lycians” (4.197). Whatever Lycia is meant, the epithet makes the god the archer's own ([[pandarus]], [[lycians]]).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle reports the tale that wolves give birth only on twelve days of the year, because it took twelve days to bring Leto from the land of the Hyperboreans to Delos, in the form of a she-wolf, for fear of Hera (*History of Animals* 6.35). In cult Apollo was widely worshipped as Λύκειος.
