## Meaning

καρχαρόδους = a first element καρχαρ- (“jagged, sharp”) + ὀδούς, ὀδοντ- (“tooth”): “with jagged teeth.” In the Iliad it describes dogs twice. In Book 10 Odysseus and Diomedes chase Dolon like two sharp-toothed hounds, skilled in the hunt, that press a fawn or a hare (10.360–364). In Book 13 the two Ajaxes carry off the body of Imbrius “as two lions snatch a goat away from sharp-toothed dogs” (13.197–200). Compare [[swift-dogs]] (κύνες ἀργοί) and [[white-tusked]], the epithet of boars and, once, of hounds (11.292).

## Use

{{quote:6ea5eed3-1e05-5b43-982e-076b17ebb948}}

The two Achaeans have let Dolon run past, cut him off from his own lines, and chase him toward the ships ([[two-hounds]], [[dogs]]). The simile is in the dual throughout: two dogs, “skilled in the hunt,” press the quarry “without letting up,” and in the next verse the son of Tydeus and Odysseus, sacker of cities, chase Dolon “without letting up” (ἐμμενὲς αἰεί, 10.361, 10.364; [[sacker-of-cities]], [[deer]], [[hare]]). Dolon is called swift-footed when he enters the poem (10.316; [[swift-footed]]); here he is the hunted animal. See [[dolon]].

## Outside Homer

The Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles* has the same pair in its hunting scene: huntsmen chase swift-footed hares with “two sharp-toothed dogs” running ahead (καρχαρόδοντε κύνε, 302–304). In Hesiod's *Theogony* the word describes the jagged sickle with which Cronus castrates Heaven (ἅρπην καρχαρόδοντα, 175, 180).
