## Meaning

ὑψίκομος = ὕψι (“on high”) + κόμη (“hair,” and of a tree its foliage): “with its leaves high up.” Homer uses it six times, always of oaks (δρῦς): the oaks in the simile of the shout (14.398); the oaks that the Achaeans fell on the spurs of Ida for Patroclus' pyre (23.117–118); the oaks, with tall pines, around the Cyclops' fold (*Odyssey* 9.185–186); the oak whose leaves Odysseus' men use for barley when they sacrifice the cattle of the Sun (*Odyssey* 12.357); and the oak of Zeus at Dodona, from which his will is heard (*Odyssey* 14.328 = 19.297). Compare [[with-high-foliage]] (ὑψιπέτηλος), formed with πέταλον, “leaf,” and the oaks of the similes ([[oaks-on-the-mountains]], [[the-oak-struck-by-the-thunderbolt]]).

## Use

{{quote:df270364-d518-5c60-975f-8deaf5468be2}}

The shout of the armies, as Poseidon and Hector bring them together, is louder than the wave driven by the North Wind, louder than a forest fire in the glens, louder than the wind that cries “around the high-leafed oaks, / the wind that roars the loudest in its rage” (14.394–401; [[louder-than-sea-fire-and-wind]], [[grievous]], [[blazing-fire]], [[boreas]]). The tall oaks are where the wind is loudest. In *Iliad* 23 the same trees fall for the pyre of Patroclus, cut with the long-edged bronze, ταναήκεϊ χαλκῷ (23.118; [[long-edged]]).
