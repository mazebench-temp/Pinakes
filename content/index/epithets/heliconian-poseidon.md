## Meaning

Ἑλικώνιος is an adjective of place, “of Helice” or “of Helicon”. Walter Leaf derives it from Helice in Achaea, a seat of the worship of Poseidon, and infers from the *Homeric Hymn to Poseidon*, which calls the god the one “who holds Helicon and broad Aegae”, that Helicon was another form of the name of Helice, distinct from the Boeotian mountain (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.404; *Homeric Hymn* 22.3). In the Iliad Helice and Aegae are named together as the places where the Achaeans bring Poseidon gifts, “many and pleasing” (8.203–204), and “broad Helice” closes the list of Agamemnon's towns (2.575; see [[helice]] and [[aegae]]). ἄναξ, “lord”, is the god's title, as in Ποσειδάωνος ἄνακτος, “lord Poseidon” (20.67; see [[anax]]).

The god is not named: he is “the Heliconian lord”, around whose altar, as Leaf takes ἀμφί, the young men drag the bull, and in the next verse “the earth-shaker” who is glad of them (20.404–405; Leaf on 20.404; see [[poseidon]], [[earth-shaker]], [[the-bull-of-the-heliconian-lord]] and [[hippodamas]]). Leaf reports a note of the A scholia that the god was thought to accept the sacrifice when the oxen bellowed, and that their silence grieved the sacrificers, as a sign of his anger (on 20.404; see [[sacrifice]] and [[bulls]]). The simile comes soon after Poseidon has carried Aeneas out of the battle (20.318–340).

## Outside Homer

Strabo reports that the sea, raised by an earthquake, drowned Helice and its temple of Heliconian Poseidon two years before the battle of Leuctra; that the Ionians still honored the god with the sacrifice of the Panionia in the territory of Priene, whose people were said to come from Helice; and that some took Homer's simile for that sacrifice, at which the bull's bellowing was the good omen, and inferred that the poet lived after the Ionian migration, while others referred it to Helice itself (*Geography* 8.7.2). Herodotus names the Panionium on Mycale as the place the Ionians set apart in common for Poseidon Heliconius, where they held the festival they called the Panionia (1.148.1).
