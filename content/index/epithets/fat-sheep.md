## Meaning

ἴφιος is probably related to ἶφι (“by force”) and ἴς (“strength”), as is ἴφθιμος ([[mighty]]): “stout, well-fed.” Homer uses it almost only in ἴφια μῆλα, “fat sheep,” most often after βόας καί, “cattle and fat sheep” (5.556; Il. 8.505, 8.545, 9.406; Od. 11.108, 12.128, 12.322, 18.278, 20.51, 23.304). The pair is the wealth of a household in flocks and herds, and the booty of a raid. Achilles says that “cattle and fat sheep can be taken in raids,” but a man's life, once it has passed the barrier of his teeth, cannot be taken back or won (Il. 9.406–409). See [[sheep]] and [[cattle]].

## Use

{{quote:92830825-5dee-55c8-b25f-69ee5e72a61a}}

Aeneas has killed the twin sons of Diocles, and the simile turns them into young lions, reared together by their mother, who raid the farmsteads until the farmers kill them ([[two-lions]], [[crethon]], [[orsilochus-son-of-diocles]]). The fat sheep are the lions' prey, and the young men are killed like the beasts that took them.
