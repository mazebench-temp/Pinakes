## Meaning

δαΐφρων joins φρήν (“mind, heart”) to a first element that can be read two ways:

- δαΐ-, as in δαΐ, dative of δάϊς (“battle”): “with a mind for battle, warlike”;
- δα-, the root of δαῆναι (“learn, know”), as in δαήμων (“skilled”): “with a knowing mind, wise.”

The Iliad gives the word mostly to warriors, which suits the first sense. The Odyssey gives it to Odysseus as a man of cunning, to the Phaeacian king Alcinous, to a craftsman who made a purple ball (Od. 8.373), and to a woman, Odysseus' mother (Od. 15.356), which suits only the second. The two senses may once have belonged to two different words that the formulas later merged. The translation takes the second: “wise-hearted.”

## Use

Book 2 uses the epithet at its beginning and its end. The Dream opens its message with a formal address to Agamemnon through his father:

{{quote:836e20ea-a0df-5a3c-b96c-dacb08847e3a}}

The half-line υἱὲ δαΐφρονος ἱπποδάμοιο (“son of wise-hearted …, tamer of horses”; [[tamer-of-horses]]) is a ready-made frame for addressing a man through his father. Agamemnon uses it in Book 4 to reproach Diomedes, “son of wise-hearted Tydeus” (Il. 4.370). Here it lends the lying message the courtesy of a proper greeting, and Agamemnon repeats it word for word when he reports the dream ({{ref:Iliad 2.60}}).

The last use is in the book's second-to-last line. One of the Carian leaders, Nastes or his brother Amphimachus (the Greek does not say which), came to war wearing gold like a girl, and it did not save him:

{{quote:23b85784-5f14-5a0e-a864-4125ff74640c}}

Achilles has spent the whole book idle by his ships ([[achilles-withdraws]]). The Trojan Catalogue ends by looking ahead to the battle in the river, where he will kill the Carian and carry off the gold ([[the-river-battle-foretold]]). The epithet is given to him at the moment he takes the spoil.
