## Meaning

τανυγλῶχιν = τανυ- (“long, stretched out,” as in τανύω, “stretch”) + γλωχίς (“point, projecting end”): “with long points” or “long barbs.” The same γλωχίς is the end of the yoke strap (24.274), and the bronze point of Achilles' ash spear, μελίη χαλκογλώχιν (22.225). Two other arrows are τριγλώχιν, “three-barbed”: the one Heracles shot into Hera's breast (5.392–394) and the one with which Paris wounds Machaon (11.505–507). The barbs that held Pandarus' arrow in Menelaus' wound are called ὄγκοι (4.151, 4.214). τανυγλῶχιν itself occurs only here. See [[arrows]].

## Use

{{quote:a4d5e393-2ea3-5a83-9d96-b21c0a2aec2f}}

Teucer is answering Agamemnon's praise: he needs no urging, and he has not stopped shooting since the Achaeans drove the Trojans back toward Ilios (8.292–296; [[dear-heart]]). His count is exact. The narrator has named eight men brought down, one after another: Orsilochus, Ormenus, Ophelestes, Daetor, Chromius, Lycophontes, Amopaon and Melanippus (8.273–277; [[orsilochus-killed-by-teucer]], [[chromius-killed-by-teucer]], [[melanippus]]). Every arrow has struck one of the “young men swift in war” ([[swift-in-war]]); none has struck Hector, “this mad dog” ([[mad-dog]]). The next two arrows, aimed at Hector, kill Gorgythion and then Archeptolemus (8.300–315; [[gorgythion]], [[archeptolemus]]). See [[teucer]] and [[back-bent]].
