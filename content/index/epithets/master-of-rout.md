## Meaning

μήστωρ comes from the root of μήδομαι (“devise, plan”): “deviser, counselor.” Zeus is Ζῆνʼ ὕπατον μήστωρα, “Zeus, the highest counselor” (Il. 8.22; [[most-high]]), and a wise man is θεόφιν μήστωρ ἀτάλαντος, “a counselor equal to the gods” (Il. 7.366, of Priam). φόβος is panic flight, the rout of an army ([[rout]]). μήστωρ φόβοιο is the one who brings the enemy to flight. The phrase describes Diomedes (Il. 6.97, 6.278), Hector (Il. 12.39) and Patroclus (Il. 23.16), and in the dual the horses of Aeneas (5.272; Il. 8.108). Compare [[masters-of-the-war-cry]] (μήστωρες ἀϋτῆς).

## Use

{{quote:2abfabbc-ca86-5c46-9def-e89ddfa31c49}}

Diomedes tells Sthenelus why Aeneas' horses are worth taking. They are of the breed that Zeus gave to Tros for his son Ganymede, the best under the sun; Anchises stole the strain by putting his mares to them, and of the six foals he gave two to his son ([[tros]], [[ganymede-and-the-horses-of-tros]], [[anchises-steals-the-horses]]). The warrior's epithet is given to the horses, which carry the fighter into the enemy's ranks and out again: Aeneas boasts that they know “how to pursue and to flee very swiftly this way and that” (5.223). Sthenelus takes them (5.323–327; [[fine-maned]]). In Book 8 Diomedes has them in his chariot, and names them again as the horses “I took them once from Aeneas, those masters of rout” (Il. 8.108). In Book 6 Helenus, and then Hector, tell the Trojan women to pray that Athena will keep Diomedes himself from Ilios, “that savage spearman, mighty master of rout” (Il. 6.96–97, 6.277–278).
