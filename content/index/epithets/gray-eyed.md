## Meaning

γλαυκῶπις = γλαυκός (“gleaming, pale, blue-gray”) + ὤψ (“eye, face”): “with gleaming eyes.” γλαυκός names a brightness as much as a color. In the Iliad the sea is γλαυκή (Il. 16.34), gleaming and grayish. “Gray-eyed” is the traditional English rendering, and the translation keeps it.

Since antiquity the epithet has also been connected with γλαύξ (“owl”), Athena's bird, and read as “owl-eyed” or even “owl-faced.” The owl may take its own name from its glaring eyes, so the two words are probably related. But Homer never associates Athena with the owl. Whatever its origin, the epithet points to her eyes, and her gaze is part of her power.

## Use

The formula θεὰ γλαυκῶπις Ἀθήνη, “the gray-eyed goddess Athena,” fills the second half of the verse after the caesura in the middle of the third foot, the place held by θεὰ λευκώλενος Ἥρη ([[white-armed]]). In Book 1 it occurs once:

It follows a line about eyes. When Achilles turns and recognizes Pallas Athena, “her eyes blazed terribly” (δεινὼ δέ οἱ ὄσσε φάανθεν, {{ref:Iliad 1.200}}):

{{quote:42fc11e1-fd07-59e4-b5c7-7b5d7b4fdbaf}}

Whose eyes are meant is disputed; the Greek could refer to Athena's or to Achilles' own. The translation takes them as Athena's, and then the epithet six lines later names what Achilles has just seen.

In the scene Athena has three names within a few lines: Παλλὰς Ἀθηναίη ([[pallas]]), “child of aegis-bearing Zeus” ([[child-of-aegis-bearing-zeus]]), and γλαυκῶπις. Each adds a facet: the goddess of war, the daughter who brings her father's authority, and the gleaming presence that only Achilles can see ([[divine-epiphany]]).

## Outside Homer

The owl became Athena's bird and the emblem of Athens, stamped on the city's silver coins from the late sixth century BCE on. In Homer the connection is at most latent in the epithet.
