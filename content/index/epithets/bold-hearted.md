## Meaning

θρασυκάρδιος = θρασύς (“bold, daring”; [[bold]]) + κραδίη, καρδίη (“heart”): “bold of heart.” Homer uses it twice, both times in the Iliad and both times in the same turn of phrase, of a man who is only imagined: μάλα τις θρασυκάρδιος ἔσται, “he would have to be very bold of heart” (10.41), and μάλα κεν θρασυκάρδιος εἴη, “he would be very bold of heart” who could look on the battle line of Book 13, bristling with long spears and glaring with bronze, and feel glad and not grieve (13.339–344). The word names a courage beyond the ordinary, which the speaker doubts anyone has. Compare [[bold-in-endurance]] (θρασυμέμνων) and [[stout-hearted]]; the noun of the same root is [[tharsos]].

## Use

{{quote:60fe9ebd-acb3-5a6e-b033-184fae840b44}}

Menelaus finds his brother arming at night by his ship and guesses why: Agamemnon wants a spy among the Trojans. He doubts that anyone will offer to go out alone through the ambrosial night ([[ambrosial]], [[hostile-men]]). The doubt is answered later in the night. When Nestor asks for a volunteer, Diomedes offers himself but asks for a companion, since one man alone sees less (10.220–226; [[when-two-go-together]]), and Hector's spy Dolon, who does go out alone, never comes back (10.336–337; [[dolon]], [[spies-and-scouts]]). See [[menelaus]].
