## Meaning

δίκαιος, from δίκη (“right, the custom and judgment of what is due”), means “just, observant of right.” The superlative occurs twice in the Iliad. At the start of Book 13 Zeus turns his shining eyes away from Troy to the lands of the Thracians, the Mysians and the Abii, δικαιότατοι ἄνθρωποι, “most just of men” (13.3–6). Here it sets Chiron apart from his own kind. The other Centaurs of the poem are the “beasts” of the mountains whom the Lapiths fought (1.262–268, 2.742–744; [[centaurs]]). Chiron gave drugs to Asclepius (4.218–219) and the Pelian ash spear to Peleus (16.143–144 = 19.390–391), and he taught Achilles the healing that Achilles taught Patroclus.

## Use

{{quote:e45d38e8-05bf-52ab-8edd-6ace34239600}}

Eurypylus, shot in the thigh, asks Patroclus to cut out the arrow, wash the wound with warm water and spread on it the good drugs “which they say you have learned from Achilles, whom Chiron taught” (11.830–832; [[soothing-drugs]]). The two healers are out of reach: Machaon lies wounded in the huts, and Podalirius is fighting on the plain (11.833–836). Patroclus does what he asks (11.842–848; [[bitter]], [[pain-killing]]).
