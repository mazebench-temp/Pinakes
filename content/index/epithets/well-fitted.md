## Meaning

ἐϋήρης = εὖ (“well”) + the root of ἀραρίσκω (“fit, join”): “well-fitted,” easy to handle. Merry denies any connection with ἐρέσσω, “row,” and gives it the sense of Latin *habilis* (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.129). Homer has it only of oars, six times, all in the Odyssey: five in Teiresias' prophecy and its retelling, and one on Elpenor's tomb. Odysseus is to “take a well-fitted oar and go” inland until he comes to men who know nothing of the sea, “or well-fitted oars, which are wings to ships,” and there “fix your well-fitted oar in the ground” and sacrifice to Poseidon (*Odyssey* 11.121–131); he tells Penelope the prophecy again in the same verses (*Odyssey* 23.268, 23.272). Between the two stands the oar of Elpenor, fixed on his tomb at his own request (*Odyssey* 11.77–78, 12.15), and Merry notes that the planting of the oar as a memorial recalls that request (on 11.129).
