## Meaning

ὀρεσίτροφος = ὄρεσι, the dative plural of ὄρος (“mountain”), + τρέφω (“rear”): “reared in the mountains.” Its first part is a case form, as in κηρεσσιφόρητος ([[borne-by-the-fates]]). It always describes the lion of a simile, the beast of the mountains that comes down to the herds and folds of men: Sarpedon going at the wall (12.299); Menelaus over the body of Euphorbus, like a lion that seizes the best heifer from a grazing herd (17.59–63); Odysseus stepping out naked before Nausicaa and her maids, like a lion that goes among cattle, sheep or deer (Od. 6.130–134); and the Cyclops eating Odysseus' men like a lion that leaves nothing behind (Od. 9.292–293). See [[lion]].

## Use

{{quote:9454edff-ccdf-5e42-b379-657e141cdfc7}}

The verses that send Sarpedon at the wall return in the Odyssey for Odysseus: “βῆ δʼ ἴμεν ὥς τε λέων ὀρεσίτροφος” begins the simile, and its last verse is the same as 12.301, “make an attempt on the sheep, and go even into the close-built fold” (Od. 6.130, 6.134). What drives the lion differs: here his proud spirit (θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ, 12.300; [[proud]]), there his belly (γαστήρ, Od. 6.133). See [[the-mountain-lion-and-the-fold]] and [[sarpedon]].
