## Meaning

λυγρός means “baneful, grievous, sad”: what brings grief, or is full of it. Its origin is uncertain. The translation renders it “grim” or “grievous.” It describes destruction in the formula λυγρὸς ὄλεθρος, “grim destruction” (2.873, 6.16), hatred (ἔχθεα λυγρά, “grim hatreds,” in Aphrodite's threat to Helen, 3.416), and the old age and sorrows of Phaenops, who lost both his sons in one day (γήραϊ λυγρῷ, κήδεα λυγρά, 5.153, 5.156). The adverb λυγρῶς makes the blow that Hera wishes to give Ares “a grievous blow” (5.763). In Andromache's last lament the death she fears for her son, thrown from the tower, is a λυγρὸς ὄλεθρος (Il. 24.735).

## Use

The formula of destruction closes two obituaries in the same words. Of the Carian leader who came to war wearing gold like a girl: “the fool, and it did nothing to keep off grim destruction” (2.872–873). Of Axylus, who lived by the road and welcomed all men:

{{quote:ede2cb55-9eac-5da2-b257-286bc1d25213}}

Neither gold nor hospitality kept off the end ([[axylus]], [[xenia]], [[the-war-in-small]]).

The second use in Book 6 is the most discussed in the poem. Proetus sent Bellerophon to Lycia with “grim signs,” scratched on a folded tablet, “many things to destroy a man's life,” and the king of Lycia “received the evil sign” (σήματα λυγρά, 6.168–169; σῆμα κακόν, 6.178). Whether the signs were letters or some other marks is disputed ([[writing]], [[bellerophon]]). The epithet says what they meant, not what they were.
