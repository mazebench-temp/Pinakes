## Meaning

ἠλίβατος is an epithet of rocks whose origin is unknown. Homer always uses it of πέτρη, “rock,” six times. In the Iliad: the rock that shelters a hunted stag (15.273), the great rock by the sea to which the Achaeans are compared as they hold against Hector (15.619), and the lofty rocks that Patroclus says must have borne Achilles, so harsh is his mind (16.34–35; [[achilles]]). In the Odyssey: the huge stone that the Cyclops sets in the door of his cave (*Odyssey* 9.243), the cliff that rings the harbor of the Laestrygonians (*Odyssey* 10.88), and the rocks of Ithaca that Odysseus does not know when he wakes there (*Odyssey* 13.196). The translation renders it “lofty” at 15.273 and 15.619, as at 16.35. Compare [[sheer]] (αἰγίλιψ), [[rocky]] (πετρήεις) and [[steep]] (αἰπύς).

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod the water of Styx, the great oath of the gods, trickles down from a high and lofty rock (*Theogony* 785–787; [[styx]]), and the newborn Zeus is hidden in a lofty cave on Crete (*Theogony* 481–484). In the *Shield of Heracles* Cycnus falls as an oak falls, or a lofty pine struck by the thunderbolt of Zeus (*Shield* 421–422), and in the *Homeric Hymn to Hermes* Apollo sees the hides of the stolen cattle on a lofty rock (*Hymn to Hermes* 403–404).
