## Meaning

ἀνιπτόπους = ἀ- (“not”) + νιπτός (“washed,” from νίζω, “wash”) + πούς (“foot”); χαμαιεύνης = χαμαί (“on the ground”) + εὐνή (“bed”). The second word has a cousin in the Odyssey, where Circe's swine and Eumaeus' sows are χαμαιευνάδες, “lying on the ground” (*Odyssey* 10.243, 14.15).

The words describe a way of life, and it is not the one that Homer's men keep at prayer. Hector will not pour wine to Zeus “with unwashed hands” (6.266), and Achilles, before he calls on the Zeus whom the Selli serve, cleanses the cup with sulfur, washes it, and washes his own hands (16.228–230; [[libation]], [[purification]]). The servants of this Zeus keep their feet unwashed and sleep on the earth; the poem does not say why.

## Outside Homer

Strabo read the two words as proof that the people round the sanctuary were barbarians (*Geography* 7.7.10), and the Suda quotes them in its entry on ἀνίπτοις ποσίν, “with unwashed feet,” which it explains as “unprepared,” with the proverb of a man who climbs onto the roof with unwashed feet (Suda α 2477).
