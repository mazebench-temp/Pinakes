## Meaning

ἀκάκητα is a masculine noun in -α, of the same old form as μητίετα, “the counselor” ([[counselor]]), and νεφεληγερέτα, “cloud-gatherer” ([[cloud-gatherer]]). It looks like a compound of ἀ- (“not”) and κακός (“bad”), “doing no harm,” but its sense is uncertain. The translation renders it “the helper”; the same English word renders ἀοσσητήρ (15.254).

Homer has it twice, both times of Hermes and in the same position, after his name at the head of the verse: in the story of Eudorus' birth, “Hermes the helper, and he gave her a splendid son” (16.185), and in the Odyssey as he leads the souls of the suitors down the dank ways, κατʼ εὐρώεντα κέλευθα, to the dead (*Odyssey* 24.9–10). See [[hermes]] and [[argeiphontes]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod gives the epithet to Prometheus, in the lines that close the story of his deceit: not even the son of Iapetus, ἀκάκητα Προμηθεύς, escaped Zeus' heavy anger, but for all his cunning a great chain holds him (*Theogony* 613–616).
