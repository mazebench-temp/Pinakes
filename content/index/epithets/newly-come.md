## Meaning

νέηλυς = νέος (“new”) + the stem ἐλυθ- of ἦλθον (“came”): “newly come.” Homer uses it twice, both times in Book 10 and both times in the plural, of the Thracians who have just reached Troy with their king Rhesus. Other Thracians are already among the Trojan allies in the Catalogue (2.844–845); these have come late and camp apart. Compare [[called-from-many-lands]], the epithet of the allies as a whole.

## Use

{{quote:da76b026-1430-5e60-876b-d87c8e405607}}

Dolon, after listing the allies' places in the camp, offers more than he is asked: if the two want to slip into the Trojan throng, here are the Thracians, “apart, newly come, the farthest out of all,” with their king Rhesus and his horses ([[rhesus]], [[thracians]], [[the-horses-of-rhesus]]). The newcomers are the easiest prey: they lie at the edge of the camp, and they sleep, since the allies leave the watch to the Trojans (10.420–422; [[night-watch]]). When Odysseus and Diomedes ride back, Nestor asks how they came by horses like the rays of the sun, and Odysseus answers with the same word: “these horses you ask about, old sir, are newly come; they are Thracian” (10.558–559). See [[dolon]] and [[odysseus]].
