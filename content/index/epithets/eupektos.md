## Meaning

εὔπηκτος = εὖ (“well”) + the verbal adjective of πήγνυμι (“fix, fasten, put together”): “well put together,” solidly built. It describes the places where people live and sleep: a hall (μέγαρον), where Tlepolemus was reared (2.661) and where Agamemnon's daughters live (9.144, 9.286); the hut of Achilles, in whose innermost part he sleeps (9.663, and again 24.675); and in the Odyssey the inner chambers of Odysseus' house, where the women shut themselves in during the killing of the suitors (Od. 23.41). The translation renders it “well-built,” as it renders the commoner [[well-built]] (ἐϋκτίμενος), which is used of cities and islands. Compare [[well-joined]] (κολλητός) and [[high-roofed]].

## Use

{{quote:6fe13aea-71c8-5d38-89ce-9d0171ec9f1c}}

Book 9 uses the word for the two dwellings set against each other in the offer and its refusal. Agamemnon has three daughters “in my well-built hall,” and Achilles may take the one he likes (9.144–147). At the end of the night Achilles sleeps “in the inner part of the well-built hut,” and beside him lies Diomede, the woman he brought from Lesbos (9.663–665; [[diomede]], [[hut]]). The same verse puts him to sleep beside Briseis after Priam's visit in Book 24 (24.675–676).
