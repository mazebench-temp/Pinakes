## Meaning

ἄγραυλος = ἀγρός (“field, open country”) + the root of αὐλή (“farmyard, fold”) and αὐλίζομαι (“lodge, pass the night”): “lodging in the fields,” out in the open rather than in a stall or a house. Its commonest use is the verse-end βοὸς ἀγραύλοιο, “of an ox of the fields,” where the ox is usually there for its hide or its horn: the hide on which Diomedes sleeps (10.155); the ox that a young man fells with an axe behind the horns, in the simile of the death of Aretus (17.520–522); the oxhide boxing thongs that Diomedes gives Euryalus (23.684; [[well-cut]]); the ox that is the Locrian Ajax's prize in the footrace, whose horn he holds as he spits out the dung he slipped in (23.779–781); and the horn on a fishing line, in the simile of Iris plunging into the sea (24.80–82). Herdsmen are ἄγραυλοι too, who cannot drive a hungry lion from a carcass (18.161–162), and in the Odyssey calves (Od. 10.410). Compare [[broad-browed]] and the other epithets of [[cattle]].

## Use

{{quote:dfe10bab-beb0-55ff-8fbb-1a97c9b49806}}

Nestor finds Diomedes sleeping outside his hut among his armed companions, their spears driven upright beside them, on the hide of an ox of the fields, with a shining rug under his head ([[phaeinos]], [[spear]]). The hero sleeps on the ground like a soldier in the field, and the epithet belongs to the hide he lies on. See [[diomedes]] and [[hypnos]].
