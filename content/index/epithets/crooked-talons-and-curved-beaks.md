## Meaning

γαμψῶνυξ = γαμψός (“crooked, bent”) + ὄνυξ (“claw, talon”); ἀγκυλοχείλης = ἀγκύλος (“curved,” as in [[crooked-counseling]], ἀγκυλομήτης) + χεῖλος (“lip,” here the beak). The two fill the second half of the verse after αἰγυπιοί, “vultures,” the large birds of prey of Homer's similes ([[vultures]]).

The pair comes twice in Homer, each time in a simile of fighting men: Sarpedon and Patroclus rushing at each other (16.428–430), and Odysseus and his companions falling on the suitors like vultures from the mountains on smaller birds (*Odyssey* 22.302–306). γαμψώνυχες alone describes the birds, sea eagles or vultures, whose young country people have taken from the nest, to which the weeping Odysseus and Telemachus are compared (*Odyssey* 16.216–218), and ἀγκυλοχείλης alone is the eagle of Penelope's dream that kills her geese (*Odyssey* 19.538).

## Outside Homer

The Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles* uses the same likeness for Heracles and Cycnus: like vultures with crooked talons and curved beaks that fight with loud cries on a high rock over a mountain goat or a deer that a hunter has shot, “so they rushed at one another, screaming,” in the same last verse as *Iliad* 16 (*Shield* 405–412).
