## Meaning

ἀλφεσίβοιος = ἀλφάνω (“earn, fetch a price”) + βοῦς (“ox”): “earning oxen.” Walter Leaf explains it of the oxen that a girl brings her parents as ἕδνα, the bride-price, and notes that ἀλφάνω is always used in Homer of a person who fetches a price (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.593): Lycaon, sold by Achilles into Lemnos, fetched him the price of a hundred oxen (ἑκατόμβοιον δέ τοι ἦλφον, 21.79). Oxen are the measure of a woman's worth elsewhere in Homer: Laertes bought Eurycleia for twenty (*Odyssey* 1.430–431), and a woman skilled in handiwork, a prize in the funeral games, was valued at four (23.704–705). ## Outside Homer

The Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite has the same phrase, παρθένοι ἀλφεσίβοιαι: the goddess, disguised as a mortal girl, tells Anchises that Hermes snatched her from the dance of Artemis, where many nymphs and maidens who bring in oxen were playing (*Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* 117–120).
