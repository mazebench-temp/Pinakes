## Meaning

μέλας (“black, dark”) is a color of death in Homer, as of the doom that takes men ([[black-doom]], κὴρ μέλαινα) and of the night that covers their eyes ([[black-night]]). The genitive μέλανος θανάτοιο ends the verse five times. In the Iliad the sons of Merops go to war because “the fates of black death were leading them on” (2.834 = 11.332; [[ker-doom]]), and Patroclus, had he kept Achilles' word, would have escaped the evil fate of black death (16.686–687). In the Odyssey the three rows of Scylla's teeth are full of black death (Od. 12.91–92), and the fate of black death takes the dog Argus as soon as he has seen Odysseus in the twentieth year (Od. 17.326–327). See [[thanatos]].

## Use

{{quote:e8f33fb5-dc35-5f84-a93b-81ef9468c754}}

The verse is repeated from the Trojan Catalogue, where the sons of Merops are named, Adrestus and Amphius, and lead the men of Adrasteia and Apaesus (2.828–834). Here they are unnamed. Their father, the seer of Percote, foresaw their death and would not let them go to war, and Diomedes takes their lives and their armor ([[man-destroying]]). See [[the-sons-of-merops]], [[merops]] and [[diomedes]].
