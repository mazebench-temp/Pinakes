## Meaning

δωτήρ and δώτωρ, “giver,” are formed from δίδωμι, “give”; ἑάων is the genitive plural of a word for good things, the one that stands against evils in the two jars of Zeus, “one of evils, and the other of blessings” (*Iliad* 24.528; [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]). Merry takes it as a genitive plural from ἐΰς (Ionic ἠΰς), “good,” with a variable breathing; the jars would point to a neuter, though that should give another genitive, and Döderlein preferred a feminine, understanding “gifts” from δωτῆρες (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.325, and his critical note). The translation renders it “the givers of blessings” (see the Odyssey translation's key words, ἑάων).

## Use

The plural stands where the gods have gathered at Hephaestus' call: “And the gods, the givers of blessings, stood in the doorway” (*Odyssey* 8.325), and in the next verse they laugh. Apollo gives the singular to Hermes in his question about the bonds, “Hermes, son of Zeus, guide, giver of blessings” (*Odyssey* 8.335). See [[olympians]], [[hermes]] and [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod gives the gods the same title, θεοί, δωτῆρες ἐάων, of the gods descended from Earth and Heaven and of the Olympians in their war with the Titans (*Theogony* 46, 633, 664; the verse is repeated at 111, where editors bracket it). The Homeric Hymns give the singular to Hermes, ἄγγελε τῶν μακάρων, χρυσόρραπι, δῶτορ ἐάων, “messenger of the blessed gods, of the golden wand, giver of blessings” (*Hymn* 29.8), and in a closing verse that editors bracket (*Hymn* 18.12).
