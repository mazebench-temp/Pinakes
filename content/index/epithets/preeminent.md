## Meaning

ἀριπρεπής = ἀρι- (“very,” the prefix of ἀρίζηλος, “very conspicuous,” 2.318, and ἀριδείκετος, “very famous,” 11.248) + the root of πρέπω (“stand out, be clearly seen”): “standing out clearly.” Of men it is distinction among others: Hector prays that his son may be “preeminent among the Trojans,” as he is himself (6.476–478), Phoenix recalls that Achilles came to Troy too young to know the assembly, “where men become distinguished” (9.440–441), and in the Odyssey the twelve kings of the Phaeacians are ἀριπρεπέες (Od. 8.390). Of things it is what catches the eye: the stars here, the aegis that Apollo carries before the Trojans (15.307–310), and the horse with a white blaze that Idomeneus recognizes in the chariot race (23.453–455).

## Use

{{quote:653cd4d6-6c39-59e1-a2f8-ee8bc94293d0}}

In the simile of the watch-fires, the stars around the moon “shine out clear” on a windless night, when every lookout and headland and glen stands out and the shepherd rejoices ([[phaeinos]], [[untold]]). As many as the stars are the Trojan fires burning on the plain before Ilios, a thousand fires, and fifty men in the glare of each (8.560–563). The verses about the headlands and the sky breaking open (8.557–558) repeat 16.299–300, where a cloud is cleared from a peak, and are reported to have been rejected here in antiquity. The epithet that Hector used for the son he hoped would stand out among the Trojans is given here to the stars to which the Trojan fires are compared. See [[trojans]].
