## Meaning

ἀτειρής = ἀ- (“not”) + τείρω (“wear out, distress”): “not worn down, unyielding.” Homer uses it of bronze in the formula χαλκὸς ἀτειρής, χαλκὸν ἀτειρέα (5.292; Il. 7.247, 14.25, 18.474, 19.233, 20.108; Od. 13.368); of the voice a god takes when he disguises himself as a man, ἀτειρέα φωνήν (Il. 13.45, 17.555, 22.227); of the might of Heracles (Od. 11.270); and of Hector's heart, which Paris says is “always unyielding, like an axe” (3.60; [[heart-like-an-axe]]). See [[bronze]].

## Use

{{quote:ed66106b-5424-5802-a541-597394cf008d}}

Athena guides the spear into Pandarus' face, and the bronze goes through nose, teeth and tongue and comes out under the chin. The epithet of the metal that never wears out belongs to one of the most exact wounds of the book ([[androktasia]], [[pandarus]]). The verb it denies, τείρω, runs through Book 5: old Phaenops is “worn down by grievous old age” (5.153), the wounded Aphrodite is “in terrible pain” (τείρετο, 5.352), Ares is “already worn out” in the bronze jar (5.391), and Diomedes is worn out by the sweat under his shield strap (5.796–797). The bronze alone is not worn down.
