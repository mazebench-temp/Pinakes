## Meaning

δυσώνυμος = δυσ- (“ill, bad”) + -ώνυμος, the shape that ὄνομα (“name”) takes in compounds: “of evil name,” a thing ill-omened or hateful to name. The same δυσ- makes Hector's insult Δύσπαρι, “Evil Paris” (3.39; [[evil-paris]]).

Homer uses the word three times. Hecuba gives it to the Achaeans (Il. 6.255). The narrator gives it to the fate that closed over Asius before he could drive back from the ships, μοῖρα δυσώνυμος (Il. 12.116; [[moira]]). And in the Odyssey Penelope uses it of the dawn she dreads, the day that will take her from the house of Odysseus (Od. 19.571). Each time the word names something that brings death or loss.

The narrator's formula for the enemy is υἷες Ἀχαιῶν, “the sons of the Achaeans” ([[sons-of-the-achaeans]]). Hecuba adds a word that the narrator never uses of them. To the mother whose sons are fighting under the wall, the enemy is a name of ill omen.
