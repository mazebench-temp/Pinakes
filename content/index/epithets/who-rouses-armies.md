## Meaning

λαοσσόος = λαός (“the people, the host”; [[laos]]) + the root of σεύω (“set in motion, drive on”): “driving on the host.” In the Iliad it belongs to the gods of battle, four times. Athena “who rouses armies” could not have made light of the close ranks around the two Ajaxes (13.128). The same thought returns over the body of Patroclus, with the epithet on Ares: neither Ares who rouses armies nor Athena would make light of that fight if they saw it (17.398–399). When the gods go down into the battle, mighty Strife, who rouses armies, springs up (20.48; [[strife]]), and Apollo, who rouses armies, sends Aeneas straight at Achilles (20.79–80; [[apollo]]). In the Odyssey it is also a hero's word: Amphiaraus, the seer of the war against Thebes, is λαοσσόος (Od. 15.244), and Odysseus, seeing Mentor at his side among the suitors, takes him for Athena, who rouses armies (Od. 22.210). Compare [[driver-of-horses]] and [[marshals-of-the-people]].

## Use

{{quote:632c9896-e036-5d41-b9ea-ed0aaf2621a3}}

The epithet measures the Achaean line by the gods of war: the battalions that form around the two Ajaxes are such that neither Ares nor Athena, the rouser of armies, could make light of them (13.126–128; [[the-two-ajaxes]], [[battle-order]]). Poseidon has just roused the men who stand in them (13.83–125; [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]), and the gods who drive men into battle are imagined as judges of the line he has made. See [[athena]].

## Outside Homer

The Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles* gives the epithet to the mortal fathers of Heracles' family: Alcmene is θυγάτηρ λαοσσόου Ἠλεκτρύωνος, “daughter of Electryon, who roused the people” (*Shield* 3), and on the night that Zeus lay with her, Amphitryon came home, λαοσσόος, ἀγλαὸς ἥρως (*Shield* 35–38).
