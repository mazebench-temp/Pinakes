## Meaning

μενοεικής is usually explained as μένος (“spirit, desire”; [[menos]]) + the root of ἔοικα, εἴκω (“be like, suit”): “suiting the desire,” and so satisfying, plentiful, enough for all. Homer uses it of a feast (9.90; the funeral feast for Patroclus, 23.29), of food set out in plenty (9.227; Od. 5.267, 6.76, 16.429), of a load of firewood (23.139), of game (Od. 9.158), of spoil (Od. 13.273, 14.232), of acorns that swine eat their fill of (Od. 13.409), and of gifts and thanks (19.144, 23.650). Compare [[rich-feast]] and the formula of the [[equal-feast]].

## Use

{{quote:209573c9-48f5-5d65-892b-82cfc64c37f5}}

The word comes twice in Book 9, at the two meals of the night. Agamemnon takes the elders to his hut and sets before them “a satisfying feast,” and when they have eaten Nestor begins his counsel (9.89–94; [[feast]], [[elders]]). In Achilles' hut, Odysseus begins his speech by saying that there is no lack of feasting, either in Agamemnon's hut or “here now; for there is much to satisfy the heart to feast on” (9.225–228), and that the business of the feast is not their concern. The plenty of both tables is the background of a speech about want: the Achaeans are in doubt whether they will save their ships. In Book 19 Agamemnon promises Achilles “gifts to satisfy the heart” (19.144), the gifts of Book 9 at last given. See [[feast-type-scene]].
