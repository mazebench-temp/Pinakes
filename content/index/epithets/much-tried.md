## Meaning

The ancient explanations divide. Aristarchus took ταλαπείριος as the same as ταλαίπωρος, “wretched”; others as a stranger “who has come from far away” (the scholia on *Odyssey* 7.24, as Merry quotes them). Merry refers both ταλαπείριος and ταλαίπωρος to a root περ- (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 6.193). Its first part, ταλα-, belongs with τλῆναι, “endure,” as in ταλασίφρων ([[stout-hearted]]) and πολύτλας ([[much-enduring]]). The translation renders it “much-tried” (see the Odyssey translation's key words).

## Use

The Iliad does not have it. The Odyssey uses it five times, always of strangers who come to a house: ἱκέτην ταλαπείριον, “a much-tried suppliant,” in Nausicaa's promise that Odysseus will lack nothing such a suppliant should have (*Odyssey* 6.193), a verse Eumaeus uses of him again (*Odyssey* 14.511); ξεῖνος ταλαπείριος, as Odysseus calls himself to Athena in the likeness of a girl (*Odyssey* 7.24); the stranger Theoclymenus, whom Telemachus takes home from Peiraeus' care (*Odyssey* 17.71–84); and the many strangers who, Eurycleia says, have come to Ithaca (*Odyssey* 19.379). See [[xenia]] and [[supplication]].

## Outside Homer

In the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* the singer bids the maidens of Delos, if ever a much-tried stranger (ξεῖνος ταλαπείριος) comes and asks who is the sweetest of singers, to answer that it is the blind man who lives in rocky Chios (*Hymn to Apollo* 166–173).
