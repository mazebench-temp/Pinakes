## Meaning

νόστιμος, the adjective of νόστος (“homecoming”; [[nostos]]), + ἦμαρ (“day”): “the day of homecoming.” Homer names other days by what they bring, the day of slavery and the day of freedom, the evil day, the day of destruction ([[evil-day]], [[day-of-destruction]]); this one is the Odyssey's own. The phrase always ends the verse, and the verb before it says what becomes of the day. Seeing it is the hope, ἰδέσθαι (“to see”); losing it, ἀπώλετο, ἀπώλεσε, is the fear; and the god who takes it away, ἀφείλετο, is the end of the hope.

## Use

It is found eleven times, all in the Odyssey. Three are in the first book: the companions, from whom the Sun took away the day of their homecoming (*Odyssey* 1.9), and Odysseus, whose day Telemachus thinks lost (*Odyssey* 1.168, 1.354). Odysseus himself longs “to come home and see the day of homecoming,” in a verse he speaks to Calypso and again to Nausicaa (*Odyssey* 5.220, 8.466), and that Athena, in the likeness of Mentor, uses at Pylos (*Odyssey* 3.233); Nausicaa tells him how he may see it (*Odyssey* 6.311). In Ithaca the day is still spoken of as lost: by Melanthius, who wishes Telemachus dead as surely as Odysseus' day of homecoming is lost far away (*Odyssey* 17.251–253), and by Eurycleia, who mourns that Zeus has taken it from her master alone (*Odyssey* 19.363–369), while the beggar who is Odysseus bids Penelope wait for sunset and then ask him about her husband's day of homecoming (*Odyssey* 17.569–571). If mortals could choose, says Telemachus, they would choose first the day of his father's homecoming (*Odyssey* 16.148–149).
