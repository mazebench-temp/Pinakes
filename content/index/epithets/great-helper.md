## Meaning

ἐριούνης and the longer ἐριούνιος are usually derived from the intensive prefix ἐρι- (“very”) and ὀνίνημι (“help, profit”): “the great helper,” “the bringer of luck.” Walter Leaf, noting that the titles of gods are always uncertain, thinks a derivation from ἔριον, “wool,” “he who makes the wool grow,” equally possible for a pastoral god (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.34; see the translation's note on 20.34). The translation renders both forms “the great helper,” and keeps “the helper” for Hermes' other old title, ἀκάκητα ([[akaketa]]).

The word is Hermes' alone. ἐριούνης comes once in the Iliad and once in the Odyssey, both times after Poseidon in the same words: “and Poseidon the earth-holder, and the great helper / Hermes” go to the Achaean ships (20.34–35; [[earth-holder]]), and in the song of Demodocus Poseidon the earth-holder and Hermes the great helper come to see Ares and Aphrodite caught in Hephaestus' net (*Odyssey* 8.322–323). ἐριούνιος comes in the pairing of the gods, “strong Hermes, the great helper” (20.72; [[sokos]]), and four times in *Iliad* 24, as Hermes meets Priam, drives him to Achilles' hut, opens the gate for him, and stays awake to lead him back (24.360, 24.440, 24.457, 24.679). See [[hermes]] and [[argeiphontes]].

## Outside Homer

The Homeric Hymns use ἐριούνιος often. The Hymn to Hermes calls the newborn god “the messenger of the immortals, the great helper, whom Maia bore” (*Homeric Hymn to Hermes* 3; the same verse in *Hymn* 18.3), and Apollo addresses him as θεῶν ἐριούνιε δαῖμον (551). In the Hymn to Demeter, Persephone tells her mother that “Hermes, the great helper, the swift messenger” came from Zeus to bring her up (*Homeric Hymn to Demeter* 406–408).
