## Meaning

θυμοραϊστής = θυμός (“spirit, life”; [[thymos]]) + the root of ῥαίω (“shatter, destroy”): “destroyer of the spirit.” Homer uses it five times, all in the Iliad. Three times it is death's, in a verse that ends a killing: ἀμφὶ δέ οἱ (μιν) θάνατος χύτο θυμοραϊστής, “and death that destroys the spirit was poured around him” (13.544, 16.414, 16.580). In Book 16 both killings so ended are by a stone that splits the head inside its helmet: Patroclus' of Erylaus and Hector's of Epeigeus (16.411–414, 16.577–580). Twice it is the enemy's, δηΐων ὕπο θυμοραϊστέων, in two similes: of a javelin thrown in a contest or in war under spirit-destroying foes (16.589–591), and of a trumpet sounding at a city beset by them (18.219–220). Compare [[ruin-of-mortals]] (βροτολοιγός) and [[man-destroying]] (φθισήνωρ).

## Use

{{quote:7b758356-6e9c-5dcc-a6b3-1967e92f4786}}

Aeneas strikes Aphareus, son of Caletor, in the throat, “and his head sank to one side, and his shield fell with it, and his helmet, and death that destroys the spirit was poured around him” (13.543–544; [[aphareus]], [[aeneas]], [[caletor-father-of-aphareus]]). Death is poured around the fallen man as night covers the eyes of others (13.580; [[murky]], [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). Whether ἑάφθη, the verb of the shield and helmet, means that they “clung” to him or went down with him is uncertain (see the translation's note on 13.543). See [[thanatos]] and [[death-in-battle]].
