## Meaning

θρασύς is the adjective of θάρσος (“courage, confidence”; [[tharsos]]): bold, daring, and at times rash. Homer uses it of war, in the formula πόλεμος θρασύς ([[bold-war]], which keeps 6.254), of the “bold hands” from which spears are flung (θρασειάων ἀπὸ χειρῶν, 11.553, 11.571, 13.134, 15.314, 17.662), and of men.

Among men it belongs above all to Hector. Polydamas speaks to “bold Hector” when he gives him advice (12.60, 12.210, 13.725), Andromache fears that Achilles has cut “bold Hector” off from the city (22.454–456), Zeus rejects the gods' plan to have Hermes steal the body of “bold Hector” (24.71–72), and the Trojans carry “bold Hector” out to his pyre (24.786). The epithet of daring follows him to his funeral. The only other men called θρασύς in the Iliad are the charioteers of Book 8 and Laogonus, a Trojan killed by Meriones (16.603–605).

## Use

The word occurs four times in Book 8, always of the man who drives the chariot. Hector himself is first: as Nestor cuts away his wounded trace horse, “Hector's swift horses came on through the onslaught, carrying a bold charioteer, Hector” (8.88–90). Diomedes takes Nestor into his own chariot and kills Eniopeus, who held Hector's reins (8.105–123; [[eniopeus]]). Hector leaves him lying and “went after a bold charioteer,” and soon finds “Archeptolemus, the bold son of Iphitus” (8.126–128; [[archeptolemus]], [[iphitus-father-of-archeptolemus]]). Teucer's arrow, aimed at Hector, kills “Archeptolemus, Hector's bold charioteer” (8.312), and Hector gives the reins to his brother Cebriones (8.318–319; [[cebriones]]). The bold man at the reins dies in Hector's place, and another is found. See [[chariot]] and [[hector]].
