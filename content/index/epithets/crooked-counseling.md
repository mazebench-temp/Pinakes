## Meaning

ἀγκυλομήτης = ἀγκύλος (“bent, crooked”) + μῆτις (“counsel, cunning”): “whose counsel is crooked,” the devious schemer. In Homer it belongs to Cronus alone. Hesiod gives it to Cronus, who overthrows his father Ouranos by an ambush with a sickle (*Theogony* 137, 168), and some scholars have heard in “crooked” an allusion to the curved blade. Hesiod also gives it to the trickster Prometheus (*Theogony* 546; *Works and Days* 48). The compound is formed like ποικιλομήτης (“of intricate counsel”), an epithet of Odysseus. Compare [[counselor]] (μητίετα), the μῆτις of Zeus himself.

## Use

In Homer the epithet usually stands in the line-end formula Κρόνου πάϊς ἀγκυλομήτεω, “the son of crooked-counseling Cronus,” which names Zeus through his father (see [[son-of-cronus]]). The father has the epithet, the son the action. Book 2 uses the formula twice, both times in Odysseus' mouth, and both times of a gift of Zeus. He tells the commoners that there must be one king:

{{quote:a08bf104-57fa-51e0-9d0d-32ada468bbf2}}

and he tells the army that at Aulis “the son of crooked-counseling Cronus turned the serpent to stone” ({{ref:Iliad 2.319}}; [[the-portent-at-aulis]]). The scepter and the portent both come from Zeus, and both are Odysseus' arguments for staying.
