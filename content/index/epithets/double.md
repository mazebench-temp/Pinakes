## Meaning

διπλόος (contracted διπλοῦς) and the noun-adjective δίπλαξ both mean “double, twofold,” from the root of δύο (“two”) and the element of “folding” (compare Latin *duplex*). A cloak that is “double” is a large one worn folded over, so that it lies in two layers. Homer uses the words of cloaks: Nestor's “double and wide” (διπλῆν ἐκταδίην, 10.134), and in the Odyssey the cloak that the disguised Odysseus describes to Penelope, purple, woolly and double, with a golden brooch (Od. 19.225–227), and the “double cloak” he says he gave (Od. 19.241). Helen and Andromache weave a δίπλαξ, “a double-folded purple cloth” (3.125–126, 22.440–441). Of armor it is the place where the corselet overlaps itself, where the clasps of the belt meet it (διπλόος ἤντετο θώρηξ, 4.132–133, 20.414–415), and at Patroclus' funeral his bones are laid in a golden bowl “in a double layer of fat” (δίπλακι δημῷ, 23.243, 23.253). Compare [[crimson]].

## Use

{{quote:8a953499-8276-5e06-8207-313312dd9caf}}

Nestor's cloak has three epithets in two verses: it is crimson, double and wide, and a thick woolly nap lies on it ([[crimson]], [[cloak]]). ἐκτάδιος, “spread wide,” occurs only here in Homer; οὖλος is the “woolly, thick” wool of good cloaks, as in the woolly cloaks laid on the beds of Priam and his herald at Achilles' hut (χλαίνας … οὔλας, 24.646; [[thick-and-woolly]]). The old man wraps himself well against the night before he goes to wake Odysseus and Diomedes (10.137–158; [[nestor]]). The cloak the beggar describes in Odyssey 19, which Penelope recognizes as one she gave her husband, has the same marks, purple, woolly and double, with a brooch (Od. 19.225–227, 19.255–257).
