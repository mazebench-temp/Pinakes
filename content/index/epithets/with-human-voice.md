## Meaning

αὐδήεις is formed from αὐδή (“voice, speech”) with the suffix -ήεις (“having”): “having a voice.” Walter Leaf renders it “vocal, with human voice” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.407). In the Iliad it occurs once: Hera makes the horse Xanthus αὐδήεντα, “gave him speech,” so that he can answer Achilles (19.407; [[xanthus-the-horse]], [[white-armed]]). In the Odyssey it describes goddesses who speak with men. Circe and Calypso are each δεινὴ θεὸς αὐδήεσσα, a dread goddess with a human voice (*Odyssey* 10.136, 11.8, 12.150, 12.449), and Ino, the sea goddess Leucothea, was once a mortal who spoke with human voice (βροτὸς αὐδήεσσα, *Odyssey* 5.334). Odysseus, waking on Scheria, wonders whether he is near men who have speech (*Odyssey* 6.125). Compare [[like-a-god-in-his-voice]] (θεῷ ἐναλίγκιος αὐδήν).
