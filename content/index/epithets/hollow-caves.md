## Meaning

γλαφυρός, “hollow, hollowed out,” is related to γλάφω, “carve out, scoop.” Homer gives it to ships, as a synonym of κοῖλος ([[hollow-ships]]), to a rock (2.88; *Odyssey* 14.533), to a harbor (*Odyssey* 12.305), to the lyre in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 8.257, 17.262), and above all to caves. With σπέος (“cave”) it forms a fixed phrase in each of the three cases in which it is found, and the cave it describes is a dwelling: the home of a goddess, a nymph or a monster.

## Use

In the Iliad it is the cave by the stream of Oceanus where Thetis and Eurynome kept Hephaestus hidden for nine years while he forged for them (18.398–405), and the cave where Iris finds Thetis among the goddesses of the sea (24.83–84). The Odyssey has it twelve times. Calypso keeps Odysseus “in her hollow caves” in a half-verse spoken by the narrator and by Odysseus himself, ἐν σπέσσι γλαφυροῖσι, λιλαιομένη πόσιν εἶναι, “longing for him to be her husband” (*Odyssey* 1.15, 9.30; the two phrases come again, in the other order, in the account of what he told Penelope, *Odyssey* 23.333–335), and he lies beside her there at night against his will (*Odyssey* 5.155); and the genitive σπείους γλαφυροῖο names the same cave when Hermes comes to it and on the last night before Odysseus builds his raft (*Odyssey* 5.68, 5.226). In the same caves the nymph Thoosa lay with Poseidon and bore the Cyclops Polyphemus (*Odyssey* 1.73), Proteus sleeps among his seals (*Odyssey* 4.403), the Cyclopes live, each ruling his own children and wives (*Odyssey* 9.114), and Polyphemus shuts in and eats Odysseus' companions (*Odyssey* 2.19–20, 9.476, 12.209–210).
