## Meaning

ἑκατόμπυλος = ἑκατόν (“a hundred”) + πύλη (“gate”): “with a hundred gates.” The number is a round one for something past counting, like the “hundred cities” of Crete (Κρήτην ἑκατόμπολιν, 2.649) and the “hundred tassels” of the aegis (2.448). Homer uses the word only here, of Thebes in Egypt, and it is the counterpart of the epithet of Boeotian Thebes, “seven-gated” ([[seven-gated]], ἑπτάπυλος; [[thebes]]). Through every gate two hundred men march out with horses and chariots (9.383–384), so the city sends out twenty thousand. The Odyssey says the same of the city's wealth, in the same half-verse: Polybus, “who lived in Thebes in Egypt, where the most treasures lie stored in the houses,” gave Menelaus two silver bathtubs, two tripods and ten talents of gold, and his wife Alcandre gave Helen a golden distaff and a silver basket (Od. 4.125–132; [[egypt]]).

## Use

{{quote:9169bac7-9b25-53a5-bb68-9544ce6fb669}}

Achilles measures the gifts he refuses against the greatest treasures he can name: ten and twenty times what Agamemnon has, all that comes in to Orchomenus ([[orchomenus]]), all that comes to Thebes in Egypt, “where the most treasures lie stored in the houses,” as much as the sand and the dust ([[the-sand-and-the-dust]]). The hundred gates make the city's wealth visible, and the refusal larger. See [[thebes-in-egypt]].

## Outside Homer

Later Greeks knew Egyptian Thebes from travelers. Herodotus tells how the priests of Zeus at Thebes showed Hecataeus, who traced his own descent to a god in the sixteenth generation, the wooden statues of three hundred and forty-five high priests, each the son of the one before (Herodotus 2.143). Strabo, who visited the place, begins his account of Thebes, “now called Diospolis,” by quoting these verses of Homer, and adds that its ruins could still be traced for eighty stades (Strabo 17.1.46).
