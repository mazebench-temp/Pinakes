## Meaning

ἤϊος is found only in the vocative, ἤϊε Φοῖβε, twice in the Iliad (15.365, 20.152), and its sense was uncertain already to ancient readers. It is not the verb ἤϊε, “went” (1.47, 7.213), which has the same letters; the pattern of this entry is the phrase with Φοῖβε. Two explanations are given: from ἵημι, “send, shoot,” a title of the god as archer, like [[far-shooter]] (ἑκηβόλος) and [[hekatebolos]]; or from ἰή, the cry of the paean sung to Apollo ([[paean]]). The translation follows the first and renders it “archer” (see its note on 15.365).

## Use

Both times the narrator turns from his story to speak to the god in the second person ([[apostrophe]]): when Apollo has thrown down the Achaean wall in *Iliad* 15, and in Book 20, when the gods who favor the Trojans sit on the brows of Callicolone around Apollo and Ares (20.151–152). See [[apollo]] and [[phoebus]].

## Outside Homer

The *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* uses the same vocative, ἤιε Φοῖβε, when it tells how the goddesses washed the newborn god in clear water on Delos and wrapped him in a white cloth (*Hymn to Apollo* 119–122). The same hymn names Apollo Ἰηπαιήων, as the god to whom men will bring gifts at his temple (272), and ends with the Cretans following him to Pytho singing the ἰηπαιήων, the cry of the paean (500, 516–517).
