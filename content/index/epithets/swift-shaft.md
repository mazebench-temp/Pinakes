## Meaning

βέλος (“missile, shaft,” from βάλλω, “throw, shoot”) + ὠκύς (“swift”; see [[swift-footed]]): “the swift shaft.” It is usually an arrow, but a thrown spear is βέλος too: Hector is angry “that his swift shaft had flown from his hand in vain” when his spear fails to wound Ajax (Il. 14.406–407), and in the same words when it fails against Achilles (Il. 22.291–292). Its partner ὠκὺς ὀϊστός is the arrow alone (5.395; Il. 11.478; Od. 21.416). Compare the other epithets of arrows: [[bitter]] (πικρὸς ὀϊστός), [[feathered]], [[piercing]].

## Use

{{quote:420c1a66-9710-5951-b6ac-9e7ccb8d7ce4}}

Book 5 uses βέλος ὠκύ four times, always of the arrow with which Pandarus hits Diomedes in the shoulder, and always as the arrow fails. Pandarus boasts that the best of the Achaeans is hit, “but the swift shaft had not overcome him” (5.106). Sthenelus “drew the swift shaft clean through and out of his shoulder” (5.112; [[healing]]). Pandarus tells Aeneas that some god, standing near Diomedes wrapped in cloud, “turned my swift shaft aside as it reached him” (5.185–187). And when they meet, Pandarus admits it: “so my swift shaft did not overcome you after all, the bitter arrow” (5.278). The epithet of the archer's weapon is heard each time as the archer's hope comes to nothing ([[pandarus]], [[bow]]). In Book 11 Diomedes, shot in the foot by Paris, draws a swift shaft out of his own wound (Il. 11.396–398).

The other use is Heracles' arrow in Dione's story: “huge Hades endured a swift arrow” (5.395; [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]]).
