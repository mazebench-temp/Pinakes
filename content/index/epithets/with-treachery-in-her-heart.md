## Meaning

δολοφρονέων, the participle of δολοφρονέω, joins δόλος (“trick, deceit”) to φρονέω (“think, have in mind”; [[phrenes]]): “with deceit in mind.” It describes a speaker, not a deed, and it stands in the verse that introduces a speech. In the Iliad it belongs to goddesses. Helen uses it of Aphrodite, who has come to take her to Paris: “that you have come and stood here now, with treachery in your heart?” (3.405; [[aphrodite]], [[helen-and-aphrodite]]). Hera has it in a whole-verse formula, τὴν (τὸν) δὲ δολοφρονέουσα προσηύδα πότνια Ἥρη, “And queen Hera, with treachery in her heart, addressed her (him),” three times in Book 14, and once more in Book 19, when she tricks Zeus into the oath that gives Eurystheus power over Heracles (19.106; [[queenly]], [[hera]]). In the Odyssey Odysseus says it of Circe (*Odyssey* 10.339), and Odysseus himself, disguised as a beggar, speaks δολοφρονέων to the suitors (*Odyssey* 18.51, 21.274). Compare [[apate]], the deception itself.

## Use

{{quote:82104ca4-1291-5632-b591-2f09cf6ab5e9}}

The formula introduces all three of Hera's speeches of deceit in the book: her request to Aphrodite, with the false story of reconciling Oceanus and Tethys (14.197–210; [[aphrodite]], [[oceanus]], [[tethys]]); the same story told to Zeus on Gargarus (14.300–311; [[all-nourishing]], [[deep-streaming]]); and her pretended reluctance to lie with him on the open peak of Ida (14.329–340; [[most-dread]], [[closely-fitted]]). When she bargains with Sleep and swears to him, she is “ox-eyed” and “white-armed” instead (14.263, 14.277; [[ox-eyed]], [[white-armed]], [[inviolable]]): the formula marks the speeches in which she lies. See [[the-deception-of-zeus]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod uses the participle of Prometheus at Mecone. Prometheus divides an ox in two portions, Διὸς νόον ἐξαπαφίσκων, “seeking to deceive the mind of Zeus” (*Theogony* 537), in the words of Hera's plan in Book 14 (Διὸς νόον, 14.160; [[aegis-bearing]]). He offers Zeus the choice, speaking δολοφρονέων, and Zeus, whose counsels never perish, sees the trick (*Theogony* 535–551; [[prometheus]]).
