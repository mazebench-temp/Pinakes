## Meaning

στονόεις is formed from στόνος (“groan, groaning”), the root of στενάχω (“groan”): “full of groaning, causing groans.” The missiles take their epithet from the groans of the men they strike. In the Iliad it describes missiles three times: in the verse ἠχῇ θεσπεσίῃ βέλεα στονόεντα χέοντο, “with a wondrous din poured their groaning missiles” (8.159, 15.590; [[wondrous]]), and in a fight in which both sides keep their distance and dodge each other's “groaning missiles” (17.373–375). Once it describes the dirge that the singers lead over Hector's body (στονόεσσαν ἀοιδήν, 24.720–722).

The Odyssey gives it to the arrows in Odysseus' quiver (Od. 21.11–12, 21.59–60), to the missiles he shoots at the suitors (Od. 24.180), and to other causes of groaning: Odysseus' sorrows (Od. 9.12), the war cry of the Trojans that the survivors escaped (Od. 11.383), and the bed on which Penelope lies awake in tears (Od. 17.102, 19.595).

## Use

Nestor has persuaded Diomedes that Zeus' thunderbolt is a sign against them, and turns the horses (8.139–157). The Trojans pursue with the din and the missiles, and Hector shouts his taunt after Diomedes (8.160–166; [[wretched-doll]]). The half-verse and the verse that follows return in Book 15, when another man of Pylos, Nestor's son Antilochus, flees from the Trojans (15.585–590). See [[arrows]], [[nestor]] and [[diomedes]].
