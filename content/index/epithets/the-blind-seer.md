## Meaning

μάντις is the seer, who reads the will of the gods; ἀλαός, “blind,” is the plain word for a man without sight, as in Athena's praise of Odysseus' throw, “Even a blind man, stranger, could sort out your mark / by feeling for it” (*Odyssey* 8.195–196); its compound ἀλαοσκοπιή is “a blind watch,” which Apollo and Ares do not keep (*Iliad* 10.515; *Odyssey* 8.285). The genitive μάντηος is the reading of one Venice manuscript, followed by almost all modern editors; the other manuscripts give μάντιος (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on 10.493; see the translation's note on 10.493).

## Use

The phrase is said twice, both times in the genitive at the start of the verse. Circe sends Odysseus “to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias, / the blind seer, whose wits are steadfast” (*Odyssey* 10.492–493), and goes on to say what sets him apart from the other dead: “to him even in death Persephone has given a mind” (*Odyssey* 10.494). Off Thrinacia, still at sea in his ship, Odysseus hears the lowing of the cattle of the Sun and remembers the word “of the blind seer, Theban Teiresias, / and of Circe of Aeaea,” who had told him to shun the island (μάντηος ἀλαοῦ, Θηβαίου Τειρεσίαο, *Odyssey* 12.266–269, a plain rendering; [[the-cattle-of-helios]]). When the seer himself speaks in *Odyssey* 11, the poem calls him “the noble seer” (μάντις ἀμύμων, *Odyssey* 11.99; [[blameless]]). His blindness is named only in this phrase.
