## Meaning

ξείνιος is the adjective of ξεῖνος, “stranger, guest” ([[xenia]]): Zeus “of guests,” the god to whom strangers and guests belong, who protects them and punishes those who wrong them. Homer gives the title four times, always to Zeus and always with his anger in view. In the Iliad Menelaus, standing over a Trojan he has killed, reproaches the Trojans who carried off his wedded wife after she had welcomed them: they did not “fear the harsh wrath of Zeus who thunders loud, / god of guests, who will one day destroy your steep city” (*Iliad* 13.624–625; see [[who-thunders-loud]]). In the Odyssey Odysseus reminds the Cyclops that “Zeus is the avenger of suppliants and strangers, / the god of guests, who attends on revered strangers” (*Odyssey* 9.270–271; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]), and the Cyclops answers that the Cyclopes care nothing for Zeus (*Odyssey* 9.273–278). In the false tale that Odysseus tells Eumaeus, the king of Egypt spares the raider at his knees: he “had regard for the wrath of Zeus, / the god of guests, who most of all is indignant at evil deeds” (*Odyssey* 14.283–284; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]] and [[the-raid-on-egypt]]), and Eumaeus, who does not believe the tale, keeps its teller “in fear of Zeus, the god of guests” (*Odyssey* 14.389). The wrath of the god of guests is the same phrase in Menelaus' speech and in the tale, μῆνιν … ξεινίου, across the end of one verse and the beginning of the next (see [[menis]]). Beside him stands Zeus “of suppliants,” ἱκετήσιος, named once (*Odyssey* 13.213; see [[god-of-suppliants]] and [[supplication]]).

## Outside Homer

In Aeschylus' *Agamemnon* it is Zeus of guests, “the stronger,” who sends the sons of Atreus against Alexander for a woman of many husbands (ὁ κρείσσων … ξένιος Ζεύς, *Agamemnon* 60–62). The chorus reveres “great Zeus of guests,” who long drew his bow against Alexander (Δία τοι ξένιον μέγαν αἰδοῦμαι, 362–366), and Helen came to the sons of Priam “by the escort of Zeus of guests,” a Fury who brought tears to brides (πομπᾷ Διὸς ξενίου, 748–749). In Euripides' *Cyclops* Odysseus calls on him in the Cyclops' cave: “Zeus of strangers, see this” (Ζεῦ ξένιʼ, ὅρα τάδʼ, 354; see [[euripides-cyclops]]). The Athenian of Plato's *Laws* says that wrongs done to strangers are bound up with an avenging god more closely than wrongs among citizens: the stranger, without companions and kin, is more pitied by men and gods, and the spirit and god of strangers, who follow Zeus of strangers, are best able to avenge him (τῷ ξενίῳ συνεπόμενοι Διί, *Laws* 729e–730a). A city should receive strangers “honoring Zeus of strangers,” and not drive them out, as the nurslings of the Nile do now (τιμῶντας ξένιον Δία, *Laws* 953e). The Suda: “Xenios: the overseer of hospitality; Zeus too is called Xenios” (Suda ξ 37).
