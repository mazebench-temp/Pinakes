## Meaning

πόδας ὠκύς = πόδας, accusative plural of πούς (“foot”) + ὠκύς (“swift”): “swift in respect of his feet.” The accusative is one of respect, the same construction as βοὴν ἀγαθός (“good at the war cry”). ὠκύς is an old Indo-European adjective, cognate with Latin *ōcior* (“swifter”) and Sanskrit *āśú-* (“swift”).

The phrase lives in one place in the verse. πόδας ὠκὺς Ἀχιλλεύς fills the line from the caesura in the fourth foot to the end, so it follows a verb of speaking such as προσέφη or μετέφη:

{{quote:07da883a-43fd-517e-9641-fbae46a650a1}}

Achilles has other nominative formulas of other lengths, and Book 1 shows them side by side: δῖος Ἀχιλλεύς ([[brilliant]]) after the break between the fourth and fifth feet ({{ref:Iliad 1.7}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.292}}), and ποδάρκης δῖος Ἀχιλλεύς ([[podarkes]]) after the caesura in the middle of the third foot ({{ref:Iliad 1.121}}). Milman Parry (*L’Épithète traditionnelle dans Homère*, 1928) made this family of phrases a central example of the formulaic system: for each case and each metrical slot the poet has, as a rule, one ready phrase. The same slot can hold other names of the same shape. Compare νεφεληγερέτα Ζεύς ([[cloud-gatherer]]) at {{ref:Iliad 1.560}}.

For the other cases Homer uses the compound ποδώκης, as in ποδώκεος Αἰακίδαο (“of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus”).

## Use

The formula occurs six times in Book 1: {{ref:Iliad 1.58}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.84}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.148}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.215}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.364}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.489}}. Five times it closes a line that introduces Achilles’ words. It is used whatever his state: rising to speak in the assembly (58), glaring at Agamemnon (148), yielding to Athena (215), groaning to his mother on the shore:

{{quote:3788fa8c-2391-5efe-93db-bda9495a7ca4}}

The last occurrence is the most pointed. The swift-footed hero no longer moves at all:

{{quote:ed9e05f2-8359-5fc6-86e9-b48c98701db6}}

Here the epithet does not describe what Achilles is doing. It states what he is, and the gap between the two is the subject of the book. He sits (παρήμενος) beside his [[swift-sailing-ships|swift-sailing ships]], away from the assembly and the war, and his swiftness is idle.

The adjective carries a second sense in Book 1. Thetis calls her son ὠκύμορος, “swift-fated” ([[swift-fated]]), and asks Zeus to honor the one who is ὠκυμορώτατος, “swifter to his fate than any other” ({{ref:Iliad 1.417}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.505}}). The same ὠκύς lies in both words: the quickness of his feet and the quickness of his death.

In the Iliad as a whole the epithet is proved in action most famously in Book 22, when Achilles chases Hector three times around the walls of Troy.
