## Meaning

Homer's words for the color of blood are μέλας (“black, dark”) and κελαινός (“dark, murky”). A third, κελαινεφής (“dark-clouded”), is otherwise an epithet of Zeus as the storm god ([[lord-of-dark-clouds]]); used of blood, its picture has faded to “dark.” All three describe the thick dark red of blood from a wound or a sacrifice, and the translation renders them “black” and “dark.” The phrases are formulas. μέλαν αἷμα is common in the fighting of later books (for example Il. 13.655), and αἷμα κελαινεφές returns when Zeus tells Apollo to wash the dark blood from the body of Sarpedon (Il. 16.667) and when the ghosts gather to drink at Odysseus' pit (Od. 11.36).

## Use

Achilles threatens that Agamemnon's “dark blood” will gush out around his spear ({{ref:Iliad 1.303}}). In Book 4 it is the blood of Menelaus. The arrow grazes the skin, “and at once the dark blood flowed from the wound” (4.140). Then comes the simile of ivory stained with purple, red on the white of his thighs (4.141–147; [[ivory-stained-with-purple]]); and when it ends, Agamemnon sees “the black blood flowing down from the wound” and shudders (4.148–149). The two plain epithets frame the one bright image of the blood ([[menelaus]], [[lord-of-men]]).
