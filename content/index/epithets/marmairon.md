## Meaning

μαρμαίρω means “flash, sparkle.” It belongs with μάρμαρος, the gleaming boulder ([[jagged]]), and with μαρμάρεος, “gleaming,” of the sea (14.273) and of the rim of Achilles' shield (18.480). The participle describes things that catch the light. Of armor it ends the verse in three phrases: ἔντεα μαρμαίροντα, the armor stripped from the dead at the Achaean gate (12.195); χάλκεα μαρμαίροντα, Sarpedon's armor carried off to the ships (16.663–664), Achilles' armor worn by Hector (18.130–131), and the armor the Myrmidons take off after mourning Patroclus (23.26–27); and τεύχεα μαρμαίροντα, the new armor that Thetis carries down from Olympus (18.616–617). It describes men in armor, the Trojans “gleaming with bronze” (χαλκῷ μαρμαίροντες, 13.801) and Patroclus and his charioteer in theirs (σὺν ἔντεσι μαρμαίροντας, 16.278–279); the golden house of Poseidon in the depths at Aegae (13.21–22); and once a goddess' eyes, by which Helen knows Aphrodite, “her lovely breasts and her flashing eyes” (3.396–397). Compare [[gleaming-all-over]] (παμφανόων), [[gleaming-bronze]] (νῶροψ) and [[phaeinos]] (φαεινός).

## Use

{{quote:3d1d300b-4e6b-5a98-a443-602d8b4992ae}}

While the Lapiths strip the men they have killed at the left gate, Hector's column still stands hesitating at the ditch, and the eagle appears (12.195–200; [[stripping-the-dead]], [[the-eagle-and-the-serpent]], [[polypoetes]], [[leonteus]]). The armor is the prize of the killing, and the epithet gives it its value as it passes from the dead to the victors ([[bloody-spoils]]).
