## Meaning

ταμίης is a steward, the one who keeps the stores of a house and deals them out: the stewards beside the Achaean ships are “dealing out the food” (*Iliad* 19.44), and in the Odyssey the ταμίη is the housekeeper who brings the bread (*Odyssey* 1.139). Zeus is the steward of war, “the dispenser of war for men” (ταμίης πολέμοιο, *Iliad* 4.84, 19.224; [[dispenser-of-war]]), and the Odyssey gives the same office over the winds, ἄνεμοι, to Aeolus. Merry sets the two phrases side by side (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.21).

## Use

The phrase is spoken once, in Odysseus' account of the gift that should have brought him home. Aeolus gave him the skin of an ox nine years old, “and in it he bound up the paths of the blustering winds; / for the son of Cronus had made him dispenser of the winds, / both to still and to rouse whichever he wishes” (*Odyssey* 10.20–22). He tied the skin in the ship and left the West Wind alone to blow, and when the companions loosed it, “all the winds rushed out” (*Odyssey* 10.23–26, 10.47; [[zephyrus]], [[son-of-cronus]]).

The office is a gift. Merry notes that Homer's Aeolus is not the king of the winds but only their manager, by the leave of Zeus, and that other powers send a favoring wind when they please (on 10.21): Athena sends Telemachus “a following wind, / a fresh West Wind” (*Odyssey* 2.420–421), Calypso sends Odysseus “a fair wind, harmless and warm” (*Odyssey* 5.268), and Circe sends a following wind behind his ship that fills the sail (*Odyssey* 11.6–8, 12.148–150). See [[aeolus]].

## Outside Homer

Virgil makes the steward a king. His Aeolus is *rex Aeolus*, who in a vast cave holds the struggling winds and the sounding storms in check with his command, with chains and with a prison, and sits on a high citadel holding his scepter (*Aeneid* 1.52–57). Juno, asking him for a storm against the Trojans, gives Homer's two infinitives to the waves: the father of gods and king of men has granted him “both to calm the waves and to raise them with the wind” (*et mulcere dedit fluctus et tollere vento*, *Aeneid* 1.65–66). Merry contrasts this king of the winds with Homer's manager of them (on 10.21; see [[virgil]] and [[virgil-juno-and-aeolus]]).
