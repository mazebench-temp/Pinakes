## Meaning

ἱππόδαμος = ἵππος (“horse”) + δάμνημι (“tame, break in”): “horse-taming,” the breaker and master of horses. In a world where the chariot was the mark of the fighting noble, it is high praise. The Trojans as a people are Τρῶες ἱππόδαμοι, and the epithet is given to single heroes on both sides: Diomedes, Antenor, Nestor in the Odyssey, and Hector.

## Use

In Book 2 it names a king of the Achaeans and then the enemy. The Dream addresses Agamemnon as “son of wise-hearted Atreus, tamer of horses” ({{ref:Iliad 2.23}}; [[wise-hearted]]), and Agamemnon repeats the words ({{ref:Iliad 2.60}}). The whole half-line is formulaic: it serves for Tydeus in Book 4 (Il. 4.370) and for the Trojan Hippasus in Book 11 (Il. 11.450).

Thersites uses the Trojans' epithet as he mocks Agamemnon's greed for ransom:

{{quote:5a142e7b-cf07-52ce-8788-e831bb95f538}}

In the soldier's complaint the Trojans are what they are in the narrator's formulas, horse-tamers, and also a source of ransom that the king keeps for himself.

The Iliad ends with the epithet. Its last line reads: “So they held the funeral of Hector, tamer of horses” (ὣς οἵ γ’ ἀμφίεπον τάφον Ἕκτορος ἱπποδάμοιο, Il. 24.804).
