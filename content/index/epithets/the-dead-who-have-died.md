## Meaning

νέκυς is a dead man, a corpse on the field or a shade below; κατατεθνηώς is the perfect participle of καταθνῄσκω, “die.” The phrase says the same thing twice, “the dead who have died,” and Merry compares two other pleonasms, νεκύεσσι καταφθιμένοισι, “the dead who have perished” (*Odyssey* 11.491), and νῆσος ἀμφιρύτη, “a sea-washed island” (*Odyssey* 1.50; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.530). It always ends the verse, in the genitive plural.

## Use

In the Iliad the phrase is said of bodies. Agamemnon grants the Trojans a truce to burn their dead, “for one should not grudge the corpses of the dead, / once they have died, the quick comfort of fire” (*Iliad* 7.409–410; [[the-comfort-of-fire]]), and in the night Odysseus, seeing Dolon come, wonders whether he is a spy or comes “to strip one of the corpses of the dead,” and asks him the same when they have caught him (*Iliad* 10.341–343, 10.387; [[stripping-the-dead]]).

In the Odyssey it names the souls. Circe tells Odysseus that when he has sacrificed the ram and the black ewe at the pit, “many / souls of the dead who have died will come” (*Odyssey* 10.529–530). In *Odyssey* 11 it names the souls that gather out of Erebus to the blood and stay there while the great dead speak with Odysseus. The translation keeps the Iliad's “the corpses of the dead” for the bodies and gives the souls “the dead who have died” (see the Odyssey translation's key words).
