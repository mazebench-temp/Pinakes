## Meaning

φίλη (“dear, one's own”) + πατρίς (“of the fathers,” an adjective from πατήρ) + γαῖα or αἶα (“land, earth”): “one's own land of the fathers.” φίλος in Homer marks what belongs to a person as much as what he loves: “dear native land” is his own country. The phrase φίλην ἐς πατρίδα γαῖαν closes the verse and occurs twenty-nine times in Homer; the genitive φίλης ἀπὸ πατρίδος αἴης (“far from his dear native land”) is rarer. It is the language of [[nostos]], the return home.

## Use

In Book 2 the formula is the refrain of the flight to the ships. Agamemnon, testing the army, uses it at the climax of his speech:

{{quote:878c5554-79f9-5d32-9f2d-595a7a0e252c}}

Hera takes it up in horror (“is it so, then, that home to their dear native land the Argives will flee?” {{ref:Iliad 2.158}}) and adds the other side of the matter: many Achaeans have already died in Troy “far from their dear native land” ({{ref:Iliad 2.162}}). Athena repeats both lines to Odysseus ({{ref:Iliad 2.174}}, {{ref:Iliad 2.178}}). When the army is turned and Athena fills it with the will to fight, the formula appears for the last time: war became sweeter to them “than to go home in the hollow ships to their dear native land” ({{ref:Iliad 2.453-454}}).

Agamemnon's line returns in Book 9, where he proposes flight in earnest (Il. 9.27). And Achilles, who alone may choose between long life at home and glory at Troy, uses the phrase when he chooses: “since I am not going home to my dear native land” (Il. 18.101).
