## Meaning

τόξον (“bow”) + -φόρος (“bearing,” from φέρω, “carry”): “bow-bearing.” Homer uses it once, of Artemis, in Hera's concession, “bow-bearer though you are” (τοξοφόρῳ περ ἐούσῃ, 21.483). Artemis' standing epithet is [[of-the-showering-arrows]] (ἰοχέαιρα), and Apollo's bow gives him his own ([[god-of-the-silver-bow]]).

## Use

{{quote:7334e08f-e587-56f3-a504-a3573bdbad14}}

The bow is the point of the rebuke. Walter Leaf takes γυναιξί, “to women,” as the emphatic word: even if she has a bow, it was given her to use against women, not against goddesses; he takes the lion as a figure of death, and Artemis as a goddess of death for women (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.483; see the translation's note on 21.483, and 6.205, 6.428). Hera then takes the bow from Artemis' shoulders and beats her about the ears with it, and the arrows fall out (21.489–492; [[curved-bow]], [[a-lion-to-women]]). See [[artemis]], [[hera]] and [[bow]].

## Outside Homer

In the Hymn to Apollo, Leto rejoices “because she bore a bow-bearing and mighty son” (οὕνεκα τοξοφόρον καὶ καρτερὸν υἱὸν ἔτικτε, *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* 13, 126).
