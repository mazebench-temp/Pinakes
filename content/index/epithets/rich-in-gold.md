## Meaning

πολύχρυσος = πολύς (“much”) + χρυσός (“gold”): “rich in gold.” Homer uses it five times. Three are of Mycenae, in the line-end phrase πολυχρύσοιο Μυκήνης: in the army's prayer at the lots (7.180); when Hera and Athena thunder as Agamemnon arms, “honoring the king of Mycenae rich in gold” (11.45–46); and in Nestor's story of Aegisthus, who ruled Mycenae rich in gold for seven years after he killed Agamemnon (Od. 3.303–305). Twice it is paired with πολύχαλκος, “rich in bronze” ([[brazen]]): of Dolon, the son of a herald (10.314–315), and of Troy itself, which all men once called rich in gold and bronze, before the war drained its treasures away to Phrygia and Maeonia (18.288–292). Compare [[wealthy]].

The gold of Mycenae became famous again in 1876, when Heinrich Schliemann opened the shaft graves inside the citadel and found gold death masks, cups and jewelry. The graves belong to the sixteenth century BC, long before the traditional date of the war, but they show that the epithet was not an empty one.

## Use

{{quote:acc204b4-9c69-52f1-a980-000ce4a77f7b}}

Nine men have offered to fight Hector, and the army prays for three of them: Ajax, the son of Tydeus, and Agamemnon, who is not named but described by his city and its gold. The prayer puts the commander of the largest contingent last and grandest (2.576–580; [[mycenae]], [[agamemnon]]). The lot that leaps from the helmet is the one “that they themselves wanted,” the lot of Ajax, the first of the three (7.182–183; [[lots]]).

The same half-verse names Agamemnon again as he arms for his great day of fighting in Book 11 (11.45–46). See [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]].
