## Meaning

τόξων (“bows,” genitive plural of τόξον; the plural can mean a single bow) + εὖ, ἐΰ (“well”) + εἰδώς (“knowing,” from οἶδα): “knowing the bow well.” In Homer to “know” a skill is to have it: men “know” war, battle, or a craft. The phrase τόξων ἐῢ εἰδώς closes the verse and describes archers: Philoctetes, Teucer (Il. 12.350), and the unnamed bowman who wounds Menelaus (Il. 4.196). The Trojan Catalogue uses the same turn for fighters in general: the sons of Antenor are “well skilled in every kind of fighting” (μάχης εὖ εἰδότε πάσης, {{ref:Iliad 2.823}}).

## Use

{{quote:6e494f3d-fce7-51ce-895b-22bbd9e31ac0}}

Philoctetes is the Catalogue's archer, and his men are archers too: the phrase is used of the leader and then, two lines later, of the fifty rowers in each ship. The next lines explain why he is not with them. He lies on Lemnos with a snake's wound, abandoned by the army, though the Argives “were soon to remember” him ({{ref:Iliad 2.721-725}}; [[philoctetes-on-lemnos]]).

## Outside Homer

In the later epic tradition and in Sophocles' *Philoctetes*, Troy cannot be taken without Philoctetes and the bow he received from Heracles.
