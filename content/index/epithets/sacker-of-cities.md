## Meaning

πτολίπορθος = πτόλις (the epic form of πόλις, “city”) + πέρθω (“sack, destroy”): “who sacks cities.” Homer uses it eighteen times: of Odysseus above all, of Achilles (Il. 8.372, 15.77, 21.550, 24.108), of the war god Ares (Il. 20.152) and the war goddess Enyo (Il. 5.333), and of fathers of the older generation, Oileus here and Otrynteus (Il. 20.384). In the Odyssey Odysseus names himself πτολιπόρθιος when he tells the Cyclops who blinded him (Od. 9.504).

## Use

{{quote:7210e753-84ee-5aa3-b63d-da7269169528}}

Odysseus stands up with the scepter after the Thersites episode, and the narrator names him by the deed that will end the war. It is Odysseus' stratagem of the wooden horse that will take Troy, a story the Iliad never tells but the Odyssey does (Od. 8.492–520). He rises to argue that the army should stay until Troy falls, and the epithet anticipates his success.

In the Catalogue the epithet goes to Oileus, father of the Locrian Ajax and of Medon: “Oileus, sacker of cities” ({{ref:Iliad 2.728}}). Nothing more is known of his sackings. The title marks the fathers' generation as warriors like their sons.
