## Meaning

ἐρίγδουπος = ἐρι- (an intensive prefix, “very,” as in [[deep-soiled]], ἐριβῶλαξ) + δοῦπος (“a heavy sound, thud, din”): “loud-sounding.” δοῦπος and the verb δουπέω are the noises of battle and of a warrior's fall, as in δούπησεν δὲ πεσών, “he fell with a thud” ([[fell-with-a-thud]]). Of Zeus the noise is thunder. Homer uses the word eleven times, ten of them of Zeus. Most are in the line-end formula ἐρίγδουπος πόσις Ἥρης, often in oaths and wishes: “As for the oaths, let Zeus be witness, the loud-thundering husband of Hera” (Il. 7.411; also Il. 10.329, 13.154, 16.88; Od. 8.465, 15.112, 15.180). The genitive occurs three times: Διὸς υἱὸν ἐριγδούποιο (5.672), Ζηνὸς μὲν ἐριγδούποιο (Il. 12.235), and the contracted Ζηνὸς ἐριγδούπου (Il. 15.293). Once it describes the hooves of horses that raise the dust of battle (Il. 11.152). Compare Zeus' other epithets of thunder: [[thunders-on-high]], [[delighting-in-thunder]], [[lord-of-the-lightning]].

## Use

Book 5 uses it once, of Sarpedon's father. Tlepolemus has fallen, Sarpedon has been carried off with a spear in his thigh, and Odysseus sees the Achaeans carry Tlepolemus from the field:

{{quote:e0d50c03-6eb5-55b5-a967-3e0466433a03}}

It was not fated that Odysseus should kill “the mighty son of Zeus,” and Athena turns his heart against the other Lycians ([[pondering-two-ways]], [[son-of-zeus]], [[sarpedon]]). The father's epithet reminds the listener who stands behind the wounded man: a few lines before, “his father still kept ruin away” from him (5.662). Sarpedon's death is kept for Book 16, where Zeus sheds drops of blood upon the earth in honor of his son (Il. 16.459–461).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod uses the genitive Ζηνὸς ἐριγδούποιο of the Muses' father on Olympus (*Theogony* 41).
