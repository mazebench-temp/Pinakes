## Meaning

διιπετής = Διι- (“in, from Zeus,” the god of the sky) + -πετής, from πίπτω (“fall”) or πέτομαι (“fly”). Of a river it means “fallen from Zeus,” filled by the rain he sends. The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* gives the word to birds, which fly in the sky (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 4), and so the second part has also been taken from πέτομαι.

In Homer it is said only of rivers, and only in the genitive phrase διιπετέος ποταμοῖο at the end of the verse. In the Iliad: the Spercheius, father of the Myrmidon Menesthius (16.173–174; [[spercheius]]); the mouths of a river where the sea roars against the stream, in the simile of the Trojans' shout (17.263); and the Scamander, whose wave strikes Achilles (21.268, 21.326; [[scamander]]). In the Odyssey: the river of Egypt, the Nile, where Menelaus must sail back to sacrifice (*Odyssey* 4.477, 4.581), and the river of Scheria, away from which Odysseus slept in the bushes (*Odyssey* 7.284).

## Outside Homer

Strabo, defending Homer's knowledge of Egypt, says that the poet calls all rivers διιπετέες, not only the torrents, because all are filled by rain; the common epithet becomes a special one when it is said of the greatest, and the Nile, filled by its flood beyond all torrents, is διιπετής twice over (*Geography* 1.2.30).
