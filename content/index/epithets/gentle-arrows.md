## Meaning

ἀγανός is “gentle,” the word of soothing speech ([[gentle-words]]); with βέλεα, “arrows,” it describes a death that comes at once, without the wasting of a sickness. The whole verse is a formula, οἷς ἀγανοῖς βελέεσσιν ἐποιχόμενος (ἐποιχομένη) κατέπεφνε(ν), “came upon him (her) with his (her) gentle arrows and killed him (her),” with Apollo or Artemis as the archer. Merry gathers the uses: the shafts are “painless,” the goddess generally bringing death to women and the god to men, and such a death was easy, as Penelope prays Artemis for a soft death (*Odyssey* 18.202); in the underworld it is set against a long sickness (*Odyssey* 11.172); but it was not always a mercy, since Artemis kills in anger and in jealousy; and he asks whether the phrase may describe the sudden effect of sunstroke (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.280).

## Use

In the Iliad the verse is Hecuba's, who says that Hector lies in her halls fresh as dew, like one whom Apollo has come upon and killed with his gentle arrows (*Iliad* 24.757–759; [[fresh-as-dew]]). In the Odyssey Apollo kills Menelaus' helmsman Phrontis with them at Sunium (*Odyssey* 3.279–283; [[phrontis-son-of-onetor]]), and Artemis kills Orion in Ortygia (*Odyssey* 5.123–124). Odysseus asks the shade of his mother whether a long sickness or Artemis with her gentle arrows overcame her, and she answers that it was neither, but longing for him (*Odyssey* 11.171–173, 11.197–203). In the island of Syria, where there is no sickness, Apollo and Artemis kill men in this way when they grow old (*Odyssey* 15.403–411). See [[apollo]] and [[artemis]].
