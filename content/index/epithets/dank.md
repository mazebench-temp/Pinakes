## Meaning

εὐρώεις is formed from εὐρώς, “mold, decay”: “moldering, dank.” Some ancient readers took it for “spacious,” as if from εὐρύς, “wide” (see the translation's note on 20.61–65). Walter Leaf reports that scholiasts and late poets understood it so, and rejects that sense, which weakens every passage where the word stands (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.64).

Homer uses it only of the world below. In the Iliad Hades leaps from his throne in fear that Poseidon will break the earth open, “and his dwellings be laid bare to mortals and immortals, / terrible and dank, which even the gods hate” (20.61–65; [[hades]], [[earth-shaker]]). In the Odyssey it is the house of Hades to which Circe sends Odysseus (*Odyssey* 10.512) and of which he tells Penelope (*Odyssey* 23.322), and the “dank ways” down which Hermes leads the souls of the suitors (*Odyssey* 24.10; [[akaketa]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod uses it of the same places. The Titans are hidden “in a dank place, at the ends of the huge earth” (*Theogony* 731); at the springs and ends of earth, Tartarus, sea and sky lie places “grim and dank, which even the gods hate” (*Theogony* 739 = 810), a half-verse that differs from the Iliad's only in its first word, ἀργαλέʼ for σμερδαλέʼ; and the men of bronze, destroyed by their own hands, “went down into the dank house of chill Hades” (*Works and Days* 152–153).
