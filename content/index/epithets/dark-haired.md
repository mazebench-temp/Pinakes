## Meaning

κυανοχαίτης = κυάνεος (“dark, blue-black,” from κύανος, the dark blue enamel of the bands on Agamemnon's corselet, 11.24) + χαίτη (“flowing hair,” and of a horse “mane”): “with dark hair.” The form κυανοχαῖτα is an old nominative in short -τα, like ἱππότα ([[gerenian-horseman]]) and νεφεληγερέτα ([[cloud-gatherer]]), and serves also as the vocative. Compare the dark brows of Zeus, κυανέῃσιν ἐπʼ ὀφρύσι ([[dark-brows]]).

The epithet belongs to Poseidon, eight times in Homer: κυανοχαῖτα Ποσειδάων (13.563, 14.390), the address γαιήοχε κυανοχαῖτα (15.174, 15.201; Od. 9.528; [[earth-holder]]), the dative ἐνοσίχθονι κυανοχαίτῃ, of the all-black bulls the Pylians sacrifice to him (Od. 3.6), and κυανοχαίτης alone as his name (20.144; Od. 9.536). Once it is a horse's: Boreas, in love with the mares of Erichthonius, lay with them in the likeness of a dark-maned stallion (20.223–224). The god of the sea is also a god of horses (see below), and the word for his hair is the word for a mane.

## Use

{{quote:bff81bc1-3606-50b4-af10-9013deb98904}}

Adamas, son of Asius, stabs the middle of Antilochus' shield, “but his spear-point was made powerless by dark-haired Poseidon, who begrudged him the other's life” (13.562–563; [[adamas]], [[antilochus]], [[poseidon]]). Half the spear stays in the shield like a fire-hardened stake, and half lies on the ground (13.564–565). In the next book the same words name the god as he and Hector stretch tight the strife of war, each helping his own side (14.389–391; compare [[the-rope-of-war]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod uses the word alone as Poseidon's name: with Medusa, alone of the Gorgons, the Dark-haired one lay in a soft meadow among spring flowers (*Theogony* 278–279), with the verb παρελέξατο that the Iliad uses of Boreas and the mares. The *Shield of Heracles* gives it to the great horse Arion (*Shield* 120). The Homeric Hymn to Poseidon, which says that the gods gave him a double office, to be a tamer of horses and a savior of ships, greets him as γαιήοχε κυανοχαῖτα (*Hymn* 22.4–6), and in the Hymn to Demeter Hermes addresses Hades, the lord of the dead, as Ἅιδη κυανοχαῖτα (*Hymn to Demeter* 347).
