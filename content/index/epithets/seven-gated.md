## Meaning

ἑπτάπυλος = ἑπτά (“seven”) + πύλη (“gate”): “seven-gated.” It is the epithet of Thebes in Boeotia, in the phrase Θήβης ἕδος ἑπταπύλοιο, “the seat of seven-gated Thebes.” Homer has the phrase twice: in Sthenelus' boast (4.406), and in the Odyssey of Amphion and Zethus, “who first founded the seat of seven-gated Thebes and fortified it with towers” (Od. 11.263–264). Egyptian Thebes, by contrast, “has a hundred gates” (Il. 9.381–383; [[hundred-gated]]). The seven gates belong to the city's legend: seven champions came against them ([[the-seven-against-thebes]]).

## Use

{{quote:962da432-0690-53a3-9676-5c0e0b672ce4}}

Sthenelus answers Agamemnon's praise of Tydeus. The fathers, Tydeus and Capaneus, perished before Thebes through their own recklessness; the sons, the Epigonoi, took the city with a smaller army ([[the-epigonoi]], [[sthenelus]], [[capaneus]]). The epithet measures what they took, and the next verse measures the wall they took it under (on the reading “the wall of Ares” at 4.407, see [[wall-of-ares]]). Agamemnon's story had given the city its other title: its walls are “sacred” (4.378; [[hieros]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod names the war in which heroes died “at seven-gated Thebes, the land of Cadmus,” fighting for the flocks of Oedipus, in the lines that call it “evil war and the dread din of battle” (*Works and Days* 161–163; [[dread-din-of-battle]]). In Aeschylus' *Seven against Thebes* a messenger names the champion at each of the seven gates, and Eteocles sets a defender against each (375–676).
