## Meaning

ταναήκης = ταναός (“long, stretched out,” from the root of τείνω, τανύω, “stretch”) + ἀκή (“point, edge,” as in ἀκωκή, “point”): “long-edged.” A second form of the compound, τανυήκης, describes swords (ἄορ τανύηκες, 14.385, 16.473; Od. 10.439, 11.231; ξίφεσιν τανυήκεσιν, Od. 22.443) and, in a simile of trees in a storm, their long-pointed branches (16.768).

The dative ταναήκεϊ χαλκῷ closes the verse four times in Homer. Three times it is the bronze that kills a man in battle (Il. 7.77, 24.754; Od. 4.257), and once the axes that fell oaks on Ida for the pyre of Patroclus (Il. 23.118). The bronze is the blade itself, as in the other formulas of the weapon: [[pitiless-bronze]] (νηλέϊ χαλκῷ) and [[sharp]] (ὀξέϊ χαλκῷ). See [[bronze]].

## Use

Hector sets out the terms of his challenge, beginning with his own death:

{{quote:f740682a-56fe-523b-a7ea-091f95c88a1c}}

The victor may keep the armor, but the body must go home to be burned ([[stripping-the-dead]], [[cremation]]). He promises the same for his enemy, whose body he will send back to the ships for burial under a mound by the Hellespont (7.81–86; [[broad-hellespont]]).

The verses come back at the end of Hector's life. Dying, he begs Achilles to take ransom and give his body back, and his last two lines of entreaty are these two, word for word (22.342–343 = 7.79–80); Achilles refuses (22.345–354). In the last book Hecuba laments that Achilles took her son's life “with the long-edged bronze” and then dragged him around the tomb of Patroclus (24.754–756). The death that Hector imagined in his challenge came in the formula he used for it. See [[hector]] and [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]].
