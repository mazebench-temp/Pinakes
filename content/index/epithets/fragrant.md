## Meaning

κηώεις, “fragrant,” is a word of uncertain origin. In Homer it describes a θάλαμος, the inner room of a house, often the storeroom from which precious things are brought: Hecuba goes down to a fragrant chamber to choose a robe for Athena (Il. 6.288), and Priam to take out the ransom for Hector (Il. 24.191). εὐώδης = εὖ (“well”) + ὄζω (“smell”): “sweet-smelling.” νεκτάρεος (“nectar-like”) is formed from νέκταρ, the drink of the gods, and gives a garment a divine fragrance. θυήεις is formed from θύος (“offering”; θύεα, the offerings that Hecuba is to bring to Athena, 6.270): an altar fragrant with the smoke of offerings. It occurs only in the line-end τέμενος βωμός τε θυήεις, “a precinct and a fragrant altar”: of Zeus on Gargarus (8.48), of the river Spercheius at its springs (23.148), and of Aphrodite at Paphos (Od. 8.363).

## Use

Aphrodite snatches Paris from the duel, hides him in mist, “and set him down in his fragrant, perfumed chamber” (θαλάμῳ εὐώδεϊ κηώεντι, 3.382). Then she finds Helen on the tower and plucks at “her fragrant robe” (νεκταρέου ἑανοῦ, 3.385). A few lines before, the scene was dust, bronze and the strap that choked Paris; the goddess's world is fragrant. Elsewhere in the Iliad νεκτάρεος describes the tunic of Achilles, on which the ashes of mourning settle when he hears of Patroclus' death (Il. 18.25).
