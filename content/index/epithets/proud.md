## Meaning

ἀγήνωρ is usually analyzed as an intensive ἀγα- (“very”) + ἀνήρ (“man”): “very manly,” and so “bold, headstrong, proud.” It is at home in the formula θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ, “the proud heart” (twenty-four times in Homer), which urges a man to dare or presumes too far ([[thymos]]). The same word is the name of the Trojan Agenor.

ἀγέρωχος is of uncertain origin; it means “lordly, proud,” sometimes with a touch of arrogance. Homer gives it to peoples, above all the Trojans (Τρώων ἀγερώχων, “of the proud Trojans”), the Mysians, and the Rhodians, and once to Nestor's brother Periclymenus (Od. 11.286).

The translation renders both “proud.”

## Use

Both words appear once in Book 2. After Thersites has been beaten, the soldiers laugh: “Surely his proud spirit will never drive him again to rail at the kings with abusive words” ({{ref:Iliad 2.276-277}}). The heroic formula, used of the ugliest man at Troy, is mockery ([[odysseus-and-thersites]]).
