## Meaning

γναμπτός is the verbal adjective of γνάμπτω (“bend”): “bent, curved,” and so “flexible.” Homer uses it of things shaped by bending, the curved spirals that Hephaestus made in the cave of Thetis and Eurynome (18.400–401) and fish-hooks (Od. 4.369, 12.332); of a boar's curving jaws (11.416); and once of the mind, when Apollo says that Achilles has no mind that can be bent (νόημα γναμπτόν, 24.40–41). Most often it describes a man's limbs, in the verse-end ἐνὶ γναμπτοῖσι μέλεσσι(ν), “in his supple limbs,” where strength lives or fails: Nestor's lost strength (11.669); the hair that stands up on Priam's limbs when he sees Hermes coming (24.358–359); the shade of Agamemnon, who has no strength left (Od. 11.393–394); Odysseus' skin, which Athena withers on his limbs to disguise him (Od. 13.398, 13.430); and his strength at the contest of the bow (Od. 21.282–283). The verse that ends in it, ἔσθʼ οἵη πάρος ἔσκεν ἐνὶ γναμπτοῖσι μέλεσσιν, is said of a man's strength, lost or kept, in the Iliad and the Odyssey alike (11.669; Od. 11.394, 21.283).

## Use

{{quote:3dd011ed-771e-5d09-b4c2-36ba44019499}}

Nestor has listed the wounded and asked whether Achilles will wait until the ships burn (11.656–668). Then he turns to himself: his strength is not what it was, and he wishes he were young as when he fought the Eleans (11.668–672; [[old-age-and-memory]], [[nestor-and-the-epeians]]). The long story that follows is his proof. Earlier in the book the same adjective gave the boar of a simile its “curving jaws” (11.416), in which it whets its white tusk against the hounds and young men around it ([[white]], [[boar]]). See [[nestor]].

## Outside Homer

In the Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite the same verse describes Tithonus in his endless old age: his voice runs on, but there is no strength in his supple limbs such as there was before (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 237–238; [[tithonus]], [[the-tithonus-verse]]). Hesiod uses the word of the hawk's curved talons that pierce the nightingale (*Works and Days* 203–205).
