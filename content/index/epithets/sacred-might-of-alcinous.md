## Meaning

ἱερόν, “sacred” ([[hieros]]), + μένος, “might, force” ([[menos]]), + the genitive of the name: “the sacred might of Alcinous,” for Alcinous himself. The Odyssey names Telemachus in the same way, ἱερὴ ἲς Τηλεμάχοιο, “the sacred strength of Telemachus” ([[sacred-strength-of-telemachus]]), and once Antinous, ἱερὸν μένος Ἀντινόοιο (*Odyssey* 18.34); the Iliad has such periphrases with βίη, as “the might of Heracles” ([[might-of-heracles]]). ἱερός here is one of the uses of the word that are hard to explain as “holy”; the translation keeps “sacred” (see the Odyssey translation's formulas).

## Use

{{quote:6cce2284-a105-5a06-8233-db1df6ba7534}}

The phrase always closes the verse, and it belongs to the king in his public acts. Alcinous is “the sacred might of Alcinous” when he hears Echeneus' rebuke and raises the suppliant from the hearth (*Odyssey* 7.167); when he rises at dawn and leads the Phaeacians to their place of assembly by the ships (*Odyssey* 8.2, 8.4); when he is glad at Odysseus' praise of the dancers (*Odyssey* 8.385); when he leads the lords back to his house with the gifts (*Odyssey* 8.421); and on the day of the departure, when he stows the gifts beneath the benches of the ship and sacrifices an ox to Zeus (*Odyssey* 13.20, 13.24). Without the epithet, μένος Ἀλκινόοιο, “the might of Alcinous,” speaks to the herald Pontonous (*Odyssey* 7.178 = 13.49), to Arete (*Odyssey* 8.423), and sends the herald to lead Odysseus to the ship (*Odyssey* 13.64; [[pontonous]], [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). See [[alcinous]].
