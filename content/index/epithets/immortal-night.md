## Meaning

ἀβρότη is taken as the feminine of ἄβροτος, “immortal, divine”: ἀ- (“not”) + βροτός (“mortal”), the compound that is more often ἄμβροτος, with a μ (see the translation's note on 14.78). The usual epithet of night from the same root is ἀμβρόσιος, in the verse-end ἀμβροσίην διὰ νύκτα, “through the ambrosial night” (2.57; [[ambrosial]]). Both words make the night a thing of the gods, which comes from them and brings rest. The phrase νὺξ ἀβρότη occurs only here in Homer. Night's other epithets are [[black-night]], [[swift-night]] and [[murky]]; as a goddess she is “the tamer of gods and men” ([[tamer-of-gods-and-men]], [[night]]).

## Use

{{quote:f240e5ed-67c9-5859-9741-95431c26082b}}

Agamemnon, wounded, and sure that Zeus means the Achaeans to perish far from Argos (14.69–70; [[supreme-in-might]]), proposes to haul the ships nearest the sea down to the water and anchor them out on their mooring-stones until night comes; then, if even at night the Trojans hold off from war, all the ships could be dragged down (14.75–79; [[agamemnon]], [[ships]], [[shining-sea]]). He ends with a saying: “For there is no blame in fleeing from evil, not even by night” (14.80). The night he waits for would cover a flight. Odysseus rejects the plan: if the ships were dragged to the sea in the middle of the battle, the army would look away and stop fighting (14.96–102; [[of-many-wiles]]). See [[war-and-night]].
