## Meaning

οἶνοψ = οἶνος (“wine”) + ὤψ (“eye, face, look”): “with the look of wine.” Homer uses it of the open sea, πόντος, usually in the phrase ἐπὶ οἴνοπα πόντον (“over the wine-dark sea”), and twice of a pair of plowing oxen (Il. 13.703; Od. 13.32). The sea is not red, and oxen are not purple. The epithet seems to name the look of the thing, dark and glinting, like wine in a bowl, rather than a hue.

The phrase has drawn much comment. W. E. Gladstone (*Studies on Homer and the Homeric Age*, 1858) used it among his evidence that Homer's color words are unlike ours, and the debate about Greek color terms continues. Most scholars now think that Homeric color words stress brightness and darkness more than hue.

## Use

{{quote:4dfff20e-2a16-553c-b456-a32d9b85d1a6}}

The only wine-dark sea in Book 2 is the one the Arcadians could not cross alone. Theirs is an inland country, “beneath the steep mountain of Cyllene” ([[steep]]), and its men “cared nothing for the works of the sea,” so Agamemnon gave them well-benched ships ([[well-benched]]) to cross it. The formula ἐπὶ οἴνοπα πόντον is the traveler's phrase: it recurs in the Iliad when Hector imagines a man of later times sailing past a hero's tomb (Il. 7.88), and often in the voyages of the Odyssey.
