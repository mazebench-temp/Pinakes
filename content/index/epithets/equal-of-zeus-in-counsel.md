## Meaning

Διί (“to Zeus,” dative) + μῆτιν (“in counsel,” an accusative of respect, from μῆτις) + ἀτάλαντος (“of equal weight,” from τάλαντον, “scale, balance,” with a prefix meaning “same”): “equal to Zeus in counsel.” The image is of the scales: set against Zeus, the hero's μῆτις would weigh as much. μῆτις is practical intelligence, the skill of finding a way. It is the quality in Odysseus' other epithets, [[of-many-wiles]] (πολύμητις) and [[of-many-devices]] (πολυμήχανος), and in Zeus' own, [[counselor]] (μητίετα).

The formula fills the second half of the verse. It occurs six times in the Iliad: four times of Odysseus and twice, in the vocative, of Hector (Il. 7.47, 11.200).

## Use

Book 2 uses it three times of Odysseus, each time at a turn in his part. Athena finds him when the army is fleeing:

{{quote:11f43dec-ef22-5407-a3cf-0ade45e0de79}}

The man equal to Zeus in counsel is the one man not running. He is next named so when Agamemnon invites him, sixth of the elders, to the sacrifice before battle ({{ref:Iliad 2.407}}), and last when he leads his twelve ships in the Catalogue ({{ref:Iliad 2.636}}). The comparison with Zeus is bold, and in Book 2 it is earned: it is Odysseus who turns the army back, puts down Thersites, and persuades the Achaeans to stay.
