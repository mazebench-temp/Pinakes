## Meaning

θερμός means “warm, hot.” The phrase δάκρυα θερμὰ χέων (“shedding hot tears”) is a formula that opens the verse, and Homer uses it six times. Five times they are tears of grief: for the dead of both armies here (7.426); Patroclus' for the Achaeans dying at the ships, “like a spring of dark water” (16.2–4); Antilochus', as he brings Achilles the news of Patroclus' death (18.16–18); Achilles', when he sees Patroclus on the bier (18.233–236); and the Danaans' over the body of Achilles, as his ghost is told in the Odyssey (Od. 24.43–46). Once they are tears of joy: Agamemnon, landing at home, kisses the ground and weeps hot tears, not knowing that Aegisthus is waiting (Od. 4.521–523). See [[tears]] and [[shedding-tears]].

## Use

{{quote:eb92c69c-e76d-580f-88b5-2de23e902772}}

The dead of the day before lie on the plain, and it is hard to tell one man from another. The Trojans and Achaeans wash away the clotted blood with water and, shedding hot tears, lift them onto the wagons (7.424–426; [[clotted-blood]]). Priam forbids his people to wail, and they heap the dead on the pyre in silence (7.427–428). The tears are the only mourning the scene allows. See [[the-truce-for-the-dead]], [[cremation]] and [[lament]].
