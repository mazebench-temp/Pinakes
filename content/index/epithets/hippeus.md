## Meaning

ἱππεύς, from ἵππος (“horse”), is the man who fights from a chariot. In the plural the Iliad uses it for the chariot-fighters as a body, set against the men on foot: in Nestor's battle order, “The horsemen he set in front with their horses and chariots” (4.297), and in Agamemnon's charge foot soldiers kill foot soldiers “and horsemen horsemen” (11.151). As a word of address it is said only of Patroclus.

The older title of the horseman is ἱππότα, given to heroes of the older generation, Nestor, Peleus, Tydeus, Oeneus and Phyleus ([[gerenian-horseman]]); the translation renders both words “horseman.” In Book 16 the two stand thirteen lines apart: the narrator calls Patroclus “horseman” as he begins his plea (16.20), and Patroclus in his reproach calls Achilles' father “the horseman Peleus” (16.33).
