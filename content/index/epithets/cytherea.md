## Meaning

The ancients took the name from the island of Cythera, off Cape Malea, where Aphrodite was said to have come ashore on her way from the sea to Cyprus (see [[cythera]]). The vowels of the two words differ, Κυθέρεια with ε and Κύθηρα with η, and the connection may be no more than the ancient one.

## Use

Both uses are at the end of the verse, with ἐϋστέφανος, “well-crowned” ([[well-crowned]]). In Demodocus' song Ares goes to the house of Hephaestus “eager for the love of well-crowned Cytherea” (*Odyssey* 8.288), and in Book 18 of the Odyssey Athena cleanses Penelope's face with the ambrosial beauty with which well-crowned Cytherea anoints herself when she goes to the dance of the Graces (*Odyssey* 18.192–194; see [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]]). The Iliad does not use the name.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod explains the name from the island: gods and men call the goddess Cytherea “because she reached Cythera,” ὅτι προσέκυρσε Κυθήροις, after she was born of the foam and before she went on to Cyprus (*Theogony* 192–199); the verse that names her ἐυστέφανον Κυθέρειαν beside ἀφρογενέα is bracketed by editors (*Theogony* 196). The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* uses the Odyssey's verse-end, ἐυστεφάνου Κυθερείης, “of well-crowned Cytherea” (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 6, 175; the dative 287), the sixth Hymn has “violet-crowned Cytherea” (*Hymn* 6.18), and the tenth Hymn begins with “Cyprus-born Cytherea” (*Hymn* 10.1).
