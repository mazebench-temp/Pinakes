## Meaning

εὐπατέρεια = εὖ (“well, nobly”) + πατήρ (“father”), with a feminine ending: “she whose father is noble.” Homer uses it three times: of Helen here; of Tyro, daughter of Salmoneus, the first of the heroines whom Odysseus meets among the dead (Od. 11.235–236); and of Helen again, “white-armed Helen, daughter of a noble father,” when Athena, in the likeness of Mentor, reminds Odysseus how he fought for her at Troy (Od. 22.227). In the Iliad Helen's father is Zeus: she is “child of Zeus” (3.199, 3.418; [[daughter-of-zeus]]).

## Use

Hecuba goes down into her fragrant chamber for a robe to give Athena (6.288; [[fragrant]]):

{{quote:ee8d70a6-1700-5b5d-9f29-89bc6c3e804f}}

The robes are spoils of the voyage that brought Helen to Troy, and the epithet of her birth ends the sentence ([[sidonians]], [[sidon]], [[the-abduction-of-helen]]). Hecuba chooses the finest of them for Athena (6.293–295; [[robe]]). Helen's father is named in the same phrase that names her abduction, and the gift for the goddess comes from the same cargo as the woman for whom the war is fought ([[helen]], [[paris]]).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus quotes 6.289–292 in his argument that Homer knew the story of Paris' voyage by way of Sidon and Egypt, which he heard from the priests in Egypt, and that the poet set it aside as less suited to epic (Herodotus 2.116; [[helen-in-egypt]]).
