## Meaning

κλυτόπωλος = κλυτός (“famous, heard of”; compare [[famous]] and [[famed-for-his-spear]]) + πῶλος (“foal,” in poetry a horse): “with famous horses.” Homer uses it three times, always of Hades and always in the same half-verse, at the end of a threat of death: ψυχὴν δʼ Ἄϊδι κλυτοπώλῳ (5.654; Il. 11.445, 16.625). Homer does not describe the horses. In the Homeric Hymn to Demeter Hades seizes Persephone and carries her off with his immortal horses on a golden chariot (Hymn to Demeter 16–20), and Hermes drives the same horses to bring her back to her mother. They fly “not unwilling,” in the half-verse Book 5 uses of the horses of Ares and of Hera (Hymn to Demeter 375–383; Il. 5.366, 5.768).

## Use

{{quote:1728708c-49e4-5782-8ad4-d1379e686a42}}

Tlepolemus has boasted that Sarpedon, brought down by him, “will pass the gates of Hades” (5.646). Sarpedon turns the threat back on him in the language of the victor's boast (εὖχος; [[euchomai]]): the dead man gives glory to his killer, and his soul to the god below ([[psyche]], [[hades]]). The threat is fulfilled at once. Sarpedon's spear goes through Tlepolemus' neck, while Tlepolemus' spear wounds Sarpedon in the thigh; and when the spear is drawn out, “his soul left him,” though he breathed again (5.655–698; [[tlepolemus-and-sarpedon]]). In Book 11 Odysseus threatens Socus with the same three verses, with one change in the middle (Il. 11.443–445), and in Book 16 Meriones ends his answer to Aeneas' taunt with the last of them (Il. 16.625).
