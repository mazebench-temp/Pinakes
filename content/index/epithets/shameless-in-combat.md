## Meaning

ἀναιδής = ἀ- (“not”) + αἰδώς (“shame, respect”; [[aidos]]): “shameless,” knowing no restraint. With the genitive δηϊοτῆτος (“of combat”; see [[grim-combat]]) it means “shameless in combat,” relentless. The phrase occurs only here. ἀναιδής is used of the stone of Sisyphus that rolls back down the hill ([[shameless-stone]]), of Agamemnon in Achilles' insult ([[utterly-shameless]]), and in the Odyssey of the suitors. Compare [[clothed-in-shamelessness]].

## Use

{{quote:1903bc9b-5886-5292-8bae-ad4279a4698f}}

Hector's charge brings the war gods with it: Ares, and lady Enyo, who brings Tumult with her ([[tumult]], [[enyo]], [[queenly]]). The din of battle is a person without shame, a power that nothing checks. Agamemnon's exhortation a little earlier asked the Achaeans for the opposite: “feel shame before one another in the fierce battles; when men feel shame, more are saved than are killed” (5.530–531; [[aidos]], [[exhortation]]).
