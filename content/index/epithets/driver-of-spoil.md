## Meaning

ἀγελείη has two ancient explanations. One takes it from ἄγω (“drive, lead away”) and λεία or ληΐς (“booty,” above all cattle driven off in a raid; [[raiding]]): “she who drives off the spoil.” Athena has a similar title in Book 10, where Odysseus dedicates the spoils of Dolon to her as ληΐτις, “goddess of the spoil” (Il. 10.460). The other takes it from ἄγω and λαός (“the host, the army”): “leader of the host.” The translation follows the first. Homer uses the epithet only of Athena, often in the genitive Ἀθηναίης ἀγελείης (Il. 6.269, 15.213; Od. 16.207), and in the nominative with Διὸς θυγάτηρ, “daughter of Zeus” (4.128; Od. 13.359).

## Use

{{quote:90ce77e5-b5e5-516b-bda1-7e6075564a18}}

The narrator turns to Menelaus as the arrow flies: the gods did not forget him, and first of all the daughter of Zeus, driver of spoil ([[blessed-gods]], [[daughter-of-zeus]]). The warlike title comes as the goddess performs a mother's act, brushing the arrow away as a mother brushes a fly from her sleeping child ([[mother-and-fly]]). She is the goddess who, a few lines before, persuaded Pandarus to shoot (4.86–104), and she does not stop the arrow. She guides it to the place where the golden clasps of the belt met the double corselet, and it only grazes the skin (4.132–140; [[athena]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod uses ἀγελείη of Athena in the story of Heracles and the Hydra (*Theogony* 316–318), and he calls her ἀγέστρατος, “leader of the host,” at her birth from the head of Zeus (*Theogony* 924–925), a word that supports the second ancient explanation.
