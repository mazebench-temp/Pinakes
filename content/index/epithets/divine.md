## Meaning

θεῖος is the adjective of θεός (“god”): “of the gods, sent by the gods,” and of people, “godlike, excellent.” It says in plain words what [[brilliant]] (δῖος) says by an older image. δῖος comes from the root of Ζεύς and the bright sky, θεῖος from the ordinary word for a god. The translation keeps them apart: δῖος is “brilliant,” θεῖος is “divine.”

Of things, θεῖος marks what comes from the gods: a divine voice, a divine drink, the gods' assembly (θεῖον ἀγῶνα, Il. 7.298, where the Trojan women will go to pray; the tripods of Hephaestus roll into it at 18.376). Of people it is given to heroes, to kings, to the herald Talthybius (Il. 4.192), and in the Odyssey above all to the singer, θεῖος ἀοιδός, whose song comes from the Muse.

## Use

The first three uses in Book 2 belong to the deception of Agamemnon. The Dream, which Zeus has sent as “baneful” ([[baneful]]), speaks as a divine Dream:

{{quote:35ebf63a-b420-5abe-aec6-8794cc2671ea}}

When Agamemnon wakes, “the divine voice was poured around him” (θείη … ὀμφή, {{ref:Iliad 2.41}}), and he tells the council, “a divine Dream came to me in my sleep” ({{ref:Iliad 2.56}}). The epithet is true, since the Dream does come from Zeus, and it is what makes the lie persuasive. The same line opens a trick in the Odyssey. In a story told by the disguised Odysseus, Odysseus claims a divine dream so as to send a man running to the ships, leaving his cloak behind for a freezing companion (Od. 14.495).

The fourth use passes the word from the god's messenger to a man. When Odysseus has turned the army back and argued for staying, the Argives shout their approval of “the word of divine Odysseus” (μῦθον … Ὀδυσσῆος θείοιο, {{ref:Iliad 2.335}}). Ὀδυσσῆος θείοιο closes the verse twice more in the Iliad (Il. 9.218, 11.806) and often in the Odyssey. The day began with a false promise brought by a divine messenger. It is saved by a divine man who reminds the army of a true one, the prophecy at Aulis ({{ref:Iliad 2.299-330}}).
