## Meaning

ἱππόμαχος = ἵππος (“horse”) + μάχομαι (“fight”): “fighting from horses, fighting with horses.” Homer uses it only of the Phrygians, in the line of Dolon's catalogue that sets them beside the Maeonians, “who fight from chariots” (ἱπποκορυσταί, 10.431; [[chariot-fighters]]). Whether the poet thought of mounted fighters or of chariot warriors is not clear: in the Iliad men fight from chariots, and riding a horse is mentioned only in a simile (15.679–684) and on the night of Book 10, when Diomedes mounts the horses of Rhesus to ride them back to the ships (10.513, 10.529; [[horses]]). The translation renders the two compounds apart, “who fight from horses” and “who fight from chariots.” The same compound is the name of a Trojan, Hippomachus, son of Antimachus, killed by Leonteus (12.188–189).

The Phrygians are horsemen elsewhere in the poem: Priam remembers the Phrygians “with their quick horses” encamped on the Sangarius in his youth (3.185; [[with-quick-horses]]).

## Use

{{quote:7f7baf55-76e2-5b17-904e-9964c19283fd}}

Dolon names the allies in two groups, those camped toward the sea and those toward Thymbre, and nearly every people has its epithet ([[with-curved-bows]], [[brilliant]], [[proud]]). The last verse pairs two compounds of ἵππος: Φρύγες ἱππόμαχοι καὶ Μῄονες ἱπποκορυσταί. The next verses turn to the Thracians, newly come, whose king has the finest horses Dolon has seen (10.434–437; [[newly-come]], [[the-horses-of-rhesus]]). See [[phrygians]] and [[trojan-allies]].
