## Meaning

ἠπύτα is formed from ἠπύω (“call out, shout”) with the old nominative ending in short -τα that also appears in ἱππότα ([[gerenian-horseman]]), μητίετα ([[counselor]]) and νεφεληγερέτα ([[cloud-gatherer]]): “the caller, the loud-voiced one.” Homer uses it only once, of the Trojan herald Idaeus (7.384). The same root gives the name of Epytus, the father of the herald Periphas, who grew old heralding for Aeneas' father (κήρυκι Ἠπυτίδῃ, 17.323–325): a herald's family bears a herald's name.

A herald's voice is his instrument. Heralds summon the army to assembly and call for silence (2.50–52, 2.96–98), and the best of them are praised for it: the heralds are “clear-voiced” ([[clear-voiced]]), and Stentor, whose shape Hera takes, could shout as loud as fifty men (5.785–786; [[bronze-voiced]]). See [[heralds]].

## Use

Idaeus comes to the Achaean assembly at dawn with Paris' offer and Priam's request for a truce:

{{quote:a5dd33b4-b391-5218-a1af-8b9806d27637}}

The herald stands in the middle and speaks to the whole assembly, and the epithet gives him the voice his office needs. His message spells out Priam's instructions and adds two comments of his own: that Paris should have perished before he brought the possessions to Troy, and that the Trojans urge him to give Helen back (7.390, 7.393; [[message-repeated]]). The Achaeans answer with silence, then with Diomedes' refusal, and Agamemnon grants the truce for the dead (7.398–411; [[idaeus]], [[the-truce-for-the-dead]]).
