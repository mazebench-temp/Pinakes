## Meaning

μέρμερος, “grim, baneful,” is used in Homer only in the neuter plural, of deeds; Mermerus, a Trojan killed by Antilochus, bears it as a name (14.513). It is probably related to μερμηρίζω (“ponder anxiously,” 8.167–169; [[pondering-two-ways]]): deeds that give cause for care.

The phrase μέρμερα ἔργα occurs three times. Zeus uses it here of the war that Hera and Athena did not reach. In Book 10 Diomedes tells how his father Tydeus, coming back from Thebes, “devised very grim deeds” against the men who lay in wait for him (10.285–290; [[tydeus-at-thebes]]), and the Trojans, waking, gaze at “the grim deeds” that Odysseus and Diomedes have done in the camp of Rhesus (10.523–524). The neuter alone describes the work of a great killer: Agamemnon has never known one man to devise so many grim deeds in a day as Hector (10.47–48), Hector goes among the Achaeans “doing grim deeds” (11.502), and the river Scamander tells Achilles to do his grim deeds on the plain, not in his stream (21.217).

## Use

{{quote:62ec26fc-7ecb-59df-857e-e6293232aa59}}

Zeus has come home to Olympus, and Hera and Athena sit apart from him in silence (8.438–445; [[well-wheeled]]). He mocks them: they cannot be tired from killing Trojans in battle, since they turned back trembling before they had seen “the war and the grim deeds of war” (8.447–453; [[where-men-win-glory]], [[phaidimos]]). The two goddesses who had armed to kill Trojans are told that they never came near the killing. See [[zeus]], [[hera]] and [[athena]].
