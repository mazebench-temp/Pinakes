## Meaning

ἀλεγεινός is related to ἄλγος (“pain, sorrow”; [[algos]]) and ἀλεγίζω (“care for, heed”): “painful, troublesome, hard to bear.” Homer uses it of many kinds of trouble: of news, “with grievous news” (2.787; 18.17), of the funeral pyre (4.99, 9.546), of a spear point that goes through the flesh (5.658) and of pain (11.398), of battle (μάχης ἀλεγεινῆς, 18.248 = 19.46 = 20.43), of Ares (13.569) and of war and the sea (πτολέμους ἀλεγεινά τε κύματα, 24.8; Od. 8.183), of the hard contests of boxing and wrestling (23.653, 23.701), and of horses that are hard for a mortal to manage (10.402 = 17.76). The translation renders it “grievous,” and also “painful” and “troublesome” where the sense asks for them.

## Use

{{quote:ab8c2061-6e2c-5e76-b6bc-af74c9bf9470}}

Book 9 uses it twice, both in Phoenix's speech. The first is tender. Phoenix fed the child Achilles on his knees, cutting up his meat and holding the wine to his lips, and the child often wet the tunic on his chest, “sputtering out the wine in your troublesome infancy” (9.488–491; [[phoenix]], [[nepios]]). The second is the grievous pyre to which the boar of Calydon sent many men before Meleager killed it (9.543–546; [[lurker-in-the-grass]], [[the-calydonian-boar]]). The same phrase is in Athena's temptation of Pandarus in Book 4: Alexander would give him splendid gifts if he saw Menelaus brought down by his arrow “and laid on the grievous pyre” (4.95–99).
