## Meaning

μῶνυξ is generally explained as μόνος (“single”) + ὄνυξ (“claw, nail, hoof”): “with a single hoof,” an uncloven hoof, as a horse has and an ox has not. It is the plainest and most frequent of the epithets of horses, used more than thirty times in Homer, nearly always in μώνυχας ἵππους at the end of the verse. The same ὄνυξ is in [[strong-hoofed]] (κρατερῶνυξ). Compare the epithets of the horse's speed and beauty: [[swift-footed]], [[fine-maned]], [[high-neighing]], [[with-golden-headbands]].

## Use

Book 5 uses the formula five times, always of chariot horses in the fighting. Pandarus fears that Diomedes will kill him and Aeneas “and drive off the single-hoofed horses” (5.236). Sthenelus holds his own single-hoofed horses away from the din while he takes Aeneas' (5.321–324). Mydon, Pylaemenes' charioteer, is turning his single-hoofed horses when Antilochus hits him with a stone (5.580–582; [[mydon]]). The last two uses are an order and its fulfillment. Athena tells Diomedes, “drive your single-hoofed horses at Ares first” (5.829), and a few lines later, with the reins in her own hands, “at once she drove the single-hoofed horses at Ares first” (5.841). The goddess gives the command and carries it out herself ([[athena]], [[diomedes-wounds-ares]]).
