## Meaning

ὁμοίιος is an epic word related to ὁμοῖος (“like, the same”), and the ancient explanation took it as “common, the same for all”: war, strife, old age and death come alike to everyone. Because it is used only of evils, it has also been taken as “evil, grievous,” and the sense is disputed; the translation follows the ancient explanation. Homer uses it of war, above all in the line-end formula ὁμοιΐου πολέμοιο, “of war, common to all” (for example Il. 9.440, 13.635); of strife and the quarrel (4.444; Od. 18.264); of old age (4.315); and of death, which not even the gods can keep from a man they love (Od. 3.236–238). The Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite uses it of old age, as Book 4 does (*Hymn* 5.244).

## Use

Book 4 uses the word twice, once of old age and once of strife. Agamemnon wishes that Nestor's strength matched his spirit: “But old age, the common lot, wears you down; I wish that some other man had it, and you were among the younger” (4.315–316; [[nestor]], [[old-age-and-memory]]). Nestor answers that the gods do not give men all things at once (4.320). When the armies meet, Strife “then too cast the quarrel, common to all, into their midst” (4.444; [[strife]], [[neikos]]). In both places the word names what no one escapes: old age for the old man, and the quarrel for both armies.
