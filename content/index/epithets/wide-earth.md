## Meaning

εὐρεῖα, feminine of εὐρύς (“wide, broad”), + χθών (“earth, ground”; [[earth]]): “the wide earth.” It belongs with the other epithets of the earth: [[black-earth]], [[bountiful-earth]], [[life-giving-earth]] and [[grain-giving]]. The phrase ends the verse, as in the description of the healer Agamede, who knew all the drugs that the wide earth nourishes (Il. 11.740–741), and in the wish τότε μοι χάνοι εὐρεῖα χθών, “then may the wide earth gape for me,” which Homer uses twice (4.182, Il. 8.150).

## Use

{{quote:2af136c1-2ed3-54a5-a017-c97315b8b133}}

Agamemnon ends his lament over Menelaus with the wish. He has imagined a Trojan leaping on his brother's tomb and mocking the failed expedition ([[overweening]], [[tis-speech]]), and he would rather the earth swallowed him than hear it. In Book 8 Diomedes speaks the same half-line after imagining Hector boasting that the son of Tydeus fled before him (Il. 8.146–150). Both wishes answer an enemy's imagined words, and both put shame before death ([[aidos]]). The earth has a second part in the lament: it “will rot your bones as you lie in Troy” (4.174–175).
