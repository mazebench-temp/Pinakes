## Meaning

κυνάμυια = κύων, κυν- (“dog”) + μυῖα (“fly”). The word is found only in these two verses. Walter Leaf finds no compound like it in Homer, and compares only a few such words of later Greek, ἱππαλεκτρυών, “horse-cock”, and ἰατρόμαντις, “physician-seer”; he prints κυνόμυια, and calls the -α- of the manuscripts and editions indefensible (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.394). The ancient explanation that he quotes joins two vices: ὁ μὲν γὰρ κύων ἀναιδής, ἡ δὲ μυῖα θρασεῖα, “the dog is shameless, the fly bold” (see the translation's note on 21.394). The dog is Homer's image of shamelessness (see [[dogs]], [[dog-face]] and [[shameless-dog]]), and the fly is bold: Athena herself once put into Menelaus “the daring of a fly”, which keeps on trying to bite however often it is driven off (17.570; see [[the-daring-of-a-fly]] and [[flies]]).

## Use

{{quote:39f65814-90cb-5936-91b8-525e92e33b40}}

Ares opens the battle of the gods with it, springing at Athena (21.391–395; see [[ares]], [[athena]] and [[the-battle-of-the-gods]]). Her offense is to drive the gods together in strife, with θάρσος ἄητον, “your gale of daring” (21.395; see [[tharsos]]): the insect's boldness stands beside its name. Athena answers with a stone (21.403–408). Then Hera turns the word on Aphrodite, who is leading the fallen Ares away:

{{quote:634476c1-4e4b-5592-8be5-7ff97c0971a1}}

καὶ δʼ αὖθʼ, “there again”, is the language of vexation, and the article ἡ, “that”, is contemptuous (Leaf on 21.421). Athena runs after Aphrodite and strikes her down (21.423–426; see [[aphrodite]] and [[hera]]). In the same battle Hera calls Artemis “you shameless dog” (κύον ἀδεές, 21.481), so that three goddesses are called dogs in less than a hundred verses.
