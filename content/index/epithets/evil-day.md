## Meaning

κακόν (“evil, bad”) + ἦμαρ (“day”): the day of disaster, death and the fall of a city. Homer has a family of such “days”: the day of freedom (ἐλεύθερον ἦμαρ, 6.455) and the day of slavery (δούλιον ἦμαρ, 6.463), the pitiless day (νηλεὲς ἦμαρ, [[pitiless]]), the day of fate (αἴσιμον ἦμαρ, 8.72). κακὸν ἦμαρ stands at the end of the verse, usually as the object of a verb of warding off: to ward off the evil day from the Danaans (9.251) or the Aetolians (9.597), or, in the oath of Hera and Athena and again of the river Scamander, never to ward it off from the Trojans (20.315 = 21.374; [[ravening]]). In the Odyssey Eurylochus hopes the companions may still escape it (Od. 10.269), Hermes gives Odysseus the herb that will keep it from his head (Od. 10.288), and Telemachus wonders whether Zeus will bring it on the suitors before the marriage (Od. 15.524).

## Use

{{quote:39ce92f6-0b0e-5b03-9b99-1626e7151be7}}

The formula comes twice in Book 9, in two of the envoys' speeches, and its two uses make an argument. Odysseus urges Achilles to consider “how you will ward off the evil day from the Danaans” before it is too late (9.249–251). Phoenix's story ends with Meleager doing just that, too late: “so he warded off the evil day from the Aetolians, yielding to his own heart; but they no longer paid him the gifts” (9.597–599; [[meleager]], [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). The man who wards off the evil day without the gifts loses the honor the gifts would have brought (9.600–605). See [[achilles]].
