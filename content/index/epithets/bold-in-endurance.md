## Meaning

θρασυμέμνων joins θρασύς (“bold”) to a second element from the root of μένω (“stand fast, endure”), or perhaps of μέμονα (“be eager”): “bold and steadfast,” or “bold of purpose.” The analysis is not certain. The translation renders it “bold in endurance.” Homer uses it twice, both times of Heracles and both times in the same pair, θρασυμέμνονα θυμολέοντα: in Book 5 and when Odysseus sees Alcmene among the dead, who “bore Heracles, bold in endurance, lion-hearted, lying in the arms of great Zeus” (Od. 11.266–268). The same θρασ- is in θάρσος (“daring”; [[tharsos]]).

## Use

{{quote:7400d433-c36f-5e27-9b06-3e4f3c1393ad}}

Tlepolemus, son of Heracles, sets his father against the man he is about to fight. Sarpedon, he says, is called a son of Zeus falsely, since he falls far short of the men born of Zeus in the old days; but Heracles was bold in endurance and lion-hearted, and once sacked Troy with six ships ([[heracles-sacks-troy]], [[son-of-zeus]]). The pair of epithets is a son's praise of a father, and it serves as a taunt. Sarpedon answers that Heracles destroyed Ilios because of Laomedon's folly, and kills Tlepolemus (5.648–659; [[tlepolemus-and-sarpedon]]).
