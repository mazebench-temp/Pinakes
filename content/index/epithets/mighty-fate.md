## Meaning

κραταιός is formed from κράτος (“strength, mastery”), like κρατερός ([[krateros]]): “mighty, overpowering.” With μοῖρα (“portion, fate”; [[moira]]) it closes the verse, most often after death: “purple death and mighty fate” seize a man's eyes (5.83; Il. 16.334, 20.477), and “death and mighty fate” stand near a man about to die, as Patroclus tells Hector and Thetis tells Achilles (Il. 16.852–853, 24.131–132). Achilles tells Lycaon, before he kills him, that “for me too there are death and mighty fate” (Il. 21.110). Fate is sometimes a power in person: the horse Xanthus says that not he but “a great god and mighty Fate” will kill Achilles (Il. 19.409–410). κραταιός is used also of a beast of prey (Il. 11.119) and of Zeus and Poseidon, the two mighty sons of Cronus (Il. 13.345). See [[thanatos]].

## Use

{{quote:25287042-9cd7-5eda-a487-cfe698409f20}}

Eurypylus shears off Hypsenor's arm with his sword, and death and fate seize his eyes ([[hypsenor]], [[eurypylus]]). The second use sends a man to his death before he fights: “Tlepolemus, son of Heracles, tall and brave, was roused against godlike Sarpedon by mighty fate” (5.628–629; [[brave]]). Fate brings the grandson and the son of Zeus together, and Tlepolemus dies at Sarpedon's hand (5.655–659; [[tlepolemus-and-sarpedon]]).
