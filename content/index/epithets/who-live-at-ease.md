## Meaning

θεοί (“gods”) + ῥεῖα (“easily”) + ζώοντες (“living,” from ζώω): “the gods who live easily.” The formula closes the verse. Homer uses it three times: here, and twice in the Odyssey, when the phantom that Athena sends tells Penelope that “the gods who live at ease” do not let her weep (Od. 4.805), and when Calypso reproaches the gods who begrudged Dawn her mortal lover Orion (Od. 5.118–124). Compare [[blessed-gods]] (μάκαρες θεοί) and [[gods-who-live-forever]] (θεοὶ αἰὲν ἐόντες). Achilles states the contrast plainly to Priam: the gods have spun it for wretched mortals to live in grief, while they themselves are free from care (Il. 24.525–526).

The formula of the gods' ease comes in the verse of their anger. Their life is easy, and their punishment of a mortal is total: blindness from the son of Cronus, and a short life (see [[lycurgus]], [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]] and [[who-fights-the-gods]]). Four verses later Diomedes names men by their own formula, “the mortals who eat the fruit of the field” (6.142; [[who-eat-the-fruit-of-the-field]]). The two phrases define the two races by the way they live, and between them stands his conclusion: “Nor would I wish to fight with the blessed gods” (6.141).
