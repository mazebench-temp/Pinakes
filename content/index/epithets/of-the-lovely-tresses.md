## Meaning

καλλιπλόκαμος = κάλλος, καλός (“beauty, beautiful”) + πλόκαμος (“lock, braid,” from πλέκω, “plait”): “with beautiful braids.” It is formed like [[of-the-lovely-ankles]] (καλλίσφυρος) and is close in sense to [[with-lovely-tresses]] (ἐϋπλόκαμος), with καλός for εὖ; the translation renders both “of the lovely tresses.” Hera, dressing for Zeus, plaits her own hair, πλοκάμους ἔπλεξε, “she plaited the shining tresses” (14.176; [[phaeinos]]). Homer uses the epithet six times: of Demeter (14.326); of Thetis, to whom Hephaestus owes his life (18.407) and who is the mother of Achilles (20.207); of Ariadne, for whom Daedalus made a dancing floor in Cnossus (18.590–592); and in the Odyssey of Circe, at whose doors Odysseus' men and then Odysseus himself stand (*Odyssey* 10.220, 10.310). Compare [[lovely-haired]] (ἠΰκομος).

## Use

{{quote:e3f87abb-2f75-5513-8021-e237cb0cb9a7}}

Zeus' list of his loves is full of epithets: Peirithous is “a counselor equal to the gods,” Danae is “of the lovely ankles,” Perseus “preeminent among all men,” Phoenix “far-famed,” Rhadamanthys “godlike,” Heracles “a fierce-hearted son,” Leto “glorious,” and Demeter “of the lovely tresses, the queen,” the last but one before Zeus comes to Hera herself (14.317–328; [[demeter]], [[the-loves-of-zeus]], [[counselor-equal-to-the-gods]], [[of-the-lovely-ankles]], [[preeminent]], [[famous]], [[antitheos]], [[fierce-hearted]], [[glorious]]). It is the only time the Iliad speaks of Demeter's union with Zeus.

## Outside Homer

The Homeric Hymn to Apollo gives the epithet to Leto, as she is about to give birth to Apollo on Delos (*Hymn* 3.101). Hesiod tells that Zeus came to the bed of Demeter, πολυφόρβη, “all-nourishing,” and that she bore Persephone (*Theogony* 912–913; [[all-nourishing]]).
