## Meaning

The origin of ἠθεῖος is uncertain; it is often connected with ἦθος (“accustomed place, custom”), as if “familiar, one's own.” In Homer it is a word of address, in the Iliad nearly always to a brother: Paris to Hector (6.518), Menelaus to Agamemnon when he finds him arming by night (Il. 10.37), and Athena, in the likeness of Deiphobus, to Hector (Il. 22.229, 22.239). Achilles greets the ghost of Patroclus as ἠθείη κεφαλή, “dear head”, “dear heart” in the translation (Il. 23.94). In the Odyssey Eumaeus says that he calls his absent master ἠθεῖος even when he is far away (Od. 14.147). Compare [[my-dear]] (πέπον), the address of comrades, and [[strange-one]] (δαιμόνιε), the address of reproach.

## Use

Paris catches up with Hector at the gates:

{{quote:35770d6a-7564-5907-90d2-8b46beb646fc}}

He opens with the word of a brother's respect and an apology. Hector answers with δαιμόνιε, “Strange one,” and a judgment: no fair-minded man would dishonor Paris' work in battle, since he is valiant, but he holds back of his own will (6.521–523; [[strange-one]], [[strong-spear]]). The address comes back at Hector's death. Athena, disguised as Deiphobus, calls him ἠθεῖε twice, promises to stand beside him, and leaves him alone before Achilles (Il. 22.226–247, 22.294–299; [[paris]], [[hector]]).

## Outside Homer

The Suda explains ἠθεῖε as a word used neither simply of a brother nor of an elder, but as a respectful form of address, with which a younger man addresses an elder brother (Suda η 153).
