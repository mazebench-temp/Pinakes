## Meaning

δειλός means “wretched, miserable,” and also “cowardly,” as when Achilles says he would be called “a coward and a nobody” if he gave way to Agamemnon in everything (1.293). With the cry ἆ it opens a speech to someone whose lot the speaker sees and he himself does not. In the Iliad it is Odysseus' word to Socus, wounded and then dead (11.441, 11.452); Patroclus' word for the Achaean leaders, who are to glut the dogs of Troy (11.816); Hector's to the dying Patroclus (16.837); Zeus' to Hector as he puts on the armor of Achilles, not knowing that his death is near (17.201); Zeus' to the immortal horses of Achilles as they weep for Patroclus (ἆ δειλώ, 17.443); and Achilles' to Priam (24.518). It is common in the Odyssey as well. The tone runs from contempt to pity, and the last use in the Iliad, Achilles' to Priam, is the gentlest. Compare [[hard]] (σχέτλιος) and [[strange-one]] (δαιμόνιε), other words of address.

## Use

{{quote:1a818b9c-d039-5af2-807c-d9aa1699534c}}

Socus has driven his spear through Odysseus' shield and corselet and torn the skin from his ribs. Odysseus knows the wound is not deadly and answers: “Ah, wretch, sheer destruction is surely overtaking you” (11.441; [[steep]]). Socus turns to run and is speared in the back, and Odysseus begins his boast over the body with the same words: his father and mother will not close his eyes, but birds will tear him (11.450–455; [[eating-raw-flesh]], [[queenly]]). Later Patroclus, pitying the wounded Eurypylus, turns the address on his own side: “Ah, wretched leaders and rulers of the Danaans” (11.816–818; [[white]], [[swift-dogs]]). See [[odysseus]], [[socus]] and [[patroclus]].
