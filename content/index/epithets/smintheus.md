## Meaning

Smintheus is a cult title, not a descriptive epithet. Ancient sources connect it with σμίνθος, said to be a local word for “mouse,” so that Smintheus would be Apollo “of the mice.” Others tied it to local place-names, of which Strabo lists several in the Troad. Neither explanation can be tested from Homer, who says nothing of mice.

Modern readers who link rodents with plague should note that the poem makes no such connection. Chryses invokes Smintheus as the lord of the shrine he has served, and it is Apollo's arrows, not any animal, that bring the sickness.

## Use

{{quote:b2d52fe4-6282-554d-8148-4c0f865af324}}

Chryses' prayer names Apollo by his local titles and places: the god of the silver bow who guards Chryse and holy Cilla and rules Tenedos ([[god-of-the-silver-bow]], [[zatheos]]), and then Smintheus, set emphatically at the start of the verse. The cult title reminds the god of his own sanctuary and of the priest who has served it: “if ever I roofed a temple to your pleasure.” The title does not return in the second prayer, which repeats the opening two lines and then goes its own way ({{ref:Iliad 1.451-456}}).

## Outside Homer

Strabo (*Geography* 13.1.48) describes the Smintheum at Chryse in his own day. Its statue of Apollo, by Scopas of Paros, had a mouse under the god's foot, as a sign of the name's meaning. He also reports a foundation story: settlers from Crete were told by an oracle to stay where “the earth-born” attacked them, and they did so when field mice gnawed the leather of their gear by night. A Hellenistic temple of Apollo Smintheus survives at Gülpınar in the Troad.
