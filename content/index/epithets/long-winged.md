## Meaning

τανυπτέρυξ = τανυ- (“stretched, long,” from τανύω, “stretch”) + πτέρυξ (“wing”): “with outstretched wings,” a bird in flight. The Iliad has it twice: of the birds of omen in Hector's answer to Polydamas (12.237), and of the ἅρπη, a bird of prey whose likeness Athena takes as she leaps down from the sky through the upper air to drop nectar and ambrosia into Achilles' breast, so that hunger may not weaken him (19.347–354). The Odyssey has τανυσίπτερος, from the same τανυ- and πτερόν, of the birds that roost in the wood around Calypso's cave (Od. 5.63–65) and of thrushes or doves caught in a net, in the simile of the hanged maids (Od. 22.468–469). See [[birds]].

## Use

{{quote:390f4e53-ec32-5e6c-b60e-3e6e82713603}}

Hector sets the god's promise against the birds: “and you tell me to obey long-winged birds, which I do not heed at all” (12.237–238; [[loud-thundering]], [[divination]]). The epithet, which describes the bird in flight, the thing the seer watches, comes in the speech that refuses to watch it; two verses later οἰωνός, “bird,” becomes “omen,” in Hector's one omen, to fight for one's native land (12.243; [[one-omen-is-best]]). See [[hector]] and [[polydamas]].
