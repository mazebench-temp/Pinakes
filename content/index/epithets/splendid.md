## Meaning

ἀγλαός means “bright, shining,” and then “splendid, glorious.” It belongs with ἀγλαΐη (“splendor”) and the verb ἀγάλλομαι (“glory in, delight in”); its further origin is uncertain. Of things it describes what is fine to look at: gifts and ransom ([[apoina]]; ἀγλαὰ ἄποινα, “splendid ransom,” {{ref:Iliad 1.23}}), water, a grove. Of people it is a general word of praise, used above all in the phrase ἀγλαὸς υἱός, “splendid son.”

## Use

Book 2 uses it for a spring, a sanctuary, and three pairs of father and son.

At Aulis the sacrifice that brought the portent was offered “beneath a fine plane tree, where bright water flowed”:

{{quote:4f8cabb1-6790-5f7b-aee6-dfca34d17b3e}}

Here the adjective keeps its old sense of brightness. In the Catalogue it names the grove of Poseidon at Onchestus, “the splendid grove” ({{ref:Iliad 2.506}}), and it names sons by their fathers: Eurypylus, “the splendid son of Euaemon” ({{ref:Iliad 2.736}}); Pandarus, “the splendid son of Lycaon” ({{ref:Iliad 2.826}}); and Nastes and Amphimachus, “the splendid children of Nomion” ({{ref:Iliad 2.871}}). The last is ironic. One of those splendid children came to war dressed in gold like a girl and was killed ({{ref:Iliad 2.872-875}}).
