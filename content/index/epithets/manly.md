## Meaning

ἀγαπήνωρ = ἀγαπάω (“love, be content with”) + ἀνήρ (“man”), as in ἠνορέη (“manliness,” 8.226): “loving manhood,” manly. The same compound is the name of Agapenor, son of Ancaeus, who led the Arcadians to Troy in sixty ships (2.603–614; [[agapenor]]). The pattern keeps the name apart by its capital, as with [[proud]] and Agenor ([[agenor]]).

The epithet is used five times in the Iliad: of Eurymedon, Nestor's attendant, here; of Polydamas, to whom the Trojans run in Book 13 (13.756); of Eurypylus, in whose hut Patroclus sits tending his wound (15.390–392); and twice of Idomeneus, in the verse “Meriones, the attendant of manly Idomeneus” (23.113, 23.124). In the Odyssey it is given to Laodamas, son of Alcinous (Od. 7.170). In Book 8 the epithet describes an attendant (θεράπων); in Book 23 it describes the master, in the verse about his attendant.

## Use

{{quote:95ffd98d-3d19-5b06-b075-3388c6f2539f}}

Diomedes has taken Nestor into his chariot, and the two attendants, Diomedes' companion Sthenelus and Nestor's Eurymedon, take charge of Nestor's team (8.105–115; [[sthenelus]], [[eurymedon-attendant-of-nestor]], [[mighty]]). Eurymedon is not Agamemnon's charioteer of the same name (4.228; [[eurymedon]]). The two attendants of Book 8 receive a warrior's epithets, “mighty” and “manly,” in the verse in which they take charge of the old man's horses. See [[nestor]].
