## Meaning

ἀδάμαστος = ἀ- (“not”) + the root of δαμάζω, δάμνημι (“tame, subdue,” of horses, of women given in marriage, and of enemies killed in battle): “not to be tamed,” a word that Homer uses only once, in Agamemnon's speech (9.158). The same root runs through the verse in which it stands: δμηθήτω, “let him be tamed,” and ἀδάμαστος, “untamable,” as the translation keeps them. Compare [[tamer-of-horses]] (ἱππόδαμος), from the same verb, and [[implacable]], its partner in the verse. See [[privative-adjectives]].

## Use

{{quote:07e672bc-3b87-50b2-8055-78166e3440b0}}

Agamemnon's argument is that a man who will not be tamed is like the god whom mortals hate most, and that Achilles should give way to the man who is more kingly and older (9.158–161; [[time]]). Odysseus does not repeat it. When Phoenix speaks, he takes up the same verb in a gentler form: “tame your great spirit” (δάμασον θυμὸν μέγαν, 9.496), since even the gods can be turned ([[pitiless]]). See [[hades]] and [[agamemnon]].
