## Meaning

κακομήχανος = κακός (“evil”) + μῆχος, μηχανή (“means, contrivance”): “contriving evil, mischievous.” Homer uses it three times. Helen uses it of herself (6.344). In Book 9 Odysseus reminds Achilles of Peleus' parting advice, to keep his proud heart in check and to “cease from evil-scheming strife” (ἔριδος κακομηχάνου, Il. 9.254–258; [[eris]]). And in the Odyssey Penelope calls Antinous “evil-scheming” when she learns that he has plotted to kill her son (Od. 16.418).

The three genitives, κυνὸς κακομηχάνου ὀκρυοέσσης, agree with the “me” in ἐμεῖο: literally “brother-in-law of me, an evil-scheming, chilling dog.” It is the construction of her self-reproach on the wall in Book 3, where κυνώπιδος, “dog-faced,” agrees with the “me” implied in ἐμός (3.180; [[dog-face]], [[chilling]]). The word she chooses makes her the contriver of the war's evils, the thing Priam would not say of her: “you are not to blame for anything in my eyes” (3.164; [[who-is-to-blame]]). She ends the speech with the other side of the matter: Zeus set an evil fate on her and Paris, so that they might be a theme of song for men to come (6.357–358; [[helen]]).
