## Meaning

μεγακήτης = μέγας (“great”) + κῆτος, the word for a sea monster, such as the monster from which Heracles took refuge behind a wall on the Trojan shore (20.145–148). The compound may mean “huge as a sea monster” or “with a great hollow,” and its uses suit either: ships, a dolphin, and the sea. The translation renders the ship's epithet “huge in the hull.”

It describes a ship three times. Two are the same verse, in which a figure takes a stand on Odysseus' ship to be heard by the whole camp: Agamemnon in Book 8, and Strife, sent by Zeus, at the start of Book 11 (8.222–226, 11.5–9). The third is the ship on whose stern Achilles stands to watch the rout of the Achaeans (11.599–601). In a simile the Trojans flee from Achilles into the river as small fish flee from a huge dolphin (21.22–24), and in the Odyssey a god smooths the great sea for Nestor's fleet (Od. 3.158).

## Use

{{quote:388fe7b0-bf54-5147-9e93-387bcfdf4c79}}

Hera has put it in Agamemnon's mind to rally the army (8.218–219; [[queenly]]). He carries his purple cloak in his hand ([[cloak]], [[strong-hand]]) and takes his stand on Odysseus' ship, because it lies in the middle of the line, and from there a shout carries to both ends, where Ajax and Achilles have drawn up their ships, trusting in their manhood ([[balanced-ships]]). The huge ship at the center is the platform for the king's voice, and his rebuke follows (8.227–244; [[empty-boasting]]). The verses that name Ajax and Achilles at the ends of the line (8.224–226) are reported to have been rejected here in antiquity, since Achilles is out of the fighting; in Book 11 the same verses place Strife on the ship (11.7–9).
