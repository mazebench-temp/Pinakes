## Meaning

φύλοπις is an old word for battle whose origin is unknown; the translation renders it “the din of battle.” αἰνός means “dread, terrible” ([[most-dread]], [[grim-combat]]). The phrase φύλοπις αἰνή ends the verse, often in the half-line Τρώων καὶ Ἀχαιῶν φύλοπις αἰνή, “the dread din of battle between the Trojans and the Achaeans” (4.65; compare Il. 5.379, 6.1). Book 4 pairs it with πόλεμος κακός, “evil war,” in the whole-line formula πόλεμόν τε κακὸν καὶ φύλοπιν αἰνήν (4.15, 4.82).

## Use

The formula marks the gods' choice and the men's question. Zeus puts the alternative to the gods: “whether we shall rouse evil war again and the dread din of battle, or put friendship between the two sides” (4.15–16). Hera asks that Athena be sent “into the dread din of battle” (4.65). When Athena comes down like a star, the soldiers of both armies say the same words to one another, wondering whether war will come again or friendship be set between them by Zeus (4.82–84; [[dispenser-of-war]]). The men repeat the gods' alternative almost word for word, not knowing that it has already been decided ([[philotes]], [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]]). The Odyssey ends with the same question. Athena asks Zeus whether he will make evil war and the dread din of battle among the Ithacans, or set friendship between the two sides (Od. 24.472–476).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod uses the same pair for the wars that destroyed the race of heroes at seven-gated Thebes and at Troy: “evil war and the dread din of battle” (*Works and Days* 161–165; [[seven-gated]]).
