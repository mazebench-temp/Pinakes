## Meaning

Merry reports two explanations. The grammarians glossed ἀλφησταί as “shrewd, inventive,” which agrees with a derivation from the root of ἀλφάνω, “earn, win”: men as hard-working, “gain-getting” creatures, in contrast with the gods who live at ease; he cites Aeschylus for this sense (*Seven against Thebes* 770). Others took it as “bread-eating,” from ἄλφιτον (“barley meal”) and ἔδω (“eat”), like σιτοφάγος, “eating bread,” of a man (*Odyssey* 9.191), and the phrases for men “who eat the fruit of the field”; he finds this sense in Sophocles (*Philoctetes* 707). Both views are reported here as Merry gives them (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.347). The translation renders the word “toiling” (see the Odyssey translation's note on 1.349). Compare [[who-eat-the-fruit-of-the-field]].

## Use

It is found three times, all in the Odyssey, always in the plural with ἀνήρ. Telemachus says that Zeus gives “to toiling men, to each, as he wills” (*Odyssey* 1.349); Nausithous settled the Phaeacians in Scheria, “far from toiling men” (*Odyssey* 6.7–8); and in the false tale Odysseus tells Athena, Orsilochus of Crete outran toiling men with his swift feet (*Odyssey* 13.259–261).
