## Meaning

This οὖλος (“destructive, deadly”) belongs with ὀλοός and οὐλόμενος, from the root of ὄλλυμι (“destroy”). The wrath in the first lines of the Iliad is οὐλομένη, “ruinous” ({{ref:Iliad 1.2}}; [[menis]]). Homer gives the adjective to Ares (οὖλος Ἄρης, Il. 5.461; οὖλον Ἄρηα, Il. 5.717) and once to Achilles, whom Priam fears as “the deadly man” (Il. 21.536).

It must be kept apart from two words of the same sound: οὖλος “woolly, curly,” of hair and fleece, and οὖλος “whole,” the Ionic form of ὅλος (ἄρτον οὖλον, “a whole loaf,” Od. 17.343).

## Use

The Dream is called οὖλος twice, three lines apart at the start of Book 2, and nowhere else in Homer:

{{quote:de36e79b-2678-5b3a-9b3c-c57b9c8f5849}}

The narrator names it by its effect before it has done anything. Zeus then addresses it with the same epithet as he gives it his orders, βάσκ’ ἴθι, οὖλε ὄνειρε (“Go, baneful Dream,” {{ref:Iliad 2.8}}). The Dream itself is not harmful; it only carries a message. The harm is in what the message sets in motion. Agamemnon will lead the army into battle believing that Troy will fall that day, and the plan of Zeus, to honor Achilles by the death of many Achaeans ({{ref:Iliad 2.3-4}}; [[plan-of-zeus]]), begins with it.

Once the Dream reaches Agamemnon it has a new epithet. It speaks as the “divine Dream” ([[divine]], θεῖος ὄνειρος, {{ref:Iliad 2.22}}), and Agamemnon calls it that when he reports it to the council ({{ref:Iliad 2.56}}). The audience knows it as baneful; the king knows it as divine.
