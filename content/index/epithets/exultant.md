## Meaning

ὑπερκύδας (genitive ὑπερκύδαντος) = ὑπέρ (“over, beyond”) + κῦδος (“glory, the radiance of success”; [[kydos]]): “glorious beyond measure,” or “exulting in glory.” In Homer it occurs only in one verse, spoken twice in Book 4 (4.66, 4.71). Whether it is plain praise or hints at pride cannot be decided from Homer; Hesiod's use points to pride (see Outside Homer). The translation's “exultant” leaves room for both.

## Use

{{quote:6641d430-827c-5d56-95ba-0ffbb6f9fa71}}

Hera proposes the plan, and Zeus gives it to Athena in the same words (4.64–67, 4.70–72): she is to contrive that the Trojans are the first to break the oaths. The Achaeans are exultant because they have won the duel, and Agamemnon has claimed the victory under the terms of the oath (3.455–461). The gods' plan turns on that moment. The oath will be broken against men who believe the war is won, and the side that breaks it will be in the wrong ([[oaths-and-their-breaking]], [[the-bargain-of-zeus-and-hera]]). The Trojans get the opposite word from Agamemnon, who calls them “overweening” (4.176; [[overweening]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod gives the word to Menoetius, the brother of Atlas and Prometheus, whom Zeus struck down to Erebus with a thunderbolt “for his recklessness and his overbearing manhood” (*Theogony* 510, 514–516).
