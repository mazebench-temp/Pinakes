## Meaning

εὐρυσθενής = εὐρύς (“wide, broad”) + σθένος (“strength”): “whose strength reaches far.” The first part is the one in [[wide-ruling]] (εὐρὺ κρείων), a title that Homer gives to Agamemnon and once to Poseidon himself (11.751), and in [[far-seeing]] (εὐρύοπα) and [[wide-sky]]; the same two elements make the name of Eurystheus, the king who set Heracles his labors (19.123, 19.133). The strength that reaches far suits the god who shakes the earth and who received the gray sea as his share when the three sons of Cronus divided the world by lot (15.187–193).

Homer uses the word only in the vocative, in one address to Poseidon that opens the verse: ὢ πόποι, ἐννοσίγαιʼ εὐρυσθενές, “Ah, shaker of the earth, wide in strength.” Zeus speaks it here and in the Odyssey, in the same whole verse (7.455; Od. 13.140), and Hera once (8.201). See [[shaker-of-the-earth]].

## Use

Zeus is answering Poseidon's complaint about the Achaean wall (7.446–453; [[earth-shaker]]). His first words grant what the complaint was about. Poseidon fears that the fame of his own work will be forgotten, and Zeus begins by calling him wide in strength, then tells him that only a weaker god, “one far feebler than you in hands and might,” could fear such a thing (7.455–457). The epithet is the first answer to his fear. See [[poseidon]] and [[the-achaean-wall]].
