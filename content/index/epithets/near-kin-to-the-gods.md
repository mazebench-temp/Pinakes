## Meaning

ἀγχίθεος = ἄγχι (“near”) + θεός (“god”): “near to the gods.” The translation renders it “near kin to the gods,” and Merry takes it of their divine lineage (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.34). Their kings descend from Poseidon: Nausithous, who settled them in Scheria (*Odyssey* 6.7–8), was his son by Periboea, daughter of the king of the Giants, and Nausithous was the father of Alcinous (*Odyssey* 7.56–63). Alcinous himself says that the gods have always appeared to them openly when they sacrifice, and dine sitting beside them, “since we are near to them,” as are the Cyclopes and the tribes of the Giants (*Odyssey* 7.201–206). Merry sets beside the word Poseidon's διοτρεφέεσσι, an epithet of kings given here to the whole people (Merry on 5.378; [[zeus-nurtured]]).

## Use

The word is used only of the Phaeacians, and only in one verse, “the land of the Phaeacians, who are near kin to the gods” (*Odyssey* 5.35, 19.279). Zeus speaks it when he sends Hermes to Calypso: Odysseus will come to their land on the twentieth day, and they “will honor him with all their hearts like a god” and send him home (*Odyssey* 5.34–40). The disguised Odysseus speaks it to Penelope in *Odyssey* 19, telling her that the sea cast Odysseus ashore there and that they honored him with all their hearts like a god (*Odyssey* 19.278–282): the plan of Zeus comes back as news of what has been done. See [[phaeacians]] and [[scheria]].

## Outside Homer

In the *Hymn to Aphrodite* the goddess tells Anchises that “of all mortal men those nearest to the gods” in looks and stature have always come from his race, and names Ganymede, whom Zeus carried off for his beauty, and Tithonus, whom Dawn carried off (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 200–218).
