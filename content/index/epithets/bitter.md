## Meaning

πικρός means “sharp, pointed,” and then “bitter” to the taste and to the feelings. In the Iliad it is above all the epithet of the arrow, in the formula πικρὸς ὀϊστός, which recurs wherever archers shoot (for example Il. 8.323, 23.867). It also describes things that sting: the “bitter root” that Patroclus lays on the wound of Eurypylus to kill the pain (Il. 11.846–847), and the bitter pangs of childbirth (Il. 11.271). Compare [[piercing]] (ἐχεπευκής) and [[sharp]] (ὀξύς).

## Use

{{quote:7057652a-ac2f-5bda-9217-2029e5b26a5a}}

Pandarus' arrow gathers a string of epithets: “feathered, never shot before, a source of black pains” (4.117; [[black-pains]]), “bitter” (4.118), “sharp-pointed” (4.126; [[sharp]]), and, as Athena wards it off, “the piercing shaft” (4.129; [[piercing]]). Athena had called it simply “a swift arrow” (4.94). The formula of the bitter arrow follows it to the end: it falls on Menelaus' belt (4.134), and Machaon looks at the wound “where the bitter arrow had struck” (4.217) before he sucks out the blood and lays on his drugs ([[soothing-drugs]]). In Book 5 Pandarus' next bitter arrow strikes Diomedes in the shoulder (Il. 5.95–110). See [[arrows]], [[pandarus-breaks-the-truce]].
