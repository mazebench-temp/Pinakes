## Meaning

δυσμενής = δυσ- (“ill”) + μένος (“spirit, intent”; [[menos]]): “ill-disposed, hostile.” In Homer it most often names the enemy, as a noun, δυσμενέες, “enemies” (as in Hector's “a joy to your enemies,” 3.51), and in the formula ἀνδράσι δυσμενέεσσι, “to hostile men,” which begins or ends the verse. The formula occurs seven times: twice in Books 5 and 6, three times more in the Iliad (Il. 17.158, 19.168, 19.232), and twice in the Odyssey (Od. 3.90, 22.234).

## Use

Both uses in Books 5 and 6 are in speeches that picture the Trojans conquered. Sarpedon warns Hector that if the Trojans are caught as in a net, they will “become the prey and spoil of hostile men” (5.487–488; [[the-net]]). In Book 6 Hector foresees his own family's fate: he cares less for the Trojans' sorrow, for Hecuba and Priam, or for his brothers, “who, many and brave, may fall in the dust at the hands of hostile men,” than for Andromache's slavery (6.450–455; [[captive-women]]). The enemy is named not as persons, but as a force into whose hands one falls ([[hector]], [[andromache]]).
