## Meaning

σχέτλιος is probably formed from σχε-, the root of ἔχω (“hold”) in σχεῖν: one who holds on and will not give way, hard, stubborn, cruel. In Homer it is a word of reproach, said of someone or to someone, never a fixed epithet of a name. The translation renders it “hard” (2.112, 5.403, 8.361), and once, in Aphrodite's anger at Helen, “wretched” (3.414).

It is said of those whom the speaker cannot move. Of Zeus: by Agamemnon, in the speech with which he tests the army in Book 2 and again in earnest in Book 9, “hard god, who once promised me and nodded his assent” that he would sack Ilios and go home (2.111–113, 9.18–20); and by Athena here. Of Heracles, by Dione: “Hard man, doer of violent deeds,” who shot arrows at the gods (5.403–404; [[doer-of-violent-deeds]]). Of Achilles, by Ajax in the embassy (9.628–630), by the Myrmidons, as Achilles reports their complaint (16.203), and by Priam and Hecuba (22.41, 22.86). Diomedes says it fondly to Nestor, who never rests from his labor (10.164); Glaucus says it to Hector, who left Sarpedon's body (17.150); Achilles says it of Patroclus, who did not heed his warning (18.13); and Apollo says it to the gods, who let Achilles abuse Hector's body (24.33).

## Use

Hera has asked Athena whether they will no longer care for the dying Danaans, and Athena answers with a complaint against her father: “but my father rages with a mind that is not good, hard as he is, forever perverse, the thwarter of my purposes” (8.360–361). The verse stacks three reproaches. ἀλιτρός (“offender, perverse one”) is the word Antilochus uses when he would rather give up his prize than be “an offender against the gods” (23.595), and Calypso calls Odysseus a rogue with it (Od. 5.182); ἀπερωεύς, “thwarter,” is found only here. Like Agamemnon in Book 2, she complains of a Zeus who does not give what she thinks she is owed: she saved his son Heracles many times, and he has granted the wishes of Thetis instead (8.362–372; [[heracles]], [[shrewd-mind]], [[thetis-supplicates-zeus]]). She expects his mood to change: “Yet the day will come when he calls me his dear gray-eyed one again” (8.373; [[gray-eyed]]). See [[athena]] and [[zeus]].
