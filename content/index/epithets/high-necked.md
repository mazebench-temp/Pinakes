## Meaning

ἐριαύχην = ἐρι- (“very,” the intensive prefix of [[loud-thundering]] ἐρίγδουπος and [[trusty]] ἐρίηρος) + αὐχήν (“neck”): “with a high, strong neck.” It describes horses five times in the Iliad: the two that Hector promises for a spy (10.305); the horses that rattle empty chariots along the lines of battle as Agamemnon cuts down their drivers (11.159–160); the team of Achilles, driven by Automedon, which Hector and Aeneas hope to capture (17.495–497); the horses that Achilles will drive to exhaustion under the walls, Polydamas warns, before he goes back to the ships (18.279–281); and the four horses that Achilles throws on Patroclus' pyre (23.171–172). Compare [[single-hoofed]], [[fine-maned]], [[high-neighing]] and [[swift-footed]].

## Use

{{quote:88d07aed-bc91-508c-8a26-0450fbf6e588}}

Hector calls the Trojan leaders together and offers a reward for a spy: a chariot and “two horses with high-arched necks, the best there are at the swift ships of the Achaeans” (10.305–306; [[swift-ships]]). The prize is still to be taken from the enemy, and Hector does not name the horses. Dolon names them: he will go if Hector swears to give him the horses and chariot of Achilles (10.321–323; [[the-horses-of-achilles]], [[dolon]]). In Book 17 Hector and Aeneas set out to take that team, and the poet calls them fools (17.483–497; [[nepios]]). See [[hector]] and [[horses]].
