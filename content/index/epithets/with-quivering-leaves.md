## Meaning

εἰνοσίφυλλος is usually analyzed as ἔνοσις (“shaking,” from the root of ἐνοσίχθων, “earth-shaker,” the epithet of Poseidon) + φύλλον (“leaf”): “whose leaves shake,” a mountain covered in trees stirring in the wind. The first vowel is lengthened to εἰ- to fit the verse, as in εἰνοσίχθων. Homer uses the word four times, always at the end of the verse and always of one of two mountains: Neritum (Il. 2.632; Od. 9.22) and Pelion (Il. 2.757; Od. 11.316).

## Use

{{quote:9ffc8cb3-07d1-5bdc-9e58-ed1ffeaf54b1}}

Odysseus' entry in the Catalogue begins with his own island and its wooded mountain. When he names himself to the Phaeacians in the Odyssey he describes Ithaca the same way: “on it is a mountain, Neritum with its quivering leaves, conspicuous” (Od. 9.21–22). The other wooded mountain is Pelion, home of the Magnetes, from which Peirithous drove the Centaurs ({{ref:Iliad 2.743-744}}, {{ref:Iliad 2.757}}).
