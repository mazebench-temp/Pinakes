## Meaning

γέρων (“old man”) + ἅλιος (“of the sea”, from ἅλς, “salt, sea”) + νημερτής (“unerring”, from νη-, “not”, and the root of ἁμαρτάνω, “miss the mark”). The Old Man of the Sea is a title that Homer gives to more than one god: to Nereus, the father of Thetis, in the Iliad, and in the Odyssey to Phorcys and to Proteus ([[old-man-of-the-sea]], [[phorcys-the-sea-god]]). νημερτής is elsewhere a word for speech, the unerring counsel of the gods (*Odyssey* 1.86) or the truth that a questioner asks for, νημερτὲς ἐνίσπες, “tell me this truly” (*Odyssey* 4.314); of a person it is said only here.

## Use

The whole phrase is Proteus' alone, four times in Menelaus' tale (*Odyssey* 4.349, 4.384, 4.401, 4.542) and once more when Telemachus repeats it to Penelope (*Odyssey* 17.140). The epithet is the point of the ambush: the old man knows the depths of the whole sea and what has been done in Menelaus' halls, and when he has been held fast through all his changes he answers every question (*Odyssey* 4.385–393, 4.417–424, 4.461–569). See [[proteus]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod gives the same qualities to Nereus: Pontus begot Nereus, “unlying and truthful”, the eldest of his children, “and they call him the Old Man / because he is unerring and gentle, and does not forget what is right” (*Theogony* 233–236). One of his daughters is Nemertes, “who has the mind of her immortal father” (*Theogony* 262); the Iliad names her among the Nereids beside Apseudes, “Unlying” (*Iliad* 18.46; see [[nemertes]] and [[apseudes]]).
