## Meaning

μείλιχος, “gentle, mild, kind,” is akin to μειλίχιος, the adjective of soothing words ([[soothing-words]]); its opposite is ἀμείλιχος, “implacable,” the word for Hades (9.158). In the Iliad it describes a man's way with others. Menelaus says that Patroclus “knew how to be kind to all” (17.671); Briseis, lamenting him, weeps for one who was always gentle (19.300); and Andromache says over Hector that his father was not gentle in grim battle (24.739). In the Odyssey Eumaeus says that from his mistress one hears no kind word (*Odyssey* 15.374).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod sets the two sons of Night against each other: Sleep goes over the earth and the sea “quiet and gentle to men,” ἥσυχος … καὶ μείλιχος ἀνθρώποισι, while Death has a heart of iron and a pitiless spirit of bronze (*Theogony* 762–766).
