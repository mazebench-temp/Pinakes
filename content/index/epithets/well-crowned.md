## Meaning

ἐϋστέφανος = εὖ (“well”) + στέφανος (“crown, circlet,” from στέφω, “set around”): “well-crowned.” Of a goddess it is the circlet she wears; of a city, Walter Leaf explains, it means “well crowned with walls,” as the battlements of Troy are its κρήδεμνα, the veil that Achilles wished he and Patroclus alone might loose (16.100; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.99). In the Iliad it is used once of a city, Thebes, the city of Heracles' birth (ἐϋστεφάνῳ ἐνὶ Θήβῃ, 19.99), and once of a goddess, Artemis, as she answers Zeus after Hera has beaten her (21.511). Leaf notes that Homer gives it to a city only here; in the Odyssey Mycenae is ἐϋστέφανος among the women of old, beside Tyro and Alcmene (*Odyssey* 2.120), and Aphrodite is “well-crowned Cytherea” (*Odyssey* 8.288, 18.193). See [[thebes]], [[artemis]] and [[aphrodite]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod uses the phrase of Thebes in the same words when Harmonia bears Polydorus to Cadmus “in well-crowned Thebes” (*Theogony* 975–978), and in the *Shield of Heracles* Heracles tells Iolaus how Amphitryon came “to well-crowned Thebes” from Tiryns after he killed Electryon (*Shield of Heracles* 78–82). It is the standing epithet of Demeter in the *Homeric Hymn to Demeter* (224, 307, 384, 470) and in the *Works and Days* (300), and of Cytherea in the *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* (6, 175, 287).
