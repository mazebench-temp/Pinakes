## Meaning

κάρη κομόωντες = κάρη (“head,” an accusative of respect) + κομόωντες, participle of κομάω (“wear the hair long,” from κόμη, “hair”): “long-haired as to the head.” κομόωντες is the contracted form κομῶντες “stretched” by epic diction to four syllables to suit the verse, as ὁρῶ is stretched to ὁρόω.

Long hair is the mark of the free fighting man. Achilles has let his grow as an offering to the river Spercheios (Il. 23.141–151; [[fair-haired]]), and in mourning the hair is cut. The formula κάρη κομόωντες Ἀχαιοί fills the second half of the verse after the caesura in the middle of the third foot. It occurs thirty times in Homer, twenty-six of them in the Iliad.

## Use

The formula belongs to Book 2's commands and summonses. Zeus gives it to the Dream: bid Agamemnon arm the long-haired Achaeans ({{ref:Iliad 2.11}}). The Dream repeats the order word for word ({{ref:Iliad 2.28}}), and Agamemnon repeats it again to the council ({{ref:Iliad 2.65}}; [[message-repeated]]). Twice Agamemnon has the heralds call the long-haired Achaeans out, first to the assembly and then, when the test has failed and been repaired, to war:

{{quote:0752ff08-0e87-517d-bf87-50f19f6c1d59}}

The second summons repeats the first three lines with one change: πόλεμον (“war”) in place of ἀγορήν (“assembly”) ({{ref:Iliad 2.442-444}}). The day that began in speech now turns to fighting.

Calchas used the formula at Aulis, addressing the army that stood silent at the portent ({{ref:Iliad 2.323}}), and the narrator uses it for the last time in the book at the end of the simile of the flies around the milk pails: “so many were the long-haired Achaeans” ({{ref:Iliad 2.472}}).

In the Catalogue a variation marks a people with a custom of its own. The Abantes of Euboea wear their hair long only behind, ὄπιθεν κομόωντες ({{ref:Iliad 2.542}}).

## Outside Homer

Plutarch explains the Abantes' custom: they cut the hair in front so that no enemy could seize it in close fighting, and Theseus, he says, cut his own hair the same way (*Theseus* 5). In classical Greece the Spartans kept the old style, and Herodotus describes them combing their long hair before the battle at Thermopylae (7.208–209).
