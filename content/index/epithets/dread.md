## Meaning

ἐπαινή is usually taken as a form of αἰνός (“dread, terrible”) with the prefix ἐπι- (“very”): “dread, terrible.” Ancient readers also connected it with ἔπαινος (“praise”), “praised, venerated,” but the word is used only of the queen of the dead and always in a context of death, and “dread” is the usual rendering. Homer gives it to Persephone and to no one else, always in the formula ἐπαινὴ Περσεφόνεια: twice in the Iliad, in Book 9 (9.457, 9.569), and four times in the Odyssey, where Circe sends Odysseus “to the house of Hades and dread Persephone” (Od. 10.491, 10.564) and the prayers at the edge of the world of the dead go “to mighty Hades and dread Persephone” (Od. 10.534, 11.47). Compare the epithets of Hades, [[gatekeeper]], [[implacable]], [[untamable]], and his name in Book 9, [[zeus-of-the-underworld]].

## Use

{{quote:43f8c035-4e67-57b3-b084-9bbef95742a5}}

Both uses come in Phoenix's speech, each time in a curse that the powers below fulfill. Amyntor's curse of childlessness on his son is fulfilled by “Zeus of the underworld and dread Persephone” (9.453–457; [[amyntor]]). Althaea, kneeling and beating the earth with her hands, calls on “Hades and dread Persephone” to give death to her son Meleager, and the Erinys hears her out of Erebus (9.566–572; [[althaea]], [[meleager]], [[erinyes]], [[all-nourishing]]). The queen of the dead is invoked by a father against his son and by a mother against hers. See [[persephone]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod repeats the formula: before the house of “mighty Hades and dread Persephone” a pitiless hound keeps guard, fawning on those who enter and devouring those who try to leave (*Theogony* 767–773).
