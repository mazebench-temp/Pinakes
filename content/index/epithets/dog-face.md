## Meaning

κυνώπης = κύων, κυν- (“dog”) + ὤψ (“eye, face”): “with the eyes (or face) of a dog.” In Homer the dog stands for shamelessness. Dogs eat the unburied dead (as in the proem, {{ref:Iliad 1.4-5}}), and a person without αἰδώς (“shame, respect for others”) is called a dog. Helen calls herself κυνῶπις, the feminine of the word, in self-reproach (Il. 3.180), and Hephaestus uses it of his mother Hera in Book 18.

## Use

{{quote:36c21eb8-c1e1-5c2b-8166-87bc999d042d}}

The insult comes at the climax of Achilles' first speech of abuse. It follows at once on ὦ μέγ’ ἀναιδές, “you utterly shameless one” ([[utterly-shameless]]), and says the same thing in an image: Agamemnon has no shame. The army came to Troy to win satisfaction for him and his brother, and he takes the prize of the man who fights for him.

Achilles returns to the dog in his second outburst, “with the eyes of a dog and the heart of a deer” ([[dog-eyes-deer-heart]], {{ref:Iliad 1.225}}). In Book 9 he uses it once more: Agamemnon, “dog though he is,” would not dare to look him in the face (Il. 9.372–373).
