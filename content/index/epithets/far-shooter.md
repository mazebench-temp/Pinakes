## Meaning

ἑκηβόλος is traditionally analyzed as ἑκάς (“far off”) + βάλλω (“throw, shoot”): “shooting from afar.” The analysis fits Apollo the archer, whose arrows strike from a distance, and the translation follows it. It is not certain. Some modern scholars connect the first element instead with ἑκών (“willing”) and ἕκητι (“by the will of”), which would give “who shoots at will,” a god whose arrows go where he wishes.

The same idea appears in several related forms, all used of Apollo in Book 1: ἑκατηβόλος ([[hekatebolos]]), ἑκατηβελέτης ([[hekatebeletes]]), the short form ἕκατος ([[hekatos]]), and ἑκάεργος ([[far-worker]]). With [[god-of-the-silver-bow]] (ἀργυρότοξος) they make the bow his defining attribute.

## Use

The epithets cluster wherever the plague is in question. The narrator introduces Chryses holding the fillets “of far-shooting Apollo”:

{{quote:3ce3cc13-9a7d-58f3-91b3-03e9bdf1d63b}}

Chryses' plea ends with the same formula in the accusative: the Achaeans are asked to revere “the son of Zeus, far-shooting Apollo” ({{ref:Iliad 1.21}}; [[son-of-zeus]]). The request is refused, and the god shoots.

In the assembly the epithet becomes a name. Calchas uses it for the god who has caused the suffering, and Agamemnon throws it back at him:

{{quote:b367ba0c-b4cf-5d87-b47c-8cac6fefa705}}

{{quote:a6601d59-7389-53fe-be50-053cfa173d0d}}

Both lines join ἑκηβόλος to ἄλγεα (“sorrows”), the word the proem used for the suffering caused by Achilles' wrath ({{ref:Iliad 1.2}}). Used alone, the epithet names Apollo by what he is doing to the army.

The formula returns in Achilles' retelling ({{ref:Iliad 1.373}}), and then, when the plague is over, at the moment of reconciliation: at Chryse the Achaeans bring the hecatomb ashore “for far-shooting Apollo” ({{ref:Iliad 1.438}}). The epithet that named the threat now names the god who is being appeased.
