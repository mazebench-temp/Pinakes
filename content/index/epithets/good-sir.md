## Meaning

φέριστος is the superlative that belongs with φέρτερος (“better, stronger”), the word used of Proetus, “far the mightier” (6.158): “best, most excellent.” Homer uses it only in the vocative, φέριστε, as a courteous address. Three times in the Iliad it stands in the same half-verse, τίς δὲ σύ ἐσσι φέριστε, “who are you, good sir,” a question put to a stranger: by Diomedes to Glaucus (6.123), by Hector to Apollo, whom he does not recognize (Il. 15.247), and by Priam to Hermes, disguised as a young Myrmidon (Il. 24.387). In the Odyssey Eurymachus uses it to Telemachus (Od. 1.405), and Odysseus in his plea to the Cyclops, “respect the gods, good sir” (Od. 9.269).

## Use

{{quote:42eaca3f-4db9-5cd9-9908-200883c9efd9}}

The courtesy opens a challenge. Diomedes has never seen this man in battle, and his daring suggests that he may be a god, and Diomedes will not fight the gods. He tells the story of Lycurgus to show why, and ends with the threat: if the stranger is a mortal, let him come nearer and meet his death (6.130–143; [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]], [[who-eat-the-fruit-of-the-field]]). Readers have asked why he needs to ask, since Athena gave him the power to tell a god from a man (5.127–128; [[achlys]]). The stranger is a man, and the question leads to Glaucus' answer about the generations of leaves (6.145–149; [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]). In Books 15 and 24 the same words are put to real gods.
