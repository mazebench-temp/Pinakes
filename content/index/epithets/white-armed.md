## Meaning

λευκώλενος = λευκός (“white, bright”) + ὠλένη (“forearm, arm”): “white-armed.” Pale skin was a mark of beauty in a woman, the sign of a life spent indoors rather than in the fields. Greek vase painters followed the same convention, painting women's skin white and men's dark.

The epithet is not Hera's alone. Homer gives it to mortal women too: Helen (Il. 3.121), Andromache (Il. 6.371), and in the Odyssey Nausicaa (Od. 6.101). With Hera it is fixed in a line-end formula, θεὰ λευκώλενος Ἥρη, “the white-armed goddess Hera.”

## Use

The formula occurs four times in Book 1 in the nominative ({{ref:Iliad 1.55}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.195}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.208}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.595}}) and once in the dative, λευκωλένῳ Ἥρῃ ({{ref:Iliad 1.572}}).

The first three show Hera acting out of sight on the human plot. She puts the idea of an assembly into Achilles' mind:

{{quote:87a4238f-ffc5-53d7-b997-7458bd8b1b1c}}

and she sends Athena to stop him from killing Agamemnon. The narrator says so ({{ref:Iliad 1.195-196}}), and Athena repeats the narrator's two lines almost word for word to Achilles:

{{quote:0777f88c-f2dd-5d04-8d86-1d9383faf80c}}

On Olympus the formulas change. When Hera challenges Zeus and is silenced, she is βοῶπις πότνια Ἥρη, “ox-eyed queen Hera” ([[ox-eyed]], {{ref:Iliad 1.551}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.568}}). When Hephaestus soothes his mother and she smiles, she is white-armed again:

{{quote:02590d94-91da-5505-a434-049e80dfe8a6}}

The two formulas have exactly the same metrical shape, and both fill the second half of the verse after the caesura in the middle of the third foot (compare 551 with 595). Meter does not decide between them. In Book 1 the distribution is nonetheless clear: the white-armed goddess is Hera the helper of the Achaeans and the mother, and the ox-eyed queen is Hera in confrontation with her husband. Whether the poet chose with that difference in mind cannot be proved, but the pattern is there to be heard.

The book ends with a third formula, [[golden-throned]] (χρυσόθρονος), as Hera sleeps beside Zeus ({{ref:Iliad 1.611}}).
