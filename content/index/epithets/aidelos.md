## Meaning

The origin of ἀΐδηλος is uncertain. It is often explained from ἀ- (“not”) and the root of ἰδεῖν (“see”): “making unseen,” that is, destroying utterly. In Homer it means “destructive, annihilating.” It describes fire, which leaves nothing behind: πῦρ ἀΐδηλον, the destroying fire of a forest blaze (2.455; Il. 11.155; [[forest-fire]]), and the fire that Phoenix fears Achilles will not keep from the ships (Il. 9.434–436). In the Odyssey Hephaestus complains that Aphrodite loves “destructive Ares” (Od. 8.309); it is used of the suitors (Od. 16.29, 23.303) and, by Eumaeus, of the treacherous goatherd Melanthius (Od. 22.165). Compare the other words of destruction: [[destructive]] (δήϊος), [[deadly]] (ὀλοός, οὐλόμενος), [[baneful]] (οὖλος).

## Use

{{quote:5ba787f6-d568-5608-83c5-3863485cbf41}}

Ares, wounded, blames Zeus for his daughter: he begot her himself, she is senseless and “ruinous” ([[deadly]]), and he lets her loose, “this destructive child.” Zeus answers in kind. Ares is the most hateful to him of the gods, with his mother's temper; if he had been born of any other god, “destructive as you are,” he would long ago have been lower down than the sons of Uranus (5.889–898; [[heavenly-gods]], [[strife-is-always-dear]]). The father of both gives back to the son the word the son used of the daughter. The quarrel on Olympus is a family quarrel ([[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]], [[overbearing]]).
