## Meaning

φυσίζοος = φύω (“grow, bring forth”) + ζόος, ζωή (“life”): “bringing forth life.” The earth is the source of all that grows. Yet Homer uses the word where the earth holds the dead. Achilles wonders whether Lycaon, back from Lemnos, will come back from below as well, or whether “the life-giving earth” will hold him, “which holds down even the strong” (Il. 21.62–63). The Odyssey uses the Iliad's own words of Helen's brothers: “both of them, alive, the life-giving earth holds” (τοὺς ἄμφω ζωοὺς κατέχει φυσίζοος αἶα, Od. 11.301). There they live and die on alternate days, honored by Zeus (Od. 11.301–304).

## Use

{{quote:6e2a25e6-f610-5278-8aee-a6803789e5b9}}

Helen has looked for Castor and Polydeuces among the Achaeans. She supposes that they did not come, or that they came and will not fight for shame of her (3.236–242). The narrator corrects her: they are already dead, and buried in their homeland ([[dear-native-land]]). The epithet of life stands beside the fact of death, and it is not bent to the sadness of the moment. See [[the-death-of-the-dioscuri]], [[good-with-his-fists]], and the other epithet of earth in the same book, [[bountiful-earth]].

## Outside Homer

John Ruskin made this passage an example of the truth of great poetry in “Of the Pathetic Fallacy” (*Modern Painters*, vol. 3, 1856, part 4, chapter 12). Homer, he argued, feels the sorrow of the brothers' death, but does not let it change what he says of the earth: though they are dead, the earth is still the mother of life.
