## Meaning

ἀκάματος = ἀ- (“not”) + κάματος (“toil, weariness,” from κάμνω, “grow tired”): “untiring.” In Homer it is the epithet of fire, always in ἀκάματον πῦρ at the end of the verse, ten times in the two poems (5.4; Il. 15.598, 15.731, 16.122, 18.225, 21.13, 21.341, 23.52; Od. 20.123, 21.181). A related form, ἀκάμας, describes the sun (Il. 18.239, 18.484) and the river Spercheius (Il. 16.176). Fire that does not tire is fire that goes on burning: the fire of the ships in Books 15 and 16, of Patroclus' pyre in Book 23.

## Use

{{quote:5c92f968-814b-539d-ae95-4959fc6c142d}}

The first image of Diomedes' day is a tireless fire burning from his helmet and his shield, like the star of late summer ([[the-autumn-star]], [[the-aristeia]]). In Book 18 the same goddess kindles the same fire over the head of Achilles when he shows himself at the trench, and the Trojan charioteers are struck with terror when they see “the tireless fire” (Il. 18.225–227). The epithet belongs to the fire, not to the man. At the end of Book 5 Athena asks Diomedes whether “the weariness of much charging” has sunk into his limbs (κάματος, 5.811), the word the epithet denies ([[tydeus-at-thebes]]).
