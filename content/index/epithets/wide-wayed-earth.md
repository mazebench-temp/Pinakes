## Meaning

εὐρυόδεια = εὐρύς (“wide”) + ὁδός (“road, way”): the earth with its wide roads, broad enough to go anywhere. It is always said of χθών, in the genitive at the end of the verse, after a preposition: ἀπὸ χθονὸς or ὑπὸ χθονὸς εὐρυοδείης. Homer has it once in the Iliad, of the din rising “up from the wide-wayed earth” in the fight over Sarpedon (16.635), and three times in the Odyssey: Nestor's sons lift the heifer for sacrifice from the earth (*Odyssey* 3.453), Odysseus sees smoke rising from the earth on Circe's island (*Odyssey* 10.149), and Elpenor's body is not yet buried beneath it (*Odyssey* 11.52). The translation renders it “wide-wayed,” as it renders εὐρύπορος of the sea ([[wide-wayed]]). Compare the earth's other epithets, [[bountiful-earth]] (πουλυβότειρα) and [[black-earth]] (μέλαινα).

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod the formula is frequent, above all of what lies beneath the earth: Tartarus in a recess of the wide-wayed earth (*Theogony* 119), the stone that Zeus set up at Pytho (*Theogony* 498), and the Hundred-Handers and the Titans kept beneath it (*Theogony* 620, 717); in the *Works and Days* Shame and Retribution go from the wide-wayed earth to Olympus at the end of the race of iron (*Works and Days* 197–200).
