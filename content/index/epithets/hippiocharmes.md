## Meaning

ἱππιοχάρμης = ἵππος (“horse”; in the plural, a chariot team) + χάρμη (“battle, the joy of battle”): “one who fights from horses,” that is, from a chariot, as Homer's warriors do. Compare [[chariot-fighters]] (ἱπποκορυστής), the epithet of men who “fight from chariots.” Walter Leaf quotes Aristonicus: because Homer called Troilus ἱππιοχάρμης, the later poets had him pursued on horseback, and some made him a boy, though the epithet shows him a grown man (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.257).

## Use

Priam, rebuking the sons who are left, names three of the best who are dead: “godlike Mestor and Troilus, who fought from his chariot, / and Hector” (24.257–258; [[antitheos]], [[sons-of-priam]]). The Odyssey gives the epithet to Amythaon, son of Tyro and Cretheus, in the catalogue of the heroines (*Odyssey* 11.258–259). See [[troilus]].
