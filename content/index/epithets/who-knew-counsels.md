## Meaning

μήδεα, “counsels, plans” (the plural of μῆδος, from the root of μήδομαι, “devise”), + εἰδώς, the participle of οἶδα, “know”: “knowing counsels,” a man who can plan and advise. The word before the pair says what kind they are: πεπνυμένα, “wise” ([[wise]]), ἄφθιτα, “imperishable” ([[imperishable]]), or θεῶν ἄπο, “from the gods.” With φίλα φρεσί, “kindly in his mind,” the phrase describes a disposition rather than a skill, and the translation renders it so (*Iliad* 17.325).

## Use

It closes the verse five times in Homer. Three times it is a herald's: the Trojan Idaeus, who parts Hector and Ajax at nightfall (*Iliad* 7.278), and Peisenor in the assembly of Ithaca (*Odyssey* 2.38), in the same verse with one name put for the other, κῆρυξ … πεπνυμένα μήδεα εἰδώς, “the herald … who knew wise counsels”; and Periphas, the old herald of Anchises, whose likeness Apollo takes, φίλα φρεσὶ μήδεα εἰδώς, “a man whose mind was kindly toward him” (*Iliad* 17.323–325). Once it is Zeus', when Iris summons Thetis to him: “Zeus calls you, whose counsels are imperishable” (*Iliad* 24.88). And once it is a king's: “and Alcinous was ruling then, who knew counsels from the gods” (*Odyssey* 6.12), the only place where the counsels are said to come from the gods. ## Outside Homer

Hesiod has the verse-end of Zeus, Ζεὺς ἄφθιτα μήδεα εἰδώς, three times in the story of Prometheus (*Theogony* 545, 550, 561), and Zeus addresses Prometheus as Ἰαπετιονίδη, πάντων πέρι μήδεα εἰδώς, “son of Iapetus, who know counsels beyond all others” (*Theogony* 559; *Works and Days* 54). The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* has Zeus' form (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 43), and the *Catalogue of Women* names Odysseus among Helen's suitors as “the son of Laertes, who knew crafty counsels,” πολύκροτα μήδεα εἰδώς (fr. 68 Evelyn-White).
