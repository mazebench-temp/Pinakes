## Meaning

διογενής = Διός (“of Zeus”) + γένος, γίγνομαι (“birth, be born”): “Zeus-born.” It is a title of kings and heroes in general rather than a claim about each one's parentage, like [[zeus-nurtured]] (διοτρεφής). In the Odyssey the vocative is part of Odysseus' standing address, διογενὲς Λαερτιάδη, πολυμήχαν’ Ὀδυσσεῦ.

For Achilles it is also true by descent. In Book 21 he boasts that his father Peleus was the son of Aeacus, and Aeacus the son of Zeus (Il. 21.187–189).

## Use

{{quote:e328d3fa-f846-5d02-be24-a277aebc9262}}

Achilles uses the honorific when he tells Patroclus to hand Briseis over to the heralds. It is a courtesy at the moment of his loss, and the first time in the poem that Patroclus is addressed.

{{quote:f3e30585-702e-5835-9c17-38647c20a88c}}

At the end of the book the narrator piles up the titles of Achilles in a single verse: sprung from Zeus, son of Peleus ([[son-of-peleus]]), swift-footed ([[swift-footed]]). The full naming comes when the hero is doing nothing at all, and the contrast is the point.
