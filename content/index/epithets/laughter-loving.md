## Meaning

φιλομμειδής = φίλος (“fond of”) + μειδάω (“smile”): “fond of smiles.” The verb comes from the same root as English *smile*, and the double μ of the usual spelling keeps a trace of the root's lost initial *s*. The text at {{ref:Iliad 3.424}} prints the word with a single μ. The smile is that of charm and seduction rather than laughter aloud.

The epithet is Aphrodite's in both poems (Il. 4.10, 5.375, 14.211; Od. 8.362). It belongs to her whatever her mood. In Book 5 she is “laughter-loving” as she answers her mother Dione, weeping, with the wound Diomedes has given her (Il. 5.375). Hesiod explains the word by a pun of his own. He derives it from μήδεα, the genitals of Ouranos, from whose foam the goddess was born (*Theogony* 200). In Homer she is the daughter of Zeus and Dione (Il. 5.370–371; [[daughter-of-zeus]]).

## Use

{{quote:23b23e3f-dc2a-58df-b128-e067ee9d4b04}}

A few lines earlier the goddess threatened Helen with her hatred (3.414–417). Now, as “laughter-loving Aphrodite,” she carries a chair herself like a servant and sets it facing Paris. This is the work that Zeus assigns her in Book 5: “the lovely works of marriage,” and not war (Il. 5.428–429).

Book 3 names her by four epithets, each fitted to her part. To Paris she is “golden Aphrodite,” the giver of beauty ([[golden]], 3.64). When she snatches him from the duel she is “daughter of Zeus” ([[daughter-of-zeus]], 3.374). When she speaks to Helen in disguise and in anger she is “brilliant” ([[brilliant]], 3.389, 3.413). When she brings the lovers together she is laughter-loving.
