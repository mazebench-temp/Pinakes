## Meaning

ὀψίγονος = ὀψέ (“late”) + the root of γίγνομαι (“be born”): “late-born.” With ἄνθρωποι it means the men of generations to come. Homer uses it five times, always of the generations to come who will hear of a man's deeds or be the better for them. Menelaus prays that he may punish Paris, “so that any man of those born hereafter may shudder” to wrong a host (3.351–354); Patroclus asks Achilles what any man, even one born hereafter, will have from him if he does not save the Argives (16.29–32); and Athena, in the likeness of Mentes, and Nestor tell Telemachus to be brave, so that someone among the men born hereafter may speak well of him (Od. 1.301–302 = 3.199–200). Compare the other words for posterity: “those who will be hereafter” (οἳ μετόπισθεν ἔσονται, Od. 24.84), and Helen's “men who are yet to be” (6.358).

## Use

The tomb by the sea is built for the man who dies ([[broad-hellespont]], [[tomb]]), but the words that the men born hereafter will say belong to Hector: “my fame will never perish” (7.91; [[kleos]]). Helen said in Book 6 that Zeus had set an evil fate on her and Paris so that they might be a theme of song for men to come (6.357–358). Hector expects the same future audience to remember his victory. See [[tis-speech]] and [[hector]].
