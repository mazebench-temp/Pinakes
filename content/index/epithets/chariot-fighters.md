## Meaning

ἱπποκορυστής = ἵππος (“horse”) + κορύσσω (“arm, equip; marshal”): “equipped with horses,” a warrior armed for chariot fighting. Homer uses it five times: of men in general in the line that opens Book 2 and is repeated in Book 24 (Il. 24.677), and of two peoples among the Trojan allies, the Maeonians (Il. 10.431) and the Paeonians (Il. 16.287, 21.205).

## Use

{{quote:659fed74-3105-52bf-b828-b374dcbc9c9e}}

Book 2 opens with the whole world asleep, gods and “men who fight from chariots,” all but Zeus. The epithet names men by what they will be doing when the day comes. Book 24 begins its last night with the same line: the gods and the chariot-fighters sleep, but not Hermes, who is planning how to bring Priam safely out of the Achaean camp (Il. 24.677–681). The two nights frame the poem's action: one begins the slaughter that Zeus has promised Thetis, the other ends it with Hector's ransom.
