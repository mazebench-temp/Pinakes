## Meaning

ποικιλομήτης = ποικίλος (“many-colored, intricate”; [[inlaid]]) + μῆτις (“cunning, counsel”; [[metis]]): “of intricate cunning.” In the Iliad it occurs only here, in the verse-end Ὀδυσῆα δαΐφρονα ποικιλομήτην, “wise-hearted Odysseus of subtle wiles” ([[wise-hearted]]). The Odyssey repeats the verse-end when Alcinous takes Odysseus by the hand and raises him from the hearth (Od. 7.167–169) and three times in the fight with the suitors (Od. 22.115, 22.202, 22.281); Nestor uses it with ἄνακτα, “lord,” in his account of the return from Troy (Od. 3.162–163). Athena greets Odysseus with it when he lies to her on Ithaca: σχέτλιε, ποικιλομῆτα, δόλων ἆτʼ, “hard one, of subtle wiles, insatiable of tricks” (Od. 13.293), the last words the same as Socus' taunt (11.430; [[insatiable-of-war]]). Compare [[of-many-wiles]] (πολύμητις), [[of-many-devices]] (πολυμήχανος) and [[crooked-counseling]] (ἀγκυλομήτης), the epithet of Cronus.

## Use

{{quote:48afa135-d98d-560b-a6dd-4fcd340c529b}}

The verse closes the simile of the jackals: “so then around wise-hearted Odysseus of subtle wiles the Trojans pressed, many and valiant” (11.482–483; [[strong-spear]]). The epithet comes when cunning can do nothing for him. Alone and wounded, he darts with his spear and keeps off the pitiless day until Ajax and Menelaus reach him (11.483–488; [[pitiless]], [[ravager]], [[the-jackals-and-the-stag]]). Earlier in the same fight he chose to stand rather than flee (11.403–410; [[pondering-two-ways]]). See [[odysseus]].

## Outside Homer

In the Homeric Hymns the epithet belongs to gods. Hera reproaches Zeus with the same words, σχέτλιε, ποικιλομῆτα (*Hymn to Apollo* 322), and in the Hymn to Hermes the newborn Hermes is ποικιλομήτης to his mother Maia and to Apollo (*Hymn to Hermes* 155, 514).
