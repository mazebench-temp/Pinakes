## Meaning

θεράποντες, plural of θεράπων (“attendant, squire, companion in arms”) + Ἄρηος (“of Ares”): “attendants of Ares.” A θεράπων is a free man who serves a greater one by choice: Patroclus is Achilles' θεράπων, and heralds and charioteers are called so. To call warriors the attendants of the war god is to say that war is their service and their honor.

The whole verse, ὦ φίλοι ἥρωες Δαναοί, θεράποντες Ἄρηος (“Friends, Danaan heroes, attendants of Ares”), is used four times in the Iliad to address the army: by Agamemnon here and in Book 19 (Il. 19.78), by Nestor (Il. 6.67), and by Ajax at the ships (Il. 15.733). The phrase also describes the two Ajaxes (Il. 8.79, 10.228).

## Use

{{quote:301f2cec-8d25-55d0-8d65-6578c864b1b2}}

Agamemnon begins his test of the army with the fullest honor the formula can give. He calls them heroes and servants of Ares, and then tells them to run away. The grandeur of the address is part of the test: it names the men he expects to stand firm when he pretends to despair.
