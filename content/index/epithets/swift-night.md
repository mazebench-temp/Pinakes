## Meaning

θοός means “quick, nimble,” the word of the [[swift-ships]] and of quick fighters ([[swift]]). Why night is swift is not said: the epithet may describe the speed with which darkness falls, or night's passage across the sky. A different sense, “sharp, pointed,” has been proposed for θοός in the Odyssey's “pointed” islands (νῆσοι θοαί), but of night the translation keeps “swift.”

The commonest phrase is θοὴν διὰ νύκτα μέλαιναν, “through the swift black night,” which ends the verse four times, always of a journey through enemy ground at night: twice in Book 10 (10.394, 10.468) and twice when Priam drives to the Achaean camp, first in Hermes' question and then in Achilles' warning that he might be seen “through the swift black night” (24.366, 24.653). Night is also swift as a goddess: Zeus, Sleep tells Hera, held back his anger against him “for fear of displeasing swift Night” (14.259–261). And when Hector bursts through the gate of the Achaean wall, he is “like swift night in his face” (νυκτὶ θοῇ ἀτάλαντος ὑπώπια, 12.462–463). In the Odyssey Eurylochus protests that Odysseus would have his men wander “through the swift night” (Od. 12.284). Compare [[black-night]], [[dark]] and [[murky]].

## Use

{{quote:7faf4cf8-9291-530a-981c-2aa4d4f8918e}}

Dolon tells Odysseus what Hector ordered: “to go through the swift black night and come close to the enemy men” (10.394–395). Hector's own words, which Dolon repeats in the next verses, spoke of the night only as the time of the Achaeans' watch (10.308–312); the verse of the swift black night is Dolon's addition. Odysseus uses the same verse-end when he marks the tamarisk where he has hung Dolon's spoils, “so that they would not miss it when they came back through the swift black night” (10.465–468; [[stripping-the-dead]], [[odysseus]]). The two men come back the same way, and Diomedes jumps down there to take up the bloody spoils (10.526–529; [[bloody-spoils]]). See [[war-and-night]].
