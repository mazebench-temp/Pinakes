## Meaning

βρότος is blood from a wound, blood that has dried and clotted, and αἱματόεις (“bloody,” from αἷμα, “blood”) doubles the picture. The noun gives the adjective βροτόεις, which describes the spoils stripped from the slain ([[bloody-spoils]]). The phrase βρότον αἱματόεντα ends the verse four times in the Iliad, always after the verb of washing: the dead of both armies are washed here (7.425); Hecamede will heat water to wash the blood from the wounded Machaon (14.5–7); Achilles has a cauldron set on the fire to wash the body of Patroclus (18.343–345); and Achilles himself refuses to wash the gore from his own body until he has burned Patroclus and cut his hair (23.39–47). See [[dark-blood]].

## Use

On the morning of the truce the Trojans and the Achaeans go out to gather their dead:

{{quote:eb92c69c-e76d-580f-88b5-2de23e902772}}

The dead lie where they fell the day before, and “it was hard to tell each man apart” (7.424). Washing away the blood is the first care given to a body, and here it is also the way to know who the dead man was. Then, weeping, they lift the dead onto the wagons ([[hot-tears]], [[the-truce-for-the-dead]], [[cremation]]).
