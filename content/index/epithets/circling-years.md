## Meaning

περιτέλλομαι = περί (“around”) + τέλλομαι (“rise, come to pass”): to come round, of a year that returns to its beginning. The phrase occurs three times in the Iliad. In the Catalogue the young men of Athens propitiate Erechtheus with bulls and rams “as the years come round,” at a yearly sacrifice (2.550–551; [[erechtheus]]). In Book 8 it measures a punishment: not in ten circling years would the goddesses heal (8.404, 8.418). The Odyssey has the year itself come round with its seasons (ἂψ περιτελλομένου ἔτεος, Od. 11.295, 14.294), and a similar participle, περιπλόμενος, “revolving,” in the verse that dates Odysseus' homecoming (Od. 1.16) and in Achilles' promise of iron enough for five years (23.833).

## Use

{{quote:87700646-a67a-5016-a902-f4fba1b419a5}}

Gods cannot die, and Zeus measures his punishment in time. Ares, wounded by Diomedes, was healed by Paeeon at once (5.899–904); the wounds of the thunderbolt would last ten years ([[gods-wounded-by-mortals]]). Ten years is the measure of the war itself, which Agamemnon's army must fight for nine years and win in the tenth (2.134, 2.328–329). Iris carries the threat to the goddesses in the same words (8.416–420; [[message-repeated]], [[swift-iris]]), and on Olympus Zeus tells them what would have happened: struck by the thunderbolt, they would not have come back to Olympus (8.454–456).

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod a god who swears falsely by the water of Styx lies without breath for a full year, and for nine more is kept apart from the councils and feasts of the gods; in the tenth he joins them again (*Theogony* 793–804).
