## Meaning

ἀμφήκης = ἀμφί (“on both sides”) + the root ἀκ- (“sharp, point,” as in ἀκωκή, “point,” and ἀκαχμένος, “sharpened”): “sharp on both sides, two-edged.” Homer uses it four times, always of a sword, in the neuter ἄμφηκες: the φάσγανον that Thrasymedes gives Diomedes (10.256); the ξίφος that Achilles plunges to the hilt beside Lycaon's neck, “the two-edged sword went in” (21.116–118); and in the Odyssey the sword that Telemachus and Penelope promise the beggar, with a cloak and sandals (Od. 16.79–80, 21.341). Compare [[long-edged]] (ταναήκης), [[sharp]] and [[silver-studded]].

## Use

{{quote:5fdcd4f3-31a4-5510-af52-c501071559f3}}

Diomedes has left his own sword at his ship, and Thrasymedes, the son of Nestor who commands the sentries, arms him: a two-edged sword, a shield, and a cap of bull's hide without ridge or crest ([[steadfast-in-battle]], [[of-bulls-hide]], [[without-ridge-or-crest]], [[thrasymedes]]). The two spies are armed from the men who stay behind, Diomedes by Thrasymedes and Odysseus by Meriones (10.260–271; [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]]). The sword is the weapon of the night: with a sword Diomedes kills Dolon and the twelve Thracians (10.455–456, 10.483–489; [[sword]]). See [[diomedes]].
