## Meaning

δολόεις is formed from δόλος, “trick, snare” ([[dolos]]), with the suffix -όεις, “full of”: “full of tricks, wily.” The translation renders it “wily” (see the Odyssey translation's key words). Merry takes it of Calypso not as “treacherous” or “false” but only as “sly,” scheming to keep Odysseus for her husband, and finds in it Odysseus' own experience of her rather than her settled character (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.245). Compare [[schemer]] (δολομήτης, δολόμητις), the epithet of Aegisthus, and [[of-subtle-wiles]] (ποικιλομήτης), Odysseus' own.

## Use

{{quote:73b630a8-55b3-5bb8-81de-042360377433}}

Homer uses the word three times, all in the Odyssey. Odysseus calls Calypso “wily” as he begins his story to Arete, beside her father and her lonely island (*Odyssey* 7.244–247; [[calypso]], [[atlas]], [[ogygia]]). In the proem of his tale to the Phaeacians he sets Circe beside her in the same terms: Calypso kept him in her hollow caves and Circe in her halls, Αἰαίη δολόεσσα, the wily one of Aeaea, each longing for him to be her husband, and neither persuaded the heart in his breast (*Odyssey* 9.29–33; [[circe]]). That last verse is the one with which he ends his account of Calypso to Arete (*Odyssey* 7.258), so that the epithet names a goddess who tried to hold him. The third use is of a thing: the bonds that Hephaestus forged to catch Ares and Aphrodite, fine as cobwebs and invisible even to the blessed gods, πέρι γὰρ δολόεντα τέτυκτο, for they were made exceedingly cunning (*Odyssey* 8.280–281; [[dolos]]).

## Outside Homer

The Suda explains Calypso's name from the epithet: she is “not simple, but hiding what she has in mind,” for Homer calls her δολόεσσα (Suda κ 273).
