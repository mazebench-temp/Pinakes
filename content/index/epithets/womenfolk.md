## Meaning

θηλύτερος has the form of a comparative of θῆλυς (“female”), but it compares nothing. Like other adjectives in -τερος, it sets one of two groups against the other: women as distinct from men. Zeus uses the same pair when he forbids “no female god … and no male either” to help the armies (8.7; θήλεια … ἄρσην).

The formula is found once in the Iliad. In the Odyssey the goddesses, “female” ones, stay at home out of modesty when the gods come to laugh at Ares and Aphrodite (Od. 8.324), and Agamemnon's ghost says that Clytemnestra has poured shame on herself and on all women to come, “even on one who does good” (Od. 11.432–434; the verse recurs at Od. 15.422 and 24.202).

The heralds summon the boys and the old men to the walls ([[in-first-youth]], [[gray-templed]], [[god-built]]); the women, each in her own hall, keep a great fire burning, so that no ambush may enter the city while its fighting men are away ([[ambush]]).
