## Meaning

πολύδωρος = πολύς (“much, many”) + δῶρον (“gift”): “of many gifts.” Which gifts is disputed. The translation takes it as “richly dowered,” a wife who brought many gifts to her husband's house. It can also mean “won with many gifts,” since in Homer a man gives gifts for his bride (ἕδνα), and Hector “gave countless bride-gifts” for Andromache (ἐπεὶ πόρε μυρία ἕδνα, Il. 22.472). Both kinds of gift are Homeric: in Book 9 Agamemnon offers Achilles a daughter without bride-price and with gifts of his own besides (Il. 9.146–148). See [[marriage]].

Homer uses the word three times, always in the phrase ἄλοχος πολύδωρος and always of a hero's wife: Andromache (Il. 6.394, 22.88) and Penelope (Od. 24.294).

## Use

{{quote:38c6b0e6-eee9-5361-b56b-211532637a0d}}

The epithet of the wife leads straight into her lineage, the daughter of great-hearted Eetion, king of Thebe under Placus ([[eetion]], [[thebe]], [[wooded]]). What she brought to Hector's house came from a house that no longer exists: in her speech she tells how Achilles sacked the city and killed her father and her seven brothers (6.414–424; [[sack-of-thebe]]).

The two later uses name the wife in a verse about mourning a dead husband on his bier. Hecuba begs Hector not to face Achilles, for if he is killed “neither I nor your richly dowered wife” will weep over him, since the dogs will eat him by the ships (Il. 22.86–89). In the Odyssey the disguised Odysseus tells his father that his son died far from home, and “his richly dowered wife, prudent Penelope,” did not wail for him on the bier (Od. 24.294–296). In both the phrase names a wife kept from the one rite that is hers ([[lament]], [[andromache]]).
