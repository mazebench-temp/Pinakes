## Meaning

αὐλῶπις joins αὐλός (“pipe, tube”) to -ωπις, from the root of ὤψ (“eye, face”). It is usually understood as “with a tube,” a socket in which the plume was set; others take it as “with tube-like eye-holes.” The translation follows the first. Homer uses it four times, always of the τρυφάλεια, a kind of helmet: Diomedes' (5.182), Hector's, “three-layered, with its socket for the plume, which Phoebus Apollo had given him” (Il. 11.352–353), the helmet of the dead Ascalaphus, which falls ringing to the ground from Deiphobus' hand (Il. 13.527–530), and Patroclus', which Apollo strikes from his head (Il. 16.793–796). Compare [[horsehair-crested]] and [[with-two-ridges-and-four-plates]]; see [[helmet]].

## Use

{{quote:06d860a8-8284-50ab-ac43-e65139454107}}

Pandarus knows Diomedes by his arms and his horses, but the arms cannot tell him whether there is a god inside them. The helmet he recognizes is the one from which Athena kindled fire at the beginning of the book (5.4). Diomedes has been given the power Pandarus lacks: Athena has taken the mist from his eyes, “so that you may clearly tell god from man” (5.127–128; [[achlys]]).
