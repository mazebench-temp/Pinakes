## Meaning

Both words are rare, and their sense comes from ancient glosses. ἦνις was explained as “a year old” and ἠκέστας as “ungoaded,” never driven to work; the translation follows them. ἠκέστας occurs only in the three verses of Book 6, and its meaning is uncertain. ἦνις occurs once more, in a vow repeated in the Iliad and the Odyssey: a yearling heifer, broad of brow, “unbroken, which no man has yet led under the yoke,” with gold poured on its horns (βοῦν ἦνιν εὐρυμέτωπον / ἀδμήτην, Il. 10.292–294; Od. 3.382–384). In both passages the victim is prized because it has never been put to work. See [[sacrifice]] and [[cattle]].

## Use

The whole vow is spoken three times in Book 6, in the same words: by Helenus, who tells Hector what to have his mother promise (6.93–94), by Hector to Hecuba (6.274–275), and by Theano in the prayer itself:

{{quote:6d83ccd3-0ec9-5127-93d6-ddbc7cd049d1}}

Every ἦνις in Homer is promised to Athena. Diomedes and Nestor vow her the unbroken yearling in the other passages (Il. 10.291–294; Od. 3.380–384). Their prayers are heard, and each ends with the same half-verse (Il. 10.295; Od. 3.385): ἔκλυε Παλλὰς Ἀθήνη, “Pallas Athena heard.” The Trojan women's is not: Pallas Athena shakes her head (6.311; [[prayer]], [[athena]], [[the-robe-for-athena]]).
