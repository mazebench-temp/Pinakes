## Meaning

ἀργυρόηλος = ἄργυρος (“silver”) + ἧλος (“nail, stud”): “with silver studs.” On a sword the studs are the rivets that fix the hilt to the tang of the blade. Bronze Age swords were made this way, and on fine weapons the rivet heads were capped with precious metal. On a chair or throne the studs are decorative nails. Compare Achilles' scepter, [[studded-with-golden-nails]].

## Use

The silver-studded sword belongs to the arming of a hero. The whole line ἀμφὶ δ’ ἄρ’ ὤμοισιν βάλετο ξίφος ἀργυρόηλον recurs when Paris (Il. 3.334), Patroclus (Il. 16.135) and Achilles (Il. 19.372) arm for battle. In Book 2 it has a different setting. Agamemnon dresses in the dark after the Dream, and the sword comes between the sandals and the scepter:

{{quote:e33c248a-9e1b-596b-9d6c-bac21690432c}}

This is not yet an arming for battle. Agamemnon puts on a soft tunic and a cloak, not armor, and he takes the scepter, not a spear ([[dressing-scene]]). The sword is part of a king's dress. Its line is the one piece of a warrior's arming in the scene, and it hints at the battle Agamemnon thinks he will win that day.
