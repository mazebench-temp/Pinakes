## Meaning

δεινός (“terrible, dread,” from the root of δέος, “fear,” and δείδω, “be afraid”; [[deos]]) + ὅπλα (“gear, tools”). In Homer ὅπλα are most often tools and tackle: the ship's gear of the Odyssey, and the tools of Hephaestus' forge (18.409, 18.412). Four times in the Iliad they are arms: the dread armor of the two spies (10.254, 10.272), and the armor that Hephaestus makes for Achilles (18.614, 19.21). The phrase is in the dual, ἐδύτην, “the two put on,” and it occurs only in Book 10. Compare the other epithets of dread, [[dread-din-of-battle]] (φύλοπις αἰνή) and [[dread-goddess]] (δεινὴ θεός).

## Use

{{quote:93834274-4b44-5d32-a322-b87d448f3f13}}

The verse frames the arming. Odysseus answers Diomedes' praise and says that the night is far spent: “So saying, the two put on their dread armor” (10.254). The next verses tell what they put on, the sword, shield and bull's-hide cap that Thrasymedes gives Diomedes, the bow, quiver, sword and boar's-tusk helmet that Meriones gives Odysseus, with the helmet's history from Autolycus to Meriones (10.255–271; [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]], [[arming-scene]]). Then the verse is repeated, “And when the two had put on their dread armor,” and they set out (10.272–273). The spies' helmets are of leather and hide, not bronze, but the formula calls their armor dread. See [[odysseus]] and [[diomedes]].
