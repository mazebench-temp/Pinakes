## Meaning

ἄφθιτος = ἀ- (“not”) + φθίνω, φθίω (“waste away, perish”): “that does not decay.” It belongs to what the gods make or own: a wheel of Hera's chariot (Il. 5.724), the golden houses of the gods (Il. 13.22), the throne Hera promises Sleep (Il. 14.238), the house of Hephaestus (Il. 18.370), and the plans of Zeus, ἄφθιτα μήδεα (Il. 24.88).

The most famous use is Achilles' κλέος ἄφθιτον, the fame that “will be imperishable” if he dies at Troy (Il. 9.413). Sanskrit poetry has a phrase of the same meaning built from related words, *śrávas … ákṣitam*. The comparison, first made by Adalbert Kuhn in 1853, is one of the chief pieces of evidence for an inherited Indo-European poetic language ([[kleos]]).

## Use

In Book 2 the epithet belongs to the scepter of Agamemnon, in a whole-line formula that describes it twice:

{{quote:415fc276-cc68-5e78-8299-393f6b7c5a67}}

The scepter is “of his fathers” (πατρώϊον) and “imperishable forever” because it is a god's work. Its history is told fifty lines later: Hephaestus made it for Zeus, and it passed through Hermes, Pelops, Atreus and Thyestes to Agamemnon ({{ref:Iliad 2.100-108}}; [[the-scepter-of-agamemnon]]). The word marks it as the one lasting thing in a book about wavering men.

The second time, Odysseus takes it from Agamemnon in the same words ({{ref:Iliad 2.186}}) and goes through the camp with it, restraining the kings with gentle words and beating the commoners ([[gentle-words]]). With it he strikes Thersites ({{ref:Iliad 2.265-268}}). The imperishable staff of kings becomes an instrument of discipline.
