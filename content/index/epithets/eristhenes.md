## Meaning

ἐρισθενής = ἐρι- (“very,” the intensive prefix of [[who-thunders-loud]] and [[loud-thundering]]) + σθένος (“strength”): “of great strength.” It belongs to Zeus alone, four times in Homer, always in the genitive ἐρισθενέος, and always of Zeus as a father. Poseidon says that Hector “claims to be the son of mighty Zeus” (13.54). Athena, having dropped nectar and ambrosia into Achilles' breast, goes back to the close-built house of her mighty father (19.352–356). Achilles tells the dead Asteropaeus that it is hard for one sprung from a river to contend with the children of the mighty son of Cronus (21.184–185; [[asteropaeus]]). In the Odyssey Aphrodite has just come from her father, the mighty son of Cronus, when Ares finds her (Od. 8.288–289). Compare [[supreme-in-might]] (ὑπερμενής) and Poseidon's εὐρυσθενής ([[wide-in-strength]]). The translation renders it “mighty,” as it renders ἴφθιμος ([[mighty]]) and ὄβριμος ([[obrimos]]).

## Use

{{quote:c04733e3-e0b5-5c93-a301-c5a80f95e2c6}}

The god's epithet is set against the man's claim. Hector, “who claims to be the son of mighty Zeus” (13.54; [[hector]], [[son-of-zeus]], [[frenzied]]), is the one man whom Poseidon fears; at the end of the book Hector himself wishes that he were the son of aegis-bearing Zeus and of Hera (13.825–826). See [[zeus]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod opens the *Theogony* with the Muses dancing on Helicon around the altar of the mighty son of Cronus (βωμὸν ἐρισθενέος Κρονίωνος, *Theogony* 4), and in the *Works and Days* the autumn rain is the rain of mighty Zeus (Ζηνὸς ἐρισθενέος, *Works and Days* 415–416).
