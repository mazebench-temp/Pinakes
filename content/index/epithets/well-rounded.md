## Meaning

εὔκυκλος = εὖ (“well”) + κύκλος (“circle, ring, wheel”): “well-rounded.” Homer uses it of shields five times (5.453, 5.797; Il. 12.426, 13.715, 14.428), and in the Odyssey of the high wagon on which Nausicaa takes the washing to the river, where it means “with good wheels” (Od. 6.58, 6.70). Compare [[balanced-on-every-side]] (ἀσπὶς πάντοσʼ ἐΐση), another epithet of the round shield.

## Use

The first use is in the fight over the phantom of Aeneas. Around it the Trojans and Achaeans “hacked at the oxhide shields around one another's chests, the well-rounded shields and the fluttering bucklers of hide” (5.452–453; [[the-phantom-of-aeneas]], [[feathered]]). The armies strike real shields over a false body. The verse returns in the fight at the Achaean wall (Il. 12.426).

The second is Diomedes' own shield, when Athena finds him resting from his wound:

{{quote:16c9ee62-de97-5eb3-a5ad-6ddf9ade6f1f}}

The strap of the round shield chafes the shoulder that Pandarus' arrow struck, and Diomedes lifts it to wipe away the blood ([[shield]], [[dark-blood]]). The hero of the book is seen worn out, and Athena's rebuke starts from what she sees: either weariness has sunk into his limbs, or fear has hold of him (5.800–813; [[tydeus-at-thebes]]).
