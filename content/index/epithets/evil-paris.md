## Meaning

Δύσπαρις = δυσ- (“ill, bad,” the prefix of misfortune and hostility) + Πάρις: “Paris of ill omen,” “evil Paris.” Epic makes other names of reproach with a prefix of this kind. Penelope calls Troy Κακοΐλιος, “Evil Ilios, not to be named” (Od. 19.260), and Telemachus calls his mother δύσμητερ, “my mother, unmotherly mother” (Od. 23.97).

The vocative opens a whole line of abuse, and every word of it is an insult:

{{quote:450c586e-307a-5d7b-8b64-fee70366ca71}}

εἶδος ἄριστε, “best in looks” ([[best-in-looks]]); γυναιμανές, “mad for women,” a word found only in this line; ἠπεροπευτά, “deceiver, seducer,” from ἠπεροπεύω (“beguile”). Hector speaks the same verse again when he rebukes Paris in Book 13 (Il. 13.769).

## Use

Hector's rebuke follows Paris' retreat from Menelaus (3.30–37). The first line compresses the charges of the speech: beauty without courage (3.44–45), and a foreign woman carried off, a grief to his father and his city (3.46–51). The word for seducer returns at the end of the book in Helen's mouth, turned against the goddess who serves him: “Strange one, why do you long to beguile me (ἠπεροπεύειν) like this?” (3.399; [[strange-one]]). Paris accepts the rebuke as fair and proposes the duel (3.59–75). See [[paris]], [[hector]].
