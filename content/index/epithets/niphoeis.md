## Meaning

νιφόεις = νιφ- (“snow,” as in νιφάς, “snowflake,” and νίφω, “to snow”) + the suffix -όεις (“full of”): “snowy.” Homer uses it five times, always of mountains: the mountains of the Thracians (14.227); Olympus, from which Thetis leaps down like a hawk with Achilles' new armor (18.616); Tmolus, under which a Naiad bore Iphition (20.385); and in the Odyssey the mountains of Crete (*Odyssey* 19.338). Once Hector rushes into the battle like a snowy mountain (13.754; [[like-a-snowy-mountain]]). Olympus has a stronger epithet of snow, ἀγάννιφος, “deep in snow,” which the translation also renders “snowy” ([[snowy-olympus]]).

## Use

{{quote:3dbdd621-4576-5d4a-8275-0b3cf5769186}}

Hera's route from Olympus to Lemnos runs over Pieria and Emathia and the mountains of the Thracians to Athos, and from Athos over the sea (14.225–230; [[pieria]], [[emathia]], [[thracians]], [[athos]], [[lovely]], [[horse-herding]], [[billowing]]). She passes “over the snowy mountains of the horse-herding Thracians, / over their topmost peaks, and her feet did not touch the ground” (14.227–228). The snowy peaks are the highest points of her flight, and the goddess goes over them without touching them. See [[hera]].

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod νιφόεις is above all the epithet of Olympus, in the phrase κάρη νιφόεντος Ὀλύμπου, “the head of snowy Olympus” (*Theogony* 42, 118, 794); on snowy Olympus Heracles takes Hebe as his wife (*Theogony* 950–953). The Homeric Hymns give it to Parnassus (*Hymn* 3.282), to Olympus (*Hymn* 15.7), and to the mountain crests that belong to Pan (*Hymn* 19.6).
