## Meaning

ἀγήρως (also ἀγήραος) = ἀ- (“not”) + γῆρας (“old age”): “not growing old.” In Homer it never stands alone. It is joined to ἀθάνατος ([[immortals]]), and the pair names what divides the gods from men. The aegis that Athena carries is “ageless and immortal” (2.447; [[aegis]]). Sarpedon tells Glaucus that if the two of them, once they had escaped the war, “were to be ageless and immortal forever,” he would not fight among the foremost (12.322–325). Hector, boasting, wishes he were “immortal and ageless all my days” (8.538–539; [[immortal-and-ageless]]). And Zeus asks the horses of Achilles why the gods gave them to a mortal, “while you are ageless and immortal” (17.443–444; [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). In the Odyssey Calypso's offer to make Odysseus immortal and ageless all his days is told three times (*Odyssey* 5.135–136, 7.256–257, 23.335–336), and Odysseus answers her that Penelope is mortal and she immortal and ageless (*Odyssey* 5.218); the gold and silver dogs that Hephaestus made to guard the house of Alcinous are immortal and ageless (*Odyssey* 7.91–94).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod uses the pair for the divine members of families that are part mortal. Of the three Gorgons, Medusa was mortal and her two sisters ἀθάνατοι καὶ ἀγήρῳ, “immortal and ageless” (*Theogony* 276–277). Echidna is “an immortal nymph and ageless all her days” (*Theogony* 304–305). Zeus made Ariadne immortal and ageless for Dionysus (*Theogony* 947–949), and Heracles, his labors done, lives among the immortals “unharmed and ageless all his days” (*Theogony* 950–955). In the Homeric Hymns Zeus makes Ganymede immortal and ageless (*Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* 214), and Demeter would have made the child Demophon so (*Homeric Hymn to Demeter* 242, 260).
