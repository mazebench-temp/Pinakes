## Meaning

θεσπέσιος is usually explained as θεός (“god”) + the root σπ- of ἐννέπω (“say, tell”): “spoken by a god,” and so “divine, wondrous.” The explanation is old and likely but not certain. It suits the word's frequent use of sound: song, the voice of the Sirens, a clamor of battle. It is also used of anything extraordinary: wealth, bronze, a cloud, the threshold of Olympus from which Zeus hurled Hephaestus ({{ref:Iliad 1.591}}), where the translation renders it “divine.” The dative θεσπεσίῃ serves as an adverb, “by the will of heaven.”

## Use

Book 2 has the word four times, in all its senses. Nestor tells Agamemnon that the test of the army will show whether he fails to take Troy “by the will of heaven, or by the cowardice of men” ({{ref:Iliad 2.367-368}}). As the army marches out, “the dazzling gleam from their marvelous bronze” rises to the sky ({{ref:Iliad 2.457-458}}; [[forest-fire]]). The Muses take from Thamyris his “wondrous song” ({{ref:Iliad 2.599-600}}; [[thamyris-and-the-muses]]), and the son of Cronus pours “wondrous wealth” on Rhodes ({{ref:Iliad 2.670}}).

The song that the Muses took away was divine because it came from them. Thamyris' boast was to have forgotten that.
