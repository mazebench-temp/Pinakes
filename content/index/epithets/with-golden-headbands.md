## Meaning

χρυσάμπυξ = χρυσός (“gold”) + ἄμπυξ (“band”): the band worn on the head, a woman's headdress, like the one Andromache throws from her head when she sees Hector dragged behind Achilles' chariot (Il. 22.469), or the frontlet on a horse's forehead. Homer uses the compound four times, always of the horses of the gods and always in χρυσάμπυκας ἵππους at the end of the verse (5.358, 5.363, 5.720; Il. 8.382). Gold marks the gods' possessions ([[gold-and-silver]]).

## Use

{{quote:73ac6651-37c4-540a-a47e-5b015f7a4b88}}

Two teams of divine horses carry the action between Olympus and the plain. The wounded Aphrodite kneels to her brother Ares and asks for his horses with golden headbands, and Iris drives them to Olympus, where she sets ambrosial fodder before them (5.364–369; [[iris]], [[ambrosial]]). Later Hera harnesses her own team with the same words (5.720; [[august-goddess]]) and drives it through the gates of heaven that the Hours keep (5.748–752; [[hours]]). Both teams fly “not unwilling” (5.366, 5.768). The first carries a wounded goddess out of the war, and the second carries two goddesses into it, to see that a god is wounded.

## Outside Homer

In the Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite that tells of her arrival on Cyprus, the Hours who dress her are themselves χρυσάμπυκες, “with golden headbands” (Hymn 6.5, 6.12).
