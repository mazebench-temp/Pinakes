## Meaning

θωρηκτής is formed from θωρήσσω (“arm, put on the corselet,” from θώρηξ, “corselet”; [[corselet]]): “a man in armor.” πύκα is the adverb “close, thickly,” as in the doors of the gate “fitted close and strong” (πύκα στιβαρῶς ἀραρυίας, 12.454): “closely armored,” in armor that fits close or in close ranks. The phrase πύκα θωρηκτάων ends the verse three times in the Iliad: of the Lycians in Sarpedon's speech (12.317), and of the Trojans, when Hector does not stay in the throng of the close-armored Trojans (15.689) and when Ajax tells the Achaeans that they sit in the plain of the close-armored Trojans, far from home (15.739). Without πύκα, θωρηκτής describes the Trojans and the Argives in Book 21 (21.277, 21.429). Compare [[bronze-armored]] (χαλκοχίτων) and [[with-linen-corselet]] (λινοθώρηξ).

## Use

{{quote:03de8e0a-16ce-5668-80cd-042e29f9cc20}}

Sarpedon's reasoning turns on what the Lycians will say. The honors the kings receive oblige them to stand among the foremost and meet the blazing battle, “so that one of the close-armored Lycians may say this” (12.317; [[blazing]], [[sarpedons-speech]]). The epithet gives the judge his own warrior's quality: the man who will praise the kings for fighting is himself armored for the fight. See [[lycians]].
