## Meaning

κύων, “dog,” is a word of abuse for shamelessness ([[dogs]], [[dog-face]], [[mad-dog]]). ἀδεής = ἀ- (“not”) + δέος (“fear”; [[deos]]): “without fear,” and so, of a dog, without the respect that should hold it back ([[aidos]]). The vocative κύον ἀδεές occurs three times in Homer, always from a woman to a woman: Iris to Athena here; Hera to Artemis in the battle of the gods, asking how she dares to stand against her (21.479–481); and Penelope to Melantho, the maid who insults the disguised Odysseus (Od. 19.91).

Zeus uses the same image for Hera at the end of the scene, in the comparative of κύων: “there is nothing more shameless than you” (οὐ σέο κύντερον ἄλλο, 8.483).

## Use

{{quote:c5c3bf3d-3348-584f-9e51-1f3ae8b2ca7d}}

Iris has carried Zeus' threat to the goddesses in his own words (8.416–422 = 8.402–408; [[message-repeated]]). The last two verses are her own. Zeus had named Athena only as “the gray-eyed one” (8.406; [[gray-eyed]]), and had said he was less angry with Hera, who always thwarts him (8.407–408); Iris turns his anger into abuse of Athena, “most dread” ([[most-dread]]), and the insult falls on the goddess who would lift her huge spear against Zeus ([[huge]]). Hera gives up at once: let the mortals live or die as chance falls (8.427–431). The verses 8.420–424 are reported to have been rejected in antiquity. See [[iris]] and [[athena]].
