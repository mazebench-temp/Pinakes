## Meaning

ῥόδον (“rose”) with the adjectival suffix -όεις: “of roses.” The rose itself is never named in Homer, only this adjective and the compound ῥοδοδάκτυλος, “rosy-fingered” ([[rosy-fingered]]). Walter Leaf notes that the noun first appears in the Hymn to Demeter (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.186), where Persephone is gathering ῥόδα καὶ κρόκον ἠδʼ ἴα καλά, roses, crocus and violets (*Homeric Hymn to Demeter* 6).

## Use

{{quote:909d5ddc-fa57-582a-91ad-5aeb384c0faa}}

Leaf thinks that ῥοδόεις may mean no more than “fragrant” ([[fragrant]]), or that it alludes to the old belief that oil of roses keeps things from decay, and quotes Pausanias, who says that rose oil preserves even wooden images from rotting (Leaf on 23.186, citing Pausanias 9.41.7). The oil is also “ambrosial,” and its purpose here is that Hector's skin should not be torn when Achilles drags him (23.187; [[ambrosial]], [[daughter-of-zeus]]). Walter Leaf reports that 184–191 were rejected by Fäsi and Düntzer, and the defense of them by von Christ (on 23.184; see the translation's note on 23.184–191). See [[hector]] and [[aphrodite]].
