## Meaning

Ἀλαλκομενηΐς is an adjective formed from the name of Ἀλαλκομεναί, a small town in Boeotia on the southern shore of Lake Copais, under Mount Tilphossion. Homer uses it only in the verse Ἥρη τʼ Ἀργείη καὶ Ἀλαλκομενηῒς Ἀθήνη, “Argive Hera and Athena of Alalcomenae” (4.8, Il. 5.908), where each goddess is named from a place of her cult ([[argive]]). The Catalogue of Ships does not name the town.

A second explanation, also ancient, takes the epithet from ἀλαλκεῖν (“to ward off”), making Athena “the Protectress.” The town's own name may come from the same verb. The translation renders the place.

## Use

Zeus names Menelaus' helpers by their titles of place and mocks them for sitting apart while Aphrodite saves Paris (4.7–12; [[argive]]). Athena says nothing, “sulking at father Zeus, and a savage anger gripped her” (4.22–23). When she acts, it is on both sides of the arrow. In the likeness of Laodocus she persuades Pandarus to shoot (4.86–104), and then she stands in front of Menelaus and brushes the arrow away “as a mother brushes a fly from her child” (4.130–131; [[mother-and-fly]]). If the gloss “Protectress” is right, the second act is the one her title names.

## Outside Homer

Strabo describes Alalcomenae as a small town with an ancient and much honored temple of Athena, where the goddess was said to have been born, as Hera was at Argos; that, he says, is why Homer named both goddesses from their homelands. He suggests that the Catalogue passes over the town's men because, being sacred, they were excused from the war, and he adds that the town was never sacked: even the Thebans took refuge there when the Epigonoi attacked their city (9.2.36; [[the-epigonoi]]). Pausanias derives the name from Alalcomeneus, a son of the soil who reared the goddess, and records a small stream there called the Triton (9.33.5–7; [[tritogeneia]]).
