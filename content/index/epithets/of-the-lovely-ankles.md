## Meaning

καλλίσφυρος = κάλλος, καλός (“beauty, beautiful”) + σφυρόν (“ankle”): “with beautiful ankles.” The ankle showed beneath the long robe, and a woman's walk and figure are praised by it, as by [[fair-belted]] and [[with-trailing-robes]]. Homer uses it four times: of Marpessa (9.557, 9.560), of Danaë, Acrisius' daughter, in Zeus' list of his loves (14.319), and in the Odyssey of Ino, the sea goddess who saves Odysseus with her veil (Od. 5.333), and of Hebe, Heracles' wife among the gods (Od. 11.603). Hesiod gives it to Victory, to the daughter of Oceanus whom Iapetus married, and to Alcmene (*Theogony* 384, 507, 526, 950), and the Homeric Hymn to Demeter to the goddess herself (*Hymn* 2.453).

## Use

{{quote:3cb79c24-ee08-56f4-b333-21499a986205}}

Meleager's wife Cleopatra is the daughter of “Marpessa of the lovely ankles, daughter of Evenus,” and of Idas, who took up his bow against Apollo “for the sake of the bride of the lovely ankles” (9.557–560; [[marpessa]], [[idas]], [[evenus-father-of-marpessa]], [[idas-and-apollo]]). The epithet is repeated within four verses, once in the genealogy and once in the story of the god and the man who fought over her. See [[cleopatra]].
