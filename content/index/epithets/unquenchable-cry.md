## Meaning

ἄσβεστος = ἀ- (“not”) + σβέννυμι (“quench, put out”): “unquenchable,” the word of the gods' laughter ([[unquenchable-laughter]]). With βοή (“cry, shout,” and so the din of battle; [[good-at-the-war-cry]]) it makes a formula for a battle that will not die down. It is used six times in the Iliad: ἄσβεστος δὲ βοὴ γένετο, “an unquenchable cry went up” (11.50), and the verse-end βοὴ δʼ ἄσβεστος ὀρώρει, of the fighting by the Scamander (11.500), twice in Book 13 (13.169, 13.540) and when the Myrmidons pour out from the ships behind Patroclus (16.267); Cebriones uses it in his own words to Hector (ὄρωρεν, 11.530).

Homer gives the same adjective to the flame on a burning ship (16.123), to the flame of Hephaestus to which Hector is compared (17.88–89), to Hector's fury (ἄσβεστον μένος, 22.96), and in the Odyssey to fame (κλέος ἄσβεστον, Od. 4.584, 7.333).

## Use

{{quote:1fb32700-085d-5766-a950-d6c2905c7aee}}

At dawn of the third day of battle the charioteers hold the horses at the ditch, and the men go forward on foot, armed; the cry goes up before the day is fully light (ἠῶθι πρό, 11.50; [[the-third-day-of-battle]]). The formula returns twice where the fighting is fiercest. On the left of the battle, beside the Scamander, “where most of all the heads of men were falling,” an unquenchable cry goes up around Nestor and Idomeneus (11.498–501; [[scamander]]), and Cebriones, driving Hector, points him to the place where horsemen and foot soldiers destroy one another and “an unquenchable cry has gone up” (11.528–530; [[cebriones]]). Hector's charge there sends “an evil tumult among the Danaans” (11.538–539; [[tumult]]).
