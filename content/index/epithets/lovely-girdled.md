## Meaning

καλλίζωνος = καλός (“beautiful”) + ζώνη (“girdle,” the belt that gathers a woman's robe at the waist): “with a beautiful girdle.” It is one of several epithets of women's dress: [[fair-belted]] (ἐΰζωνος), βαθύζωνος, “deep-girdled,” [[well-robed]] (ἐΰπεπλος) and [[with-trailing-robes]] (ἑλκεσίπεπλος).

Homer uses it three times, always paired with men: “men and lovely-girdled women.” The phrase names everyone in a community. Here they gave Areithous his nickname (7.139). In the last book of the Iliad Cassandra is the first “of the men and lovely-girdled women” of Troy to see Priam bringing Hector's body home (24.697–700), and in the Odyssey the phrase describes the dancing that fills Odysseus' house on the night of his return, staged so that passers-by will think a wedding is being held (Od. 23.143–151).

His nickname was given by everyone, “both men and lovely-girdled women,” because he fought with an iron mace and not with bow or spear ([[areithous]], [[mace]]). The whole community names him, and the name records what was strange about his way of fighting. See [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]].
