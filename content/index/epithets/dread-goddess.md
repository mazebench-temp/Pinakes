## Meaning

δεινός (“dread, terrible, awe-inspiring”) + θεός, which in Homer serves for a goddess as well as a god: “the dread goddess.” δεινός is the word for what inspires fear and awe: the eyes that blaze as Achilles recognizes Athena, hers as the translation takes them (δεινὼ δέ οἱ ὄσσε φάανθεν, 1.200), the Gorgon's head on the aegis (5.741–742), and the crest of Hector's helmet “nodding terribly” over his frightened son (6.470). In the Odyssey the phrase belongs to a line-end formula for the goddesses who keep Odysseus, δεινὴ θεὸς αὐδήεσσα, “a dread goddess who speaks with a human voice” (of Circe, Od. 10.136, 11.8, 12.150; of Calypso, Od. 12.449). There it is often joined with ἐϋπλόκαμος, “with lovely tresses” (Od. 7.41, of Athena; 7.246, 7.255, of Calypso).

## Use

Hector asks the slave women whether Andromache has gone to the temple of Athena, “where the other Trojan women with lovely tresses are appeasing the dread goddess” (6.379–380), and the housekeeper answers in the same words (6.384–385; [[with-lovely-tresses]], [[handmaids]]). The two epithets that the Odyssey joins in one formula for a goddess are divided here between the women and the goddess they pray to.

The verb, ἱλάσκομαι (“appease, win the favor of”), is the one used in Book 1 of the Achaeans' efforts to appease Apollo, which succeed: “all day long they sought the god's favor with song and dance” (1.472; [[propitiation]]). The Trojan women's efforts have already failed, since Athena has refused the robe (6.311). The only time the Iliad calls her the dread goddess is when her anger cannot be turned ([[athena]], [[the-robe-for-athena]]).
