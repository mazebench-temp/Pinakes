## Meaning

ἀμενηνός, “strengthless, feeble,” is in Merry's view ἀ- (“not”) + μένος (“strength”): the dead are powerless, as Anticlea describes them to her son, and their voices are thin and squeaking (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 10.521, citing *Odyssey* 11.217–222). The souls of the suitors squeak like bats as Hermes leads them down (*Odyssey* 24.5–9), and the soul of Patroclus goes beneath the earth “like smoke, / gibbering” (*Iliad* 23.100–101). Merry rejects Döderlein's derivation from μένω, “abide,” of one who flees like a shadow and never stays in one place, since Homer gives the word to a wounded god: Ares tells Zeus that but for his swift feet he would have suffered long among the heaps of the dead, “or lived on strengthless from the blows of the bronze” (*Iliad* 5.887). The Odyssey has it once more, of the two gates of strengthless dreams (ἀμενηνῶν ὀνείρων, *Odyssey* 19.562).

κάρηνα, “heads,” stands for persons when they are many and are dying: under Agamemnon “fell the heads / of the fleeing Trojans” (*Iliad* 11.158–159), and by the Scamander, where the fighting was thickest, “the heads of men were falling” (*Iliad* 11.500). The same plural is the peaks of mountains (*Iliad* 2.869; *Odyssey* 9.113) and the heads of horses and oxen (*Iliad* 9.407, 23.260).

## Use

The phrase belongs to the rite at the edge of the world of the dead. Circe bids Odysseus, when he has poured the drink offerings, to “earnestly implore the strengthless heads of the dead” with vows of sacrifice when he comes home, and later, sword in hand, not to “let the strengthless heads of the dead / come near the blood until you have questioned Teiresias” (*Odyssey* 10.521, 10.536–537). In *Odyssey* 11 he does both, and the verses are spoken again in the first person (*Odyssey* 11.29, 11.49). Merry notes that the black sheep for Teiresias was to be sacrificed apart, so that the offering in his honor should not be confounded with the general offering to the strengthless heads (on 10.524; [[teiresias]]). ## Outside Homer

Euripides' Hecuba, led off to slavery, calls herself a drone, “the form of a corpse, a strengthless image of the dead” (νεκροῦ μορφά, νεκύων ἀμενηνὸν ἄγαλμα, *Trojan Women* 191–193). Merry cites the line, and reports that Sophocles uses the word of the crazed Ajax (*Ajax* 890), where Schneidewin rendered it “wandering, restless” (on 10.521).
