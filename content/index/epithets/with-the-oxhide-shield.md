## Meaning

ταλαύρινος is usually analyzed as the root of τλῆναι (“bear, endure”; compare τάλας) + ῥινός (“hide,” and so a shield of hide): “bearing the oxhide shield.” Hector's boast in Book 7 supports this sense. He knows how to swing his dry oxhide shield to the right and to the left, “and that for me is to fight ταλαύρινον” (Il. 7.238–239), that is, as a shield-bearing warrior. Others understand it as “enduring, stubborn in fight.” The translation takes the first sense.

The adjective lives in one verse, αἵματος ἆσαι Ἄρηα ταλαύρινον πολεμιστήν, which Homer uses three times, each time of a fight to the death between two men: here, of Achilles longing to meet Hector (Il. 20.78), and in Achilles' answer to Hector before their duel (Il. 22.267). The verb ἆσαι (“glut, sate”) is from the root of [[insatiable-of-war]] (ἆτος).

## Use

{{quote:58fa538c-d329-53b9-95db-82a486a46488}}

Pandarus has thrown his spear through Diomedes' shield and boasted that he has hit him in the flank (5.280–285). Diomedes corrects him and promises a death: the fight will not end before one of the two, Pandarus or Aeneas, has fallen and glutted the war god with his blood. The war god is named as a shield-bearing spearman just after the archer has made his first cast with the spear. Pandarus had come to Troy on foot, trusting in his bow (5.192–205), and the spear serves him no better than his arrows ([[pandarus]], [[bow]]). In the next verse Athena guides Diomedes' spear into his face (5.290).

Achilles speaks the same second half of the sentence to Hector in Book 22: there will be no oaths between them “before one or the other of us has fallen” (πρίν γʼ ἢ ἕτερόν γε πεσόντα, Il. 22.266 = 5.288).
