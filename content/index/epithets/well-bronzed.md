## Meaning

εὔχαλκος = εὖ (“well”) + χαλκός (“bronze”): “well made of bronze, well fitted with bronze.” Homer uses it four times: of the helmet of Eioneus (7.12), of Peisander's axe on its long polished haft of olive wood (13.611–613), of the ash spear of Achilles, which Poseidon draws out of Aeneas' shield (20.321–323), and in the Odyssey of the tripods a host might give his guest (Od. 15.84). Compare [[bronze-fitted]] (χαλκήρης) and the other epithets of the metal, [[gleaming-bronze]] and [[pitiless-bronze]]. See [[bronze]].

## Use

The book's first killings are told in the brief style of the battle lists:

στεφάνη is a helmet here, or the rim of one; the translation takes it as the rim. The bronze helmet does not save its wearer: the spear goes in below it, at the neck, “and his limbs were loosened” ([[loosened-his-limbs]], [[androktasia]]). Eioneus is one of three Achaeans killed in the opening lines, by Paris, Hector and Glaucus (7.8–16; [[eioneus]], [[the-war-in-small]]).
