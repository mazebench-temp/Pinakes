## Meaning

βαθύζωνος = βαθύς (“deep”) + ζώνη (“belt, girdle”): “deep-girdled,” usually explained as wearing the girdle low on the hips, or with the dress falling in deep folds over it. It is praise of dress and figure, like [[fair-belted]] (ἐΰζωνος) and καλλίζωνος (“with a beautiful girdle”). Homer uses it twice, both times in the phrase βαθυζώνους τε γυναῖκας at the end of the verse, of the women taken as spoil: here, and in the Odyssey when Nestor recalls how the Achaeans loaded their ships with “possessions and deep-girdled women” as they left Troy (Od. 3.153–154). The Homeric Hymn to Demeter uses it of the women of Eleusis, of Metaneira, and of Persephone (*Hymn* 2.95, 2.161, 2.201, 2.304). See [[captive-women]].

## Use

{{quote:3ee36880-d486-5819-9d6f-986869c5b979}}

Cleopatra moves Meleager by telling him all the sorrows that come to men whose city is taken: “they kill the men, and fire levels the city, and strangers lead away the children and the deep-girdled women” (9.592–594; [[cleopatra]], [[meleager]], [[women-and-war]]). The epithet of beauty is given to women at the moment they become captives. It is what Andromache foresees for herself in Book 6 and what the Iliad will bring to Troy (6.454–465). Achilles has already counted such women among the wealth he will take home from Troy (9.366; [[fair-belted]]).
