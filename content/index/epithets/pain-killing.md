## Meaning

ὀδυνήφατος = ὀδύνη (“pain”) + -φατος, from the root of πεφνεῖν (“slay”): “pain-slaying.” Homer uses it three times: twice of the drugs of Paeëon (5.401, 5.900), and once of the bitter root that Patroclus crushes and lays on Eurypylus' wound (Il. 11.846–847). Compare [[soothing-drugs]] (ἤπια φάρμακα); see [[healing]].

## Use

{{quote:1ef90771-b2bb-5af4-a2ba-4bb21bf88e89}}

The healing verses are said twice in Book 5, first of Hades and then of Ares. In Dione's story Hades, shot by Heracles, goes up to Olympus, “and Paeëon spread pain-killing drugs on it and healed him; for he was not made to be mortal” (5.401–402; [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]]). At the end of the book the same two verses heal Ares (5.900–901), and the fig-juice simile shows how fast (5.902–904; [[fig-juice]], [[paeeon]]). The story that Dione told for comfort has come true in the book's own action. The reason given each time, that the god was not made to be mortal, is the difference between the wounds of gods and of men ([[gods-wounded-by-mortals]], [[ichor]]).
