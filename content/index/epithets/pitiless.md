## Meaning

νηλής (also νηλεής) = νη- (“not”) + ἔλεος (“pity”): “without pity” ([[privative-adjectives]]). Its commonest use is the formula of the weapon, νηλέϊ χαλκῷ, “with the pitiless bronze” ([[pitiless-bronze]]). Of persons it is a hard word, and in the Iliad it is spoken above all to Achilles or about him by those who love him: by Phoenix, who tells him he has no need of “a pitiless heart” (νηλεὲς ἦτορ, 9.497); by Ajax, who ends a sentence on him with the bare word, “pitiless one” (9.632); and by Patroclus, in his plea at the beginning of Book 16: “Pitiless one, your father was not the horseman Peleus” (16.33). Achilles reports that the Myrmidons used it of him too, νηλεές (16.203–204). In the Odyssey the Cyclops answers Odysseus “with a pitiless heart” (Od. 9.272, 9.287, 9.368). The formula νηλεὲς ἦμαρ, “the pitiless day,” is the day of death that warriors ward off (11.484, 11.588) or flee from (21.57; Od. 9.17). In Hesiod Death himself has “a heart of iron, and a spirit of bronze, pitiless in his breast” (*Theogony* 764–765).

## Use

{{quote:cb503f95-7816-5b28-b9f3-3e26da2743f1}}

Phoenix's appeal turns on the word: “tame your great spirit; you have no need to have a pitiless heart; even the gods themselves can be turned” ([[even-the-gods-can-be-turned]], [[untamable]]). Ajax, who speaks last, first speaks of Achilles to Odysseus, in the third person, and ends the sentence with the bare word at the head of the verse; then he gives the example of the blood-price that a man accepts even for a brother's death, and only then turns to Achilles himself (9.628–642; [[poine]]). In Book 16 Patroclus uses the same word, and Achilles gives way so far as to send him out in his own armor (16.33–45, 16.64–65). See [[achilles]].
