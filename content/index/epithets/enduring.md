## Meaning

τλήμων is formed from the root of τλῆναι, which means both “endure, bear” and “dare, bring oneself to do.” The adjective has both senses: “enduring, patient” and “daring, hardy.” In later Greek it came to mean “wretched,” one who has much to bear, but not in Homer. The Iliad uses it four times. Odysseus is “he of the enduring spirit” when he sees Sarpedon wound Tlepolemus and ponders whether to pursue him (τλήμονα θυμὸν ἔχων, 5.669–671); twice in Book 10 he is ὁ τλήμων Ὀδυσεύς, “enduring Odysseus” (10.231, 10.498); and in Book 21 Athena, having struck down Ares and Aphrodite, mocks them: may all the Trojans' helpers be “so bold and enduring” as Aphrodite was when she came to help Ares (θαρσαλέοι καὶ τλήμονες, 21.428–431), where the word stands beside θαρσαλέος, “bold.” The same root gives Odysseus his other epithets: [[much-enduring]] (πολύτλας), [[stout-hearted]] (ταλασίφρων), and the [[daring]] heart (τολμήεις). The translation renders τλήμων “enduring.”

## Use

{{quote:d48042bf-dcfe-5b25-bb26-6abf33f03da2}}

When Diomedes asks for a companion, all the chiefs are willing in turn, and the list ends with Odysseus: “enduring Odysseus was willing to slip into the throng of the Trojans; for the heart in his breast was always daring” ([[thymos]], [[attendants-of-ares]]). The second verse explains the epithet with a verb from the same root, ἐτόλμα, “was always daring,” and leans it toward the second sense, daring rather than patience. The epithet returns, with the same article, ὁ τλήμων, at Odysseus' own part of the raid: while Diomedes kills the king, “enduring Odysseus untied the single-hoofed horses” (10.498; [[single-hoofed]], [[the-horses-of-rhesus]]).
