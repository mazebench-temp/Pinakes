## Meaning

ὀξύς means “sharp, pointed,” and by extension “keen” of sight and “shrill” of sound; the neuter used as an adverb means “keenly, quickly,” as when Aphrodite was quick to see Paris in danger (ὀξὺ νόησε, {{ref:Iliad 3.374}}). As an epithet it belongs to weapons. The formulas ὀξέϊ χαλκῷ (“with the sharp bronze”), ὀξέϊ δουρί (“with the sharp spear”) and ξίφος ὀξύ or φάσγανον ὀξύ (“the sharp sword,” {{ref:Iliad 1.190}}) are among the commonest in the Iliad. It is also given to Ares, as the name of war, in the line-end formula ἐγείρομεν ὀξὺν Ἄρηα, “we rouse sharp Ares” ({{ref:Iliad 2.440}}, 4.352). The compound ὀξυβελής, “sharp-pointed” (literally “with a sharp missile”), is found in Homer only in the lines of Pandarus' shot (4.126). Compare [[piercing]], [[bitter]] and [[flesh-cutting]].

## Use

The word runs through the scene of the arrow and the first battle. The arrow leaps from the string “sharp-pointed” (4.125–126); Menelaus assures his brother that “the sharp shaft” did not stick in a deadly place (4.185); and as Machaon draws it out, “the sharp barbs” break back (4.214). Odysseus uses the formula of war in his angry answer to Agamemnon: how can he say that they hold back “whenever we Achaeans rouse sharp Ares against the horse-taming Trojans?” (4.351–352). He speaks the same verse again in Book 19 (Il. 19.237). In the fighting Antiphus throws “his sharp spear” (4.490), Thoas draws “his sharp sword” (4.530), and the book ends with a man who might go “unhit and unwounded by the sharp bronze” through the battle, if Athena led him by the hand (4.540–542).
