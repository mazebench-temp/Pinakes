## Meaning

ἑλκεσίπεπλος = ἕλκω (“drag, trail”) + πέπλος (“robe”; [[robe]]): “whose robes trail on the ground,” the long garments of women who do not work in the fields. It belongs with the other epithets of women's dress: [[long-robed]] (τανύπεπλος), [[well-robed]] (ἐΰπεπλος) and [[fair-belted]] (ἐΰζωνος). Homer uses it three times, always of the Trojan women, in the phrase Τρῶας καὶ Τρῳάδας ἑλκεσιπέπλους, “the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes,” and always in a speech of Hector's.

## Use

Andromache has asked Hector to stay on the tower. He answers:

{{quote:4042d2ed-5830-5c3d-9b18-dd32069bc8e0}}

The Trojan women are the audience of his honor. They are the women who crowded around him at the Scaean gates asking after their men (6.238–241), who pray to Athena in her temple (6.297–311, 6.380), and whose captivity he foresees in the same speech (6.454–463; [[captive-women]]). Before them he would feel shame (αἰδέομαι; [[aidos]]) if he held back.

The other two uses come from Hector too. Parting from Ajax after their duel, he says he will gladden “the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes” in the city, who will go into the gathering place of the gods praying for him (Il. 7.295–298). And outside the wall, waiting for Achilles, he repeats the verse of Book 6 word for word: he has destroyed his people by his own recklessness, “and I feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes,” for fear that someone worse than he may say that Hector trusted in his strength and lost the army (Il. 22.104–107). The line that kept him from the tower in Book 6 keeps him outside the gates in Book 22 ([[hector]]).
