## Meaning

μελάνυδρος = μέλας (“black, dark”) + ὕδωρ (“water”): “with dark water.” Homer uses it only of springs. The water is dark as deep or shaded water is, like the “dark water” (μέλαν ὕδωρ) of the Aesepus that the men of Zeleia drink (2.825) and that Charybdis sucks down (Od. 12.104). The word occurs five times: in the simile of the weeping Agamemnon (9.14) and again of Patroclus' tears (16.3); in the simile of the Myrmidons as wolves, who go in a pack to lap the dark water of such a spring (16.160–161); in the simile of a man who leads a channel from a spring of dark water to his garden (21.257); and in the Odyssey, where twenty serving women go out to such a spring for water on the morning of a feast day (Od. 20.156–158). Compare [[black-ships]] for μέλας, and the other words of this simile, [[sheer]] and [[dnopheros]].

## Use

{{quote:fdbf2d94-5382-55d5-bfcb-835ec9315033}}

Agamemnon stands up in the assembly in tears, “like a spring of dark water that pours its murky water down a sheer rock” (9.14–15). The two verses return unchanged at the beginning of Book 16, when Patroclus comes weeping to Achilles over the Achaeans' losses (16.3–4). The king who began the quarrel and the friend who will die to end its consequences weep in the same words ([[the-spring-of-dark-water]]); Achilles meets the second with a simile of his own, the little girl who runs beside her mother crying to be picked up (16.7–10). See [[agamemnon]] and [[shedding-tears]].
