## Meaning

πηκτός is the verbal adjective of πήγνυμι (“fix, fasten, fit together”): “fitted together.” A plow so described is made of separate pieces of wood fitted and pinned together, not cut from a single bent piece. Homer uses it only in the verse-end πηκτὸν ἄροτρον, three times, each time of a plow dragged through fallow land: by mules, which are better at it than oxen (10.352–353); by two wine-dark oxen pulling side by side, in the simile of the two Ajaxes who stand together in the fight (13.701–707); and by the oxen of a plowman who longs all day for his supper, as Odysseus longs for the sunset that will take him home from Scheria (Od. 13.31–35). The compound εὔπηκτος, “well fitted together,” describes halls and huts ([[eupektos]]).

## Use

{{quote:d226f8ac-af08-5f63-9662-22508a385df9}}

Odysseus and Diomedes let Dolon run past them, and when he is as far off as the furrow-length of mules, they run after him. The parenthesis explains the measure: mules are better than oxen “at dragging the jointed plow through the deep fallow.” Whether the furrow-length is the stretch a team plows without a halt, or the lead by which mules draw ahead of oxen plowing beside them, is disputed ([[mules]], [[cattle]]). The distance is taken from the farm, as the race in the Odyssey measures a runner's lead “as far as the furrow-length of mules in fallow land” (Od. 8.124). See [[dolon]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells the farmer to make two plows and keep them at home, a plow of a single piece and a jointed one (αὐτόγυον καὶ πηκτόν), so that if he breaks one he can put the oxen to the other; he names the pieces of the jointed plow and the woods to make them from (*Works and Days* 427–436).
