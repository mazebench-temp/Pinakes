## Meaning

βαθύκολπος = βαθύς (“deep”) + κόλπος (“bosom”; also the fold of the robe over the breast, and the lap): “deep-bosomed,” of a full breast, or of the deep fold of the robe that hangs over the girdle. Walter Leaf takes it as almost the same in sense as βαθύζωνος, “deep-girdled” (9.594; [[deep-girdled]]), and notes that both happen to be used in Homer only of Trojan women; Aristarchus, he reports, therefore took them for a foreign style of dress, a view Leaf doubts, since national differences of this kind are almost unknown to Homer and later poets know no such restriction (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.122). The word occurs three times in the Iliad, always at the end of the verse: Τρωϊάδων καὶ Δαρδανίδων βαθυκόλπων (18.122), Τρῳαὶ καὶ Δαρδανίδες βαθύκολποι (18.339), and Τρώων καὶ Τρωϊάδων βαθυκόλπων (24.215). Leaf also reports that Zenodotus read βαθύκολποι of the Muses at 2.484, where the text has Ὀλύμπια δώματʼ ἔχουσαι (on 18.122). See [[trojan-women]].

## Outside Homer

The Homeric Hymns give the epithet to goddesses and nymphs: Persephone was playing “with the deep-bosomed daughters of Oceanus” when Hades carried her off (*Homeric Hymn to Demeter* 5), and Aphrodite tells Anchises that the deep-bosomed nymphs of the mountains will rear their son (*Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* 256–257).
