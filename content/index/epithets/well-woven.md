## Meaning

εὖ (“well”) + πλέκω (“plait, weave”): the compounds of πλεκτός, “plaited, woven” ([[woven]]). Homer uses them four times: of the hundred golden tassels that hang from Athena's aegis, “all finely woven” (2.448–449; [[aegis]]), of the ropes the woodcutters take to Ida (23.115), and twice of the chariot (23.335, 23.436). Walter Leaf notes that the chariot is called “well-woven” only in these two verses, in place of the usual ἐΰξεστος, “well-polished,” and that the word may refer to a woven floor of the car (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.335).

## Use

Nestor tells Antilochus to lean “in the well-woven chariot / a little to the left” as he rounds the post (23.335–336), and Menelaus holds back his mares so that the horses do not “overturn the well-woven chariots” in the narrow road (23.435–436). See [[chariot]] and [[turning-post]].
