## Meaning

κεφαλή (“head”) stands in Homer for the whole person, and for a life. Achilles honored Patroclus “equal to my own head” (ἶσον ἐμῇ κεφαλῇ, 18.81–82) and greets his ghost as ἠθείη κεφαλή, “dear head” (23.94); Penelope longs for “such a head” (τοίην … κεφαλήν), her lost husband (Od. 1.343). The vocative φίλη κεφαλή occurs only here. The translation gives it the English idiom of affection, “dear heart.” Compare [[my-dear]] and [[dear-brother]].

## Use

{{quote:5e9a94e3-700c-567d-a7a9-a70bd7315697}}

Agamemnon has watched Teucer kill eight men from behind Ajax's shield (8.273–279; [[back-bent]]). His praise begins with the address of affection and goes on with Τελαμώνιε κοίρανε λαῶν, “son of Telamon, lord of the people” (8.281), the half-verse with which Ajax is addressed elsewhere, by Hector in Book 7 and by Achilles and Menelaus in later books (7.234, 9.644, 11.465; [[telamonian]]). In the next verses he names Teucer's birth: Telamon reared him in his own house, “bastard though you were” ([[nothos]], [[telamon]]); that verse (8.284) is reported to have been rejected in antiquity. The warmth and the reminder stand side by side, and then comes the promise of a gift of honor when Troy is taken (8.286–291; [[geras]]). Teucer answers that he needs no urging (8.293–299). See [[teucer]] and [[agamemnon]].
