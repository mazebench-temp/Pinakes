## Meaning

Ancient scholars analyzed ἀμύμων as ἀ- (“not”) + μῶμος (“blame”): “blameless.” The derivation is difficult, since the vowels do not match, and the word is used in ways that have nothing moral about them. The famous case is Aegisthus, adulterer and murderer, who is ἀμύμων at Od. 1.29. Such cases are the classic evidence that a Homeric epithet can be ornamental, fixed to its noun regardless of context. Anne Amory Parry (*Blameless Aegisthus*, 1973) argued that the word means “handsome, excellent,” a term of general praise. The question remains open. The translation renders it “noble” of Calchas and “blameless” of the Ethiopians.

## Use

{{quote:10431272-fdda-5d6b-889a-b5f838187ae1}}

Calchas is μάντις ἀμύμων as he speaks under Achilles' protection. The phrase is ordinary praise for a seer, but in Book 1 it stands against Agamemnon's answer fourteen lines later, μάντι κακῶν, “prophet of evils” ([[prophet-of-evils]]).

{{quote:dc12989d-0cdd-56cd-982f-df5359978008}}

The Ethiopians live by the stream of Oceanus at the edge of the world, and the gods go to feast with them. Here the traditional sense fits well: they are a pious people whose sacrifices the gods attend in person, as Poseidon does at the opening of the Odyssey (Od. 1.22–25). Their feast also explains the timing of the plot: Zeus is away for twelve days, and Thetis must wait ([[feast-type-scene]]).
