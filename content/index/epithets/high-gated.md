## Meaning

ὑψίπυλος = ὕψι (“high, on high”) + πύλη (“gate”): “with high gates,” the mark of a strong walled city. The name of Hypsipyle, whose son by Jason sends ships of wine from Lemnos to the Achaean camp, is the same compound (Il. 7.467–469). Homer counts the gates of other cities: Boeotian Thebes has seven (ἑπτάπυλος, 4.406; [[seven-gated]]), and Egyptian Thebes a hundred (ἑκατόμπυλοι, Il. 9.383).

The word occurs three times. Once it describes Thebe (Il. 6.416). Twice it describes Troy in the same verse: “then the sons of the Achaeans would have taken high-gated Troy” (ἔνθά κεν ὑψίπυλον Τροίην ἕλον υἷες Ἀχαιῶν), once of Patroclus' onslaught and once of Achilles', each time until Apollo intervenes (Il. 16.698–701, 21.544–545; [[would-have-had-not]]).

The epithet of the city's strength stands in the verse of its fall. Thebe's high gates did not keep out Achilles, and the next lines tell how he killed Eetion and his seven sons in one day (6.416–424; [[sack-of-thebe]], [[eetion]], [[thebe]]). Andromache is speaking at the Scaean gates of Troy (6.392–394; [[scaean-gates]]). The two later uses give the same epithet to Troy, and both times its gates hold only because Apollo intervenes.
