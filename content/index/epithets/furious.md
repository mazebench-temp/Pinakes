## Meaning

θοῦρος is formed from the root of θρῴσκω, aorist θορεῖν (“leap, spring”): “rushing, impetuous.” Book 4 uses the verb when Athena comes down “and leapt into their midst” (κὰδ δʼ ἔθορʼ, 4.79). The masculine is an epithet of Ares, θοῦρος Ἄρης (for example Il. 5.30, 5.35). The feminine θοῦρις describes the shield (Il. 11.32) and, above all, ἀλκή (“courage, fighting strength”) in the formula θοῦρις ἀλκή, “furious courage.” It ends a verse of exhortation that recurs through the Iliad: ἀνέρες ἔστε φίλοι, μνήσασθε δὲ θούριδος ἀλκῆς, “be men, my friends, and remember your furious courage” (for example Il. 6.112, 15.734). See [[ares]], [[exhortation]].

## Use

The phrase opens and closes Agamemnon's review of the army. His first words, to the men he finds eager, are “Argives, do not yet let go of your furious courage” (4.234):

{{quote:1115d2c5-c473-5637-a7a5-eb7acaf756eb}}

Sarpedon rebukes his Lycians with the same words in Book 12 (Il. 12.409). The review ends when Diomedes, after his silence, says to Sthenelus: “But come, let us two also think of our furious courage” (4.418), and leaps down in his armor. Hera says the same verse to Athena in Book 5 (Il. 5.718). See [[the-review]], [[the-review-of-the-army]].
