## Meaning

ἐΰθρονος = εὖ (“well, fair”) + θρόνος (“chair, throne”; [[throne]]): “with a fair throne.” A throne is the seat of a god or of a person of rank. Hera is “of the golden throne” (χρυσόθρονος, 1.611; [[golden-throned]]), and in the Odyssey Dawn often is too, in the verse “at once golden-throned Dawn came” (Od. 10.541, 12.142, 15.56, 20.91). Dawn's other epithets are [[saffron-robed]], [[rosy-fingered]] and [[early-born]].

ἐΰθρονος describes Dawn once in the Iliad and five times in the Odyssey. Three times it is a Dawn that is waited for, ἐΰθρονον Ἠῶ μίμνειν: here; when Odysseus, as a beggar, says he will keep the torches lit for the suitors even if they wish to wait for Dawn of the fair throne (Od. 18.318); and when he tells Penelope that he has waited for bright Dawn through many nights (Od. 19.342). It also brings Dawn to Nausicaa's bed and to Telemachus' landing on Ithaca (Od. 6.48, 15.495), and the housekeeper Eurynome wishes that none of the suitors may live to see it (Od. 17.495–497).

## Use

{{quote:571a0630-c99f-55ed-821c-0f311cd3e32c}}

The last verses of the book. The first verse brought in saffron-robed Dawn and the day that Zeus gave the Trojans (8.1; [[saffron-robed]]); the last leaves the Trojans' horses standing by their chariots on the plain, eating white barley and spelt, as the horses that Pandarus left at home in Lycaon's halls do in Book 5 (5.192–196; [[white]]), and waiting for the next dawn. The night between lasts through Books 9 and 10, and the dawn that the horses wait for opens Book 11 (11.1–2). See [[eos]] and [[horses]].
