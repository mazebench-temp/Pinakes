## Meaning

τριγλώχιν = τρι- (“three”) + γλωχίς (“point, barb”): “three-barbed,” of an arrowhead with three points or flanges. Homer uses it twice, both times of an arrow that wounds. In Dione's list of gods who have suffered at the hands of men, the son of Amphitryon “struck her in the right breast with a three-barbed arrow,” Hera (5.392–394; [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]]); and Paris hits Machaon in the right shoulder (11.505–507). Compare [[long-barbed]] (τανυγλῶχιν), of Teucer's arrows, and the other epithets of the arrow: [[bitter]], [[piercing]], [[feathered]].

## Use

{{quote:1a10b34a-5138-52a2-9e1b-f0201e49fe7b}}

The Achaeans would not yet have given ground by the Scamander had Paris not stopped Machaon “while he was the best in the fighting” ([[would-have-had-not]], [[husband-of-helen]], [[shepherd-of-the-people]]). They fear that he will be taken if the battle turns, and Idomeneus sends Nestor to carry him to the ships, “for a healer is a man worth many others, to cut out arrows and spread on soothing drugs” (11.508–515; [[soothing-drugs]], [[a-healer-is-worth-many]]). The healer who cuts out arrows has been wounded by one. See [[machaon]], [[paris]] and [[arrows]].
