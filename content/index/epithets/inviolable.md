## Meaning

ἀάατος = ἀ- (“not”) + the root of ἀάω (“harm, lead astray”) and of ἄτη ([[ate]]): “not to be harmed, not to be led astray,” and so, of an oath, “inviolable.” Homer uses it three times. In the Iliad it describes the water of Styx, by which the gods swear their greatest oath (14.271; [[styx]], [[oath]]). In the Odyssey it twice describes the contest of the bow, ἄεθλον ἀάατον and ἄεθλος ἀάατος (*Odyssey* 21.91, 22.5), where its sense is disputed (see the translation's note on 14.271).

The Styx is the gods' oath elsewhere in the Iliad without this epithet. The river Titaressus is “a branch of the water of Styx, the dread oath” (2.755; [[fair-flowing]]), and when Zeus wakes, Hera swears by Earth and Heaven and the down-flowing water of Styx, the greatest and most dread oath for the blessed gods (15.36–38; [[blessed-gods]]).

## Use

{{quote:512fa886-cdaa-54fa-a0dc-d7a78c459d19}}

Sleep, whom Zeus would have hurled from the upper air for helping Hera once before (14.249–262), will not lull Zeus without a guarantee. He asks her for the oath that binds the gods, by the inviolable water of Styx, with one hand on the earth and the other on the sea and the gods below as witnesses, that she will give him Pasithea, one of the younger Graces, as his wife (14.270–276; [[sleep]], [[hera]], [[pasithea]], [[bountiful-earth]], [[marmareos]]). Hera swears, naming “all the gods / beneath Tartarus, who are called Titans” (14.278–279; [[titans]], [[white-armed]]). The epithet fits a bargain between two who are deceiving a third: whatever Hera does to Zeus, her oath to Sleep cannot be broken. See [[the-deception-of-zeus]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells how Zeus sends Iris to fetch the water of Styx in a golden jug when a god has lied, and how any god who pours it and swears falsely lies a year without breath, and is cut off from the councils and feasts of the gods for nine years more; he calls it Στυγὸς ἄφθιτον ὕδωρ, “the imperishable water of Styx” (*Theogony* 782–806). Aristotle reports that some thought the earliest theologians held Thales' view, since they made Oceanus and Tethys the parents of becoming and made water, the Styx, the oath of the gods, “for what is oldest is most honored, and the oath is the most honored thing” (*Metaphysics* 1.3, 983b27–33; [[aristotle-oceanus-and-tethys]]).
