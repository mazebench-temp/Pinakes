## Meaning

ἱππότα (“horseman, charioteer,” from ἵππος) has the archaic nominative in short -τα that also appears in νεφεληγερέτα ([[cloud-gatherer]]) and μητίετα ([[counselor]]). The ending is itself a sign of the formula's age.

Γερήνιος is obscure. Ancient scholars derived it from a town Gerenia in Messenia, where Nestor was said to have been brought up; others connected it with γέρων (“old man”) or γέρας (“prize of honor”). None of the explanations is certain.

The full formula Γερήνιος ἱππότα Νέστωρ closes the verse thirty-one times in Homer. ἱππότα alone describes other men of the older generation: Phyleus here, Tydeus (Il. 5.126), Oeneus (Il. 14.117), and Peleus (Il. 16.33, 23.89).

## Use

The formula names Nestor whenever he speaks or leads in Book 2: as he rebukes the assembly for talking like children ({{ref:Iliad 2.336}}), as he urges Agamemnon to call the army to war after the sacrifice ({{ref:Iliad 2.433}}), and as he leads ninety ships in the Catalogue ({{ref:Iliad 2.601}}).

{{quote:267ace6c-1956-5c11-88cc-0e488534c636}}

Nestor's own stories show why he is the horseman. In Book 11 he tells how he fought as a young man on foot and then took the chariots of the Epeians, and in Book 23 he advises his son on chariot racing.

The single ἱππότα goes to Phyleus, father of Meges, “the horseman Phyleus, dear to Zeus” ({{ref:Iliad 2.628}}; [[dear-to-zeus]]).
