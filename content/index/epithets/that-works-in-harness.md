## Meaning

ἐντεσιεργός = ἔντεα (“gear, harness”) + ἔργον (“work”): “working in harness.” Walter Leaf takes the first part of the compound as a locative, “in harness,” like the first part of ὀρεσίτροφος, “mountain-bred,” and notes that ἔντεα means the harness of horses in Pindar too (ἱππείοις ἐν ἔντεσσιν, *Olympian* 13.20, as Leaf cites it); he calls Nauck's conjecture ἠνυσιεργούς needless (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.277). Elsewhere in Homer ἔντεα are most often a warrior's arms.

## Use

The word is used once, in the verse of the yoking of Priam's mule wagon: ζεῦξαν δʼ ἡμιόνους κρατερώνυχας ἐντεσιεργούς, “and yoked the strong-hoofed mules that work in harness, / which the Mysians had once given to Priam, splendid gifts” (24.277–278). The two epithets set the working animals beside the horses that Priam reared for himself, which draw his own chariot (24.279–280; [[strong-hoofed]], [[mysians]]). See [[mules]] and [[wagon]].
