## Meaning

ἑλκεχίτων = the stem of ἕλκω (“drag, trail”) + χιτών (“tunic”): “trailing the tunic,” wearing a tunic long enough to reach the ground. The first part is the same as in ἑλκεσίπεπλος, “trailing their robes,” Hector's epithet for the Trojan women ([[with-trailing-robes]]). Homer has the word only here.

## Use

{{quote:d55fadbc-9f08-59a4-80cb-68d671055763}}

The Ionians appear only in this verse of Homer, in the list of the peoples who hold Hector back from the ships where he first broke in, by the ships of Ajax and Protesilaus, and the epithet is all that the poem says of them (13.685; [[ionians]], [[boeotians]], [[locrians]], [[phthians]], [[epeians]]). The next lines name “the picked men of the Athenians” (13.689; [[athenians]]), and Strabo took the Ionians to be the Athenians themselves (see below).

## Outside Homer

The Homeric Hymn to Apollo has the words in reverse order, ἑλκεχίτωνες Ἰάονες: at Delos the Ionians with trailing tunics gather for the god with their children and their wives, and delight him with boxing, dancing and song at the festival (*Hymn to Apollo* 146–150). Strabo, arguing that Homer's Athenians include all the people of Attica, says that Attica was once called Ionia and Ias, and that when the poet says “there the Boeotians and the Ionians” he means the Athenians (*Geography* 9.1.5). Discussing the name of the Curetes, he also reports the view that Greek men once had a fashion of dress like women's, of which the Ionians “with trailing tunics” and the combed hair of Leonidas' men before battle are given as signs (*Geography* 10.3.8).
