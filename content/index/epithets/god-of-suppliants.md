## Meaning

ἱκετήσιος is an adjective formed from ἱκέτης, “suppliant,” one who comes (ἱκνέομαι, “come”) to another and asks his protection (see [[supplication]]): Zeus “of suppliants,” the god to whom they belong and who punishes those who wrong them. Homer has the word only once, but the belief is the Odyssey's throughout. The Phaeacians pour libations “to Zeus who delights in thunder, … who attends on revered suppliants” (*Odyssey* 7.164–165, 7.180–181; see [[delighting-in-thunder]]); Odysseus tells the Cyclops that “Zeus is the avenger of suppliants and strangers, / the god of guests” (*Odyssey* 9.270–271; see [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]]); and in the Iliad Menelaus reproaches the Trojans who did not “fear the harsh wrath of Zeus who thunders loud, / god of guests” (*Iliad* 13.624–625; see [[god-of-guests]] and [[xenia]]). In the later books of the Odyssey the king of Egypt spares the beggar of the false tale for fear of the wrath of Zeus, “the god of guests, who most of all is indignant at evil deeds” (*Odyssey* 14.283–284; see [[the-raid-on-egypt]]), and Penelope rebukes Antinous for paying no heed to suppliants, “for whom Zeus / is witness” (*Odyssey* 16.422–423, a plain rendering).

## Outside Homer

In Aeschylus' *Suppliants* the daughters of Danaus, suppliants at the altars of Argos, warn King Pelasgus that “heavy is the anger of Zeus of suppliants” (βαρύς γε μέντοι Ζηνὸς ἱκεσίου κότος, *Suppliants* 347), and that “the anger of Zeus of suppliants waits” for those who do not heed them (Ζηνὸς ἱκταίου κότος, 385). The king answers that he must respect the anger of Zeus, protector of suppliants, “for it is the highest fear among mortals” (478–479), and he wins the Argives' vote by warning them of “the great anger of Zeus of suppliants” (ἱκεσίου Ζηνὸς κότον, 616). Aeschylus' words for the title are ἱκέσιος, ἱκταῖος and ἱκτήρ, not Homer's ἱκετήσιος.
