## Meaning

ῥιγεδανός is formed from the root of ῥῖγος (“cold, shivering”) and ῥιγέω (“shudder”), with the suffix of ἠπεδανός (“feeble”) and οὐτιδανός (“worthless”): “a thing to shudder at, horrible” (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.325). The verb is the shudder of dread, as when Priam shudders at the news of the duel (3.259) and Agamemnon at Menelaus' wound (4.148). Homer has the adjective only once, of Helen, in Achilles' lament for Patroclus (19.325). Leaf compares the word Helen uses of herself, ὀκρυόεσσα, “chilling” (6.344), and reports Hentze's remark that this is the only place in the Iliad where a Greek other than Helen speaks of her in anger, as Eumaeus' wish that the race of Helen had perished is the only one in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 14.68). The translation keeps “hateful” for στυγερός ([[hateful]]). See [[helen]].
