## Meaning

ῥιγεδανός is formed from the root of ῥῖγος (“cold, shivering”) and ῥιγέω (“shudder”), with the suffix of ἠπεδανός (“feeble”) and οὐτιδανός (“worthless”): “a thing to shudder at, horrible” (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.325). The verb is the shudder of dread, as when Priam shudders at the news of the duel (3.259) and Agamemnon at Menelaus' wound (4.148). Homer has the adjective only once, of Helen, in Achilles' lament for Patroclus (19.325); the Odyssey has another of the same root once, καταριγηλός, in the plural “things to shudder at,” of the wars and weapons that the beggar of the Cretan tale loved (*Odyssey* 14.226; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]). Leaf compares the word Helen uses of herself, ὀκρυόεσσα, “chilling” (6.344), and reports Hentze's remark that this is the only place in the Iliad where a Greek other than Helen speaks of her in anger. In the Odyssey Eumaeus wishes that the race of Helen had perished utterly (*Odyssey* 14.68), and Odysseus tells the shade of Agamemnon that “for Helen's sake many of us perished” (11.438). The translation keeps “hateful” for στυγερός ([[hateful]]). See [[helen]].
