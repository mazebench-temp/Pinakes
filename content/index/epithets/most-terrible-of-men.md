## Meaning

ἐκπαγλότατος is the superlative of ἔκπαγλος, “terrible, violent, astonishing.” The adjective is usually explained from ἐκπλήσσω (“strike out of one's senses, astound”), so that its root sense is “astounding.” It can express awe or disapproval.

Book 1 also has the adverb: the Lapiths destroyed the Centaurs ἐκπάγλως, “terribly” ({{ref:Iliad 1.268}}). In Book 3 Aphrodite tells Helen that she loves her ἔκπαγλα, “beyond measure” (Il. 3.415).

## Use

{{quote:d30a30a7-d607-5f09-93a0-1907ce9c30c3}}

Agamemnon lists Ajax, Idomeneus, and brilliant Odysseus with plain names or ordinary honorifics, then turns to Achilles with a superlative. After Achilles' defiance the word sets him apart as the most dangerous man in the army, and Achilles hears it as an insult: his answer begins with a stream of abuse ({{ref:Iliad 1.149}}).

The phrase is not insulting in itself. Iris uses the same words, πάντων ἐκπαγλότατ’ ἀνδρῶν, when she rouses Achilles to save the body of Patroclus (Il. 18.170). What makes it hostile here is the speaker and the moment. Achilles himself uses it once, in his boast over Iphition, the first man he kills on his return (Il. 20.389).
