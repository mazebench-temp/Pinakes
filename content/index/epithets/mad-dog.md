## Meaning

λυσσητήρ is an agent noun formed from λύσσα, the battle frenzy of a warrior: “one who rages.” In Book 9 Odysseus tells Achilles that Hector, trusting in Zeus, rages terribly, that a mighty frenzy has entered him, and that he will come on with a deadly frenzy (9.237–239, 9.304–305); in Book 21 the frenzy holds Achilles' own heart (21.542–543). κύων, “dog,” is a common word of abuse in Homer ([[dogs]], [[dog-face]], [[shameless-dog]]). Teucer joins the two: the dog of insult, and the frenzy of the warrior. The word λυσσητήρ is found only here.

## Use

Teucer has brought down eight men with eight arrows, “but this mad dog I cannot hit” (8.296–299; [[long-barbed]]). Hector's frenzy fills the day: he drives at the ships “with the eyes of the Gorgon or of Ares, ruin of mortals” (8.348–349; [[ruin-of-mortals]]), and Hera says that he “rages past all bearing” (8.355). Teucer's next two arrows miss Hector and kill Gorgythion and Archeptolemus (8.300–315). Then the dog he could not hit leaps from his chariot with a stone and breaks his bowstring (8.320–329). Later in the book Hector turns the word back on the Achaeans: they are “dogs borne along by the fates” (8.527; [[borne-by-the-fates]]). See [[hector]] and [[teucer]].
