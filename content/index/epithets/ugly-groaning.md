## Meaning

ἀεικής (“unseemly, shameful, ugly”) is formed from ἀ- (“not”) and the root of ἔοικα (“be fitting, be like”): what is not fitting. With στόνος (“groaning, the sound of the dying”) it makes the half-verse τῶν δὲ στόνος ὄρνυτʼ ἀεικής, “and an ugly groaning rose from them,” which in all four of its uses follows ἐπιστροφάδην, “turning this way and that,” the killer striking on every side, and is followed by a verse of blood. Diomedes kills the Thracians and “the earth grew red with blood” (10.483–484); Achilles, sword in hand, kills the Trojans in the river, “and the water grew red with blood” (21.19–21); and in the Odyssey Odysseus and his men kill the suitors in the hall, “and all the floor ran with blood,” a scene that the ghost of Amphimedon tells again in the underworld (Od. 22.307–309, 24.183–185). The same adjective is in the formula ἀεικέα λοιγόν, “shameful ruin” ([[shameful-ruin]]).

## Use

{{quote:2029785c-f5e0-55ed-8ef3-21190499b5db}}

Odysseus tells Diomedes to kill the men or untie the horses, and Athena breathes might into him ([[menos]], [[gray-eyed]]). The killing is told in the formula of a massacre: “and he killed, turning this way and that; and an ugly groaning rose from the men struck by the sword, and the earth grew red with blood” (10.483–484). The men were asleep when he struck (10.471). The simile that follows is a lion among flocks without a herdsman (10.485–488; [[lion-among-the-flocks]]). See [[diomedes]] and [[thracians]].
