## Meaning

ἀκόρητος = ἀ- (“not”) + the root of κορέννυμι (“satisfy, sate”) and κόρος (“satiety, having one's fill”): “unsated.” With a genitive it names what a man never has enough of. Homer uses it six times, all in the Iliad, and five times the thing is battle: the fray (μόθου, 7.117), war (πολέμου, of the two Ajaxes, 12.335), the war cry (ἀϋτῆς, of the Trojans, 13.621), and battle (μάχης, of the Trojans, 13.639, and of Achilles, 20.2). The sixth is a Trojan taunt: the Argives are “insatiable of threats” (ἀπειλάων ἀκόρητοι, 14.479).

Menelaus puts the idea in a maxim, over the body of a Trojan he has killed: of all things there is satiety, of sleep and love and song and the dance, which a man would rather have his fill of than war, “but the Trojans are insatiable of battle” (13.636–639). Compare [[insatiable-of-war]] (ἆτος πολέμοιο), which Homer gives to Ares and to Achilles.

## Use

Agamemnon holds Menelaus back from answering Hector's challenge and tells him to sit down among his companions:

{{quote:912d59e0-d843-5fa3-a91e-2e9d544ef762}}

The two adjectives of 7.117 are fearless (ἀδειής) and insatiable of the fray (μόθος, “the fray”). Agamemnon does not say whom he means. It may be the champion the Achaeans will set up, who, however fearless and insatiable of battle he may be, will be glad to rest his knees if he comes out of the duel alive; or it may be Hector himself. The last verse and a half return in Nestor's speech of the champion chosen by lot, “if he escapes from destructive war and grim combat” (7.173–174), where they can only mean the Achaean; but Achilles uses the same thought of the Trojans in Book 19, when he says that any of them who escapes from destructive war under his spear will be glad to bend his knee and rest (19.71–73). Either way the words measure Hector, who is “far the mightier” (7.105) and whom even Achilles, Agamemnon claims, “shudders to meet” (7.113–114). See [[agamemnon]], [[menelaus]] and [[hector]].
