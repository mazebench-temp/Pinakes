## Meaning

τερμιόεις is formed from a word for the end or limit (compare τέρμα, “turning post, end”) with the suffix -όεις, “furnished with”: reaching to the end, to the feet. Homer has it twice: of the shield that Apollo strikes from Patroclus' shoulders (16.803), and in the Odyssey of the full-length tunic that the disguised Odysseus says he gave to Odysseus with a cloak and a bronze sword (*Odyssey* 19.241–242). The translation renders it “full-length,” keeping “tasseled” for θυσσανόεις ([[tasseled]]). The body shield that reaches to the feet is also ποδηνεκής, the shield on whose rim Periphetes trips (15.645–646; [[reaching-to-the-feet]]), and ἀμφιβρότη, “man-covering” ([[man-covering]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod bids the farmer put on in winter “a soft cloak and a full-length tunic,” χλαῖνάν τε μαλακὴν καὶ τερμιόεντα χιτῶνα (*Works and Days* 536–537).
