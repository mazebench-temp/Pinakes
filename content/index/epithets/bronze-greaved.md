## Meaning

χαλκοκνήμις = χαλκός (“bronze”) + κνημίς (“greave,” the guard for the shin, κνήμη): “with greaves of bronze.” It is a variant of the common formula ἐϋκνήμιδες Ἀχαιοί, “the well-greaved Achaeans” ([[well-greaved]]), and names the metal where the usual epithet praises the workmanship. It is found only here in Homer (7.41).

In the full arming scenes the greaves are put on first; Paris' are fastened with silver ankle-pieces (3.330–331; [[arming-scene]]), and Hephaestus makes Achilles' new pair of pliant tin (18.613). Bronze greaves are known from Mycenaean graves, and later from the hoplite armor of the Archaic period. See [[bronze]].

The god who favors the Trojans speaks of their enemies with a grander epithet than the narrator's. The well-greaved Achaeans become, for once, the bronze-greaved Achaeans, and their indignation is what Apollo counts on: shamed by Hector's challenge, they will in the end send out a champion (7.92–93, 7.161–169; [[aidos]]). A few lines later the narrator returns to the usual formula, when Agamemnon makes “the well-greaved Achaeans” sit down for the challenge (7.57). See [[apollo]], [[athena]] and [[single-combat]].
