## Meaning

ἄμοτον is an adverb, “insatiably, unceasingly”; its origin is uncertain. μεμαώς, feminine μεμαυῖα, is the perfect participle of μέμαα, “be eager, press on, rage,” from the root of μένος ([[menos]]). Together they mean “raging without end.” The phrase occurs five times in the Iliad. It ends the verse for Strife, Ἔρις ἄμοτον μεμαυῖα (4.440, 5.518), and it follows Hector's name in two verses of Book 13 that begin alike, Ἕκτορι Πριαμίδῃ ἄμοτον μεμαῶτ- (13.40, 13.80). Its last use is Hector's as he waits for Achilles before the gates of Troy, ἄμοτον μεμαὼς Ἀχιλῆϊ μάχεσθαι (22.36). In the Odyssey, with the finite verb, men long without end to hear a singer, ἄμοτον μεμάασιν ἀκουέμεν (Od. 17.520). The adverb alone describes Briseis' weeping for Patroclus (19.300) and Menelaus' anger at Antilochus (23.567).

## Use

The phrase first names Strife among the powers that drive the armies at their first meeting: “and Terror and Rout, and Strife, raging without end, / the sister and companion of man-slaying Ares” (4.440–441; [[strife]], [[rout]]). In Book 5 she is one of the three powers that have roused the toil of battle on the Trojan side, with Apollo and Ares: “and Ares, ruin of mortals, and Strife, raging without end” (5.517–518; [[ruin-of-mortals]]). In both places the phrase marks a power of war that is never at rest. Later books give it to men.
