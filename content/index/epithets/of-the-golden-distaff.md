## Meaning

χρυσηλάκατος = χρυσός (“gold”) + ἠλακάτη (“distaff”). In Homer ἠλακάτη is always the distaff that women spin from: Hector sends Andromache back to “the loom and the distaff” (6.491), and in the Odyssey Helen is given a golden distaff with a silver workbasket (*Odyssey* 4.131–135). The translation renders the epithet accordingly.

It is said only of Artemis, and both times in the Iliad together with [[clamorous]] (κελαδεινή): in the story of Eudorus' birth (16.183) and when Artemis takes her stand against Hera in the battle of the gods (20.70–71). The Odyssey has it once, in the simile of Helen coming out of her chamber (*Odyssey* 4.122). See [[artemis]].

## Outside Homer

The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* uses the two epithets together twice. Aphrodite never overcomes in love “Artemis of the golden distaff, the clamorous,” who delights in the bow, in hunting in the mountains, and in dances and piercing cries (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 16–20); and Aphrodite, telling Anchises a false story, says that Hermes snatched her up from the dance of Artemis of the golden distaff, the clamorous, where many nymphs and girls were playing (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 117–121), the scene of Polymele's story. The short *Hymn to Artemis* begins with the same words: Ἄρτεμιν ἀείδω χρυσηλάκατον, κελαδεινήν, “I sing of Artemis of the golden distaff, the clamorous” (*Hymn* 27.1).
