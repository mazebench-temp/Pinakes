## Meaning

πολυπῖδαξ = πολύς (“many”) + πῖδαξ (“spring”): “with many springs.” A πῖδαξ is a spring on a mountain; in a simile a lion and a boar fight on the peaks over a small one, because both want to drink (16.823–826). Ida is the mountain of the Troad's rivers. Book 12 names eight that run “from the mountains of Ida” to the sea, among them the Scamander and the Simoeis (12.19–22). Book 11 gives Ida a synonym, πιδήεσσα, “rich in springs” (11.183).

The epithet is Ida's alone. It has two places in the verse. In the accusative it stands in a whole verse that brings a god to the mountain, Ἴδην δʼ ἵκανεν πολυπίδακα μητέρα θηρῶν, “He came to Ida of the many springs, mother of wild beasts” (8.47, 14.283, 15.151; [[mother-of-wild-beasts]]). In the genitive it ends the verse, πολυπίδακος Ἴδης: Hera sees Zeus sitting “on the highest peak” of Ida (14.157), the feet of the mountain shake when Poseidon shakes the earth in the battle of the gods (20.57–59), the Dardanians lived on its lower slopes before Ilios was built (20.216–218), and the Achaeans cut the oaks for Patroclus' pyre on its spurs (23.117–119).

## Use

{{quote:6709371f-855c-5e81-a9a4-8106563d4945}}

Zeus' drive from Olympus ends on Gargarus, the peak of Ida, where he has a precinct and an altar ([[gargarus]], [[fragrant]], [[bronze-footed]]). The same verse brings Hera and Sleep to the mountain in Book 14, when she comes to beguile him, and Iris and Apollo in Book 15, when he has woken and sent for them (14.283, 15.151). From his seat on Ida Zeus watches the war in Book 8, thunders, and sends his lightning among the Achaeans (8.51–52, 8.75–77, 8.170–171; [[watching-the-war]], [[who-rules-from-ida]]). See [[ida]].
