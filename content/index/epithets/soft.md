## Meaning

μαλακός means “soft” to the touch, and so “gentle, mild.” Homer uses it of things that ease the body: sleep, “overcome by soft sleep” (μαλακῷ δεδμημένοι ὕπνῳ, 10.2, 24.678; Od. 15.6); a bed, in the half-verse εὐνῇ ἔνι μαλακῇ, “in a soft bed” (9.618, 10.75, 22.504); a tunic, in the dressing verse μαλακὸν δʼ ἔνδυνε χιτῶνα, “put on his soft tunic” (2.42); the grass and flowers that the earth sends up under Zeus and Hera (14.347–349), the fallow of the shield (18.541), the robes in which Hector's bones are wrapped (24.796). Of words it means “gentle” ([[gentle-words]]), and the dead Hector's body is “softer to handle” than when he burned the ships (22.373). Compare [[sweet-sleep]] and [[honey-sweet]], the other epithets of sleep, and [[ambrosial]].

## Use

{{quote:dcd22b52-4b02-5038-98b5-9b7c0d8635f4}}

The book opens with a pattern the poem uses three times. All the others sleep, “overcome by soft sleep”; one does not ([[hypnos]], [[sweet-sleep]]). In Book 2 it is Zeus, planning the slaughter he has promised (2.1–4); here it is Agamemnon, who lies awake pondering (10.1–4); in Book 24 it is Hermes, planning how to bring Priam out of the camp, and the verse of soft sleep is repeated (24.677–681). The Odyssey has the same contrast at Sparta: Peisistratus sleeps, “overcome by soft sleep,” but sweet sleep does not hold Telemachus, whose cares for his father keep him awake through the ambrosial night (Od. 15.4–8). Later in the night Agamemnon finds Nestor “in a soft bed,” but awake (10.74–80; [[nestor]]). See [[agamemnon]] and [[war-and-night]].
