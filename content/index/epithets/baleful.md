## Meaning

οὔλιος is a longer form of οὖλος, “destructive, baneful,” the word of the “baneful Dream” that Zeus sends to Agamemnon (2.6) and of “baneful Ares” (5.461; [[baneful]]). Both are usually connected with ὄλλυμι, “destroy,” the verb of ὀλοός, “deadly” ([[deadly]]). Homer uses οὔλιος only here, of a star that comes out from the clouds and hides again.

The star is not named. It is usually taken as Sirius, the Dog Star of late summer, which the poem calls the brightest of the stars and an evil sign, bringing much fever to wretched mortals, when Priam sees Achilles running over the plain toward the city (22.25–31). A star of late summer also blazes from Diomedes' helmet and shield when Athena kindles a fire on them (5.4–6). Compare [[like-a-star]] and the simile of this passage, [[the-baleful-star]].

## Use

{{quote:04a43c5b-c561-55f9-b041-352db5522f91}}

The Trojans are drawn up on the rise of the plain around Hector, Polydamas, Aeneas and the three sons of Antenor (11.56–60). Hector is everywhere, now among the foremost and now among the hindmost, giving orders, so that he is seen and lost again like a star among clouds; in his bronze he shines “like the lightning of aegis-bearing father Zeus” (11.66; [[aegis-bearing]], [[father-zeus]]). The comparison looks forward: on this day Zeus will give Hector power to kill until he reaches the ships (11.192–194; [[plan-of-zeus]]), and in Book 22 it is Achilles, coming at Hector, who shines like the star that brings fever.
