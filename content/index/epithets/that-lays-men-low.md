## Meaning

The word occurs only in the line-end τανηλεγέος θανάτοιο, “of death that lays men low.” Its formation is uncertain. It is usually explained as “laying men out at length,” from τανυ- (“stretched out,” as in τανύω, “stretch”) and λέγω in its old sense, “lay down,” and the translation follows that explanation ([[thanatos]]).

Every use joins the epithet to the power that brings death. In the Iliad it is the κῆρε, the two “fates,” that Zeus sets in his scales (8.70, 22.210; [[ker-doom]]). In the Odyssey it is the κήρ about which Odysseus asks his mother and Agamemnon among the dead, “what fate of death that lays men low overcame you?” (Od. 11.171, 11.398), and the “deadly fate” (μοῖρʼ ὀλοή) that will take Laertes down (Od. 2.100, 19.145, 24.135; [[moira]]). The same verse ends Athena's words to Telemachus that not even the gods can ward off death from a man they love, when that fate takes him (Od. 3.236–238).

## Use

{{quote:77eb0408-3f65-5f0c-b442-d4815e180737}}

At noon of the second day of battle Zeus weighs the fates of the two armies. The Achaeans' fates sink and settle on the earth, the Trojans' rise toward the sky, and Zeus thunders from Ida and sends his lightning among the Achaeans (8.72–77; [[the-scales-of-zeus]], [[bountiful-earth]], [[wide-sky]], [[pale-fear]]). The verse with the epithet returns in Book 22, when Zeus weighs the fates of Achilles and Hector and Hector's day of fate sinks toward Hades; Apollo leaves him (22.209–213). In Book 8 the scales weigh armies; in Book 22, two men. See [[zeus]] and [[plan-of-zeus]].
