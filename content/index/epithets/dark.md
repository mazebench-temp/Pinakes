## Meaning

ὀρφναῖος is formed from ὄρφνη (“darkness, the dark of night”), a noun that Homer does not use: “dark, murky.” It occurs only in the phrase νύκτα διʼ ὀρφναίην, “through the dark night,” which fills the beginning of the verse in the same way as νύκτα διʼ ἀμβροσίην, “through the ambrosial night” ([[ambrosial]]). Three of its four uses are in Book 10 (10.83, 10.276, 10.386). The fourth is in the Odyssey, where Odysseus' ships come in to the island off the land of the Cyclopes “through the dark night,” and nothing could be seen, since a god was guiding them (Od. 9.142–143). Night has other epithets: [[black-night]], [[swift-night]], [[murky]], and [[ambrosial]].

## Use

{{quote:13818e1c-cada-51fa-a1f0-cdca3443d47b}}

Nestor, raised on his elbow in his bed, challenges the man who comes through the camp “through the dark night, when other mortals are asleep” (10.82–85; [[when-other-mortals-are-asleep]], [[mules]], [[nestor]]). It is Agamemnon. Later in the night the phrase describes what the darkness hides: the heron that Athena sends on the right; the two men “did not see it with their eyes through the dark night, but they heard it cry” (10.274–276; [[heron]], [[athena]]). Then Odysseus puts Nestor's question to Dolon, in the same verse: why is he coming toward the ships alone, “through the dark night, when other mortals are asleep” (10.385–386; [[dolon]]). The challenge of a sentry to a friend becomes the interrogation of a spy. The verse belongs to the night journey: in Book 24 Hermes stops Priam with it, as Priam drives to Achilles “through the ambrosial night, when other mortals are asleep” (24.362–363).
