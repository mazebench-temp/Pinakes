## Meaning

πόσις (“husband”) + Ἥρης (“of Hera”): Zeus named by his marriage, as Paris is named “husband of Helen” ([[husband-of-helen]]). The phrase always ends the verse. Four times in the Iliad and three times in the Odyssey it is part of the longer formula ἐρίγδουπος πόσις Ἥρης, “the loud-thundering husband of Hera” ([[loud-thundering]]), and nearly every time it closes a sentence in which Zeus is called on to witness, grant or fulfill something: Agamemnon's oath at the truce for the dead, “let Zeus be witness” (7.411), Hector's oath to Dolon (10.329), Hector's claim that the god has roused him (13.153–154), Achilles' “if he grants you to win glory” to Patroclus (16.87–88), and in the Odyssey the farewell wishes of Odysseus to Nausicaa and of Menelaus and Telemachus at Sparta (Od. 8.465, 15.112, 15.180). Once the phrase stands without the thunder, in a simile, and Hera has an epithet of her own: πόσις Ἥρης ἠϋκόμοιο, “the husband of Hera of the lovely hair” (10.5; [[lovely-haired]]). The verse-end is shaped like the one that names Paris, Ἑλένης πόσις ἠϋκόμοιο. See [[zeus]] and [[hera]].

## Use

{{quote:eb8ca461-10ef-5e52-b297-7e7e43eaa2a4}}

Dolon asks Hector to hold up his scepter and swear to give him the horses and chariot of Achilles, and Hector swears by Zeus, the loud-thundering husband of Hera ([[oath]], [[scepter]]). The formula of the god who guarantees an oath is followed by the narrator's comment: “So he spoke, and swore an oath that would prove false” (10.332). The oath fails because Dolon never comes back to claim the horses (10.336–337); the poem does not say that Hector meant to deceive ([[dolon]], [[hector]], [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). The same formula ended Agamemnon's oath at the truce, which was kept (7.411). The first use in the book is the simile of Agamemnon's groans, as thick as the lightning flashed by the husband of Hera when he makes ready rain or hail or snow, or war (10.5–9; [[lightning-before-the-storm]], [[endless]]).
