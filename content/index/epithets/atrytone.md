## Meaning

The word is usually analyzed as ἀ- (“not”) + τρύω (“wear out, exhaust”): “the unwearied one,” the goddess who never tires in war. The analysis is old and plausible but not certain. With its ending -ώνη the word looks more like a name than an adjective, and Homer never uses it outside this address.

It occurs seven times in Homer, always at the end of the verse and always in address to Athena, usually in the formula αἰγιόχοιο Διὸς τέκος, Ἀτρυτώνη (“child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone”; [[child-of-aegis-bearing-zeus]]). Hera uses the line three times (Il. 2.157, 5.714, 21.420), Diomedes twice in prayer (Il. 5.115, 10.284), Penelope and Odysseus once each (Od. 4.762, 6.324).

## Use

{{quote:04171b59-a89d-5cfe-b37e-146e1fd4a0d0}}

When the army runs for the ships, Hera speaks to Athena and the story turns. The formal title of her address to the unwearied goddess stands against the picture of an army exhausted by nine years of war and fleeing home. The same words open Hera's appeals in Book 5, when the Achaeans are hard pressed by Ares and Hector (Il. 5.714), and in Book 21, when she sends Athena after Aphrodite (Il. 21.420).
