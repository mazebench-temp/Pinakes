## Meaning

πολύτροπος = πολύς (“many”) + τρόπος (“a turn, a way,” from τρέπω, “turn”): “of many turns.” The turns may be those of the mind, the shifts and devices of a resourceful man, or those of the road, a man driven this way and that. Merry reports both readings. He glosses the word “of many devices,” Latin *versutus*, and fixes its sense by its equivalents among the epithets of Odysseus, πολύμητις ([[of-many-wiles]]), πολύφρων ([[of-much-wisdom]]), πολυμήχανος ([[of-many-devices]]) and ποικιλόμητις; Nitzsch took it as πολύπλαγκτος, “much-wandering,” with the clause that follows it in the proem, ὃς μάλα πολλὰ πλάγχθη, “who was driven far astray,” as its explanation (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.1). The translation's “of many turns” keeps both senses (see the Odyssey translation's note on 1.1).

## Use

The word occurs twice, both times of Odysseus. The Odyssey opens with it: the Muse is asked to tell of “the man of many turns” (*Odyssey* 1.1), and the hero's name is held back until the twenty-first verse. In *Odyssey* 10 Circe, whose drug has failed to bewitch him, begins with the question put to strangers, “Who are you among men, and from where?”, and answers it herself: “Surely you are Odysseus of many turns, whom the slayer of Argus always told me would come” (*Odyssey* 10.325–331; [[who-are-you-among-men]]). Merry compares the way Odysseus presents himself to the Phaeacians, “I am Odysseus, son of Laertes, who am known to men for all my wiles” (*Odyssey* 9.19–20; Merry on 1.1).

## Outside Homer

Merry quotes the first verse as Livius Andronicus rendered it in his Latin Odyssey, “Virum mihi, Camena, insece versutum,” where *versutus*, “turning, versatile,” answers πολύτροπον (on 1.1).
