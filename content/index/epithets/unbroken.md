## Meaning

ἀδμής = ἀ- (“not”) + the root of δάμνημι, δαμάζω (“tame, break, subdue”): “untamed.” The same verb is used of breaking animals to the yoke and of a girl given to a husband, and ἀδμής has both uses. In the Iliad it describes animals: the heifer “unbroken, which no man has yet led under the yoke” that Diomedes vows to Athena (10.293); the mare “six years old, unbroken, carrying a mule foal” that is the second prize in the chariot race (23.265–266); and the mule, “six years old, unbroken, the hardest to break,” that is the prize for boxing (23.653–655). In the Odyssey Nestor vows the same heifer in the same verses (Od. 3.382–384), Noemon has unbroken mules at pasture in Elis (Od. 4.635–637), and Nausicaa among her maids is παρθένος ἀδμής, “an unwed girl” (Od. 6.109, 6.228). Compare [[untamable]] (ἀδάμαστος), the epithet of Hades, and the cattle “never touched by the goad” of Book 6 ([[never-touched-by-the-goad]]); see [[privative-adjectives]].

## Use

{{quote:22403eed-4545-5df6-a313-cfcc580aa9d1}}

Diomedes ends his prayer with a vow: a heifer, a yearling, broad of brow, unbroken, with gold poured around her horns ([[yearling]], [[broad-browed]], [[sacrifice]]). The relative clause explains the epithet: no man has yet led her under the yoke. The victim is worth more for never having worked, as the gold on her horns makes her finer. The three verses are spoken again in the Odyssey by Nestor at Pylos, where the sacrifice is carried out and the goldsmith gilds the horns (Od. 3.382–384, 3.432–438). In the Iliad the vow is heard (10.295) and its fulfillment is not told. See [[diomedes]] and [[athena]].
