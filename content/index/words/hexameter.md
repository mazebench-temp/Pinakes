## The pattern

Each line of the Iliad has six feet. The first five are dactyls (— ∪ ∪) or spondees (— —); the fifth is almost always a dactyl, and the sixth has two syllables, the last of which may be long or short. Syllables count as long by nature (a long vowel or a diphthong) or by position (a short vowel followed by two consonants).

The first line of the poem scans like this:

| μῆνιν ἄ | ειδε θε | ὰ Πη | ληϊά | δεω Ἀχι | λῆος |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| — ∪ ∪ | — ∪ ∪ | — — | — ∪ ∪ | — ∪ ∪ | — — |

{{quote:4630ecf7-c829-5e61-b61f-793096803b5a}}

Two features of epic language show here. In Πηληϊάδεω the ending -εω is pronounced as one syllable (synizesis), and Ἀχιλῆος has one λ, where the name is often spelled Ἀχιλλεύς. The poets used such alternate forms to fit the verse. The sixth foot, λῆος, is a spondee.

## Meter and formula

Homer's standing epithets and repeated half-lines are shaped by the meter. πόδας ὠκὺς Ἀχιλλεύς ("swift-footed Achilles") and ποδάρκης δῖος Ἀχιλλεύς ("brilliant swift-footed Achilles") fill the end of the line from different starting points. A singer can choose whichever fits what has come before. Milman Parry's studies of these systems (1928 onward) showed how an oral poet could compose fluently in hexameter.
