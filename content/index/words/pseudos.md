## Meaning

ψεῦδος is what is false: a lie told on purpose, a mistaken report, or a promise or sign that does not come true. The verb ψεύδομαι covers both lying and being wrong. Nestor, hearing horses in the night, asks himself, “Shall I be wrong, or speak the truth?” (ψεύσομαι, ἦ ἔτυμον ἐρέω; 10.534). Agamemnon admits to him, “Old man, not falsely have you recounted my follies” (οὔ τι ψεῦδος, 9.115). The opposite is ἔτυμος, “real, true”.

The Iliad condemns the lie that breaks faith. Father Zeus, says Agamemnon, will be no helper to liars, meaning the Trojans who broke the truce (4.235). Priam, grieving for Hector, calls the sons he has left “liars and dancers” (ψεῦσταί τʼ ὀρχησταί τε, 24.261). But its gods deceive when it suits them (see [[apate]]), and the Dream that Zeus sends Agamemnon in Book 2 is a lie (see [[agamemnons-dream]]).

The Odyssey is full of lies, most of them its hero's. Back on Ithaca he tells false stories of himself to almost everyone he meets, and to Penelope “he made the many lies he told like the truth” (ἴσκε ψεύδεα πολλὰ λέγων ἐτύμοισιν ὁμοῖα, Odyssey 19.203).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod's Muses tell him that they “know how to tell many lies like the truth, and know, when we wish, how to utter true things” (*Theogony* 27–28). “Many lies like the truth” (ψεύδεα πολλὰ … ἐτύμοισιν ὁμοῖα) is what the Odyssey says of Odysseus. The proverb “bards tell many lies” (πολλὰ ψεύδονται ἀοιδοί) is quoted by Aristotle (*Metaphysics* A 2, 983a3–4) and ascribed to Solon (fr. 29 West).

Plato's Socrates will not allow that a god lies, and rejects the dream Zeus sent to Agamemnon (*Republic* 2, 382e–383a; see [[plato-lying-dream]]). Aristotle, on the other hand, praises Homer for having taught the other poets “to tell lies as one should” (ψευδῆ λέγειν ὡς δεῖ), that is, by false inference (*Poetics* 24, 1460a18–19).
