## Meaning

γαστήρ is the belly, the stomach and the womb. In the Iliad it is most often the place where the spear goes in. A man is struck “full in the belly” (4.531), “in the middle of the belly” (13.506, 17.313), where the bronze corselet he wore did not protect him (13.371–372, 13.397–398), or “in the lower belly” (5.539, 5.616, 16.465, 17.519), and Achilles strikes Asteropaeus in the belly beside the navel, so that all his bowels pour out on the ground (21.180–181; see [[death-in-battle]]). It is the womb: Agamemnon would not spare even the boy whom his mother carries in her womb (6.57–60), and ὁμογάστριος, “of the same womb”, is a brother born of the same mother: Lycaon pleads that he is not of the same womb as Hector (21.95), and Apollo counts among the losses a man may bear “a brother of the same womb, or even a son” (24.46–47; see [[kasignetos]]). The wolves of the Myrmidons' simile, who have torn a great stag in the mountains and go to drink, have fearless hearts and gorged bellies (16.156–163; see [[myrmidons]]), γάστρη is the belly of a tripod over the fire (18.348; *Odyssey* 8.437), and in the Cyclops' cave Odysseus clings beneath the shaggy belly of the best of the rams (*Odyssey* 9.432–434).

**The belly's commands.** The belly is also hunger, and Homer's speakers make it a power that gives orders. Odysseus tells Achilles that the Achaeans cannot mourn a corpse with the belly, that is, by fasting, while men fall every day; let them bury the dead and remember food and drink, so as to fight (19.225–233; see [[fasting]], [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]] and [[food-and-wine-are-might-and-courage]]). In the Odyssey the belly bids. The lion of the simile goes against the fold because “his belly bids him” (κέλεται δέ ἑ γαστήρ, *Odyssey* 6.133; see [[the-hungry-lion]]), and Menelaus' companions on Pharos fish with curved hooks, “and hunger wore at their bellies” (*Odyssey* 4.368–369; again 12.330–332). At Alcinous' table Odysseus says that there is nothing more shameless than the hateful belly, which bids a man remember it however much he grieves (*Odyssey* 7.215–221). κύντερος, “more doglike”, is the comparative of κύων, “dog”, the emblem of shamelessness (see [[dogs]]): Zeus tells Hera that nothing is more doglike than she (8.483), and the shade of Agamemnon says it of a woman who plots her husband's death (*Odyssey* 11.427).

**The beggar's belly.** In the second half of the Odyssey the belly is the beggar's. Disguised as a beggar, Odysseus tells Eumaeus that nothing is worse for mortals than wandering, but that men have evil sorrows for the sake of the accursed belly (*Odyssey* 15.341–345); Melanthius says that the beggar would rather beg through the land to feed his insatiable belly than work (*Odyssey* 17.226–228), and Eurymachus taunts him in the same words (*Odyssey* 18.362–364); even Eumaeus, bringing Penelope's message, tells him that he may feed his belly by begging through the land (*Odyssey* 17.558–559). Odysseus answers that no one can hide a ravening belly, “accursed, which gives many evils to men”, for whose sake ships are fitted out to bring evils to enemies (*Odyssey* 17.286–289), that Antinous struck him “for the sake of the wretched belly” (*Odyssey* 17.473–474), and that “the evil-working belly” drives him to fight Irus (*Odyssey* 18.53–54), a beggar known for his ravening belly (*Odyssey* 18.1–3); the renderings are plain renderings. The prize of that fight is a goat's paunch stuffed with fat and blood (*Odyssey* 18.44–49, 18.118–119), and the night before the killing of the suitors Odysseus tosses on his bed like such a paunch turned this way and that over a blazing fire (*Odyssey* 20.24–28).
