## Meaning

γόνυ, plural γούνατα (genitive γούνων), is the knee. Clasping the knees of the person one begs is the central gesture of supplication, often with a hand under the chin. The verb γουνάζομαι, “clasp the knees”, means simply “supplicate”. John Gould's study “Hiketeia” (1973) showed how the gesture binds: the one who is held must answer.

The knees are also the seat of a man's strength; a warrior killed in battle has his knees “loosened”. And the outcome of what men cannot control “lies on the knees of the gods” (17.514).

## In Book 1

{{quote:6241d154-ec20-5d10-80e4-710ec750ab83}}

Achilles tells his mother exactly what to do: sit beside Zeus, remind him of her service, and take him by the knees. Thetis promises that she will (γουνάσομαι, 1.427).

{{quote:9ddc5d69-31b1-59bc-94fe-c81394392a38}}

She finds Zeus sitting apart, sits before him, takes his knees with her left hand and his chin with her right.

{{quote:11854ed9-4cf0-5f6b-9a62-8d5b50785dff}}

When Zeus sits silent, she does not let go. She clings to his knees, “grown fast to them”, and asks again. Hera learns of the scene without seeing it: “early this morning she sat beside you and clasped your knees” (1.557).
