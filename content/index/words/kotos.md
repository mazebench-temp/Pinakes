## Meaning

κότος (“grudge, resentment”) is anger that waits. Where [[cholos|χόλος]] flares up and may be swallowed the same day, κότος is kept in the chest “until he has fulfilled it” (ὄφρα τελέσσῃ). The verb κοτέω means “bear a grudge, resent”. Gods have κότος as well as men. The etymology is unknown.

## In Book 1

{{quote:50697479-23fc-5582-baed-856434e97255}}

The seer explains why he needs protection before he names the cause of the plague (see [[a-kings-grudge]]). A king who is angry with a lesser man may digest his χόλος for the day, but he keeps κότος afterwards. Calchas is right about the man he fears. Agamemnon greets him as a “prophet of evils” (1.106).

{{quote:72dacd98-c076-57ea-87e1-af753d30b092}}

Agamemnon dismisses Achilles' resentment in two words, οὐδʼ ὄθομαι κοτέοντος, “nor do I mind your resentment”. It is the kind of anger Calchas described, now aimed at the king himself, and the poem will show that he should have minded it.

## Related words

- [[cholos|χόλος]] (“anger”) and [[menis|μῆνις]] (“wrath”).
- [[stethos|στῆθος]] (“chest”): where the grudge is kept (1.83).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle quotes 1.82 in his chapter on anger. People expect regard from those beneath them in birth, power or excellence, he says, which is why the poet speaks of the great anger of Zeus-nurtured kings (Iliad 2.196) and of the king who “keeps a grudge thereafter” (*Rhetoric* 2.2, 1379a; see [[aristotle-anger]]).

Hesiod gives the verb a kinder sense in his account of good strife, the rivalry that makes men work: “potter resents (κοτέει) potter, and builder builder” (*Works and Days* 25).
