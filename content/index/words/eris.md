## Meaning

ἔρις is strife: quarrel, rivalry, the will to contend with someone. The verbs ἐρίζω and ἐριδαίνω mean “strive, quarrel, vie with”. The same word covers a quarrel in words and the contest of battle, and rivalry in anything from beauty to handiwork.

In the Iliad Eris is also a goddess, the sister and companion of Ares. She is small when she first rises, but soon her head strikes the sky while she walks on the earth (4.440–443). Zeus sends her to the ships to start a day of battle (11.3).

Achilles, grieving for Patroclus, wishes that strife might perish from among gods and men, and anger with it (18.107–108).

## In Book 1

{{quote:4657be09-7bfb-5274-a617-9c6c2029e172}}

The proem dates the story from a single event: the day when the son of Atreus and Achilles “first stood apart in strife” (διαστήτην ἐρίσαντε). The next line asks which god brought them together to fight in ἔρις.

{{quote:e6b47a75-7802-5b64-bdf2-4f6fe8b1a16f}}

Agamemnon accuses Achilles of loving strife. In Book 5 Zeus says the same line to Ares, after telling him that he is the most hateful to him of all the gods (5.890–891). Agamemnon's “most hateful to me of the Zeus-nurtured kings” (1.176) sets him in the place of Zeus and Achilles in the place of the war god (see [[strife-is-always-dear]]).

Athena tells Achilles to cease from strife (λῆγʼ ἔριδος, 1.210). Nestor tells him not to strive with a king (1.277). The narrator reports that Agamemnon did not cease from the strife he began (1.319).

{{quote:157f33ab-cf31-5365-9c9b-72ee8c35b032}}

Hephaestus uses the verb of his parents: if they quarrel like this for the sake of mortals, there will be no pleasure in the feast. The strife of the camp has become a strife of the gods.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod distinguishes two Strifes. One promotes war and quarreling; the other, good for men, stirs even the idle to work in rivalry with a neighbor (*Works and Days* 11–26). In the *Theogony* Eris is a daughter of Night and the mother of Toil, Battles, Quarrels, Lawlessness and Ate (225–232).

In Proclus' summary of the *Cypria*, Eris comes to the wedding of Peleus and Thetis, Achilles' parents, and starts the quarrel among Hera, Athena and Aphrodite that leads to the Trojan War.

Heraclitus rebuked Homer for wishing that strife might perish from among gods and men (Iliad 18.107), since without opposites there would be no harmony (Aristotle, *Eudemian Ethics* 7.1, 1235a25–29).
