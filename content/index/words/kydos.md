## Meaning

κῦδος is glory of a particular kind: a visible superiority, the shine of success that a god grants for a time. Zeus gives it to kings and to warriors, and he can take it away. It differs from κλέος, fame, which lives in what people say about a man. Émile Benveniste argued that κῦδος is a talisman of supremacy, granted by a god and effective while it lasts.

The superlative κύδιστος, “most glorious”, is a formal way of addressing a king. The compound κυδιάνειρα, “bringing glory to men” (κῦδος and ἀνήρ), is usually an epithet of battle.

## In Book 1

{{quote:8fcb2ba6-e97c-5f56-a2c0-932c3112f138}}

Nestor's case for Agamemnon rests on a gift from Zeus. The scepter-bearing king has been given κῦδος, and so a greater share of honor.

{{quote:57820f3f-204d-5568-85ec-bea9f4b2bb23}}

Achilles uses the honorific and spoils it with the next word. Agamemnon is κύδιστε, “most glorious”, and φιλοκτεανώτατε, “most covetous of all men”.

Briareus, summoned by Thetis, sits beside Zeus “exulting in his glory” (κύδεϊ γαίων, 1.405), and the other gods shrink back from him.

{{quote:71e9711c-dc57-5f22-adf4-496eadd25fb2}}

The assembly is called κυδιάνειρα, “where men win glory”, at the moment Achilles stops going to it. His withdrawal cuts him off from the place where glory is won by speech as well as from the battle where it is won by arms.

## Related words

- [[time|τιμή]] (“honor”) and [[geras|γέρας]] (“prize”).
- [[basileus|βασιλεύς]] (“king”): the scepter-bearer to whom Zeus gives κῦδος.
- [[aristos|ἄριστος]] (“best”).

## Outside Homer

English *kudos* comes from this word, by way of nineteenth-century university slang.
