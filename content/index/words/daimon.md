## Meaning

δαίμων is a god or divine power. The word is used most often when the power is felt rather than identified: a δαίμων gives courage, sends a storm, leads a man astray. In the plural it can simply mean “the gods”. It is often derived from δαίομαι, “divide, allot”, as if the δαίμων were the one who deals out each man's fortune, but the etymology is uncertain.

The vocative δαιμόνιε (feminine δαιμονίη) is addressed to someone whose behavior seems incomprehensible, as if a δαίμων were driving them. It can be reproachful or tender. The translation renders it “strange one”.

## In Book 1

{{quote:03bcde88-1b7b-5c74-a060-8268b522edf6}}

After restraining Achilles, Athena goes back to Olympus “among the other δαίμονες”. Here the word means no more than the gods.

{{quote:42fcc61a-0742-5086-a860-b1dc5ce326a9}}

Zeus answers Hera's suspicions with δαιμονίη: she is always suspecting him, and he can never escape her notice. The address is the prelude to a threat.

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod the men of the golden race became δαίμονες after death, guardians of mortals (*Works and Days* 121–126). In Plato's *Symposium* Eros is a great δαίμων, between gods and mortals (202d–e). Socrates spoke of a δαιμόνιον, a divine sign that held him back (Plato, *Apology* 31c–d).
