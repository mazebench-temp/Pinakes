## Meaning

χόλος (“anger”) is the commonest word for anger in Homer, and its first sense is physical. Like χολή, it means “bile, gall”, and it is related to Latin *fel* and English *gall*. The Myrmidons, Achilles reports, used to say that his mother must have reared him on χόλος (16.203).

The metaphor stays close to the body. Anger rises in the chest and swells there. In Book 18 Achilles wishes that strife and χόλος might perish, “which, far sweeter than dripping honey, swells in men's breasts like smoke” (18.107–110). It can be “digested” (καταπέσσω, 1.81), stopped (παύω, 1.192), let go (μεθίημι, 1.283). It seizes a man (1.387), and he can cease from it (1.224).

The verb χολόω means “anger someone”, and in the passive “become angry”. χώομαι (“be angry, resentful”) is a different verb, of uncertain origin. In Book 1 it names the same passion, and the translation renders it “angry” or “enraged”.

χόλος differs from its neighbors. [[kotos|κότος]] (“grudge”) is anger kept for later; [[menis|μῆνις]] (“wrath”) is the lasting anger of gods and of Achilles. [[Calchas]] draws the first distinction himself.

{{quote:d3652440-90d6-564f-bbe4-5ee6a0e8c4a4}}

A king, he says, may swallow his χόλος the same day, but he keeps κότος in his chest until he has satisfied it. The seer is asking for protection before he speaks, and his short theory of royal anger proves exact.

## In Book 1

Almost every major figure in the book is angry at some point, and the same words serve for all of them.

{{quote:41b52045-2487-5e89-a3c6-c5f79e71b8d4}}

Apollo, in anger at the king (χολωθείς), sends the plague. He comes down from Olympus “angry at heart” (χωόμενος κῆρ, 1.44), and his arrows rattle on the shoulders of the angry god (1.46). Achilles asks the assembly why the god is so enraged (ἐχώσατο, 1.64).

Agamemnon's anger is foreseen before it arrives. Calchas expects to anger a powerful man (1.78). When Agamemnon threatens to take the prize of Achilles, Ajax or Odysseus, he adds that the man he robs will be angry (κεχολώσεται, 1.139). In Achilles' retelling, anger simply seized the son of Atreus (χόλος λάβεν, 1.387).

{{quote:8031e0a0-b5e5-5727-b9bb-46202ec4492d}}

Achilles' anger is the test of the book. He weighs killing Agamemnon against checking his anger and restraining his spirit. [[athena|Athena]] arrives while he is still drawing the sword.

{{quote:7743a6f0-eea7-5ed1-81cc-f3824bfe5913}}

He obeys the goddess “however angry in his heart”. But obedience does not end the anger. The narrator adds at once that he did not yet cease from it (οὔ πω λῆγε χόλοιο, 1.224); he turns it into words instead. [[Nestor]] begs Agamemnon to let go his anger against Achilles (1.283), with no effect.

χώομαι closes two scenes. Chryses, refused, goes home in anger (1.380). Thetis leaves Achilles by the sea “angry at heart” over the woman taken from him (1.429). And Achilles predicts that Agamemnon's anger will one day turn on himself, when he regrets that he did not honor the best of the Achaeans (1.244).

## Related words

- [[menis|μῆνις]] (“wrath”): the lasting anger of gods and of Achilles.
- [[kotos|κότος]] (“grudge”): anger stored for later.
- [[thymos|θυμός]] (“spirit”): the seat where anger rises (1.217, 1.429).
- [[menos|μένος]] (“fury”): the force that fills Agamemnon's mind (1.103) and that Athena comes to stop in Achilles (1.207).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle's account of anger in the *Rhetoric* (2.2, 1378a30–b10) defines it as a painful desire for revenge for an apparent slight, and supports the pleasure that goes with it by quoting Iliad 18.109–110, anger “far sweeter than dripping honey”. The same chapter quotes Book 1 twice: Achilles' complaint that Agamemnon “has dishonored me” (1.356), and Calchas' line on the grudge that kings keep (1.82). See [[aristotle-anger]].
