## Meaning

βασιλεύς is the usual Homeric word for a king or chief. It is not a single office. Every leader of a contingent at Troy is a βασιλεύς, and the word even has degrees. Nestor tells Agamemnon that he is βασιλεύτατος, “most kingly” (9.69), and Agamemnon insists that he is βασιλεύτερος, “more kingly”, than Achilles (9.160).

A βασιλεύς holds the scepter and the [[themis|θέμιστες]] from Zeus. He leads in war, presides in assembly and receives the largest share of honor. Kings are διοτρεφέες, “nurtured by Zeus” (1.176). In Odysseus' words, the anger of a Zeus-nurtured king is great, his honor comes from Zeus, and Zeus loves him (2.196–197).

The word has no accepted etymology. In the Mycenaean tablets *qa-si-re-u* is a local official, well below the *wanax*. In Homer the relation has shifted: βασιλεύς is the general term for a king, and [[anax|ἄναξ]] is the lord.

## In Book 1

The book opens by calling Agamemnon simply “the king”: Apollo was angry βασιλῆϊ (1.9). Calchas then states a principle.

{{quote:92002bbc-47c8-5258-9888-a029e6b7e5e1}}

A king is stronger than a lesser man who angers him, and he does not forget. The seer asks for protection before he speaks.

Achilles is also a βασιλεύς. Agamemnon calls him the most hateful to him of all the Zeus-nurtured kings (1.176), and the heralds stand before Achilles “in fear and awe of the king” (1.331).

{{quote:20d90042-760f-596c-bf73-a35e9a0c3da9}}

Achilles' insult δημοβόρος βασιλεύς, “people-devouring king”, accuses Agamemnon of consuming what belongs to the community (δῆμος). He can do it only because he rules over nobodies; otherwise this insult would be his last.

{{quote:51d08bcf-1c73-5967-9058-23cf79b8b7d3}}

Nestor answers with the scepter-bearing king to whom Zeus has given glory, and with a principle of scale: the stronger man must yield to the one who rules over more.

Achilles' last words about the king in Book 1 are bitter. He calls the heralds to witness before gods, men and “that harsh king” (1.340), and he asks Thetis to have Zeus pen the Achaeans by their ships, “so that they may all have joy of their king” (1.410).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod complains of “gift-devouring kings” (δωροφάγοι) who give crooked judgments (*Works and Days* 39, 221, 264), a charge close to Achilles' δημοβόρος.

Aristotle takes the Homeric king as his example of heroic kingship: Agamemnon put up with abuse in the assemblies, but on campaign he had the power of life and death (*Politics* 3.14, 1285a3–14).
