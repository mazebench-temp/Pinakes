## Meaning

γενναῖος is formed from γέννα, “birth, descent”, from the same root as γένος, “stock, family”, and γίγνομαι, “be born”. It describes what belongs to one's birth, and so what befits it. Homer has it only once. Sthenelus sees Aeneas and Pandarus driving at them and begs Diomedes to draw back in the chariot, and Diomedes answers:

{{quote:c04a7601-6a9f-59f3-a261-40eeb7c51bb9}}

οὐ γάρ μοι γενναῖον, “it is not in my blood”, is a claim about what he was born to. He is the son of Tydeus, and Athena has just put into his breast “your father's might, undaunted, such as the horseman Tydeus, wielder of the shield, used to have” (5.125–126). He will not shrink back, and he will not take the chariot: he means to kill both men and take Aeneas' horses, which are “of that breed” (τῆς γενεῆς) that Zeus gave to Tros (5.257–273). Birth is the theme of the whole exchange, the birth of heroes and the breeding of horses. Late in the book Athena reproaches him with the same father, “Tydeus has begotten a son little like himself” (5.800), and he answers that it is not fear that holds him back but her own command (5.815–824). See [[fathers-and-sons]], [[small-but-a-fighter]] and [[diomedes]].

## Related words

- γένος (“stock”), γενεή (“breed, generation”) and γενέθλη (“offspring”): Zeus tells Ares, “you are of my stock” (ἐκ γὰρ ἐμεῦ γένος ἐσσί, 5.896).
- [[menos|μένος]] (“might”) and [[tharsos|θάρσος]] (“daring”), the gifts of Athena that make him his father's son.
- [[aidos|αἰδώς]] (“shame”), the other thing that keeps a man from retreating.

## Outside Homer

In classical Greek γενναῖος is a common word for “noble”, whether of birth or of character. It is the word of Plato's “noble lie” (γενναῖόν τι ἓν ψευδομένους, *Republic* 3.414b–c). Aristotle distinguishes it from εὐγενής, “well-born”: εὐγενής refers to the excellence of one's family, γενναῖος to not falling away from one's own nature, which, he adds, the well-born mostly do (*Rhetoric* 2.15, 1390b21–23; compare *History of Animals* 1.1, 488b18–20). Diomedes uses it in something like Aristotle's sense. He will not fall short of what he was born to be.
