## Meaning

φιλότης is the noun of [[philos|φίλος]] (“dear, one's own”): the state of being φίλοι to one another. It names a bond more than a feeling, and Homer uses it for three bonds that later Greek kept apart.

**A treaty.** Enemies who make peace swear φιλότης together with oaths: φιλότητα καὶ ὅρκια πιστὰ ταμεῖν, which the translation renders “making friendship and swearing a trusty truce” (3.73, 3.94, 3.256). The verb τάμνω (“cut”) is the one used of cutting the victims' throats at the oath sacrifice (see [[oath]]). After their duel in Book 7, Hector and Ajax part “in friendship, reconciled” (ἐν φιλότητι, 7.302; see [[parted-in-friendship]]).

**Hospitality.** A host “offers φιλότης” to his guest (3.354). Émile Benveniste argued that the whole family of φίλος belonged first to the institution of hospitality (see [[xenia]]).

**Love.** Most often φιλότης is the love of a man and a woman. To “mingle in φιλότης” (μίσγεσθαι ἐν φιλότητι), or “in φιλότης and bed” (φιλότητι καὶ εὐνῇ), is to lie together (2.232, 3.445). On Olympus, Hera asks Aphrodite for “φιλότης and ἵμερος” to seduce Zeus (14.198), and φιλότης is the first power worked into Aphrodite's embroidered band, with longing and seductive talk (14.216–217).

The three senses are one idea: a bond that makes strangers or enemies one's own. The word's opposite is ἔχθος (“hatred”), and Book 3 sets the two against each other at its end (3.415–416, 3.453–454).

## Related words

- [[philos|φίλος]] (“dear”) and φιλέω (“love”, and “entertain as a guest”, 3.207).
- [[xenia]]: guest-friendship.
- [[eros|ἔρος]] (“desire”) and [[himeros|ἵμερος]] (“longing”).
- μίσγομαι (“mingle”), its usual verb in the sense of love. Book 3 uses the same verb for Paris mingling with foreigners (3.48), for lying in the dust (3.55), for the envoys mixing with the Trojans (3.209) and for wine mixed in the bowl (3.270).
- ἔχθος (“hatred”) and ἀπεχθάνομαι (“become hateful”).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes Philotes a child of Night, born with Deceit (Ἀπάτη), destructive Old Age and Strife (*Theogony* 223–225). For Empedocles, Φιλότης (Love) and Νεῖκος (Strife) are the two forces that by turns unite and separate the elements (fragment B17 DK; see [[neikos]]).
