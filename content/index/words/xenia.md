## Meaning

ξεῖνος (Attic ξένος) is at once the stranger, the guest and the host: anyone joined to another by the bond of hospitality. Homer's words for it are ξεῖνος, ξεινίζω (“receive as a guest, entertain”), ξεινοδόκος (“host”, literally “one who receives guests”) and ξείνια (“guest-gifts”). Later Greek called the institution ξενία.

A stranger who comes to a house is taken in, fed and given a bed before he is asked who he is, and he leaves with gifts. The gifts and the meal make him and his host ξεῖνοι for good, and the bond passes to their sons. In Book 6 Glaucus and Diomedes meet on the battlefield and learn that Diomedes' grandfather Oeneus once entertained Glaucus' grandfather Bellerophon for twenty days and exchanged gifts with him. They agree not to fight each other and exchange armor instead (6.212–236). Guest-friendship holds across the lines of war.

The guest is under the protection of Zeus, who is called ξείνιος, “of guests”. Over the body of a Trojan he has killed, Menelaus reproaches the Trojans: they carried off his wedded wife and many possessions after she had welcomed them as friends, and they did not fear “the harsh wrath of Zeus who thunders loud, / god of guests”, who will one day destroy their city (13.620–627). In the *Odyssey* the rule is stated plainly: “strangers and beggars all come from Zeus” (*Odyssey* 6.207–208, 14.57–58), and the poem is built on hospitality given, refused and abused.

The Trojan War is a war about a guest. Paris came to Sparta as Menelaus' guest and left with his wife and his goods. The Iliad never tells the visit, but it names the offense in Menelaus' prayer in Book 3 and in his reproach in Book 13.

## Related words

- [[philotes|φιλότης]] (“friendship”) and φιλέω (“love, entertain”): Antenor “received them as guests and welcomed them” (ἐξείνισσα καὶ … φίλησα, 3.207), and a host is one who “has shown him friendship” (3.354).
- [[doron|δῶρον]] (“gift”): the gifts that seal the bond.
- [[supplication]]: the suppliant, like the guest, is under the protection of Zeus.

## Outside Homer

The *Cypria*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, told the visit. Alexander was entertained by the sons of Tyndareus in Lacedaemon and then by Menelaus at Sparta, where he gave Helen gifts at the feast. Menelaus sailed away to Crete, telling Helen to provide for the guests until they left, and Aphrodite brought Helen and Alexander together.

In Aeschylus' *Agamemnon* it is Zeus of guests (ξένιος Ζεύς) who sends the sons of Atreus against Alexander (60–62), and the chorus sings that Paris came to the house of the Atreidae and shamed the table of hospitality by stealing a wife (399–402).
