## Meaning

μοῖρα is a share: a portion of meat at a feast, of land, of booty, of honor. From there it becomes one's lot in life, and above all the death that is one's portion. The word belongs with μείρομαι (“receive a share”; perfect ἔμμορε, “has as his share”) and with μόρος (“doom, death”).

Personified, Μοῖρα is a power beside Zeus. Agamemnon names Zeus, Moira and the Erinys as the authors of his blindness (19.87), and Apollo says that the Moirai gave men an enduring heart (24.49).

κατὰ μοῖραν means “in due measure, rightly”. It is the regular formula of approval for something said.

## In Book 1

{{quote:61fe4540-fc21-5739-86ce-b33d8cfe12c3}}

Nestor speaks of honor as a portion. The scepter-bearing king “has had no ordinary share (ἔμμορε) of honor”.

{{quote:3205ba55-4a0c-5367-b288-2c5c2badec4c}}

Agamemnon grants that Nestor has spoken κατὰ μοῖραν, “fair and right”, and then refuses the advice.

{{quote:ee00f60b-70a0-554c-b4a4-d40bcf98aa91}}

{{quote:12f86521-5d0b-5f1a-8902-220a1ef22b34}}

μόρος appears in the compound ὠκύμορος, “swift-fated” (ὠκύς, “swift”, and μόρος). Thetis says it to her son (1.417), and to Zeus she calls him ὠκυμορώτατος, “swifter to his fate than any other man” (1.505). The adjective ὠκύς serves twice for Achilles: he is πόδας ὠκύς, swift-footed, and he is swift to his death.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod names three Moirai, Clotho, Lachesis and Atropos. In one passage they are daughters of Night (*Theogony* 217–219), in another of Zeus and Themis (*Theogony* 904–906). In Plato's myth of Er they are the daughters of Necessity (*Republic* 10.617b–c).
