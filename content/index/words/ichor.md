## Meaning

ἰχώρ is what flows from a wounded god. Homer has the word twice, both times of Aphrodite's hand, which Diomedes has cut through her robe with his spear. The first time the poet stops to explain it:

{{quote:22e0313e-ee00-5cfb-8120-bb9d50179d2e}}

The line before calls it ἄμβροτον αἷμα, “immortal blood” (5.339), and the same words are used when Ares shows Zeus his own wound (5.870). The explanation runs from food to blood to death. Mortals eat σῖτος (“bread”) and drink wine; the gods live on ambrosia and nectar (see [[ambrosia]] and [[nectar]]), and even their horses graze on ambrosia (5.369, 5.777). In the Odyssey men are “those who eat bread” (σῖτον ἔδοντες, *Odyssey* 8.222, 9.89), and Calypso eats ambrosia at the table where Odysseus eats “such food as mortal men eat” (*Odyssey* 5.196–199). So the gods are ἀναίμονες, “bloodless”, a word found only here, and so they are called ἀθάνατοι, “immortals” (see [[immortals]]). The verse gives a reason for a name, which Homer seldom does.

The reasoning is not carried through. Ichor is still called blood, and it behaves like blood. It flows from the wound, Dione wipes it from her daughter's hand with both hands, and then the hand heals and the pain eases (5.416–417). Ares' ichor is described in the words used of a mortal's blood: he “showed him the immortal blood flowing down from the wound” (5.870), as Agamemnon saw “the black blood flowing down from the wound” of Menelaus (αἷμα καταρρέον ἐξ ὠτειλῆς, 4.149). The wound hurts as a man's does. Aphrodite goes away “in terrible pain”, and her beautiful skin darkens (5.352–354). What the ichor lacks is only the death that blood can bring. The god who bleeds it cannot die of the wound, “for he was not made to be mortal” (5.402, 5.901). See [[gods-wounded-by-mortals]].

No etymology of ἰχώρ is agreed, and it may be a loanword. Its accusative ἰχῶ (5.416) is contracted like ἱδρῶ, the accusative of ἱδρώς, “sweat” (4.27).

## Related words

- αἷμα (“blood”), and the mortal food it comes from: σῖτος (“bread”) and wine.
- [[ambrosia]] and [[nectar]], the food and drink of the gods.
- [[immortals]], the name the ichor explains, and [[thanatos|θάνατος]] (“death”).
- [[psyche|ψυχή]] (“soul”), which leaves a mortal through a wound.

## Outside Homer

In later Greek ἰχώρ is also the watery discharge from a wound or a sore, the sense it has in the medical writers, and English has taken both meanings from Greek. Plutarch tells that Alexander, wounded by an arrow, told his friends that what flowed from him was blood, and not “ichor, such as flows in the blessed gods” (5.340; Plutarch, *Alexander* 28).
