## Meaning

αἴτιος means “responsible, to blame”. The noun αἰτία (Epic αἰτίη) is the charge and, later, the cause; the verb αἰτιάομαι is “accuse, blame”. The compound ἐπαίτιος means the same as αἴτιος. The words come from the language of accusation: the αἴτιος is the one against whom a charge can be brought. Their etymology is uncertain.

In the Iliad the word is spoken most often to deny guilt, and each denial moves the blame elsewhere. The three in Books 1–3 share their form, οὔ τί μοι, “not in my eyes”:

- Achilles has no quarrel with the Trojans: “they are not to blame toward me” (οὔ τί μοι αἴτιοί εἰσιν, 1.153; the translation renders “they have done me no wrong”). He came to Troy to win satisfaction for Menelaus and Agamemnon (1.158–159).
- He tells the heralds who come for Briseis, “You are not to blame in my eyes, but Agamemnon” (1.335).
- Priam tells Helen, “you are not to blame for anything in my eyes; it is the gods who are to blame” (3.164).

The best-known plea in the poem is Agamemnon's. Before the army in Book 19 he says, “I am not to blame (οὐκ αἴτιός εἰμι), but Zeus and Moira and the Erinys who walks in darkness”, who cast savage ἄτη into his mind on the day he took Achilles' prize (19.86–89; see [[ate]]). He pays the gifts all the same (19.137–138). In the *Odyssey* Zeus complains of the habit: mortals blame (αἰτιόωνται) the gods for their evils, when by their own recklessness they suffer beyond their share (*Odyssey* 1.32–34). See [[who-is-to-blame]].

## Related words

- [[ate|ἄτη]] (“blind folly”): the plea that a god, not the man, was at work.
- [[nemesis|νέμεσις]] (“blame, indignation”), [[oneidos|ὄνειδος]] (“reproach”) and μωμάομαι (“blame, find fault”, 3.412).
- [[poine|ποινή]] (“penalty”): what the one to blame must pay.

## Outside Homer

In a dithyramb of Bacchylides, Menelaus tells the Trojans that Zeus is not to blame (αἴτιος) for mortals' great sorrows (Bacchylides 15; see [[bacchylides-embassy]]). In the myth of Er, the prophet who sets out the lots of lives for the souls declares: “the blame is the chooser's; god is not to blame” (αἰτία ἑλομένου· θεὸς ἀναίτιος, Plato, *Republic* 10, 617e). Gorgias begins his *Encomium of Helen* by promising to free her from the charge (αἰτία) and to show that her accusers lie (2), and argues that she is not to blame whether she went by the will of the gods, by force, by persuasion or by love (6; see [[gorgias-helen]]).
