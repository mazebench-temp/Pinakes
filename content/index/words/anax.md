## Meaning

ἄναξ is the older of the two Homeric words for a ruler. It appears in the Mycenaean tablets as *wa-na-ka*, the title of the king at Pylos and Knossos. The initial *w-* (digamma) was lost in later Greek, but it still shapes Homer's meter. In 1.7 (Ἀτρεΐδης τε ἄναξ) and 1.36 (Ἀπόλλωνι ἄνακτι) a vowel stands before ἄναξ without elision, because a consonant once stood there.

In Homer ἄναξ means lord or master, of gods as well as men. Apollo is ἄναξ (1.36, 1.75, 1.390, 1.444), and so is Zeus (1.502, 1.529). Agamemnon's standing title is ἄναξ ἀνδρῶν, “lord of men” (1.7, 1.172, 1.442, 1.506).

The verb ἀνάσσω means “be lord, rule”, over people in the dative or genitive.

## In Book 1

{{quote:ae973487-15a4-54de-aeff-a51910ef540e}}

Chryses addresses Apollo as the god who rules Tenedos with might.

In the quarrel the verb becomes a weapon. Agamemnon tells Achilles to go home and “lord it over the Myrmidons” (1.180), a smaller realm. Achilles replies that Agamemnon rules over nobodies (1.231).

{{quote:e0ab0ff5-13c6-51a8-981d-8b70cc60c253}}

Nestor sides with rank. Achilles is stronger, but Agamemnon is greater, “since he rules over more”. Nestor has ruled too: he was lord among a third generation of men (1.252).

{{quote:77d635ac-04d6-50da-bee1-e282d1526882}}

Agamemnon's complaint is that Achilles wants to have power over all, be lord over all, give orders to all. The three verbs, κρατέειν, ἀνάσσειν and σημαίνειν, describe the king's own position.
