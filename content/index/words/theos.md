## Meaning

θεός (god) is used of male and female deities alike, and θεά is the special feminine form. A god in Homer is defined by one thing above all: gods do not die. They are ἀθάνατοι, "deathless", and θεοὶ αἰὲν ἐόντες, "who live forever" (1.290, 1.494). Mortals are βροτοί. The etymology of θεός is disputed.

## In Book 1

{{quote:4630ecf7-c829-5e61-b61f-793096803b5a}}

The poem's third word is θεά: the goddess who sings the song.

{{quote:e5b5d746-4830-5151-bb90-a2438974ea6c}}

The first question the poem asks is about the gods: "Which of the gods, then, brought the two together to fight in strife?"

The word is also a title used in address. Achilles calls Athena θεά (1.216), and Achilles calls Thetis θεά when he reminds her of her service to Zeus (1.401). "The god" without a name can mean the god at hand: in the plague, "the arrows of the god" are Apollo's (1.53, 1.383). See [[olympians]] and [[gods-and-mortals]].
