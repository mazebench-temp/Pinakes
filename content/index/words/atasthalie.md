## Meaning

ἀτασθαλίη is recklessness: the conduct of people who go ahead in spite of a warning, a sign or a limit, and bring ruin on themselves. The noun is almost always plural, ἀτασθαλίαι, “reckless deeds”, and it usually has an owner: “their own”, “my own”. The adjective ἀτάσθαλος means “reckless, presumptuous, wicked”. The etymology is unknown.

The word belongs to judgments made after the event. It explains a death or a disaster by the victims' own conduct, and it is most often said of the dead. The Iliad has the noun twice. Sthenelus uses it of the fathers who fell at Thebes (4.409). Hector, waiting outside the walls for Achilles, uses it of himself: he would not take Polydamas' advice to lead the army back into the city, “and now, since I have destroyed the people by my own recklessness” (ἀτασθαλίῃσιν ἐμῇσιν), he is ashamed to face the men and women of Troy (22.99–107).

In the Odyssey the word states the poem's moral. Its first lines say that Odysseus' companions “perished through their own recklessness” when they ate the cattle of the Sun (*Odyssey* 1.7–9), in the words Sthenelus uses (σφετέρῃσιν ἀτασθαλίῃσιν ὄλοντο). A few lines later Zeus complains that mortals blame the gods for their evils, when “through their own recklessness” they have sorrows beyond their share, and gives Aegisthus as his example: the gods warned him, and he did not listen (*Odyssey* 1.32–43). The suitors die the same way (*Odyssey* 22.413–416).

The pattern is the same each time. A warning is given and ignored, and the ruin that follows is the doer's own. The word leaves no share to the gods. [[ate|ἄτη]], “blind folly”, is a blindness a god sends, and a man can plead it afterwards, as Agamemnon does (19.86–89); ἀτασθαλίαι belong to those who commit them.

## Related words

- [[ate|ἄτη]] (“blind folly”): folly sent from outside.
- [[hybris|ὕβρις]] (“outrage”), and ὑπερφίαλος (“overbearing”), Menelaus' word for the sons of Priam (3.106).
- [[nepios|νήπιος]] (“fool”): the narrator's word for a man who does not see what his acts will bring.
- See [[who-is-to-blame]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod's race of silver could not keep “reckless outrage” (ὕβριν ἀτάσθαλον) from one another and would not serve the gods, and Zeus in his anger put them away (*Works and Days* 134–139).
