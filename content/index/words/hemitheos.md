## Meaning

ἡμίθεος is made of ἡμι-, “half”, and θεός, “god”, as ἡμίονος, the mule, is a “half-ass”. Homer has the word once, in the genitive plural, and not in the mouth of a character. The narrator has turned from the battle to the future of the Achaean wall: after Troy is taken and the Argives have sailed home, Poseidon and Apollo will turn the rivers of Ida against it (see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[the-building-of-the-wall]]).

{{quote:baebab8e-cc03-5f75-83f4-4996f989a81d}}

The heroes appear here as a race that is gone. The narrator speaks from his own time, after the wall and the men who fought around it have vanished, as he does when he measures a hero's strength against men “such as mortals are now” (5.304, 12.383, 12.449; see [[men-of-today]]). Inside the poem no one is called a demigod. To one another the fighting men are ἥρωες, “heroes”, a title of respect for the living (see [[heros]]).

The word fits men of whom many were the sons or grandsons of gods. In this same book Zeus rouses “his own son”, Sarpedon, and keeps the fates of death from him at the wall (12.292, 12.402–403; see [[sarpedon]]). But the narrator gives the name to the whole race that fell by the Simoeis, as Hesiod does to the whole generation that fought at Thebes and Troy.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells the history of mankind as a succession of races. After the race of bronze, Zeus made a fourth on the earth, “more just and better, the godlike race of hero-men, who are called demigods (ἡμίθεοι), the generation before ours on the boundless earth” (*Works and Days* 158–160). Evil war destroyed them, some at seven-gated Thebes fighting for the flocks of Oedipus, some at Troy, where war had brought them over the sea for the sake of Helen (161–165), and some of them Zeus settled apart from men at the ends of the earth, in the Islands of the Blessed (166–173). Hesiod's phrase, ἀνδρῶν ἡρώων θεῖον γένος, has the γένος and the ἀνδρῶν of Homer's. The end of the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, preserved on a Berlin papyrus, tells how Zeus devised turmoil over the boundless earth, meaning to destroy “the lives of the demigods” (ψυχὰς ἡμιθέων); the verses that give his reason are damaged (fr. 68 in Evelyn-White's numbering). Two of the Homeric Hymns end by promising the song that will follow, about “the race of mortal men, half-divine” (μερόπων γένος ἀνδρῶν ἡμιθέων, Homer's three words in another order), “whose deeds the goddesses have shown to mortals” (*Hymn* 31.18–19, to Helios), and about “the famous deeds of men half-divine, which the singers, servants of the Muses, celebrate” (*Hymn* 32.18–19, to Selene).

Plato's Socrates uses the word of the men of Troy. If someone should ask him whether he is not ashamed of a way of life that has brought him into danger of death, he would answer that by such reasoning “the demigods who died at Troy” would be worthless, above all the son of Thetis (*Apology* 28b–c), and he hopes to meet in Hades the true judges, Minos, Rhadamanthys, Aeacus and Triptolemus, “and the other demigods who were just in their lives” (*Apology* 41a). In the *Cratylus* he asks, “Don't you know that the heroes are demigods?”: all of them were born of a god who loved a mortal woman or a mortal man who loved a goddess, and he derives ἥρως from ἔρως, “love” (398c–d). Aristotle gives, as an example of praise that is too general, praising Achilles because he is a man and “one of the demigods” and went on the expedition to Ilion, which can be said of many others as well (*Rhetoric* 2.22.12; see [[aristotle]]).
