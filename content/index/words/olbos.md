## Meaning

ὄλβος is the good fortune that can be seen in a house: possessions, sons, and a life that ends in comfort. Homer's men do not win it; the gods give it and take it. They “spin” it as they spin a man's fate (ἐπικλώθω; see [[moira]]): Telemachus says that “the gods have spun no such prosperity for me, / for my father and for me” (*Odyssey* 3.208–209), and Menelaus says that the son of a man is easily known for whom the son of Cronus spins prosperity at his marriage and his birth, as he has granted Nestor to grow old in comfort in his halls, with sons who are wise and the best with the spear (*Odyssey* 4.207–211; see [[nestor]]). Nausicaa tells the shipwrecked stranger that Zeus himself, the Olympian, deals out prosperity to men, good and bad, to each as he wishes, “and he has given you these things, and you must endure them all the same” (*Odyssey* 6.188–190), the half-line of Telemachus' answer to Nestor, νῦν δὲ χρὴ τετλάμεν ἔμπης (*Odyssey* 3.209). Penelope says that Zeus has taken away her ὄλβος (*Odyssey* 18.273).

In the Iliad it is the happiness the war undoes. Bathycles “stood out among the Myrmidons for prosperity and wealth” before Glaucus killed him (16.594–596; see [[bathycles]]); Peleus excelled all men “in prosperity and wealth”, but the gods gave him one son, doomed to an early death (24.534–540; see [[peleus]]); and Achilles tells Priam, “And you too, old man, we hear, were prosperous once” (24.543; see [[priam]] and [[the-two-jars-of-zeus]]). The adjective is the word of blessing: the shade of Agamemnon greets Achilles and Odysseus with ὄλβιε, “blessed” (*Odyssey* 24.36, 24.192), and men wish prosperity on the beggar for the time to come (*Odyssey* 18.122–123, 20.199–200).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes ὄλβος the gift of Zeus to the man who knows what is just and speaks it (*Works and Days* 280–281), and of Plutus, son of Demeter, who makes rich whoever meets him and gives him much ὄλβος (*Theogony* 969–974); and he pairs it with daring: αἰδώς τοι πρὸς ἀνολβίῃ, θάρσος δὲ πρὸς ὄλβῳ, “shame goes with lack of fortune, daring with fortune” (*Works and Days* 319; see [[aidos]] and [[tharsos]]). Herodotus' Solon tells Croesus to call no man ὄλβιος before his end, only fortunate (*Histories* 1.32.7; see [[eudaimonia]]).
