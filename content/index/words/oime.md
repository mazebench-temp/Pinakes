## Meaning

οἴμη is the “strain”, literally the “way” of song, and the word is always used of song, however independently it stands (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 8.74). Merry quotes Mure's distinction: in Homer ἀοιδή is all poetry or song, epic or lyric, and ἔπη merely conversation or discourse; later ἔπη became the name of epic and ἀοιδή of song in the stricter sense, while “the longer, more continuous epic narrative, or Epopee, bears with Homer the title οἴμη” (on 8.74). The translation renders it “lay” (see its table of key words). The Suda glosses it “road” (Οἴμη· ὁδός, Suda οι 95), and the Homeric *Hymn to Hermes* has the masculine οἶμος in the same sense: Apollo, a companion of the Olympian Muses, names among their cares the dances “and the bright path of song” (ἀγλαὸς οἶμος ἀοιδῆς, *Hymn to Hermes* 451, a plain rendering; see [[muses]]).

Each of Homer's three lays comes from a god. The Muse sets Demodocus on to sing the famous deeds of men, from a lay whose fame had reached the wide sky (*Odyssey* 8.73–74); the Muse has taught singers their lays, and so they are honored among all men (*Odyssey* 8.479–481); and Phemius, begging Odysseus for his life among the dead suitors, says, “I am self-taught, and a god has planted in my mind lays of every kind” (αὐτοδίδακτος δʼ εἰμί, θεὸς δέ μοι ἐν φρεσὶν οἴμας / παντοίας ἐνέφυσεν, *Odyssey* 22.347–348, a plain rendering; see [[phemius]] and [[singers]]). The two claims stand side by side: no man taught him, and a god gave him his lays.

## Related words

- ἀοιδή (“song”) and ἀοιδός (“singer”): see [[singers]] and [[song-and-fame]].
- ὕμνος (“strains”): Alcinous wishes his guest to take delight “in hearing the strains of the song” (ἀοιδῆς ὕμνον, *Odyssey* 8.429), the only ὕμνος in Homer (Merry on 8.429).
- κλέα ἀνδρῶν (“the famous deeds of men”), the matter of the lay (see [[kleos]]).
