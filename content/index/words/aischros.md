## Meaning

αἰσχρός is the adjective of αἶσχος, “ugliness, disgrace”. One word covers what offends the eye and what offends the sense of honor. Thersites is αἴσχιστος, “the ugliest man who came to Ilios” (2.216), and it is αἰσχρόν, “shameful”, to go home empty-handed after nine years (2.298). Its opposite, καλός, is both “beautiful” and “fine, noble”, and Book 2 sets the two superlatives in the same frame of line: Thersites is the ugliest man “who came to Ilios”, Nireus the most handsome (κάλλιστος, 2.673).

Words can be αἰσχρά. Hector rebukes Paris “with shaming words” (αἰσχροῖς ἐπέεσσι, 3.38 = 6.325), words that bring shame on the man they are aimed at. The plural of the noun, αἴσχεα, means “reproaches”: Hector's heart grieves when he hears the Trojans' αἴσχεα about Paris (6.524). The verb αἰσχύνω in Homer means “disfigure, defile”. Achilles, told of Patroclus' death, defiles his handsome face with ashes (18.24), and Priam imagines dogs defiling the body of an old man killed in war (22.75).

Shame in Homer is public: what others see, and what they say. The αἰσχρόν of 2.119 lies in what men to come will hear, the reverse of [[kleos|κλέος]] (“report, fame”).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle defines the laughable as a part of the ugly (τοῦ αἰσχροῦ): “a fault or ugliness (αἶσχος) that causes no pain or harm”, like the comic mask, “ugly and distorted, without pain” (*Poetics* 5, 1449a32–37). Homer sets γελοίϊον, “laughable”, and αἴσχιστος, “ugliest”, in two consecutive lines of the portrait of Thersites (2.215–216). In the myth of Er, Thersites' soul chooses to become an ape (Plato, *Republic* 10, 620c; see [[plato-thersites]]).
