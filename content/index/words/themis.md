## Meaning

θέμις comes from the root of τίθημι, “set, lay down”. It is what has been laid down: the established order, what is right and customary among gods and men. It is θέμις for a man to speak his mind against a king in the assembly (9.33), and it is not θέμις to slight a stranger (*Odyssey* 14.56–57). In the plural, θέμιστες are the particular ordinances or judgments in which that order is applied. They come from Zeus and are held by kings. Nestor tells Agamemnon that Zeus has put into his hands “the scepter and the θέμιστες, so that you may take counsel for your people” (9.98–99).

A king who judges crookedly offends Zeus. In a simile of Book 16, Zeus pours down torrents of rain in anger at men who “by force give crooked θέμιστες in the assembly and drive out justice (δίκη), not heeding the regard of the gods” (16.386–388). On the shield of Achilles, elders sit in a sacred circle and judge a case, holding heralds' scepters in their hands (18.497–508).

The δικασπόλοι of 1.238 are the men who give such judgments. The word is formed from δίκαι, “judgments”, and πολέω, “go about, busy oneself with”. The verb δικάζω means “judge, decide”.

Themis is also a goddess. At Zeus' command she calls the gods to assembly (20.4–5), and in the *Odyssey* she dismisses and seats the assemblies of men (2.68–69). The link with the assembly matters in Book 1: Achilles' oath is sworn in one.

## In Book 1

{{quote:422c8743-f485-5542-a68a-56a24331cbca}}

Achilles' oath gives the book's only description of the θέμιστες. The scepter was cut from a tree in the mountains and stripped by the bronze of leaves and bark; it will never grow leaves again. Now the δικασπόλοι carry it, “those who guard the ordinances that come from Zeus”. Achilles swears by the emblem of judgment in a dispute where he thinks judgment has failed, and then throws it to the ground (1.245). See [[scepter]].

{{quote:bbdd9df1-05a8-5c98-9f91-80ed24736e20}}

Hera turns the language of judgment against Zeus. It is always dear to him, she says, to “decide” (δικαζέμεν) secret things apart from her. The king of the gods judges, and the question is who is admitted to his judgments. Zeus answers that some of his counsels are not hers to know (1.545–550).

## Related words

- [[basileus|βασιλεύς]] (“king”): the holder of the θέμιστες.
- [[boule|βουλή]] (“counsel”): what the king gives with them.
- δίκη (“judgment, justice”), not found in Book 1, and δικάζω (“judge”).
- [[oath]] and [[scepter]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes Themis the second wife of Zeus and the mother of the Seasons, Eunomia, Dike and Eirene, and of the Moirai (*Theogony* 901–906). He also uses Homer's language for corrupt judges: kings who “decide θέμιστες with crooked judgments” (*Works and Days* 221).
