## Meaning

σέβας is awe: the feeling that stops a person in the presence of what commands respect, a god, a wonder, or a deed too shameful to do. The verbs are σέβομαι, “feel awe”, and σεβάζομαι, “shrink from in awe”. The word is usually connected with Sanskrit *tyaj-*, “leave, abandon”, and explained as a drawing back.

The Iliad uses the family rarely, and in the sense closest to [[aidos|αἰδώς]]: an awe felt before an act, which holds a man back from it. Proetus would not kill Bellerophon himself, “for his heart held back in awe from that” (σεβάσσατο γὰρ τό γε θυμῷ, 6.167). Achilles, sacking Thebe, killed Andromache's father Eetion but did not strip his body, “for his heart held back in awe from that”, and burned him in his armor (6.416–419). When Patroclus lies dead, Iris tells Achilles to let σέβας come into his heart at the thought that his friend's body might become a plaything for the dogs of Troy (18.178–180). The one use of σέβομαι is a rebuke. Agamemnon asks the Argives who hold back from battle, οὔ νυ σέβεσθε; “have you no shame?” (4.242).

In the Odyssey σέβας is also the wonder that a sight inspires. “Awe holds me as I look” (σέβας μʼ ἔχει εἰσορόωντα), says Telemachus in the palace of Menelaus (*Odyssey* 4.75), and Odysseus says it to Nausicaa (*Odyssey* 6.161).

## Related words

- [[aidos|αἰδώς]] (“respect, shame”) and ἅζομαι (“revere”).
- [[thambos|θάμβος]] (“amazement”).
- [[elenchos|ἔλεγχος]] (“disgrace”): what the man without σέβας earns.

## Outside Homer

In classical Greek the family names the reverence owed to the gods: εὐσεβής, “pious”, and ἀσεβής, “impious”, and the nouns εὐσέβεια and ἀσέβεια.
