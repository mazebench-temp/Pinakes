## Meaning

αἶνος is speech that carries a purpose: praise of a man, or a story told to make a point. In the Iliad the noun occurs twice, both times at the funeral games and both times as praise. Achilles listens to “all the praise of the son of Neleus” (23.652), and tells Antilochus, who has praised his elders and Achilles himself, “your praise shall not be spoken in vain” (23.795). Walter Leaf takes it as “praise” in both places, as when Telemachus asks the suitors what need he has to praise his mother (μητέρος αἴνου, *Odyssey* 21.110), and finds the other sense, “a short, pithy narrative”, hardly suitable there (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.652). The other sense is in the Odyssey too. The disguised Odysseus tells Eumaeus how, at Troy, Odysseus once found him a cloak on a freezing night, and Eumaeus, who sees the point, answers, “old man, the αἶνος you told is blameless”, and lays a thick cloak over him (*Odyssey* 14.462–521).

The verb αἰνέω is “praise” and “approve”. Zeus bids the gods all together “approve” his word (αἰνεῖτε, 8.9); Odysseus asks Diomedes neither to praise him too much nor to rebuke him (αἴνεε, 10.249; see [[do-not-praise-me-too-much]]); and Antilochus tells Achilles to give Eumelus a prize from his own store, “so that the Achaeans may praise you” (αἰνήσωσιν, 23.552). In the last book Paris is remembered as the man who insulted the goddesses and praised the one who gave him lust (νείκεσσε … ᾔνησε, 24.29–30; see [[paris]]). The compound ἐπαινέω, “approve, assent”, is the commoner verb: “and all the kings approved” (7.344, 9.710; see [[assembly]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod introduces the fable of the hawk and the nightingale as an αἶνος that he will tell to kings, who understand it themselves. The hawk, carrying the nightingale high in his talons, tells her that she is a fool who contends with the stronger, and Hesiod turns to his brother: “Perses, listen to justice” (*Works and Days* 202–213; see [[hesiod]]).
