## Meaning

εὐαγγέλιον is formed from εὐάγγελος, “bringing good news” (εὖ, “well”, and ἄγγελος, “messenger”), and names what is due for good news. Homer has it only twice, in one exchange between the disguised Odysseus and Eumaeus (*Odyssey* 14.152, 14.166), where it is a reward asked for in advance and payable only when the news proves true. The bearer of news is ἄγγελος, the news ἀγγελίη, and the verb ἀγγέλλω, “bring news”. The word had a long history after Homer: in Christian Greek εὐαγγέλιον is the Gospel, the “good news”.

## Outside Homer

Aeschylus' *Agamemnon* turns on good news that is not good. The watchman waits for the beacon from Troy, “the fire of good news” (εὐαγγέλου … πυρός, 21); Clytemnestra, asked what she has heard, answers with a proverb, “bringing good news, as the proverb says, may Dawn be born from her mother Night” (εὐάγγελος μέν, ὥσπερ ἡ παροιμία, / ἕως γένοιτο μητρὸς εὐφρόνης πάρα, 264–265); and the herald, come home as the bearer of good news of deliverance, asks how he can mix good with evil and tell of the storm that struck the fleet (646–649; see [[aeschylus]]). The Suda glosses εὐαγγέλιον as “that which reports the best tidings” (τὰ κάλλιστα ἀπαγγέλλον, Suda ε 3355), and records that the Athenians honored with crowns those who brought them good news (Suda ε 3206).
