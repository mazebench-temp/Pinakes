## Meaning

ἔρος (Attic ἔρως, the form the text prints at 3.442) is desire as appetite. The formula that closes a Homeric meal says that the diners “put away the desire (ἔρον) for drink and food” (1.469, 2.432; see [[desire-for-drink-and-food]]). The same word is sexual desire, and the verb ἔραμαι means “love, desire” a person (3.446). The adjectives ἐρατός and ἐρατεινός, “lovely”, describe what is desired: the “lovely gifts” of Aphrodite (δῶρʼ ἐρατά, 3.64), a lovely town or country (see [[lovely]]).

Twice the Iliad says that ἔρος “wrapped” a man's mind (φρένας ἀμφεκάλυψεν): Paris' when he takes Helen to bed (3.442), and Zeus' when he sees Hera come to him on Ida wearing Aphrodite's band (14.294). Both passages set the moment beside the first time the two lay together: Paris in his own words (3.443–445), the narrator in the case of Zeus and Hera (14.295–296). Desire in Homer is a force that comes over a man and covers his judgment. It is not yet a god.

The further etymology of ἔραμαι and ἔρος is uncertain.

- [[phrenes|φρένες]] (“mind”): what desire covers.

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod, Eros is one of the first powers, born with Chaos, Earth and Tartarus: “the most beautiful among the immortal gods, the limb-loosener, who overpowers the mind and the wise counsel in the breasts of all gods and all men” (*Theogony* 120–122). When Aphrodite was born, Eros went with her and fair Himeros followed (*Theogony* 201). In Plato's *Symposium* Phaedrus begins his praise of Eros with Hesiod's lines (178b). In the *Cratylus* Socrates derives ἔρως from its “flowing in” (ἐσρεῖν) from outside, through the eyes (420a–b).
