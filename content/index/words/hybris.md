## Meaning

ὕβρις is behavior that violates the honor of another, usually with force and always with contempt. It is less a feeling of pride than a way of acting: taking what belongs to someone else, insulting, humiliating, overstepping. The translation renders it “arrogance” where the stress is on the attitude (1.203) and “outrage” where it is on the deed (1.214). The etymology is unknown.

The word is rare in the Iliad and common in the Odyssey. There it is the defining vice of the suitors, and the gods go about in disguise to watch men's ὕβρις and their good order (*Odyssey* 17.487).

Scholars disagree on where to put the weight. N. R. E. Fisher (*Hybris*, 1992) argued that its core is the deliberate infliction of dishonor. Douglas Cairns (1996) stressed the disposition behind the act, the habit of claiming too much honor for oneself. Book 1 shows both: a king who thinks himself above everyone, and the seizure of a prize that proves it.

ὑπεροπλίη (“overbearing pride”) is a rarer word, here in the plural: “acts of arrogance”. It is usually derived from ὑπέρ (“over”) and ὅπλα (“arms”), as if the overconfidence of a man who trusts in his strength.

## In Book 1

{{quote:56b0e460-ce37-58d6-9529-f72572caedca}}

When Athena seizes his hair, Achilles assumes she has come to see Agamemnon's ὕβρις, and he predicts that the king will soon lose his life through his ὑπεροπλίαι. At this point the outrage is still only a threat. Briseis has not yet been taken.

{{quote:08780e90-a161-5577-8980-72cbe46563e2}}

Athena accepts his description. She promises that someday he will be offered three times as many splendid gifts “because of this outrage”. The goddess treats ὕβρις as a debt that can be priced and repaid (see [[triple-gifts-foretold]]). Achilles still uses the word in Book 9, when he tells the embassy that Agamemnon took back the prize he had given “in outrage” (ἐφυβρίζων, 9.368).

The quarrel itself is full of insult. See [[oneidos|ὄνειδος]] (“reproach”) and [[words-and-violence]].

## Outside Homer

Aristotle defines ὕβρις as doing and saying things that shame the victim, not for any gain but for the pleasure of feeling superior. Dishonor belongs to it, and he quotes 1.356 as the complaint of a man so treated (*Rhetoric* 2.2, 1378b). In the *Posterior Analytics* he names Achilles among the proud men who would not tolerate ὕβρις: his answer to it was wrath (ἐμήνισεν; 2.13, 97b15–25).

In Athens ὕβρις was a crime that any citizen could prosecute; the law is quoted in Demosthenes, *Against Meidias* 21.47.

The English *hubris*, the proud man's self-destroying confidence, owes more to later readings of tragedy than to Homer. Aeschylus already joins it to ruin: in the *Persians*, ὕβρις in flower bears a crop of ἄτη (821–822).
