## Meaning

γενεή is formed from the root of γίγνομαι, “be born, come to be”, as is γένος, “race, stock, kind”. The two words overlap, and Homer uses them in four senses.

**Birth and age.** A man's γενεή fixes his rank among his kin. Podarces was the own brother of Protesilaus, “younger in birth” (ὁπλότερος γενεῇ, 2.707), and Bucolion “the eldest by birth” among Laomedon's sons (6.24). Hera claims her standing among the gods “by birth” and by marriage (4.60). Agamemnon demands that Achilles yield to him as the more kingly and “the elder by birth” (9.160–161), and Diomedes, rising to speak in council, excuses himself as “youngest by birth among you” (14.112).

**Descent.** More often γενεή is a man's descent, the line of fathers he can name. Nestor remembers how Peleus loved to ask him “the lineage and birth of all the Argives” (γενεήν τε τόκον τε, 7.128), and Agamemnon tells his brother to name each man “by his father and his lineage” to honor him (10.68). To state one's lineage is to claim it, with the verb εὔχομαι (see [[euchomai]]): Diomedes claims to be “of a good father” (γένος, 14.113), Achilles “of the race of great Zeus” (21.187). γένος also names the stock of gods and monsters: the Chimaera “was of divine race, not of men” (6.180), and Zeus tells Ares, “you are of my stock” (5.896).

**Breed.** Horses and mules have a γενεή. The horses of Aeneas are “of that breed” that Zeus gave Tros (5.265–268; see [[tros]]), and the Eneti are the land “where the breed of wild mules comes from” (2.852).

**Generation.** Last, a γενεή is all the men born at one time. In Nestor's lifetime “two generations of mortal men” had perished (1.250), and Glaucus compares the generations of men to the leaves that fall and grow again (6.146–149; see [[the-generations-of-leaves]]).

## Lineage in battle

Twice in the Iliad a warrior asked who he is answers with his lineage, in the same verses. Glaucus tells Diomedes, “if you wish to learn this too, so that you may know well our lineage, and many men know it” (6.150–151), and Aeneas says the same two lines to Achilles before he recites the descendants of Dardanus (20.213–214). Both end with the same claim, “this is the lineage and the blood I claim” (6.211 = 20.241). A third man begins with Glaucus' question: when Achilles asks the Paeonian Asteropaeus who he is, Asteropaeus answers, “Great-hearted son of Peleus, why do you ask about my lineage?” (21.153), names his grandfather, the river Axius, and dies (21.157–183). Glaucus is spared for his lineage, and Asteropaeus is killed after reciting his; Achilles, boasting over his body, declares the race of Zeus stronger than the race of rivers (21.184–191). See [[genealogy-in-battle]], [[glaucus-and-diomedes]] and [[fathers-and-sons]].

A lineage can also end. Poseidon saves Aeneas from Achilles “so that the race of Dardanus may not perish without seed”, since Zeus now hates “the race of Priam” (20.303–306).

## Related words

- γίγνομαι, “be born”, and τόκος, “birth, offspring”; γενέθλη, “stock, breed” (5.270).
- [[gennaios|γενναῖος]], “in my blood” (5.253), from the same root.
- αἷμα, “blood”, which Glaucus names with his lineage (6.211).
- [[phylon|φῦλον]], “tribe, race”: the race of gods and the race of men that are never alike (5.441).
- [[pater|πατήρ]] and πατρώϊος, “of the fathers”; see [[fathers-and-sons]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells the history of mankind as a series of γένη, “races”: golden, silver and bronze, then the race of heroes, “the demigods”, who fought at Thebes and at Troy, and last the race of iron, his own (*Works and Days* 109–201).
