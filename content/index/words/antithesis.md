In the style in clauses, Aristotle says, the period is either divided or opposed; the opposed is the one in which “in each of the two clauses, opposite is set beside opposite, or the same word is yoked to opposites” (*Rhetoric* 3.9, 1409b36–37), and nearly all his examples are from Isocrates' *Panegyricus*. It pleases because opposites are most easily known, especially side by side, and because it is like a syllogism, for refutation brings opposites together (*Rhetoric* 1410a20–23). Parisōsis is the equality of the clauses; paromoiōsis the likeness of their first words or last syllables, and when the likeness is at the end it is homoeoteleuton (*Rhetoric* 1410a24–b2). There are also false antitheses, like Epicharmus' (*Rhetoric* 1410b4–5).

## Outside the Rhetoric

The *Rhetorica ad Alexandrum*, which is not Aristotle's, treats antithesis, parisōsis and paromoiōsis together (chapters 26–28). The figures were associated with Gorgias, who made them the mark of his style.
