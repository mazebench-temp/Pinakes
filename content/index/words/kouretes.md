## Meaning

κούρητες is a by-form of κοῦροι, “young men”, as γυμνής, “a light-armed soldier”, stands beside γυμνός (Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.193, after Düntzer). The common word is κοῦρος, a boy or young man, and in the plural the young men of an army or a household: κοῦροι Ἀχαιῶν, “the young men of the Achaeans”, who sing the paean at Chryse (1.473; see [[sons-of-the-achaeans]] and [[paean]]), and the young men who fill the mixing bowls at a feast and serve the wine (1.470–471; see [[cupbearing]]). κούρητες occurs only twice, both in Book 19, and Leaf counts it among the peculiarities of the book.

The same word, with a capital and the accent Κουρῆτες in the printed text, is the name of the people who fought the Aetolians at Calydon in Phoenix's story (9.529; see [[curetes]]). The translation renders the common noun “young men” and keeps the people apart (see the translation's note on 19.193, 19.248), and the index does the same.

## Outside Homer

Strabo, discussing the Curetes, quotes both verses of *Iliad* 19 to show that Homer called young soldiers κούρητες, in a text that differs from ours: “choose young men, the best of all the Achaeans, to bring the gifts from the swift ship (δῶρα θοῆς παρὰ νηὸς ἐνεγκεῖν), all that we promised Achilles yesterday (χθιζοί)”, and “the young men of the Achaeans (κούρητες Ἀχαιοί) carried the gifts”. He thinks it likely that the armed dance, first brought in by men who took such care of their hair and dress and were called Curetes, lent the same name to others more given to war, who lived under arms, the Curetes of Euboea, Aetolia and Acarnania (*Geography* 10.3.8).
