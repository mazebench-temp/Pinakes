## Meaning

γέρων means “old man”. The adjective γεραιός, “aged”, is used in the same way, of the priest Chryses and of Priam (1.35, 3.191). γῆρας is old age, and the verb γηράσκω means to grow old, as Heracles' uncle Licymnius was doing when he was killed (2.663). Both words come from a root meaning “grow old”, the root of Sanskrit *jarant-*, “old”. In the plural γέροντες are the elders, the senior kings whom Agamemnon calls to council and to his table (2.53, 2.404). There the word names a rank rather than an age: the young Diomedes is one of them (see [[elders]]).

As an address, γέρον, “old man”, takes its tone from the speaker. Agamemnon uses it to threaten Chryses, “Old man, let me not find you beside the hollow ships” (1.26), and to brush off Nestor, “Yes, old man, all that you have said is fair and right” (1.286). In Book 4 he uses it with affection, wishing that Nestor's knees were as young as his spirit (4.313).

Old age in Homer is a burden that comes upon a man (ἔπεισιν, 1.29; ὀπάζει, 4.321) and wears him down (τείρει, 4.315). It takes the strength of the body and leaves the voice. The old men of Troy are “done with war now because of their age, but excellent speakers” (3.150), and Nestor, too old to fight, commands “with counsel and with words; for that is the prize of the old” (τὸ γὰρ γέρας ἐστὶ γερόντων, 4.323). An old man is also the one who knows: in whatever an old man takes part, “he looks both before and after” (3.109; see [[before-and-after]]).

Whether γέρων is related to [[geras|γέρας]] (“prize of honor”), as Nestor's line seems to suggest, is uncertain.
