## Meaning

ἀρετή is excellence of any kind. It is usually connected with ἀρείων, “better”, and ἄριστος, “best” (see [[aristos]]), but the etymology is not certain. The translation renders it “prowess”, because in the Iliad it is most often the worth of a fighter, and that worth has to be shown. An ambush, Idomeneus tells Meriones, is the place “where men's prowess is most clearly seen”: there the coward changes color and cannot sit still, while the brave man's color does not change (13.275–286). The verb he uses, διαείδεται, “is seen plainly”, is the verb Hector uses, in the future, when the word first appears in the poem: “Tomorrow he will learn his own prowess” (ἣν ἀρετὴν διαείσεται, 8.535), he says of Diomedes, whom he expects to kill at the ships (see [[diomedes]] and [[hector]]).

The word is not only for men in battle. At the funeral games Achilles says that his immortal horses surpass all others in it (23.276), and in the chariot race the excellence of each team shows on the last stretch (23.374–375); Menelaus complains that Antilochus has shamed his ἀρετή by a trick (23.571). Phoenix tells Achilles that even the gods can be turned by prayer, “and theirs is the greater prowess and honor and strength” (9.497–498). Excellence is also a gift that can be taken back. Aeneas tells Achilles that Zeus increases and diminishes men's prowess as he wishes, “for he is the mightiest of all” (20.242–243), and in the Odyssey the swineherd Eumaeus says that Zeus takes away half of a man's excellence on the day he becomes a slave (*Odyssey* 17.322–323; see [[doulios]]). Patroclus calls Achilles αἰναρέτης, “dread in your prowess”, and asks who in later times will have any good of him if he will not save the Achaeans now (16.29–32), and Nestor says that Achilles will have the good of his prowess alone (11.762–764).

A woman has her ἀρετή too. Penelope says that the immortals destroyed her excellence, her looks and her figure when the Argives sailed for Troy (*Odyssey* 18.251–253, 19.124–126), and the dead Agamemnon says that the fame of her excellence will never perish (*Odyssey* 24.196–198).

## Related words

- [[aristos|ἄριστος]] (“best”) and ἀρείων (“better”).
- [[kydos|κῦδος]] (“glory”) and [[kleos|κλέος]] (“fame”): what prowess, once shown, may win.
- [[time|τιμή]] (“honor”) and βίη (“strength”): Phoenix names them with ἀρετή among the gods' attributes (9.498).
- [[menos|μένος]] (“might”) and [[alke|ἀλκή]] (“courage”).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod says that the gods have set sweat before ἀρετή: the road to it is long and steep and rough at first, but easy once one reaches the top (*Works and Days* 289–292). In classical Greek the word became moral excellence, “virtue”. Plato's *Meno* opens with the question whether ἀρετή can be taught (70a), and Aristotle defines the virtue of character as a settled disposition to choose the mean between two vices (*Nicomachean Ethics* 2.6, 1106b36–1107a3). Arthur Adkins distinguished the Homeric excellences, which are competitive (courage, strength, success), from the cooperative virtues of later Greek ethics, such as justice and self-control (*Merit and Responsibility*, 1960).
