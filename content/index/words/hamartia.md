## Meaning

ἁμαρτία belongs to ἁμαρτάνω, “miss the mark”, said in Homer of a spear that misses its man: “You have missed and not hit me”, Diomedes tells Pandarus (ἤμβροτες οὐδʼ ἔτυχες, 5.287). The verb also means to go wrong in conduct. Phoenix tells Achilles that men turn the gods by prayer “whenever someone oversteps and does wrong” (9.501). ἁμάρτημα is a particular error; in later Greek, and in the New Testament, ἁμαρτία is “sin”.

## After Aristotle

“Tragic flaw”, the common English rendering, makes ἁμαρτία a defect of character: Hamlet's indecision, Othello's jealousy. Aristotle's words point at least as much to error of judgment, above all ignorance of who someone is, which is why the recognition is so central to his best plots: Oedipus kills his father and marries his mother not knowing who they are. Ingram Bywater (1909) took it as a mistake of fact; J. M. Bremer (*Hamartia*, 1969) surveyed the uses and concluded for error, not moral fault. Most modern readers allow that the word covers mistakes for which the agent bears some responsibility, without making the fall deserved; it would not then be pitiable.
