## Meaning

ἀπάτη is deceit: leading someone to believe what is false, to his harm. The verb ἀπατάω, with its stronger compound ἐξαπατάω, means “deceive”, and the adjective ἀπατηλός means “deceptive, false”. The word has no agreed etymology.

In the Iliad deceit belongs above all to the gods. Zeus assures Thetis that nothing he confirms with a nod can be taken back, or prove false (ἀπατηλόν), or go unfulfilled (1.526; see [[no-word-taken-back]] and [[neuo]]). The next night he sends Agamemnon a lying Dream (see [[agamemnons-dream]]). Agamemnon, testing the army, complains that Zeus has “devised an evil deceit” (κακὴν ἀπάτην βουλεύσατο, 2.114), and in Book 9 he repeats the line in earnest (9.21). Hera deceives Zeus himself in Book 14, whose ancient title was Διὸς ἀπάτη, “the Deception of Zeus”. Athena takes the shape of Deiphobus to make Hector stand and face Achilles, and when she vanishes he understands: “Athena has deceived me” (ἐμὲ δʼ ἐξαπάτησεν Ἀθήνη, 22.299).

Among men the Iliad condemns the deceit that breaks an oath. When Pandarus wounds Menelaus during the truce, Agamemnon foresees Zeus shaking the aegis over the Trojans, “angry at this deceit” (τῆσδʼ ἀπάτης κοτέων, 4.168).

Homer's commoner word for a trick is δόλος, “bait, snare”. The wooden horse is the δόλος that Odysseus brought into the citadel of Troy (Odyssey 8.494).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes Ἀπάτη, Deceit, a child of Night, born after Νέμεσις (*Theogony* 223–224; see [[nemesis]]). Plato's Socrates holds that a god never deceives, whether by words or by signs sent in dreams (*Republic* 2, 382e–383a; see [[plato-lying-dream]]). Gorgias turned the word into praise of poetry. Tragedy, he said, is a deception in which “the deceiver is more just than the one who does not deceive, and the deceived is wiser than the one who is not deceived” (fr. B23 Diels–Kranz, quoted by Plutarch, *On the Fame of the Athenians* 348c).
