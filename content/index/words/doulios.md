## Meaning

δοῦλος, the classical word for a slave, does not occur in Homer, though it is older than Homer: the Mycenaean tablets from Pylos already use *do-e-ro* and *do-e-ra*, male and female slaves, among them slaves “of the god”. The epic uses only the feminine δούλη and the adjectives formed from the stem. Helen tells Aphrodite to go and sit beside Paris herself, “until he makes you his wife, or his slave” (ἢ ἄλοχον … ἢ ὅ γε δούλην, 3.409), and Menelaus' son Megapenthes was born to him “of a slave woman” (ἐκ δούλης, *Odyssey* 4.12). The usual Homeric word for a slave is δμώς, feminine δμῳή, often joined with ἀμφίπολος, “handmaid”: the “slave women” among whom Helen sits at her work and to whom Hector puts his question at his own door (δμῳῇσι, δμῳαί, 6.323, 6.375–376; see [[handmaids]]).

**The day of slavery.** δούλιον ἦμαρ is the state of slavery pictured as a day that comes upon a person, the opposite of ἐλεύθερον ἦμαρ, “the day of freedom” (see [[eleutheros]]). Hector names both in his vision of Andromache's future. An Achaean will lead her away, “taking away your day of freedom”; she will weave at another woman's bidding and carry water, and someone will point her out as Hector's wife:

In the Odyssey the phrase comes twice. Odysseus, in one of his false tales, tells how the sailors who were to carry him home plotted “the day of slavery” for him as soon as they were far from land (*Odyssey* 14.339–340). The swineherd Eumaeus, himself sold as a child, gives the saying that goes with it: “far-seeing Zeus takes away half a man's worth on the day that slavery takes him” (ἥμισυ γάρ τʼ ἀρετῆς ἀποαίνυται εὐρύοπα Ζεὺς ἀνέρος, εὖτʼ ἄν μιν κατὰ δούλιον ἦμαρ ἕλῃσιν, *Odyssey* 17.322–323). The other forms of the stem are Odyssean too: Odysseus tells Laertes that nothing slavish shows in his looks (δούλειον, *Odyssey* 24.252), and the disloyal maids of the palace were taught “to bear slavery” (δουλοσύνην, *Odyssey* 22.423).

In the Iliad slavery is the fate of the captured: women and children led away from a sacked city, men sold overseas (see [[captive-women]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]]).
