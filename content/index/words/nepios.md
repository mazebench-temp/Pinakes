## Meaning

νήπιος means first a young child, too young to understand or to fend for itself: “little children” (νήπια τέκνα, 2.136), or the young of a bird (2.311). νηπίαχος is a by-form (2.338), and νηπιέη is childhood (9.491). From the child the word passes to the grown man who behaves like one: foolish, without understanding, blind to what his actions will bring.

It is a favorite word of the Homeric narrator. Set at the head of a line and followed by its explanation, νήπιος passes judgment on a character who does not know what is coming. Agamemnon believes he will take Troy that day (2.38). Patroclus begs Achilles to send him into battle, “for he was begging for his own evil death” (16.46–47). The Trojans applaud Hector's bad counsel, “for Pallas Athena had taken away their wits” (18.311). Andromache orders a bath for Hector, who is already dead (22.445). Odysseus' companions eat the cattle of the Sun (Odyssey 1.8). The narrator knows the plan of the gods and the end of the story; the character, like a child, sees only the present. The pattern “the fool, who did not know” (νήπιος, οὐδὲ τὰ ᾔδη, 2.38) recurs of the young Trojan who begs Achilles for his life (20.466).

The old derivation from νη- (“not”) and ἔπος (“word”), “one who cannot yet speak”, like Latin *infans*, is disputed.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod addresses his brother as μέγα νήπιε Πέρση, “Perses, you great fool” (*Works and Days* 286, 633). Heraclitus: “a man is called childish (νήπιος) by a god, as a child is by a man” (fr. B79 Diels–Kranz). Paul uses the word in its first sense: “When I was a child (νήπιος), I spoke as a child” (1 Corinthians 13:11).
