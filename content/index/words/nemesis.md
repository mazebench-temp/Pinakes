## Meaning

νέμεσις is the indignation people feel at something improper, a breach of what is due, and the blame in which they express it. The verbs νεμεσάω (Epic νεμεσσάω) and νεμεσίζομαι mean “be indignant at, blame”, and νεμεσσητός is “a cause for indignation”. Helen refuses to go to Paris' bed: “it would be a cause for blame” (νεμεσσητὸν δέ κεν εἴη, 3.410). The word is usually derived from νέμω, “deal out, allot”, as the anger at what is not duly given or done.

νέμεσις is felt by those who see an offense; [[aidos|αἰδώς]] is felt by the one who might commit it, and holds him back. Poseidon, rallying the Achaeans, bids each of them set both in his heart, “αἰδώς and νέμεσις” (13.121–122). Helen wishes she had a husband who “knew the indignation and the many reproaches of men” (νέμεσίν τε καὶ αἴσχεα πόλλʼ ἀνθρώπων, 6.351), one who cared what others would say.

The negative marks what is excusable. When the Trojan elders see Helen on the wall, they say it is no cause for νέμεσις that Trojans and Achaeans suffer so long for such a woman (3.156–157).

## Related words

- [[aidos|αἰδώς]] (“respect, shame”).
- [[aischros|αἰσχρός]] (“shameful”): αἴσχεα, “reproaches”, stand beside νέμεσις at 6.351.
- [[oneidos|ὄνειδος]] (“reproach”): the words in which indignation is spoken.
- [[kotos|κότος]] (“grudge”) and [[cholos|χόλος]] (“anger”): the army's anger at 2.223 is both κότος and νέμεσις.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes Nemesis a child of Night, “a bane to mortal men” (*Theogony* 223). At the end of the age of iron, Aidos and Nemesis will wrap themselves in white robes and leave mankind for the gods (*Works and Days* 197–201). Later the word came to mean the retribution that falls on excess. After Solon had left Sardis, Herodotus writes, “a great nemesis from god took Croesus”, because he thought himself the happiest of men (1.34).
