## Meaning

A τέρας is a sign that a god shows (φαίνει) to mortals: a bird of omen, a serpent, a rainbow, a flash of lightning. It is a message, and it has to be read, usually by a seer (see [[divination]] and [[prophecy]]). Most portents in the Iliad come from Zeus. When an eagle drops a live serpent among the Trojans at the Achaean ditch, they shudder at “the portent of aegis-bearing Zeus” (Διὸς τέρας αἰγιόχοιο, 12.209). A rainbow is set in the clouds as “a portent for mortal men” (11.27–28; compare 17.547–548). The Gorgon's head on the aegis is called by the same phrase, Διὸς τέρας αἰγιόχοιο (5.742).

A portent can be refused. Polydamas reads the eagle and the serpent as a warning to turn back (12.210–229). Hector answers that “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (12.243).

σῆμα is the broader word: any sign or mark. It can be a token, like the “grim signs” that Proetus sent with Bellerophon on a folded tablet (6.168–169), or a grave-marker and so a tomb (7.86; 2.814; see [[tomb]]). Of Zeus it means a sign in the sky. He shows “signs of good omen” with lightning on the right (ἐναίσιμα σήματα, 2.353), and he kept the men of Mycenae from joining the march on Thebes “by showing unfavorable signs” (παραίσια σήματα, 4.381). The verb σημαίνω, “give a sign, give orders”, is formed from it (see [[semaino]]).

πέλωρ is a monster, anything huge and dreadful; the Gorgon on the aegis is a “dread monster” (δεινοῖο πελώρου, 5.741). The neuter plural πέλωρα can name a portent as a thing of terror and wonder (2.321).

The etymology of τέρας is uncertain. It may be related to τείρεα, the constellations, the “signs” that crown the sky (18.485).

## Related words

- [[semaino|σημαίνω]] (“give a sign, give orders”).
- [[tekmor|τέκμωρ]] (“sign, token”): Zeus' nod, the greatest sign among the gods (1.525–526).
- [[theopropion|θεοπρόπιον]] (“oracle”): Calchas reads the portent at Aulis “prophesying” (θεοπροπέων, 2.322).
- [[onar|ὄναρ]] (“dream”): another way the gods send signs.
- See [[birds]] and [[calchas]].

## Outside Homer

Herodotus uses Homer's words for the portent that met Xerxes' army after it crossed into Europe: “there a great portent appeared” (τέρας ἐφάνη μέγα), when a mare gave birth to a hare (7.57). Aeschylus gave the fleet at Aulis a different portent, two eagles tearing a pregnant hare (*Agamemnon* 104–159; see [[aeschylus-aulis]]). Cicero translated Homer's portent into Latin verse and then asked why sparrows should mean years (*On Divination* 2.63–65; see [[cicero-aulis-sparrows]]).
