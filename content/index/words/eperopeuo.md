## Meaning

ἠπεροπεύω means “cheat, beguile”, and ἠπεροπευτής is “a cheat, a deceiver”. In the Iliad the words belong mostly to seduction. Hector calls Paris γυναιμανὲς ἠπεροπευτά, “mad for women, seducer”, in a line he says to him twice (3.39, 13.769). Helen asks Aphrodite why she longs to beguile her (ἠπεροπεύειν, 3.399). Diomedes, having wounded the goddess in the hand, tells her to keep away from war: “is it not enough that you deceive feeble women?” (5.349). The seducer and the goddess of desire are described with one word, and in each case the one beguiled is a woman. Menelaus uses the verb of Antilochus' trick in the chariot race, “Another time, avoid deceiving your betters” (23.605). In the Odyssey the deceiver is also the teller of false tales: Alcinous assures Odysseus that the Phaeacians do not take him for “a deceiver and a cheat” (ἠπεροπῆά τʼ … καὶ ἐπίκλοπον, *Odyssey* 11.363–364), and on Ithaca, told by Athena that he has come home, Odysseus fears that she is saying it “to deceive my mind” (*Odyssey* 13.327). The etymology is unknown.
