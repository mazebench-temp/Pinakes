## Meaning

θάρσος is daring, the boldness to go at what is dangerous. The verb θαρσέω means “be bold, take heart”, and its imperative θάρσει is the ordinary word of encouragement: the wounded Menelaus says it to his brother (4.184), Zeus to Athena (8.39), and Odysseus to the captured Dolon (10.383); Achilles bids Calchas speak “taking heart” (θαρσήσας, 1.85), afraid as he is to name the cause of the plague. θαρσύνω is “encourage”, as Agamemnon encourages the eager in his review (4.233), and θαρσαλέος is “bold”. The same root, with the vowel placed differently, gives θράσος and θρασύς. Later Greek tended to keep θάρσος for confidence and θράσος for rashness, but in Homer both are praise, as in the epithet θρασυμέμνων, “bold in endurance”, said of Heracles (5.639; see [[bold-in-endurance]]). The root is inherited: Sanskrit *dhṛṣ-* and English *dare* are related.

**A gift.** Homer seldom speaks of θάρσος without saying who gave it. Book 5 opens with it:

{{quote:55bc383e-6419-5b33-8227-16c2972361f7}}

Athena gives Telemachus the same pair, μένος καὶ θάρσος, when she leaves him after her first visit (*Odyssey* 1.320–321), and she puts θάρσος in Nausicaa's heart and takes the fear from her limbs, so that she alone stands her ground before the naked Odysseus (*Odyssey* 6.139–140). She gives Menelaus the daring of a fly, which keeps coming back to bite however often it is brushed away (17.569–570), and Apollo puts it into Agenor so that he will face Achilles (21.547). The Cretan of Odysseus' lying tale says that Ares and Athena gave him θάρσος (*Odyssey* 14.216). After his prayer, Athena's first word to Diomedes is the verb: θαρσῶν νῦν, “Take heart now, Diomedes, and fight against the Trojans” (5.124).

**Bold warriors.** When Diomedes sees Ares beside Hector, he explains Hector's reputation by the god: “how we used to marvel at brilliant Hector for being a spearman and a bold warrior; but always at his side is one of the gods” (5.601–603). The line that praises Hector here returns twice as a demand. The dying Sarpedon tells Glaucus that now he must be “a spearman and a bold warrior” (16.492–493), and Achilles says the same to Hector just before he kills him (22.268–269).

The daring can be a god's own. In the battle of the gods Ares calls Athena a “dog-fly” who drives the gods to strife with unbounded θάρσος, and reminds her of the day she set Diomedes on to wound him (21.394–399).

## Related words

- [[menos|μένος]] (“might”), with which it is given (5.2).
- [[alke|ἀλκή]] (“courage”), the strength that holds an enemy off.
- [[deos|δέος]] (“fear”), its opposite.
- θρασυμέμνων (“bold in endurance”) and θρασυκάρδιος (“bold-hearted”), compounds of θρασύς.

## Outside Homer

Aristotle makes courage the mean between excess and deficiency in fear and in confidence (θάρρος, the Attic form of θάρσος); the man who exceeds in confidence is rash, θρασύς (*Nicomachean Ethics* 2.7, 1107a33–b4).
