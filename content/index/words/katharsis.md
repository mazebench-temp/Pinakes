## Meaning

κάθαρσις is the noun of καθαίρω, “cleanse”, from καθαρός, “clean, pure”. It has three main uses in classical Greek: the purification of a person or place from pollution by rite (see [[purification]]), the purging of the body by a physician, and, in philosophy, the cleansing of the soul. Homer has the verb: the Achaeans “cleansed themselves” of the plague and threw the defilement into the sea (1.313–314; see [[plague-and-purification]]).

## After Aristotle

In the *Politics* Aristotle says that music is to be used for education, for amusement, and for catharsis, and that people overcome by pity, fear or religious ecstasy are restored by sacred songs “as if they had found healing and catharsis”; he adds that he speaks of catharsis there without explanation, and will speak more clearly in the work on poetry (8.7, 1341b32–1342a16). That explanation, if it was written, is lost with the second book of the *Poetics* (see [[the-lost-book-on-comedy]]).

Interpretations fall into families. Lessing took it as the purification of the passions, their conversion into virtuous habits (*Hamburgische Dramaturgie*, 1767–1768, nos. 77–78). Jacob Bernays read it, after the passage in the *Politics*, as a medical purgation, the pleasurable discharge of an excess of emotion (1857). Others have taken the cleansing to apply to the incidents of the play rather than the audience, or to mean an intellectual clarification of what is pitiable and fearful (Leon Golden, 1962). The translation keeps the Greek word.
