## Meaning

ὀργή first meant a disposition or temper, and in Attic prose it became the common word for anger; ὀργίζεσθαι is “to be angry”, ὀργίλος “irascible”. Homer does not use it: his anger is χόλος, the gall that rises, μῆνις, the wrath of gods and of Achilles, and κότος, the grudge that is kept (see [[cholos]], [[menis]] and [[kotos]]). θυμός, which in Homer is the spirit in the chest, is in Aristotle's Greek “temper”, the seat and the heat of anger (see [[thymos]]).

## Anger in Aristotle

The *Rhetoric* defines anger by its cause and its aim: a slight, and revenge (see [[oligoria]]). It is always against a particular person, and it is attended by pleasure, the hope of revenge, which is why Homer could call it “far sweeter than dripping honey” (see [[sweeter-than-dripping-honey]]). The *Nicomachean Ethics* names the mean in anger gentleness, πραότης, and its excess irascibility (4.5, 1125b26–1126b10; see [[praotes]]). See [[aristotle-anger]] and [[pathos]].
