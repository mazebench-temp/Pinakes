## Meaning

μῦθος in Homer is not “myth” in the modern sense. It is speech that has weight: a command, a threat, a piece of advice, a formal statement made in public. Richard Martin (*The Language of Heroes*, 1989) argued that μῦθος is a speech-act of authority, where [[epos|ἔπος]] is simply an utterance. The verb μυθέομαι means “speak, tell, explain”. The etymology is unknown.

## Related words

- [[epos|ἔπος]] (“word”).
- [[peitho|πείθω]] (“persuade, obey”): the answer a μῦθος expects.
- [[semaino|σημαίνω]] (“give orders”).

## Outside Homer

In Plato μῦθος comes to mean a story as opposed to a reasoned account (λόγος). Socrates introduces the myth of judgment at the end of the *Gorgias* as “a very fine λόγος, which you will think a μῦθος” (523a). English *myth* descends from this later sense. Aristotle's *Poetics* makes the word technical: a μῦθος is the plot of a tragedy or an epic, “the arrangement of the incidents” (1450a4–5), and the traditional stories it draws on are οἱ παραδεδομένοι μῦθοι (1451b24).
