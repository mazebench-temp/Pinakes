## Meaning

θυμός is the commonest Homeric word for the inner life. It sits in the chest, and it rises, swells, rejoices, grieves, desires and decides. It is the seat of anger (1.217, 1.429) and of love (1.196), of pleasure (1.24, 1.136) and of daring (1.228). It urges a man on (1.173) and can be held back (1.192). It is also life itself, which a man has and can lose (1.205, 1.593). The translation renders it “heart” most often, and “spirit” or “life” where the sense requires.

The word is usually connected with Latin *fumus* and Sanskrit *dhūmá-*, “smoke”, and with the verb θύω, “rush, storm”: the θυμός is something that moves, heats and surges. Achilles uses θύω of Agamemnon, who “rages with a ruinous mind” (1.342).

A man can speak to his θυμός as to a second self, and he ponders “in his mind and in his θυμός” (1.193). Bruno Snell (*The Discovery of the Mind*, 1946; English 1953) argued that Homer has no unified idea of the soul and spreads mental life among θυμός, [[phrenes|φρένες]], [[noos|νόος]] and [[psyche|ψυχή]]. Others, among them Bernard Williams (*Shame and Necessity*, 1993), answered that the Homeric person is a whole agent who describes his deliberations in these terms.

## Related words

- [[phrenes|φρένες]] (“mind”), [[ker-heart|κῆρ]] and [[etor|ἦτορ]] (“heart”), [[kradie|κραδίη]] (“heart”), [[stethos|στῆθος]] (“chest”).
- [[psyche|ψυχή]] (“soul”): the life that goes to Hades.
- [[menos|μένος]] (“fury”) and [[cholos|χόλος]] (“anger”).
- μεγάθυμος (“great-hearted”): a standing epithet of the Achaeans (1.123, 1.135).

## Outside Homer

Plato names the spirited part of the soul after it (τὸ θυμοειδές). To show that reason can rebuke it, he quotes Odysseus striking his chest and scolding his heart (*Odyssey* 20.17–18, in *Republic* 4.441b). Aristotle uses θυμός of anger itself, and quotes Iliad 18.109 about it (*Rhetoric* 2.2).
