## Meaning

ἀλκή comes from the root of ἀλέξω and ἀλαλκεῖν, “ward off, defend”. It is strength turned against an enemy: the power to hold him off, and the courage to stand and fight back. The translation renders it “courage” and keeps “strength” for βίη; Hector says that Paris has neither: “there is no strength (βίη) in his heart, and no courage (ἀλκή)” (3.45). The name of the man he is rebuking, Ἀλέξανδρος, means “defender of men” and comes from the same root.

ἀλκή is something a fighter has in him and can lose hold of. The formulas speak of it as a thing to be remembered, let go or put on. “Be men, friends, and remember your furious courage” (ἀνέρες ἔστε, φίλοι, μνήσασθε δὲ θούριδος ἀλκῆς) is one of the poem's standing calls to rally (for example 6.112, 15.487; see [[furious]]). Agamemnon tells the Argives not to let go of their furious courage (4.234). The two Ajaxes are “clothed in furious courage” (θοῦριν ἐπιειμένοι ἀλκήν, 7.164), as Agamemnon, in Achilles' insult, is “clothed in shamelessness” (1.149).

Its opposite is ἄναλκις, “without courage, weak”. Odysseus tells a commoner who runs for the ships, “You are unwarlike and weak” (ἀπτόλεμος καὶ ἄναλκις, 2.201), and Diomedes reminds Agamemnon in Book 9 that he was the first to call him “unwarlike and a coward” before the Danaans (9.34–36). Among animals the boar has ἀλκή and the deer has none. Idomeneus is “like a boar in courage” (4.253); the Argives who hang back stand like fawns with no courage in their hearts (4.243–245), and the Trojans once fled like deer, “without courage” (ἀνάλκιδες, 13.101–104).

## Related words

- βίη (“strength, force”) and ἠνορέη (“manhood”, 4.303).
- [[charme|χάρμη]] (“joy of battle”) and [[menos|μένος]] (“fury”).
- [[deos|δέος]] (“fear”): what ἀλκή overcomes.
- ἄλκιμος (“brave, strong”), said of men and of spears (see [[strong-spear]]).
- Ἀλαλκομενηΐς, Athena's epithet (4.8), which some ancient readers explained from ἀλαλκεῖν, “ward off” (see [[of-alalcomenae]]).
