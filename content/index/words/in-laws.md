## Meaning

Homeric Greek has exact words for the relatives a woman gains by marriage, and they are among the oldest words in the language:

- ἑκυρός, the husband's father, and ἑκυρή, his mother: Sanskrit *śváśura-*, Latin *socer*, Old English *swēor*.
- δαήρ, the husband's brother: Sanskrit *devár-*, Latin *lēvir*, Old English *tācor*.
- γάλως, the husband's sister: Latin *glōs*, Old Church Slavonic *zъlъva*.
- εἰνάτερες, the wives of the husband's brothers: Sanskrit *yātaras*, Latin *ianitrīcēs*.
- νυός, the son's wife, the daughter-in-law as her husband's family call her: Sanskrit *snuṣā́*, Latin *nurus*, Old English *snoru*.

πηός is a looser word, a kinsman by marriage (3.163). νύμφη is a bride or young wife, and νύμφα φίλη, “dear bride”, is how a young married woman is addressed in her husband's house (3.130; *Odyssey* 4.743).

The old words all look out from the wife's side: her husband's father, mother, brother and sister, and his brothers' wives. Émile Benveniste explained the pattern by the custom that the bride went to live in her husband's family, so that it was her relation to them that needed names (*Le vocabulaire des institutions indo-européennes*, 1969). Priam's palace, with chambers for his fifty sons and their wives and for his daughters and their husbands, is the Iliad's picture of such a household (6.242–250).

In the Iliad the words belong above all to Helen, the one wife who has two families by marriage. She calls Hector δαήρ in Book 6 (6.344, 6.355), and in her lament over his body she names all her Trojan in-laws. Whenever one of her husband's brothers, his sisters, his brothers' wives or his mother reproached her, Hector checked them with gentle words, “but my father-in-law was always gentle, like a father” (24.768–772). Andromache has hers too: looking for her, Hector asks whether she has gone to his sisters or his brothers' wives (γαλόων ἢ εἰνατέρων, 6.378, 6.383).

## Related words

- [[posis|πόσις]] (“husband”) and [[marriage]].
- γνωτοί and κασίγνητοι (“kin, brothers”): blood relatives, as against kin by marriage (3.174, 3.238).
- γαμβρός (a daughter's or sister's husband) and πενθερός (a wife's father): the words for relations made through a wife.
