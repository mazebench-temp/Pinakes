## Meaning

εἴδωλον is formed from εἶδος (“form, looks”; see [[eidos]]), from the root of ἰδεῖν, “see”: it is something seen, a likeness. In Homer it is a likeness without the person. It looks like someone, and it may speak, but it cannot be held.

**A phantom made by a god.** Apollo carries the wounded Aeneas out of the battle to his temple on Pergamus, where Leto and Artemis heal him (5.445–448). On the field he leaves a double:

{{quote:3c375408-a55f-5099-90af-3cb498be380f}}

The phantom is like Aeneas and armed like him, and the two armies fight around it as they fight around a fallen man. The real Aeneas comes back healed a few scenes later, and his companions rejoice to see him alive and unhurt, but ask him nothing (5.512–516). The phantom is not mentioned again. See [[the-phantom-of-aeneas]], [[aeneas]] and [[apollo]].

The Odyssey has one more phantom made by a god. Athena makes an εἴδωλον like Penelope's sister Iphthime and sends it to Penelope in her sleep to comfort her (*Odyssey* 4.795–841). When Penelope asks it whether Odysseus is alive, it will not say, since “it is a bad thing to talk empty words” (*Odyssey* 4.836–837; see [[anemolios]]).

**The dead.** In Hades the dead are εἴδωλα. The ψυχή of Patroclus comes to Achilles in his sleep, like him in everything, in stature, eyes and voice, and tells him that “the souls, phantoms of the worn-out, keep me far off” (ψυχαὶ εἴδωλα καμόντων, 23.65–72). When Achilles reaches for it, it goes under the earth like smoke, and he cries out that there is in the house of Hades “a ψυχή and a phantom, but no wits in it at all” (23.99–104; see [[psyche]]). In the Odyssey the dead are “phantoms of mortals who have worn out” (βροτῶν εἴδωλα καμόντων, *Odyssey* 11.476, 24.14). Three times Odysseus tries to embrace his mother, and three times she flies from his hands “like a shadow or a dream”; he asks whether Persephone has sent him a phantom (*Odyssey* 11.204–214). Heracles is divided: Odysseus sees his εἴδωλον in Hades, “but he himself is among the immortal gods”, married to Hebe (*Odyssey* 11.601–604). In the seer Theoclymenus' vision of the suitors' death the porch and the courtyard are full of phantoms going down into the dark (*Odyssey* 20.355–357).

The phantom of Aeneas and the phantoms of the dead are one idea. Each is a visible likeness, and there is no one inside it.

## Related words

- [[eidos|εἶδος]] (“looks”) and εἴδομαι (“look like”), the verb of gods who take a mortal's likeness.
- [[psyche|ψυχή]] (“soul”), which in Hades is an εἴδωλον.
- [[onar|ὄναρ]] (“dream”): Iphthime's phantom comes in a dream, and the dead flit “like a shadow or a dream”.

## Outside Homer

The Iliad's phantom was the model for later ones. In his palinode Stesichorus said that Helen never went to Troy, and Plato, retelling it, says that the Greeks and Trojans fought over her εἴδωλον “in ignorance of the truth” (*Republic* 9.586c; see [[stesichorus-palinode]]). In Euripides' *Helen* (412 BC), Hera, angry at losing the judgment of Paris, gives Paris a breathing phantom of Helen made from the sky (*Helen* 31–36; see [[helen-in-egypt]]). Virgil gives Juno a phantom of Aeneas, made of hollow cloud and armed like him, to draw Turnus out of the battle, and compares it to the shapes of the dead and to dreams (*Aeneid* 10.636–642).
