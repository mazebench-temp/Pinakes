## Meaning

κλέος (“report, fame”) is the noun of κλύω, “hear”: it is what is heard. Its root is one of the best attested in the Indo-European languages. Sanskrit *śrávas* means “fame” and is the same word; the adjective κλυτός, “famous”, matches Sanskrit *śrutá-*, Latin *inclutus*, and English *loud*. The imperative κλῦθι, “hear!”, which opens Chryses' prayer (1.37), and κλῦτε, “hear me” (2.56), come from the same verb.

Homer uses the word in two senses that shade into each other.

**Report, news.** κλέος is what reaches a person who was not there. Telemachus sails to Pylos and Sparta after “the wide report of my father” (*Odyssey* 3.83). In the invocation before the Catalogue of Ships the poet says that mortals “hear only the report, and know nothing”, while the Muses were present and know all things (2.485–486).

**Fame.** κλέος is also what is said of a man, above all after his death, and it lasts as long as it is told. Achilles' mother told him of two fates: a long life at home without fame, or a short one at Troy, where his fame, κλέος ἄφθιτον, “will be imperishable” (9.410–416). When Achilles plays the lyre in his hut he sings κλέα ἀνδρῶν, “the famous deeds of men” (9.189), the matter of epic itself, which the singer Demodocus also sings (*Odyssey* 8.73). Hector imagines a passer-by, years later, pointing out the tomb of a man he killed: “So someone will say one day; and my fame will never perish” (7.87–91). Fame depends on speech and song. It is what others will say, and the heroes act with those future speakers in mind.

The phrase κλέος ἄφθιτον corresponds word for word to the Vedic *śrávas … ákṣitam*, “imperishable fame”. Adalbert Kuhn noticed the match in 1853, and it became the classic example of a poetic phrase inherited from Indo-European. Margalit Finkelberg questioned whether the Homeric phrase is an old formula at all (*Classical Quarterly* 36, 1986), and the question is still debated.

## Related words

- κλυτός, κλειτός (“famous”), ἀγακλειτός (“very famous”), and names made with -κλῆς and -κλος: Ἡρακλέης (“glory of Hera”), Πάτροκλος (“glory of the fathers”). δυσκλεής (“ill-famed”) is its negative.
- [[kydos|κῦδος]] (“glory”): the glory of a success, often granted by a god in the moment; κλέος is what is said of it afterwards.
- [[time|τιμή]] (“honor”): worth recognized by those present; κλέος carries it to those absent and not yet born.
- [[aischros|αἰσχρός]] (“shameful”): the reverse of fame, a thing “a shameful thing, even for men to come to hear of” (2.119).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod gives the singer, “servant of the Muses”, the task of celebrating “the famous deeds (κλέα) of men of old” (*Theogony* 99–101). One of the nine Muses in his list is Κλειώ, Clio, whose name is made from the same word (*Theogony* 77).
