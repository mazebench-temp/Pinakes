## Meaning

κατηφείη is the dejection of one who hangs his head, and so the shame of a failure that others can see. It is less the inner sense of shame than its outward look: the adjective κατηφής is “downcast”, and the verb κατηφέω “be downcast”.

In the Iliad the noun is spoken three times, each time of a shame that will come to someone because of another. Hector tells Paris, who has shrunk back from Menelaus, that he is “a great grief to your father and your city and all the people, / a joy to your enemies, and a shame to yourself” (3.50–51; see [[paris]]). Sarpedon, dying, tells Glaucus, “For I will be a shame and a reproach to you hereafter, / all your days without end, if the Achaeans / strip me of my armor” (16.498–500; see [[oneidos]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]), and Athena, in the likeness of Phoenix, tells Menelaus that it will be “a shame and a reproach” to him if dogs tear the body of Patroclus under the wall of Troy (17.556–558). The verb marks the moment of a defeat. Hector stands downcast (κατηφήσας) when his spear has glanced from Achilles' shield and he has no other (22.290–293), and Priam, setting out for Achilles' hut, calls his surviving sons κατηφόνες, “disgraces”, and wishes they had all died at the ships instead of Hector (24.248–254).

The Odyssey uses the verb of the suitors, who are grieved and downcast when they learn that Telemachus has come home safe (*Odyssey* 16.342), and of their kin: Eupeithes urges the Ithacans to avenge their sons, or they will be downcast forever (κατηφέες ἐσσόμεθʼ αἰεί, *Odyssey* 24.430–435).
