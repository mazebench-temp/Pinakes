## Meaning

εὔχομαι is a verb of solemn speech: to declare something aloud and stake oneself on it. Addressed to a god, it is a prayer or a vow. Chryses “prayed” to Apollo (1.450), and Pandarus is told to “pray” to Apollo that he will offer a hecatomb of firstborn lambs (4.101–102). Addressed to men, it is a claim, of descent, of rank or of prowess, and because a claim can be proud, it often means “boast”. Glaucus ends his genealogy, “This is the lineage and the blood I claim” (6.211), and Diomedes claims to be the son of “a good father”, Tydeus (14.113–114). The noun εὐχωλή follows the verb: a vow owed to a god (1.65, 1.93), a thing to boast of (2.160), or the cry of a man who has killed (4.450). The word is related to Latin *voveo*, “vow”.

Whether one meaning lies under all these has been much discussed. Émile Benveniste saw in the verb a solemn commitment of the speaker (*Le vocabulaire des institutions indo-européennes*, 1969). Leonard Muellner studied its formulas and argued that it names a single kind of speech, a solemn and authoritative statement, whether it is made to a god or to men (*The Meaning of Homeric ΕΥΧΟΜΑΙ through its Formulas*, 1976).

The claims of the Iliad are claims to worth. Achilles says that Agamemnon “claims to be far the best of the Achaeans” (1.91), and Nestor says the same (2.82; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). Thamyris claimed he would win even if the Muses themselves sang against him, and they maimed him (2.594–600). Helen reminds Paris that he used to boast he was better than Menelaus (3.430). A claim is judged by what follows it.
