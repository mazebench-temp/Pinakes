## Meaning

ἀναιδείη (Attic ἀναίδεια) is the absence of [[aidos|αἰδώς]]: the refusal to be checked by anyone's claim, human or divine. The adjective ἀναιδής means “shameless”. It can be said even of a thing that will not yield. The stone of Sisyphus is λᾶας ἀναιδής, the “shameless stone” that keeps rolling back (*Odyssey* 11.598).

## In Book 1

{{quote:4d796167-872c-54e1-9c9e-a6d30d6eae32}}

When Agamemnon threatens to take his prize, Achilles answers with an insult. The king wears shamelessness like a garment (ἐπιειμένε), and his mind is set on gain. Achilles uses the same image of him again in Book 9 (9.372).

{{quote:9c645fc6-12c6-5d1d-bd5b-2909ef4fd1ce}}

A few lines later he calls him “utterly shameless” and “dog-face” (κυνῶπα). The two insults belong together: in Homer the dog is the image of shamelessness. The charge fits the opening scene, where Agamemnon alone refused the respect that the whole army wished to pay the priest (1.22–25).
