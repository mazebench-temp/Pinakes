## Meaning

δῶρον is a gift, from the same root as δίδωμι, “give”. In Homer gifts are never simply free. They create obligations, repair injuries and honor the receiver. A prize from the spoils, a ransom, an offering to a god and a compensation for an insult can all be called δῶρα.

## In Book 1

{{quote:0d7d40ff-20aa-51e8-84e9-216e3910c4cc}}

Athena restrains Achilles with a promise of compensation. Someday three times as many splendid gifts will be laid before him because of this outrage (see [[triple-gifts-foretold]]). The promise is kept in the offer of Book 9 (9.121–157), which Achilles refuses, “hateful to me are his gifts” (9.378), and in the gifts he finally receives in Book 19.

{{quote:c1c3c45e-e251-5961-95aa-32a8015d3a2a}}

Achilles calls the prize a gift. It is “far better”, he sneers, to strip the gifts from any man who speaks against you than to arm for battle. A γέρας is the army's gift, and taking it back is a public insult.

{{quote:2d5c10cf-57ae-5e9b-a249-ccd039c264f8}}

In Achilles' retelling, the Achaeans are sending Chryseis home and bringing gifts to the lord Apollo. The hecatomb is a gift to a god.

## Outside Homer

Plato would not let poets sing “gifts persuade gods, gifts persuade revered kings”, nor say that Achilles took gifts from Agamemnon (*Republic* 3.390e). Euripides' Medea cites the same proverb: gifts persuade even the gods (*Medea* 964).
