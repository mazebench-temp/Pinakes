## Meaning

φίλος means “dear, beloved”, and also, in many phrases, simply “one's own”: φίλον κῆρ and φίλον ἦτορ are “his own heart”. Whether the possessive sense or the affective sense came first is debated. Émile Benveniste tied the word to the institution of hospitality, the bond between host and guest. The verb φιλέω means “love, treat as dear”, and also “entertain as a guest”.

What is φίλος to someone is what he likes: “it is dear to you” means “you enjoy it”.

## In Book 1

The word gathers the book's family bonds. Chryses asks for his “dear child” (1.20) and receives her back in the end (1.447). Calchas says she must be given back to “her dear father” (1.98), and Odysseus places her in her dear father's arms (1.441). Achilles prays to his “dear mother” (1.351); Hephaestus speaks to his (1.572, 1.585). Patroclus obeys his “dear companion” (1.345).

{{quote:783aa6c9-3467-593d-a22f-672ebe743ef4}}

Calchas addresses Achilles as “dear to Zeus”, and Achilles swears by Apollo, “dear to Zeus” (1.86). Apollo answered Chryses' prayer because the priest was very dear to him (1.381).

{{quote:159f2613-aa27-530f-874c-4e1e9c1da24b}}

The phrase “always dear to you” becomes an accusation. Agamemnon tells Calchas that evil prophecy is always dear to his heart (1.107), and Achilles that strife is always dear to him. Hera tells Zeus that it is always dear to him to decide things in secret (1.541). Zeus answers that if it is so, it must be his pleasure (1.564).

The possessive sense appears when Achilles, withdrawn, wastes away “his own heart” (φίλον κῆρ, 1.491). His prize was “something small but dear” (ὀλίγον τε φίλον τε, 1.167). And the compound φιλοκτεανώτατε, “most covetous”, literally “fondest of possessions”, is his insult for Agamemnon (1.122).

Hera sent Athena “loving both men alike in her heart” (φιλέουσα, 1.196, 1.209).
