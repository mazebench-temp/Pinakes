## Meaning

ἐλεύθερος, “free”, is the old word for a member of the free people as against the slave. It is usually compared with Latin *liber*, “free”, and *līberī*, “children”, the free-born of a household, and with German *Leute*, “people”.

In Homer it is never said of a person. The Iliad has it four times, the Odyssey not at all, and always in a fixed phrase.

**The day of freedom.** ἐλεύθερον ἦμαρ, “the day of freedom”, is the life of a free people, pictured as a day, and each time it is something taken away. Hector foresees the day when an Achaean “leads you away in tears, taking away your day of freedom” (ἐλεύθερον ἦμαρ ἀπούρας, 6.455). Over the dying Patroclus he says that Patroclus had meant to sack Troy and carry off the Trojan women to his ships, “taking away their day of freedom” (16.830–832). Achilles reminds Aeneas that he sacked Lyrnessus and led away its women, “taking away their day of freedom” (20.191–194). The formula belongs to the women of a captured city. Its opposite is δούλιον ἦμαρ, “the day of slavery”, which Hector names eight lines later (6.463; see [[doulios]]).

Homer names other conditions by their day: αἴσιμον ἦμαρ, “the fated day”, which sinks in Zeus' scales (8.72); νηλεὲς ἦμαρ, “the pitiless day” of death (11.484 and often); ὀλέθριον ἦμαρ, “the day of destruction” (19.294); ἦμαρ ὀρφανικόν, “the day of orphanhood”, which leaves a child without friends (22.490); and in the Odyssey νόστιμον ἦμαρ, “the day of return” (*Odyssey* 1.9). A day in these phrases is a whole state of life, and one day can end it.

**The mixing bowl of freedom.** The last words of Book 6 are Hector's to Paris. “We will make these things right later, if ever Zeus grants us, for the everlasting gods of heaven, to set up the mixing bowl of freedom in our halls, when we have driven the well-greaved Achaeans out of Troy” (6.526–529). The phrase comes only here. The κρητήρ is the bowl in which wine was mixed for a feast and for libations to the gods, and the bowl of freedom is set up “for the gods”, as thanks for deliverance. Hector's two uses of the word frame the end of the book: the day of freedom that Andromache will lose (6.455), and the freedom the Trojans may yet celebrate (6.528). He is not granted it.
