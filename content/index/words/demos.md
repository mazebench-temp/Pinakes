## Meaning

δῆμος is at once a land and the people who live on it. It can name a territory: Athens is “the land of great-hearted Erechtheus” (δῆμον Ἐρεχθῆος μεγαλήτορος, 2.547). It can name the community as a whole: Hector tells Paris that he is a great bane “to your father, to the city and to all the people” (πόληΐ τε παντί τε δήμῳ, 3.50). And it can name the ordinary people as distinct from their leaders. A δήμου ἀνήρ, “a man of the people”, is set against “a king or a man of note” (2.188, 2.198). Polydamas complains to Hector that it is not thought fitting for one who is of the people (δῆμον ἐόντα) to speak against him, “neither in council nor ever in war” (12.213–214). The old men who sit with Priam at the Scaean gates are δημογέροντες, “elders of the people” (3.149).

The word is usually derived from the root of δαίομαι, “divide”. A δῆμος would then have been first a portion of land. In the Linear B tablets from Pylos the *da-mo* is a community that holds and allots land. One tablet records that the *da-mo* disputed a priestess's claim about the terms on which she held her plot.

δῆμος and [[laos|λαός]] overlap, but they are not the same. λαός is the people as followers, above all the army of a leader, and a king is a “shepherd of the people” (ποιμὴν λαῶν). δῆμος is the people of a place. Achilles' insult δημοβόρος βασιλεύς, “people-devouring king” (1.231), accuses Agamemnon of consuming what belongs to the community (see [[people-devouring-king]]).

## Related words

- [[laos|λαός]] (“people, army”).
- [[basileus|βασιλεύς]] (“king”): the other side of the δήμου ἀνήρ.
- πληθύς (“multitude, crowd”): the mass of the army (2.143, 2.278, 2.488; see [[commoners]]).
- [[polis|πόλις]] (“city”): the town, where δῆμος is the land and its people.
- ἐπιδήμιος (“among the people”): Nestor condemns the man who loves “war among his own people” (πολέμου … ἐπιδημίου, 9.64).

## Outside Homer

In classical Athens δῆμος named both the sovereign people, whose rule was δημοκρατία, and the local districts of Attica, the demes, into which Cleisthenes organized the citizens (Herodotus 5.69; [Aristotle], *Constitution of the Athenians* 21). Cleisthenes had the citizens called by their deme instead of their father's name, so that no one could tell the new citizens from the old (*Constitution of the Athenians* 21.4).
