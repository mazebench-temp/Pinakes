## Meaning

αἰδώς is the feeling that holds a person back before someone whose regard matters. It is respect, reverence and shame at once: respect for gods and their priests, for elders, guests, suppliants and kings; shame at the thought of what others will say. The verb αἰδέομαι means “respect” or “feel shame before”. Its opposite is [[anaideia|ἀναιδείη]] (“shamelessness”).

ἅζομαι (“stand in awe of, revere”) is a different verb, related to ἅγιος (“holy”) and to Sanskrit *yaj-* (“worship”). It is used above all of reverence for gods.

αἰδώς is the check that keeps the pursuit of honor from becoming a war of all against all. Douglas Cairns (*Aidōs*, 1993) argued that it is bound up with [[time|τιμή]]: to feel αἰδώς is to recognize another's honor, and to fail to feel it is itself an insult.

## In Book 1

{{quote:261bedb7-7947-5c18-ac20-da87ced93053}}

Chryses asks the Achaeans to free his daughter “in reverence for” (ἁζόμενοι) Apollo. The army agrees “to respect the priest” (αἰδεῖσθαι ἱερῆα). Agamemnon's refusal is a refusal of αἰδώς toward god and priest, and it brings the plague. Achilles names the fault when he calls Agamemnon “clothed in shamelessness” (1.149).

{{quote:19486f9c-5bfc-5829-908a-8e1fc8cdb12e}}

When the heralds come for Briseis they stand silent, “in fear and awe of the king” (ταρβήσαντε καὶ αἰδομένω βασιλῆα). The king they respect is Achilles. He answers with courtesy: they are messengers of Zeus and of men, and not to blame (see [[messengers-of-zeus]]).

At the end of the poem the word returns to the case of a suppliant father. Apollo complains that Achilles has lost pity and has no αἰδώς (24.44–45), and Priam asks him to respect the gods (αἰδεῖο θεούς) and pity him (24.503). The respect that Agamemnon refused the old priest in Book 1, Achilles grants the old king in Book 24.

## Related words

- [[anaideia|ἀναιδείη]] (“shamelessness”).
- [[time|τιμή]] (“honor”) and [[hybris|ὕβρις]] (“outrage”).
- [[supplication]]: αἰδώς is what a suppliant appeals to.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod foresees a time when Aidos and Nemesis will leave mankind and go to the gods (*Works and Days* 197–201). In Plato's *Protagoras* Zeus sends αἰδώς and justice (δίκη) to all men, so that cities can hold together (322c–d).

Socrates quotes a verse of the *Cypria*, “where there is fear, there is also αἰδώς”, and corrects it: where there is αἰδώς there is fear, but not always the reverse (Plato, *Euthyphro* 12a–c). The heralds of 1.331 have both.
