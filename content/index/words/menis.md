## Meaning

μῆνις (“wrath”) is the first word of the Iliad and the name of its subject. It is not a passing fit of temper. It is an anger that lasts, that its owner holds to be just, and whose effects fall on a whole people.

In the Iliad the noun belongs to gods and to [[Achilles]] alone. Athena leads Ares out of the fighting so that they may avoid the μῆνις of Zeus (5.34). Diomedes and Patroclus both give ground before the μῆνις of Apollo (5.444, 16.711). The one mortal who has a μῆνις is Achilles. His begins in Book 1 and lasts until Book 19, when Thetis tells him to renounce it and the army rejoices that he has (19.35, 19.75).

Homer has several words for anger, and they are not interchangeable. [[cholos|χόλος]] (“anger”) is the everyday word: it rises in the chest and can be swallowed. [[kotos|κότος]] (“grudge”) is anger stored up for later. μῆνις is larger than either. It is the anger of a being whose [[time|τιμή]] (“honor”) has been violated, and it works like a sanction: the offender's whole community suffers until the honor is restored. Leonard Muellner (*The Anger of Achilles: Mênis in Greek Epic*, 1996) argued that the word names a sanction against acts that threaten the order of the world. That is why it belongs to gods, and why a mortal who has it stands on their level.

The verb μηνίω means “keep up one's wrath” or “nurse one's wrath”. Book 1 uses it of Achilles twice and of [[Agamemnon]] once. The poem never gives Agamemnon the noun.

The etymology is disputed. The word has been connected with [[menos|μένος]] (“fury”), and Calvert Watkins derived it from the root of μιμνῄσκω (“remember”), which would make μῆνις an anger that does not forget. Neither view is certain.

## Related words

- [[cholos|χόλος]] (“anger”) and χώομαι (“be angry”): the ordinary words, used of Apollo and Achilles alike.
- [[kotos|κότος]] (“grudge”): anger kept for later.
- [[time|τιμή]] (“honor”): what μῆνις defends.
- [[ate|ἄτη]] (“blind folly”): what Achilles hopes his wrath will force Agamemnon to recognize (1.412).
- [[menos|μένος]] (“fury”): a possible relative.

## Outside Homer

The sophist Protagoras found fault with the first line. Aristotle reports that he objected to the imperative ἄειδε: the poet thought he was praying and instead gave the goddess an order (*Poetics* 19, 1456b15–18). Protagoras also held that μῆνις ought to be masculine, so that Homer's feminine οὐλομένην was a solecism (Aristotle, *Sophistical Refutations* 14, 173b17–22).

Looking for what men of great pride have in common, Aristotle names Alcibiades, Achilles and Ajax. None of them would tolerate insult: one went to war, one fell into wrath (ἐμήνισεν), one killed himself (*Posterior Analytics* 2.13, 97b15–25).
