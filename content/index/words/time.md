## Meaning

τιμή (“honor”) is the worth a person has in the eyes of others, and in Homer it is concrete. It is measured in things: a prize from the spoils, the best cuts at a feast, a seat of honor, land. It is measured in deference: whether others listen, give way and do what one asks. A man who is ἄτιμος (“without honor”) is diminished in fact, not only in feeling.

The noun belongs with the verb τίω (“value, honor”); τιμάω is formed from the noun. τιμή can also be the price paid to make good an injury. When Achilles says the Achaeans came to Troy “winning τιμή for Menelaus” (1.159), the translation renders it “satisfaction”: compensation exacted from the Trojans for the wrong done to Menelaus (see [[war-for-menelaus]]). Here τιμή comes close to τίνω (“pay, pay back”) and ποινή (“penalty”); how τίω and τίνω are related is debated.

Honor is apportioned. Gods have their honors, and a king has the largest share among men: Nestor says that no ordinary share of τιμή falls to a scepter-bearing king (1.278). Honor can be given, taken away and restored. In the Iliad it is usually restored at someone else's cost.

## In Book 1

The book is a chain of dishonors, each answered by suffering.

{{quote:7383d2b4-8a47-5a2d-829d-b2f524c89584}}

The narrator's explanation of the plague: Agamemnon had dishonored (ἠτίμασεν) the priest Chryses. Calchas repeats the charge (1.94). To dishonor a god's priest is to dishonor the god, and Apollo repays it on the whole army. Chryses later describes the plague the other way round, as a gift to himself: “you honored me (τίμησας) and struck the army of the Achaeans hard” (1.454).

{{quote:0096b580-5be9-5593-b60c-1174cf069218}}

In the quarrel both men speak the language of honor. Achilles will not stay at Troy ἄτιμος, piling up wealth for Agamemnon (1.171). Agamemnon answers that others will honor him, and Zeus above all (1.175). Achilles swears that the king will one day regret that he paid no honor to the best of the Achaeans (1.244), and asks Thetis for exactly that recognition (1.412).

{{quote:353696e0-9042-5bf6-8809-a5f29743cf0c}}

Alone by the sea, Achilles states the logic of the whole poem. Because his life will be short, Zeus owes him honor at least. Instead Zeus has not honored him “even a little”, for Agamemnon has dishonored him and taken his prize.

{{quote:449cde3d-ba6c-5ae9-a133-f6d9e813d45f}}

Thetis turns the complaint into a prayer, and the words of honor come five times in six lines: honor my son; he has been dishonored; honor him; until the Achaeans honor him and increase him with honor. When Zeus says nothing, she asks him to promise or refuse, so that she may know how far she is “the most dishonored goddess of all” (ἀτιμοτάτη, 1.516). Hera guesses the answer. Zeus has promised to honor Achilles and to destroy many Achaeans beside the ships (1.558–559). Honor for one man will be paid for with the lives of others.

## Related words

- [[geras|γέρας]] (“prize”): the visible token of honor.
- [[kydos|κῦδος]] (“glory”): the success that Zeus grants a king (1.279).
- [[aidos|αἰδώς]] (“respect”): the regard owed to another's honor.
- [[hybris|ὕβρις]] (“outrage”): the deliberate violation of another's honor.
- τίνω (“pay”) and ἀποτίνω (“pay back”): Chryses prays that the Danaans may “pay for” his tears (τίσειαν, 1.42); Achilles promises that the Achaeans will repay Agamemnon three and four times over (ἀποτείσομεν, 1.128).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle ties anger to a slight, says that whoever dishonors (ἀτιμάζων) slights, and quotes Achilles' complaint at 1.356, with Iliad 9.648, to show how the slighted speak (*Rhetoric* 2.2, 1378b; see [[aristotle-anger]]). In the *Nicomachean Ethics* he calls honor the greatest of external goods, the one we render to the gods (4.3, 1123b17–21).
