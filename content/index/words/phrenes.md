## Meaning

φρένες (singular φρήν) are a physical organ in the chest and the seat of thinking, understanding and feeling. Most take the organ to be the midriff or diaphragm; R. B. Onians (*The Origins of European Thought*, 1951) argued for the lungs. Things are put into the φρένες, fill them, reach them, and are dear to them. They can be sound or damaged: Agamemnon's, says Achilles, are “ruinous” (ὀλοιαί, 1.342). The gods can take a man's φρένες away (19.137).

From φρήν comes φρονέω, “think, have in mind, intend”. Calchas and Nestor both speak “with good intent” (ἐὺ φρονέων, 1.73, 1.253), and Hera accuses Zeus of pondering secret things apart from her (κρυπτάδια φρονέοντα, 1.542). The compound κερδαλεόφρων, “with mind set on gain”, is one of Achilles' insults (1.149).

## In Book 1

{{quote:407539e1-5dc0-5e69-acb2-57262a80458a}}

The physical picture is clearest here. Agamemnon's φρένες, dark all round, are filled with fury, and his eyes are like blazing fire.

The φρένες receive what the gods put into them. Hera “put it in the mind” of Achilles to call the assembly (1.55). They are the seat of intelligence: Agamemnon compares Chryseis to his wife in form, stature, mind and handiwork (1.115). They are where a man ponders (1.193), understands (1.333) and stores advice: “lay it to heart” (1.297). Grief reaches them (1.362); pleasure fills them, as when Apollo delights in the paean (1.474); fear lives there (1.555).

{{quote:12f468d0-42bb-52ff-ae76-a666a61d8a6b}}

Achilles' charge against Agamemnon is a charge about his φρένες. A man with ruinous φρένες cannot see what is coming. See [[ate|ἄτη]] and [[foresight]].
