## Meaning

The verb ὀαρίζω, “talk intimately”, and the noun ὀαριστύς are formed from ὄαρ, a rare word for a wife or companion. Sarpedon tells Hector that he should be urging the people “to stand firm and defend their wives” (ὤρεσσι, 5.486), and Achilles says that he spent bloody days “fighting with men for the sake of their wives” (ὀάρων, 9.327). The talk is that of a man and a woman. Aphrodite's embroidered band holds “love, and longing, and intimate talk, the persuasion that steals the mind even of the wise” (14.216–217; see [[philotes]] and [[himeros]]). In the Odyssey Minos of Cnossus is called the ὀαριστής of great Zeus, his familiar companion (*Odyssey* 19.179).

## In the Iliad

**Hector and Andromache.** The narrator's last word for the farewell at the Scaean gates is this verb. Paris, running down from the citadel, comes upon Hector “just as he was about to turn from the place where he had talked intimately with his wife” (ὅθι ᾗ ὀάριζε γυναικί, 6.515–516). The whole scene, with its plea, its prophecy and its laughter, is summed up as the talk of a husband and wife (see [[hector-and-andromache]]). Ancient readers named the book after it, “the meeting of Hector and Andromache” (see [[title-of-book-6]]).

**No talk with Achilles.** The word returns when Hector waits for Achilles outside the wall and weighs whether to go to him unarmed and offer terms. “There is no way now to talk with him from an oak or from a rock, as a maiden and a youth do, a maiden and a youth who talk together” (ὀαριζέμεναι, ὀαρίζετον, 22.126–128). The love-talk of the young is the image of everything that cannot now happen between the two men.

**The converse of war.** Warriors turn the word on battle itself. Idomeneus tells Meriones that a missile would strike him in the chest or the belly, never in the back, as he pressed forward “into the converse of the front-fighters” (μετὰ προμάχων ὀαριστύν, 13.291), and Hector tells his allies to face the enemy and live or die, “for this is the converse of war” (ἣ γὰρ πολέμου ὀαριστύς, 17.228). The two worlds of Book 6, the war outside the walls and the house within them, share a word (see [[the-city-and-the-household]]).
