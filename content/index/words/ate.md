## Meaning

ἄτη (“blind folly”) is a clouding of the mind that makes a man act against his own interest, and also the disaster that follows from it. In Homer it comes from outside: the gods put it into a man's [[phrenes|φρένες]], and he recognizes it only afterwards. Because the word covers both the delusion and its result, translators move between “folly”, “madness” and “ruin”. The related verb ἀάω means “blind, mislead”. The etymology is uncertain.

The fullest Homeric account is Agamemnon's, long after the quarrel. When Nestor urges him in Book 9 to make amends, he admits it at once: “I was blinded (ἀασάμην), I do not deny it” (9.116). In Book 19, before the whole army, he says he is not to blame (οὐκ αἴτιος). Zeus, Moira and the Erinys cast savage ἄτη into his mind in the assembly on the day he took Achilles' prize (19.86–89). He goes on to tell how Ate, the eldest daughter of Zeus, “who blinds all”, once deceived Zeus himself, who then threw her out of Olympus (19.91–131).

In Phoenix's story of the Prayers (Λιταί) in Book 9, Ate is strong and sound of foot (ἀρτίπος). She runs ahead of the lame Prayers and does men harm, and whoever rejects the Prayers when they come is followed by Ate (9.502–512). The story is told to Achilles, who is himself refusing a plea.

E. R. Dodds began *The Greeks and the Irrational* (1951) with this material. His first chapter, “Agamemnon's Apology”, reads ἄτη as a temporary clouding of normal consciousness that Homer's people put down to an outside power. The plea does not excuse Agamemnon from paying. He still offers the gifts.

## Related words

- [[phrenes|φρένες]] (“mind”): where ἄτη is cast.
- [[menis|μῆνις]] (“wrath”): the wrath is meant to force the recognition of ἄτη.
- [[hybris|ὕβρις]] (“outrage”): later poetry makes ὕβρις bear ἄτη as its fruit.
- ἀάω (“blind, mislead”): ἀασάμην, “I was blinded” (9.116, 19.137).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod lists Ate among the children of Eris (Strife), together with Lawlessness (Δυσνομίη) and Quarrels (*Theogony* 226–232).

In Aeschylus' *Persians* the ghost of Darius says that ὕβρις, in flower, has borne a crop of ἄτη, from which a harvest of tears is reaped (*Persians* 821–822).
