## Meaning

εἶδος is what is seen of a person: looks, form, figure. It comes from the root *weid-*, “see”, like εἶδον (“I saw”), οἶδα (“I know”, properly “I have seen”) and Latin *video*. The middle verb εἴδομαι, from the same root, means “be seen, appear”, and with a dative “look like”. Gods who come among men do so “in the likeness of” a mortal: the Dream in the likeness of Nestor (ἐεισάμενος, 2.22), Athena in the likeness of a herald (εἰδομένη, 2.280), Iris in the likeness of Priam's son Polites (2.791, 2.795). Achilles tells Agamemnon that battle “looks like” death to him (εἴδεται, 1.228).

εἶδος is often paired with φυή (“build”) and μέγεθος (“stature”). The Dream stood over Agamemnon most like Nestor, “very close to him in looks and stature and build” (2.58). The formula θυγατρῶν εἶδος ἀρίστη, “most beautiful in form of the daughters”, is used of Alcestis among the daughters of Pelias (2.715) and of Laodice among the daughters of Priam (3.124, 6.252). The adjective εὐειδής means “fair of form, lovely” (3.48).

Homer treats beauty as a gift of the gods, admires it openly, and often measures it against what it does not include. Nireus was the most handsome man who came to Ilios after Achilles, “but he was a weakling” (2.673–675). Hector tells Paris that the Achaeans took him for a champion because of his looks, “but there is no strength in his heart” (3.44–45). In the *Odyssey*, Odysseus tells a rude young Phaeacian that the gods do not give every gift to one man: one is plain in looks, but a god crowns his words with beauty; another is like the immortals in εἶδος, but has no grace in his speech (*Odyssey* 8.167–177). ## Related words

- φυή (“build”) and δέμας (“frame, body”).
- καλός (“beautiful, fine”) and κάλλος (“beauty”): Aphrodite says that Paris is “shining with beauty” (κάλλεϊ, 3.392). Its opposite is [[aischros|αἰσχρός]] (“ugly, shameful”).
- θεοειδής (“godlike”, literally “god-like in looks”), Paris' standing epithet; see [[godlike]].
- ἔοικα (“be like”) and εἴκελος (“like”), from a different root, the words of likeness and of similes.
- [[doron|δῶρον]] (“gift”): beauty as a gift of the gods.

## Outside Homer

Plato made εἶδος and its relative ἰδέα the words for the Forms, the unchanging realities that the things of sense resemble (for example *Phaedo* 102b; *Republic* 10, 596a–b). Aristotle set εἶδος, form, against ὕλη, matter, and defined the εἶδος of a thing as “what it was to be” that thing (*Metaphysics* Ζ 7, 1032b1–2).
