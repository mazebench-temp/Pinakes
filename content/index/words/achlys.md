## Meaning

ἀχλύς is a dark mist. In Homer it is nearly always a mist over the eyes, and it comes from two directions.

**From death.** “And a mist was poured over his eyes” (κατὰ δʼ ὀφθαλμῶν κέχυτʼ ἀχλύς) is said of men at the point of death. In Book 5 it is said of Sarpedon, when his companions push the spear out of his thigh: “his soul left him, and a mist was poured over his eyes” (5.696). Then “he breathed again”, and the North Wind revived him (5.697–698). The same half-line is used of a Trojan killed by Meriones (16.344) and of the suitor Eurymachus as Odysseus' arrow kills him (*Odyssey* 22.88). It comes over Hector when he sees his brother Polydorus fall (20.421): grief does to the eyes what death does. See [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]].

**From the gods.** Athena answers Diomedes' prayer by giving him strength, and something more:

{{quote:85a7e4df-a946-5a51-9baa-282144c09f8b}}

The mist that was “on them before” is the ordinary condition of mortal eyes, not a blindness sent for the occasion. Without it Diomedes can do what Athena tells him next: fight no god except Aphrodite (5.129–132). He knows Aphrodite for a feeble god (5.331), he knows that Apollo is holding his hands over Aeneas (5.433), and he recognizes Ares beside Hector in the likeness of a man (5.596–606). See [[seeing-the-gods]].

A god can also pour the mist on. Poseidon pours it over Achilles' eyes while he carries Aeneas out of the battle, then scatters it, and Achilles “saw clearly with his eyes” and marveled (20.321–344). Athena pushes a “cloud of mist” from the eyes of the Achaeans, and there is light on both sides of the battle (15.668–670). In the Odyssey she pours a mist about Odysseus so that the Phaeacians do not see him walk through their town (*Odyssey* 7.39–42), and the seer Theoclymenus sees the sun perish from the sky and an evil mist run over the house of the suitors (*Odyssey* 20.356–357).

## Related words

- ἀήρ (“mist, haze”): the mist around Ares' horses (5.356), the mist Hera pours around her own (5.776), and the murky air in which Ares goes up to the sky (5.864).
- νεφέλη (“cloud”): the cloud around the shoulders of the god beside Diomedes (5.186), and the dark cloud in which Apollo hides Aeneas (5.345).
- σκότος (“darkness”) and νύξ (“night”), which cover the eyes of the dying (5.47, 5.310, 5.659; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]).
- [[eidos|εἶδος]] (“looks”) and [[eidolon|εἴδωλον]] (“phantom”).

## Outside Homer

On the shield of Heracles, in the poem ascribed to Hesiod, Achlys is a figure among the powers of death over the battle, pale, parched and shrunk with hunger ([Hesiod], *Shield* 264–270; see [[hesiod]]). The dialogue *Alcibiades II*, handed down among Plato's works, makes Athena's gift a figure for teaching: as she took the mist from Diomedes' eyes “so that he might know god and man”, whoever would advise Alcibiades must first take the mist from his soul (*Alcibiades II* 150d–e).
