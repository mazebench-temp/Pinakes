## Meaning

ἀεικίζω is the verb of ἀεικής, “unseemly, shameful”, the negative of what is fitting (εἰκός): to treat someone as it is not fitting to treat him. In the Iliad it is always done, or feared, to a dead man's body. Its one use in the Odyssey is of a living man, in Penelope's reproach to Telemachus for letting the stranger be so mistreated in the hall (*Odyssey* 18.221–222). The noun ἀεικείη, “defilement” (24.19), and the adverb ἀϊκῶς, “shamefully” (22.336, only here), belong to it; ἀεικέλιος is the longer form of the adjective (“unworthy”, 14.84; see [[aekelios]]).

## In the Iliad

The word follows the fight for the bodies of the dead. Glaucus calls on the Trojans to stand over Sarpedon, “lest they take away his armor and defile his body” (16.545), and Patroclus gives the Achaeans the opposite wish: “if only we might seize him and defile him” (16.559; see [[the-death-of-sarpedon]]). Achilles fears that the flies will breed worms in the wounds of Patroclus “and defile the body” (19.24–27; see the translation's note on 19.24–27).

Book 22 brings the word to Hector. Before the duel he offers the terms of Book 7: “for I will not defile you terribly, if Zeus grants me / to outlast you”, if Achilles will do the same (22.256–259). Achilles refuses any agreement (22.261–272; see [[no-oaths-between-lions-and-men]]) and promises that the dogs and the birds “will tear shamefully” the body (ἀϊκῶς, 22.335–336). When Hector is dead he “devised shameful deeds against brilliant Hector” (ἀεικέα … ἔργα, 22.395), and the narrator ends the dragging with Hector's own verb: Zeus “gave him to his enemies / to defile in his own native land” (22.403–404; see [[the-dragging-of-hector]]).

In the last book the verb is used of the dragging around Patroclus' tomb, day after day (ἀείκιζεν, 24.22), while Apollo keeps all defilement from the flesh (πᾶσαν ἀεικείην, 24.19), and Apollo's complaint to the gods says that he “defiles the senseless earth” (κωφὴν … γαῖαν, 24.54; see [[the-senseless-earth]]).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle quotes Apollo's verse in his account of anger: the poet spoke well about Hector, “when he wished to stop Achilles' anger against the dead man”, since the dead can no longer feel what the angry want them to feel (*Rhetoric* 2.3, 1380b24–30; see [[the-senseless-earth]]).
