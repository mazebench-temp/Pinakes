Aristotle defines the excellence of style as being clear and “neither mean nor above the dignity of the subject, but appropriate” (*Rhetoric* 3.2, 1404b1–4). The poetic style is not mean, but it does not suit prose; words too grand for the thing are inappropriate, and so are metaphors and epithets that do not fit. Chapter 7 gives the parts of appropriateness: “The style will be appropriate if it is emotional, shows character, and is proportionate to the subject matter” (*Rhetoric* 3.7, 1408a10–11). The art must also be hidden, for the natural persuades and the artificial makes hearers suspicious (*Rhetoric* 1404b18–21).

## Outside the Rhetoric

Theophrastus, Aristotle's successor, counted appropriateness among the four virtues of style, with correct language, clarity and ornament (Cicero, *Orator* 79). Cicero calls it the hardest thing to see, in life as in speech: “The Greeks call it πρέπον; let us call it decorum” (*Orator* 70).
