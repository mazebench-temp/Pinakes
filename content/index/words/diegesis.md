The handbooks make narration a necessary part of every speech; Aristotle says it belongs to judicial speech alone (*Rhetoric* 3.13, 1414a37–b1). In epideictic it is piecemeal, actions recalled one group at a time; well-known deeds need only be recalled (*Rhetoric* 3.16, 1416b16–29). Against the rule that it should be quick, he answers with the baker's retort, “Is it impossible to knead it well?”: the good lies in due measure (*Rhetoric* 1416b30–36). The narration should show character by showing choice, and draw on the emotions and their known signs (*Rhetoric* 1417a15–b10). In deliberative speech narration is rarest, since no one narrates the future (*Rhetoric* 1417b11–20).

## Outside the Rhetoric

Later handbooks require the narration to be clear, brief and plausible, the teaching Quintilian ascribes to the followers of Isocrates (4.2.31–32); Aristotle's baker is aimed at the rule of brevity.
