## Meaning

θέσφατος is made of θεσ-, the element meaning “god” that is found also in θεσπέσιος, “wondrous” (literally “spoken by a god”), and of φατός, “spoken”, from the root of φημί, “say”. It is what a god has said. The neuter plural θέσφατα are the oracles of the gods: Phereclus, who built the ships in which Alexander sailed for Helen, “knew nothing of the oracles of the gods” (5.64; see [[phereclus]] and [[prophecy]]). The neuter singular, θέσφατόν ἐστι, “it is decreed”, says that a thing will happen because a god has spoken it.

The Iliad has the phrase once. Zeus tells Hera that Hector will not stop fighting until Achilles is roused beside the ships, on the day when they fight at the sterns “over Patroclus dead”, and he ends: “For so it is decreed” (ὣς γὰρ θέσφατόν ἐστι, 8.473–477). It is the poem's first word of the death of Patroclus, and Zeus speaks of it as a thing already declared, not as a choice still to be made (see [[plan-of-zeus]], [[the-death-of-patroclus]] and [[prophecy]]). The Odyssey uses the phrase in the same way of a man's future. Proteus tells Menelaus that “it is not decreed” for him to die in Argos, since the gods will send him to the Elysian plain (*Odyssey* 4.561–569), and Odysseus' companions urge him to remember his home “if it is decreed for you to be saved” (*Odyssey* 10.472–474). Polyphemus and Alcinous, when an old prophecy is fulfilled against them, say the same verse: “Ah, the oracles spoken long ago have come upon me” (παλαίφατα θέσφατα, *Odyssey* 9.507, 13.172).

## Related words

- θεσπέσιος (“wondrous”): the Trojans pour their missiles on Diomedes “with a wondrous din” (ἠχῇ θεσπεσίῃ, 8.159).
- ἀθέσφατος (“endless”, beyond even a god's telling): the cranes flee “the winter and its endless rain” (3.4; see [[untold]]).
- [[theopropion|θεοπρόπιον]] (“prophecy”), [[moira|μοῖρα]] (“fate”), [[aisa|αἶσα]] (“portion”).
