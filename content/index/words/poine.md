## Meaning

ποινή is what is paid to make good a wrong: a penalty, a compensation, and especially the blood-price that a killer pays to the kin of the man he has killed. Ajax reproaches Achilles with it in Book 9: a man accepts the ποινή even from the killer of his brother or his son, and the killer stays at home among his people, having paid a great deal (9.632–636). On the shield of Achilles two men quarrel in the assembly “over the blood-price of a man who had been killed” (εἵνεκα ποινῆς / ἀνδρὸς ἀποφθιμένου, 18.498–499). When no price is accepted, the penalty is taken in blood: Achilles takes twelve Trojan youths alive from the river “as ποινή for Patroclus” (21.28).

The noun belongs with the verb τίνω, “pay”; both come from an Indo-European root *kwei-* that also gives Avestan *kaēnā* (“punishment, revenge”) and Lithuanian *kaina* (“price”). Latin *poena* was borrowed from ποινή, and through it English has *penal*, *penalty*, *punish* and *pain*.

The active τίνω is “pay”: Agamemnon demands that the Trojans pay back (ἀποτινέμεν) a fitting satisfaction (3.286, 3.459), and Achilles promises Agamemnon that the Achaeans will repay him three and four times over (1.128). The middle τίνομαι is “make someone pay”, that is, “punish” or “take vengeance”. Chryses prays that the Danaans may pay for his tears (τίσειαν, 1.42); Nestor tells the army to stay until each man has paid the Trojans back for the struggles and groans for Helen's sake (2.356); Peirithous took vengeance on the Centaurs (2.743); and Menelaus prays for the chance to make Paris pay (3.351). How this family is related to τίω, “honor”, is debated (see [[time]]).

## Related words

- [[time|τιμή]] (“honor, satisfaction”): the compensation that makes honor good (1.159, 3.286).
- [[apoina|ἄποινα]] (“ransom”), formed from ἀπό and ποινή: what is paid to get back a captive or a body.
- [[aleites|ἀλείτης]] (“offender”), the man on whom vengeance is taken (3.28).
- [[neikos|νεῖκος]] (“quarrel”): the dispute over a blood-price on the shield (18.497–499).

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod, when a man harms Justice (Δίκη) with crooked words, she sits beside her father Zeus and tells him of the unjust minds of men, “so that the people may pay (ἀποτείσῃ) for the recklessness of its kings” (*Works and Days* 256–261).
