## Meaning

τροπή is a “turning”, from τρέπω, “turn”, and τροπαὶ ἠελίοιο are the turnings, or turning points, of the sun. Homer has the phrase only once, when Eumaeus begins the story of his birth: “There is an island called Syrie, if perhaps you have heard of it, / above Ortygia, where the turnings of the sun are” (*Odyssey* 15.403–404; see [[syrie]] and [[ortygia]]). The poem does not say which turning is meant, and the verse has been read in two ways (see the Odyssey translation's note on 15.403–404):

- **The solstices**, the two points of the year at which the sun, having risen and set as far north or as far south as it will, turns back. This is the sense of the phrase in Hesiod (see Outside Homer). Homer names one other place of the sun in the plural: Circe's island is where are “the risings of the Sun” (ἀντολαὶ Ἠελίοιο, *Odyssey* 12.4; see [[aeaea]]), and W. W. Merry takes the plural as an allusion to the sun's rising at different points of the horizon with the change of season (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 12.4).
- **The far west**, where the sun, at the end of its course, turns its chariot back toward the east. Merry pictures the sun's daily course as a chariot race that starts in the east and turns at a post in the extreme west, explains by it the short nights of the Laestrygonians (*Odyssey* 10.86; see [[telepylus]]), and finds the same notion in this verse; he reports Seiler's view that it describes a place in the furthest west, and Autenrieth's rendering of τροπαί as “the change of direction, when at evening the sun turns round his car eastward” (Merry on *Odyssey* 10.81). Helios himself speaks of the two halves of his course, “as I went up into the starry sky / and when I turned back again from the sky toward the earth” (*Odyssey* 12.380–381; see [[helios]]).

D. B. Monro gives the phrase no place on a map. For him Syrie and Ortygia “have a mythical character, as the ‘turning points’ of the sun (so the island of Circe has the house of the dawn and the rising of the sun, 12.4), and also in respect of the superhuman felicity of the inhabitants”, and need not be identified with real places such as Syra and Delos (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 15.403). Strabo and Merry took Syrie for Syros in the Cyclades (see [[syrie]]).

## Related words

- ἀντολαὶ Ἠελίοιο, “the risings of the Sun”, at Circe's island (*Odyssey* 12.4; see [[aeaea]] and [[eos]]).
- [[helios]], the sun, whose setting is told in the formula “And the sun set, and all the streets grew dark” (see [[sunset-lines]]).
- The paths of night and of day, “close together” at Telepylus (*Odyssey* 10.86; see [[telepylus]]).

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod the turnings of the sun are the solstices, by which the farmer and the sailor reckon the year. A man who plows the earth “at the sun's turning”, the winter solstice, will reap crouching, gathering only a little in his hand (ἠελίοιο τροπῇς, *Works and Days* 479–480). When Zeus has completed sixty wintry days “after the turnings of the sun”, Arcturus first rises at dusk, and spring begins (μετὰ τροπὰς ἠελίοιο, *Works and Days* 564–569). And the season for sailing is the fifty days “after the turnings of the sun”, when summer comes to its end (*Works and Days* 663–665).
