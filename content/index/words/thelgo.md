## Meaning

θέλγω is to bewitch: to lay a spell on the eyes, the mind or the heart, so that a man no longer sees, knows or wills as he did. The translations render it “bewitch” throughout (see the Iliad translation's table of key words, θέλγω), and its nouns θελκτήρια “enchantments”; the Odyssey's ἀκήλητος, from κηλέω, “charm”, is the mind “that cannot be bewitched” (see the Odyssey translation's table of key words, ἀκήλητος).

**Gods.** The gods bewitch the eyes and the minds of men in battle. Zeus raises a storm of wind from Ida that carries the dust straight at the ships, “and the Achaeans' / minds he bewitched” (*Iliad* 12.253–255); Poseidon beats down Alcathous, “bewitching his bright eyes and fettering his shining limbs” (*Iliad* 13.434–435); Apollo shakes the aegis and shouts, and the Achaeans' hearts “in their breasts he bewitched, and they forgot their furious courage” (*Iliad* 15.320–322; see [[aegis]]), and Zeus “bewitched the heart / of the Argives” (*Iliad* 15.594–595). Apollo bewitches Achilles “by a trick”, running ahead of him in Agenor's likeness, “so that he always hoped to catch him with his own feet” (*Iliad* 21.600–605; see [[dolos]]). Hermes' wand does the same to sleep: it is “the wand with which he bewitches the eyes of men, / of whomever he will, and again wakes others who are sleeping” (*Iliad* 24.343–344 = *Odyssey* 5.47–48; again *Odyssey* 24.3–4; see [[hermes]] and [[of-the-golden-wand]]).

**Words.** Words bewitch the heart. Achilles, fighting the river, blames “my dear mother, who bewitched me with lies” (*Iliad* 21.276; see [[thetis]]). Calypso “always with gentle and wheedling talk / … bewitches him, so that he may forget Ithaca” (*Odyssey* 1.56–57; see [[calypso]]), and Aegisthus, while the Achaeans fought at Troy, “kept bewitching the wife of Agamemnon with many words” (*Odyssey* 3.264; see [[aegisthus]]). In the later books Eumaeus bids the beggar not to bewitch him with lies (*Odyssey* 14.387); Telemachus, before he knows his father, thinks that some god bewitches him to make him grieve the more (*Odyssey* 16.194–195); the suitors' knees are loosened and their hearts bewitched with love at the sight of Penelope (*Odyssey* 18.212), and she bewitches their hearts with soothing words while she draws gifts from them, “but her mind was set on other things” (*Odyssey* 18.281–283, plain renderings).

**Song.** Penelope calls the songs of Phemius “enchantments for mortals” (θελκτήρια, *Odyssey* 1.337; see [[phemius]] and [[singers]]). The Sirens bewitch all men who come near them, with their clear song (λιγυρῇ θέλγουσιν ἀοιδῇ, *Odyssey* 12.39–44). Eumaeus tells Penelope that the stranger's tales would bewitch her heart, as a man gazes at a singer taught by the gods: “so he bewitched me, sitting beside me in my halls” (*Odyssey* 17.513–521, a plain rendering), and when Odysseus breaks off his tale to the Phaeacians, “they were held by a spell throughout the shadowy halls” (κηληθμῷ, *Odyssey* 11.334; again 13.2; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]).

**Drugs.** Circe bewitches with drugs, and the drugs work on body and mind together: her wolves and lions were bewitched by them, the companions forget their native land and are turned into swine, and Odysseus alone, with Hermes' drug, has a mind that “cannot be bewitched” (*Odyssey* 10.213, 10.236–240, 10.291, 10.318, 10.326–329; see [[circe]], [[drugs]] and the note on *Odyssey* 10).

**To appease.** The noun also serves for what charms a god: the Trojans debated whether to let the wooden horse stand “as a great offering to appease the gods” (θελκτήριον, *Odyssey* 8.509; see the Odyssey translation's note on 8.509 and [[the-wooden-horse]]).

## Related words

- θελκτήρια (“enchantments”): the girdle of Aphrodite, “in which all her enchantments were wrought” (*Iliad* 14.215; see [[aphrodites-band]]).
- ἀκήλητος (“that cannot be bewitched”) and κηληθμός (“spell”), from κηλέω, “charm”.
- [[noos|νόος]], [[thymos|θυμός]] and [[phrenes|φρένες]], the mind and heart that are bewitched; [[dolos|δόλος]] (“trick”) and [[apate|ἀπάτη]] (“deceit”).

## Outside Homer

Euripides' Iphigenia, pouring drink offerings of milk, wine and honey for the brother she thinks dead, calls them “charms for the dead” (νεκροῖς θελκτήρια, *Iphigenia among the Taurians* 159–166; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]).
