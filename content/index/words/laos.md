## Meaning

λαός, usually in the plural λαοί, is the people as a body of men, and above all the army: the men who follow a leader to war. It is the word for the relation between leader and led. The leaders are κοσμήτορες λαῶν, “marshals of the people” (1.16, 1.375), and ποιμένες λαῶν, “shepherds of the people” (1.263). The Mycenaean tablets name a high official *ra-wa-ke-ta*, “leader of the people”. A modern proposal, much disputed, derives Achilles' name from [[achos|ἄχος]] and λαός: “he whose people have grief”.

## In Book 1

{{quote:e0170932-f13c-5490-9adf-5098b287f239}}

The first thing we learn of the λαοί is that they are dying. The imperfect ὀλέκοντο describes deaths that go on day after day.

{{quote:66f89de5-c23f-58f1-b74f-2b68898605bd}}

Agamemnon's best line as a king: he would rather have the λαός safe than perishing. He demands a new prize in the next breath (see [[rather-the-people-be-safe]]).

Achilles calls the λαός to assembly (1.54). He objects that it would not be fitting for the λαοί to gather back the spoils already divided (1.126), and he taunts Agamemnon that he never arms for war together with the λαός (1.226). After the assembly Agamemnon orders the λαοί to purify themselves (1.313). In Chryses' second prayer, the plague is the god's blow against “the λαός of the Achaeans” (1.454).
