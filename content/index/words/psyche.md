## Meaning

ψυχή is usually translated “soul”, but the Homeric word is narrower than the English. It is the life a person loses at death, and the shade that goes down to Hades afterwards. It plays no part in the thoughts or feelings of the living; those belong to the [[thymos|θυμός]], the [[phrenes|φρένες]] and the [[noos|νόος]]. The word is related to ψύχω, “breathe, blow”. The ψυχή is a breath-soul, which leaves through the mouth or through a wound.

In Hades the ψυχή is an image (εἴδωλον) without wits. When the ψυχή of Patroclus slips out of his arms, Achilles cries out that there is in Hades a ψυχή and an image, “but no φρένες in it at all” (23.103–104). A dying warrior's ψυχή flies from his limbs to Hades, lamenting its fate and leaving youth and manhood behind (16.856–857, 22.362–363). Nothing can buy it back. Cattle can be raided and tripods acquired, Achilles tells the embassy in Book 9, but a man's ψυχή cannot be raided or won again once it has passed the barrier of his teeth (9.406–409).

Jan Bremmer (*The Early Greek Concept of the Soul*, 1983) called this the “free soul”, as distinct from the “body souls”, θυμός, νόος and [[menos|μένος]], that animate the living.

## In Book 1

{{quote:a7fa6e2f-d814-5a31-b617-e5dc7e0b5d8c}}

The word occurs once in the book, in the proem. The wrath hurled many ψυχαί to Hades, but made “the men themselves” (αὐτούς) the spoil of dogs and birds. For Homer the self is here the body; what goes to Hades is only a shade. The ψυχαί are called ἴφθιμοι, “mighty”, a strength that belonged to the men while they lived.

Iliad 11.55 has almost the same words with κεφαλάς (“heads”) in place of ψυχάς, and an ancient reading reported in the scholia has κεφαλάς here too. In the next line, Zenodotus is reported to have read “a feast for the birds” (see [[zenodotus-feast-for-birds]]).

The living person's life, in this book, is his [[thymos|θυμός]]: Agamemnon may lose his θυμός (1.205), and Hephaestus fell to Lemnos with little θυμός left in him (1.593).

## Related words

- [[thymos|θυμός]] (“spirit, life”).
- [[heros|ἥρως]] (“hero”): the ψυχαί in the proem are the souls of heroes (1.4).
- [[hades|Hades]], where the ψυχαί go.

## Outside Homer

Plato turns the proem's picture round. In the *Phaedo* Socrates tells his friends that he is not the corpse they will bury; he is the one who will have gone away (115c–e). For Homer, αὐτούς in 1.4 were the bodies.

In the *Republic* Plato would strike from poetry Homer's lines on the ψυχή in Hades, among them 23.103–104 and 16.856–857, because they make men fear death (3.386c–387b).
