## Meaning

τέττα occurs only at 4.412. Sthenelus has answered Agamemnon's rebuke on Diomedes' behalf, and Diomedes turns on him: τέττα, σιωπῇ ἧσο, ἐμῷ δʼ ἐπιπείθεο μύθῳ, “Friend, sit in silence, and obey my word”. The second half of the line is what Zeus said to Hera when he silenced her on Olympus (1.565). The address softens the order.

The word belongs with ἄττα, which a younger man uses to an older one he loves: Achilles to his old tutor Phoenix (“Phoenix, old father”, 9.607), and Telemachus to the swineherd Eumaeus (*Odyssey* 16.31, 17.599). Both are nursery words for “father”, made of a repeated syllable, like Latin *tata* and Sanskrit *tatá-*. Whether τέττα keeps something of that sense here, “father”, or is simply a warm address between companions, cannot be settled from one use. Sthenelus is Diomedes' companion, standing beside him at his chariot (4.366–367), and nothing in the poem says which of the two is older. The translation renders “Friend”.

Plato's Socrates quotes the line as an example of the self-control the young should learn from Homer, obedience to those who rule (*Republic* 3.389e; see [[plato-silent-achaeans]]).
