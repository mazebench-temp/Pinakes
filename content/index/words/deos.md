## Meaning

δέος is fear, and the verb δείδω (perfect δείδοικα, “I am afraid”) is “fear, be afraid”. The adjective δειλός is “cowardly” and also “wretched”, and δειδήμων (3.56) “fearful, cowardly”. The same root gives δεινός, “terrible”, what is to be feared.

Fear in Homer is often the fear of a stronger power, and it ends in obedience. A line-opening formula marks it: “So he spoke, and … was afraid” (ὣς ἔφατʼ, ἔδεισεν δʼ …). The old priest Chryses is afraid of Agamemnon's threat and obeys (1.33); Hera is afraid of Zeus' and sits in silence (1.568); Helen is afraid of Aphrodite's and goes to Paris' bed (3.418). See [[obedience-and-persuasion]].

Fear is also the heart of shame. To be called δειλός, “a coward”, is the insult a hero cannot bear: Achilles would be called a coward and a nobody if he gave way to Agamemnon in everything (1.293). Helen imagines her brothers kept from battle “for fear of the shame” that is hers (3.242; see [[aidos]]). The etymology of δείδω is secure within Greek (δέος, δεινός, δειλός), but its outside connections are uncertain.

## Related words

- ταρβέω (“be terrified”): the heralds stand “in fear and awe” of Achilles (1.331), and Thersites sits down terrified (2.268).
- ῥιγέω (“shudder”): Priam shudders at the summons to the oath (3.259), and Menelaus wants men to come to shudder at wronging a host (3.353).
- τρόμος (“trembling”), φόβος (“flight, panic”).
- [[aidos|αἰδώς]] (“respect, shame”): the fear of what others will say.
