οἰωνός is a large bird, usually a bird of prey or a carrion bird, and so also an omen, for these were the birds whose flight was watched for signs. ὄρνις is the common word for a bird of any kind, and Homer sometimes names the kind beside it, as in λάρῳ ὄρνιθι, “like a bird, a gull” (*Odyssey* 5.51; see [[gull]], [[hawk]], [[eagle]] and [[like-a-diving-bird]]).

{{notes}}

## Elsewhere in Homer

Hector dismisses bird-signs: “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (Iliad 12.243).

## Outside Homer

[[Hesiod]]'s *Works and Days* ends by calling blessed the man who works “blameless before the immortals, judging the birds and avoiding transgressions” (826–828).
