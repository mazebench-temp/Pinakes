οἰωνός is a large bird, usually a bird of prey or a carrion bird, and so also an omen, for these were the birds whose flight was watched for signs. Both senses appear in Book 1.

**Carrion.** The wrath made the dead heroes “the spoil of dogs and of every bird” ({{ref:Iliad 1.4-5}}; see [[dogs|dogs]]). The Alexandrian editor Zenodotus is reported to have read “a feast (δαῖτα) for the birds” in place of “every” (πᾶσι; see [[zenodotus-feast-for-birds]]).

**Omens.** [[Calchas]] is “by far the best of the bird-seers” (οἰωνοπόλων, from οἰωνός + πολέω, “be busy with”; {{ref:Iliad 1.69}}; see [[divination|divination]]). At Aulis he read the sign of a snake that devoured eight sparrow chicks and their mother, and foretold nine years of war (Iliad 2.301–330). Hector later dismisses bird-signs: “one omen is best, to fight for one's country” (Iliad 12.243).

## Outside Homer

[[Hesiod]]'s *Works and Days* ends by calling blessed the man who works “blameless before the immortals, judging the birds and avoiding transgressions” (826–828).
