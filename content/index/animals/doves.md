πέλεια (also πελειάς, 11.634) is the wild dove, most likely the rock dove, which nests in cliffs and in hollows in the rock. Its standing epithet is τρήρων, “timid” (see [[timid]]), from the root of τρέω, “tremble, flee in fear”, and the adjective can stand alone for the bird (Odyssey 12.63). The Catalogue calls two towns πολυτρήρων, “of the many doves”: Thisbe in Boeotia and Messe in Laconia (2.502, 2.582; see [[of-many-doves]], [[thisbe]] and [[messe]]).

**Prey.** The Homeric dove is hunted. Apollo comes down from Ida “like a swift hawk, the dove-killer” (φασσοφόνος, 15.237–238). When Achilles chases Hector round the walls, he swoops like a hawk in the mountains on a timid dove, which flees before it while the hawk darts at it again and again (22.139–142). In the Odyssey birds of omen carry doves in their claws. A hawk, “the swift messenger of Apollo”, plucks one over Telemachus' ship (Odyssey 15.525–528), and a high-flying eagle holds one as the suitors plot against him (Odyssey 20.242–243). The maids whom Telemachus hangs are like thrushes or doves caught in a snare in a thicket (Odyssey 22.468–472; see [[the-net]]).

**Goddesses.** Twice goddesses are likened to doves. Hera and Athena go from their chariot to the battle “like timid doves in their step” (5.778; see [[timid-doves]]). In the battle of the gods Artemis, beaten by Hera with her own bow, flees in tears “like a dove that flies from a hawk into a hollow rock”, since it was not fated to be caught (21.493–496).

**A target.** At the funeral games of Patroclus, Achilles ties a timid dove by the foot to the mast of a ship as the target of the archery. Teucer, who makes no vow to Apollo, misses the bird and cuts the cord, and the dove flies up toward the sky. Meriones vows a hecatomb of firstborn lambs to Apollo, and shoots it as it circles high under the clouds (23.850–883; see [[famed-for-his-bow]]).

**The doves of Zeus.** Circe warns Odysseus that no bird can fly past the Wandering Rocks, “not even the timid doves that carry ambrosia to father Zeus”: the smooth rock always takes one of them, and the father sends another to make up the number (Odyssey 12.62–65; see [[ambrosia]]). On Nestor's great cup there are four handles, and at each of them two golden doves are feeding (11.632–635).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus heard at Dodona that a black dove flew from Egyptian Thebes, settled on an oak, and said in a human voice that there should be an oracle of Zeus in that place (2.55; see [[dodona]]).
