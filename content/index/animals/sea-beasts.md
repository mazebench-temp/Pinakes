κῆτος is a great creature of the sea, whatever its kind. Homer's κήτεα are seals, monsters and the great creatures of the deep alike, and the word gives the epithet μεγακήτης, “huge as a sea monster” or “with a great hollow”, which is said of ships, of a dolphin and of the sea (see [[huge-hulled]]).

## In Homer

When Poseidon drives his chariot over the sea to the Achaean ships, the sea beasts know him:

{{quote:9e5e9bae-3cec-5ac8-9da8-2e9e019ef718}}

ἄταλλε δὲ κήτεʼ ὑπʼ αὐτοῦ / πάντοθεν ἐκ κευθμῶν, οὐδʼ ἠγνοίησεν ἄνακτα: “and the sea beasts frolicked beneath him on every side, out of their lairs, and did not fail to know their lord” (13.27–28). ἀτάλλω is to skip or gambol, and κευθμών is a hiding place, a lair. The beasts recognize their king as the son of Oileus will soon recognize the god in Calchas' shape (13.66–72; see [[poseidon]] and [[chariot-journey]]).

In the Odyssey the κήτεα belong to Amphitrite, the sea goddess, who breeds them in thousands. Scylla fishes from her rock for dolphins and dogfish, and for any greater κῆτος she can catch (*Odyssey* 12.95–97), and Odysseus, swimming for the shore of Scheria, fears that a god may send a great κῆτος against him out of the sea, such as Amphitrite breeds in numbers (*Odyssey* 5.421–422). Seals are κήτεα too: Menelaus and his men lie in ambush under sealskins among the seals of Proteus, and Eidothea puts ambrosia under their noses against the stench of the beasts (*Odyssey* 4.441–446, 4.450–452; see [[seals]]).

In the Iliad the κῆτος is also the monster from which Heracles was sheltered by a wall that the Trojans and Athena built for him on the Trojan plain (20.145–148; see [[the-wall-of-heracles]] and [[heracles]]). The Iliad does not tell where the monster came from; for the later story of Laomedon and his daughter Hesione, see [[laomedon]] and [[heracles-sacks-troy]].
