ὕδρος (“water snake”) is formed from the root of ὕδωρ (“water”), and it is cognate with English *otter*: the old word named an animal of the water. Homer names the water snake only once, as the cause of [[Philoctetes]]' wound, and calls it ὀλοόφρων, “deadly” (literally “of destructive mind”, from ὀλοός, “destructive”, and φρήν, “mind”). He gives the same epithet to a lion and a wild boar (Iliad 15.630, 17.21), and to Atlas (Odyssey 1.52).

## Outside Homer

The *Cypria*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, told how the Achaeans put in at [[tenedos|Tenedos]] on their way to Troy, and how Philoctetes was bitten by a water snake during a feast and left behind on [[lemnos|Lemnos]] because of the stench of his wound (see [[philoctetes-on-lemnos]]). Later writers put the bite elsewhere. In Sophocles' *Philoctetes* the snake is the hidden guardian of the unroofed precinct of Chryse, which Philoctetes had approached (1326–1328). Pausanias says that Chryse was an island near Lemnos, where Philoctetes met his misfortune from the water snake, and that the sea later swallowed it (8.33.4).

Its feminine twin, ὕδρα, is the name of the Hydra of Lerna, the water monster that Heracles killed (Hesiod, *Theogony* 313–318).
