ἅρπη occurs only once in Homer. Zeus sends Athena to drip nectar and ambrosia into the breast of Achilles, who will not eat, and she goes down from the sky through the upper air “like a long-winged, shrill-voiced bird of prey” (ἅρπῃ ἐϊκυῖα τανυπτέρυγι λιγυφώνῳ, 19.350–351; see [[like-a-bird-of-prey]] and [[athena]]). The poem gives the bird two epithets, τανυπτέρυξ, “long-winged”, and λιγύφωνος, “shrill-voiced”, and nothing more. Walter Leaf takes it, presumably from its name, for a bird of prey, “but of course incapable of identification” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.350; see the translation's note on 19.350). The translation renders it “bird of prey”. The Harpies, the Snatchers, have a name of the same look (see [[harpies]]).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle names the ἅρπη among the animals that are at war with one another, as those are, he says, that keep to the same places and live from the same things. Among those that live from the sea, the βρένθος, the gull (λάρος) and the ἅρπη are enemies; and in a list of friends he pairs the πίφιγξ, the ἅρπη and the kite (*History of Animals* 9.1). Of the bird called φῶυξ, which he describes after the herons as the greatest eater of eyes among birds, he says that it is at war with the ἅρπη, “for she too has the same way of life” (*History of Animals* 9.18). Leaf, on 19.350, says that Aristotle appears to have applied the name to some sea bird. Whether Aristotle's bird is Homer's cannot be told.
