φήνη is a large bird of prey. Homer names it twice, both times in the Odyssey, and says little about it. Athena leaves Pylos φήνῃ εἰδομένη, “in the likeness of a sea eagle”, and all who see it are amazed (*Odyssey* 3.371–372; see [[athena]]). In a simile, Odysseus and Telemachus, together again in the swineherd's hut, weep aloud more shrilly than birds, φῆναι ἢ αἰγυπιοὶ γαμψώνυχες, sea eagles or vultures with crooked talons, whose young the country people have taken from the nest before they could fly (*Odyssey* 16.216–218, a plain rendering; see [[crooked-talons-and-curved-beaks]] and [[the-bird-and-her-chicks]]). The simile pairs it with the vulture as a great bird with a shrill cry of grief.

Which bird the word names cannot be settled from Homer. W. W. Merry reports that it is commonly taken to be the sea eagle, the *Falco ossifragus* of Linnaeus, or else the lammergeier, the bearded vulture (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.372). The translation renders it “sea eagle” (see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 3.372). In Homer it is a bird of its own: it is never called αἰετός, the eagle of the omens and similes (see [[eagle]]), and it is named beside the αἰγυπιός, the vulture, as a different bird (see [[vultures]]).

## Outside Homer

[[Aristotle]] describes the φήνη in the *History of Animals*. It is one of the crook-taloned birds that eat flesh, named with the vulture: “Further the φήνη and the vulture; and the φήνη is larger than an eagle in size, and ash-colored in its color” (*History of Animals* 8.3). The largest of the eagles, the kind called “true-born”, is larger than the φήνη (9.32). The φήνη is good to its young and a good provider, it brings food and is gentle, and it rears not only its own young but the eagle's: the eagle casts its chicks out of the nest before they can fly, and the φήνη takes them up and feeds them (9.34; compare 6.6). It has a film over its eyes and its sight is impaired, while the sea eagle, the ἁλιάετος, is the sharpest-sighted and makes its young look at the sun before they are fledged (9.34). Aristotle's “sea eagle” is thus another bird, the ἁλιάετος, one of his kinds of eagle (9.32). Whether his φήνη is Homer's cannot be told.
