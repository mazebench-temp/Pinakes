## In Homer

The dolphin is a creature of the sea of the similes and of the sea of the wanderings. In the Iliad it is the hunter. When Achilles leaps into the river with his sword, the Trojans cower under the banks “As before a huge-bellied dolphin the other fishes / flee and fill the recesses of a harbor with good anchorage, / in fear; for he devours greedily whatever he catches” (ὡς δʼ ὑπὸ δελφῖνος μεγακήτεος ἰχθύες ἄλλοι, *Iliad* 21.22–24; see [[the-dolphin-and-the-fishes]] and [[fish]]). The poet counts it among the fishes, ἰχθύες ἄλλοι, “the other fishes”, and gives it the epithet of ships and of the sea, μεγακήτης, “huge-bellied” (see [[huge-hulled]]). In the Odyssey it is the prey of a greater hunter: Scylla, sunk to her middle in her cave, fishes from it “for dolphins and dogfish, and if anywhere she may catch a greater / sea monster, of which loud-moaning Amphitrite feeds countless numbers” (*Odyssey* 12.96–97; see [[scylla]] and [[sea-beasts]]). The dolphin and the dogfish are the lesser κήτεα, the beasts of the sea that Amphitrite breeds.

## Outside Homer

The Homeric Hymn to Apollo makes the dolphin the god's shape. When Cretan sailors were bound for Pylos, Apollo met them at sea, leaped onto their swift ship in the shape of a dolphin, δελφῖνι ἐοικώς, and lay there, a huge and terrible creature, shaking the ship until they sat in silence and fear; at Crisa he bids them pray to him as Delphinius, since he first came to them in the likeness of a dolphin, and calls their altar Delphinian (*Hymn to Apollo* 399–404, 493–496; see [[apollo]]). In the Hymn to Dionysus the pirates who have seized the god leap overboard into the shining sea when he shows himself, and become dolphins; only the helmsman, who had urged them to set him free, is spared (*Hymn to Dionysus* 48–55; see [[dionysus]]).

[[herodotus|Herodotus]] tells the story of the Corinthians and the Lesbians that the singer Arion of Methymna, thrown into the sea by the crew of the ship that was taking him home from Italy, was carried to Taenarum on a dolphin's back, and that a small bronze offering of his stood at Taenarum, a man riding on a dolphin (Herodotus 1.23–24; see [[periander]]). [[aristotle|Aristotle]] counts the dolphin with the whale among the κήτη, the sea beasts that have a blowhole and not gills: it breathes air with a lung, sleeps with its snout above the water, bears live young and suckles them (*History of Animals* 6.12). He reports the many stories of its gentleness and of its love for boys at Taras and in Caria, and that it is said to be the swiftest of all animals in the water or on land, leaping over the masts of great ships when it rushes up from the deep after a fish (*History of Animals* 9.48).
