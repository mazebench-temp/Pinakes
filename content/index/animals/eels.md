ἔγχελυς is the eel. Homer names it twice, in the plural ἐγχέλυες, and each time beside the fishes, ἐγχέλυές τε καὶ ἰχθύες, “the eels and the fishes” (21.203, 21.353; see [[fish]]). Walter Leaf takes the eels to be named apart from the fishes because they were thought of as snakes, as the name may show if it is related to ἔχις and ἔχιδνα, “viper”, and he rejects the scholiasts' explanation, “eels and the other fishes” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.203).

**On the body of Asteropaeus.** Achilles kills the Paeonian Asteropaeus, a grandson of the river Axius, draws his spear out of the bank, and leaves him lying in the sand with the dark water lapping over him:

{{quote:3f818ef2-b618-54a7-9ccf-30b403289f8e}}

ἀμφεπένοντο, “busied themselves about him”, is the verb of men tending the wounded, as the healers busy themselves over Menelaus and over the wounded Achaeans (4.220, 16.28); Leaf calls it ironical here, as it is when the dogs do not busy themselves about the body of Hector (on 21.203; 23.184). δημὸν … ἐπινεφρίδιον, “the fat about his kidneys”, has a word found only here. The scene fulfills a threat. Achilles had told Lycaon that the fish would lick the blood from his wound and eat his white fat (21.122–127; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]), and the boast over Asteropaeus was that the lineage of Zeus is mightier than a river's (21.184–191; see [[asteropaeus]] and [[zeus-is-mightier-than-rivers]]). The creatures of one river feed on the grandson of another.

**In the fire.** When Hephaestus turns his flame on the river, the creatures of the water suffer with it:

{{quote:4028e2cb-8bcc-50af-9de3-db44c71c1502}}

The eels that fed on the dead now suffer in the burning river (see [[hephaestus]], [[fire]] and [[achilles-and-the-river]]). The verb is the same, τείρω, “distress, wear down”, that is used of the river himself when the fire's blast wears him down (τεῖρε, 21.366).

## Outside Homer

The eel puzzled the naturalists. [[aristotle|Aristotle]] says that eels come neither from mating nor from eggs, and that none has ever been caught with milt or eggs; they are born of what are called “the earth's entrails” (γῆς ἔντερα), which form of themselves in mud and wet ground, and he observes that in marshy ponds that have been drained and scraped they appear again after rain, but not in times of drought (*History of Animals* 6.16). Most of them, he says, live on fresh water, which eel-breeders keep clean and running, though a few in some places feed on mud (*History of Animals* 8.2). [[strabo|Strabo]] tells of a fish near the river Ruscino at the foot of the Pyrenees that is dug out of the muddy ground with a trident and feeds on the mud “like the eels” (*Geography* 4.1.6). [[herodotus|Herodotus]] says that the Egyptians hold the eel sacred, among the creatures sacred to the Nile (*Histories* 2.72).
