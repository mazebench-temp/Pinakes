# πουλύποδος (Od. 5.432), the only form in Homer.
grc: [πουλύποδος]
en: [octopus]
---

πουλύπους, the epic form of πολύπους, “many-foot”, is the octopus. Homer names it once, in a simile of the fifth book of the Odyssey, with two more words found nowhere else in the poems: its θαλάμη, the den or hole it lives in, and its κοτυληδόνες, the suckers on its arms (*Odyssey* 5.432–433; see [[the-octopus]]). W. W. Merry calls it “the polyp”, and explains the suckers as named from their cup-like shape, from κοτύλη, “cup” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.432–433). The simile knows how the creature is caught: it is dragged out of its den, and the pebbles of the den come out sticking to its suckers.

## Outside Homer

[[Aristotle]] describes the octopus in the *History of Animals*. All the cuttlefish kind have the suckers on their arms, and the octopus uses its arms both as feet and as hands (4.1). Its den, which he too calls a θαλάμη, is its storehouse: it gathers everything into the den where it lives, and when it has eaten the most useful part it throws out the shells of crabs and shellfish and the spines of small fish; it hunts fish by changing its color to match the stones it lies near, and does the same when it is afraid. For all that, he says, it is a stupid creature, for it will even go toward a man's hand let down into the water (9.37). The female lays her eggs in the den or in a pot or some other hollow, and keeps watch at its mouth with an arm stretched out (5.18).
