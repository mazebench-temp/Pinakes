**The team.** Menelaus is the third to rise for the chariot race, and he leads under the yoke a mare and a horse: Aethe, who belongs to his brother Agamemnon, and his own Podargus (23.293–295; see [[menelaus]] and [[agamemnon]]). The mare came to Agamemnon as a gift. Echepolus, son of Anchises, a rich man of Sicyon, gave her to him “so that he might not have to follow him to windy Ilios”, and Menelaus leads her under the yoke “straining hard for the race” (23.296–300; see [[echepolus-son-of-anchises]]). Αἴθη is formed, like Αἴθων, the name of one of Hector's horses, from αἴθω, “burn, blaze” (see [[tawny]]). Πόδαργος, “White-foot” or “Swift-foot”, is the name of another of Hector's four (8.185; see [[the-horses-of-hector]]); Ποδάργη, Podarge, is the Harpy who foaled Achilles' horses (16.150; see [[podarge]]).

**Female though she is.** Antilochus, driving his father's slower horses, does not ask them to race the horses of Diomedes, to which Athena has given speed, but to catch the horses of the son of Atreus, “so that she does not pour disgrace on you two, / Aethe, female though she is” (23.407–409; see [[antilochus]]). Where the road narrows he forces past, and Menelaus holds back to avoid a crash; for a while “they hung back behind, / the mares of the son of Atreus” (23.433–434). The narrator speaks of the pair in the feminine there, αἳ … Ἀτρεΐδεω, though Podargus is a horse (τὸν ἑόν τε Πόδαργον, 23.295).

**Her strength.** Antilochus comes in second, “by crafty ways, not by speed”, with Menelaus as close behind him as a horse is to the wheel of its own chariot (23.514–522; see [[kerdos]]):

καλλίτριχος, “fine-maned” (23.525), is said of mares and horses (see [[fine-maned]]). Walter Leaf notes that εἰ with κε and the indicative occurs only here in Homer, and takes the last couplet for a poor interpolation made up of 23.490 and 23.382 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.526). The mare that Menelaus and Antilochus dispute after the race is not Aethe but the second prize, “a mare, / six years old, unbroken, carrying a mule foal in her womb” (23.265–266, 23.566–613; see [[amends-and-reconciliation]]).
