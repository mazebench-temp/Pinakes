## Names and descent

Ξάνθος, Xanthus, is “Bay”, from ξανθός, “yellow, fair”, a name that one of Hector's horses shares (8.185; see [[the-horses-of-hector]]). Βαλίος, Balius, is from βαλιός, “dappled”. The Harpy Podarge, “Swift-foot” or “White-foot”, bore them to the West Wind as she grazed in a meadow by the stream of Ocean, and they “flew with the breezes” (16.148–151; see [[zephyrus]]). Achilles calls them “far-famed children of Podarge” (19.400). They are ἄμβροτοι, “immortal”, the “glorious gifts” that the gods gave to Peleus (16.381 = 16.867), and Achilles says that Poseidon gave them to his father and his father to him (23.277–278; see [[peleus]] and [[poseidon]]). Zeus calls them “ageless and immortal” (17.444; see [[immortal-and-ageless]]). In Book 16 they run with a mortal trace horse, Pedasus, taken at the sack of Eetion's city, “who, though mortal, kept pace with immortal horses” (16.152–154).

## The best of horses

The Catalogue ends its question about the best men and horses with Achilles: while he kept up his wrath Ajax was the best of the men, but Achilles was by far the strongest, “and so were the horses that carried the noble son of Peleus” (2.768–770; see [[the-mares-of-eumelus]]). The Trojans covet them. Dolon asks for them as the price of his spying, and Hector swears to give them; Odysseus answers the captured spy that they are hard for any mortal to master and drive but Achilles, “whom an immortal mother bore” (10.401–404). Apollo says the same to Hector in the same verses when Hector is chasing them on the battlefield (17.75–78), and Zeus resolves that Hector will not ride behind them (17.448–449). Hector and Aeneas try to take them from Automedon, and fail (17.483–542; see [[automedon]] and [[hector]]).

## In Homer

When Achilles sends Patroclus out, Automedon yokes the pair (16.145–154; see [[patroclus]]). They carry Patroclus across the ditch (16.380–383), and after his death they carry Automedon away from Hector's spear (16.864–867). Then they stand apart from the battle and weep for their charioteer, as still as a pillar on a grave, their heads bowed to the ground and their manes trailing in the dust. Zeus pities them: “why did we give you to lord Peleus, a mortal, while you are ageless and immortal?” (17.426–447; see [[pity]] and [[tears]]). He breathes might into them, and they carry Automedon back into the fight (17.451–458). When Achilles goes out at last, he bids them bring their driver back alive this time. Xanthus bows his head until his mane touches the ground, and Hera gives him a voice: they will save Achilles now, but his day of death is near; it was not their slowness that cost Patroclus his armor but Apollo, who killed him; and Achilles himself is fated to be killed “by a god and a man”. The Erinyes stop his voice, and Achilles answers that he knows his fate (19.399–423; see [[hera]], [[erinyes]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]). At the funeral games he will not race them: they are immortal, and they stand mourning the charioteer who used to wash their manes and pour oil on them (23.274–284).

## Outside Homer

In the *Rhesus*, Hector tells Dolon that he too loves these horses, immortal and born of immortals, which Poseidon himself broke and gave to Peleus, “as they say” (*Rhesus* 184–188; see [[euripides-rhesus]]). Apollodorus makes them Poseidon's gift to Peleus at his wedding to Thetis, when Chiron gave him the ash spear (*Library* 3.13.5; see [[apollodorus]]). In the *Posthomerica* of Quintus of Smyrna they weep for the dead Achilles and long to leave the world of men, but the Fates had spun their service to Poseidon, to Peleus, to Achilles and last to Neoptolemus, whom they were to carry after his death to the Elysian plain (*Posthomerica* 3).
