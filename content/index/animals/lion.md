λέων is the lion. Homer also has a rarer word, λίς, used five times in the Iliad (11.239, 11.480, 15.275, 17.109, 18.318). The origin of λέων is uncertain, and Latin *leo* was borrowed from it.

In the Iliad the lion lives almost only in similes, and no animal comes into them more often. It is a creature of the mountains (ὀρεσίτροφος, “mountain-bred”, 12.299, 17.61), usually hungry, and it makes for the herds and flocks of men. It seizes the best cow of a herd while dogs and herdsmen shout from a distance and dare not come near (17.61–67); it breaks into a fold (5.136–142, 12.299–306); it is driven off by spears and torches and goes away unwilling at dawn (11.548–555, 17.657–664); it stands over its cubs (17.133–136) or grieves for them when a hunter has stolen them (18.318–322). Warriors on the attack are “like lions that eat raw flesh”, in a formula (5.782, 7.256, 15.592). The lion is courage and hunger at once. Its μένος (“fury”) and ἀλκή (“strength in defense”) are a hero's, and like a hero it goes for what it wants in the face of men's spears (12.299–306). Achilles, refusing Hector any pact, says that there are no “trusty oaths between lions and men” (ὅρκια πιστά, 22.262), the very words of the oaths of Book 3.

Outside the similes, lions appear as skins and as images. Agamemnon and Diomedes put on lions' skins for the night raid (10.23–24, 10.177–178). The Chimaera was “a lion in front, a serpent behind, and in the middle a she-goat” (6.181). On the shield of Achilles two lions seize a bellowing bull at the head of a herd, and the dogs will not face them (18.579–586). Hera tells Artemis that Zeus has made her “a lion to women” (21.483).

The first lion simile of the poem is in Book 3. When Menelaus sees Alexander, he rejoices like a hungry lion that comes upon a great carcass (3.23–28; see [[lion-on-a-carcass]]), and at the end of the book, his prey lost, he ranges the throng “like a wild beast” (3.449; see [[like-a-wild-beast]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the Odyssey, Odysseus comes out of the bushes toward Nausicaa like a mountain lion driven by hunger (Odyssey 6.130–134), the Cyclops eats his men like a mountain lion (9.292), and Odysseus stands among the dead suitors spattered with blood like a lion that has eaten an ox (22.402–406). Menelaus foretells the suitors' end in a lion simile: a deer lays her fawns in a lion's lair, and the lion comes home (4.335–340, repeated at 17.126–131).

## Outside Homer

Lions still lived in northern Greece in Herodotus' time. He says that they attacked the camels of Xerxes' baggage train in Macedonia, and that they were found only between the river Nestus in Thrace and the Achelous in Acarnania (7.125–126). Whether the poet had seen lions or knew them from stories and from art is not known. Two lions, or lionesses, stand over the main gate of Mycenae, carved in the thirteenth century BCE. Steven Lonsdale studied the lion similes together with those of herding and hunting (*Creatures of Speech: Lion, Herding, and Hunting Similes in the Iliad*, 1990).
