Homeric dogs guard herds and houses, hunt, and eat carrion. They are loyal and they are shameless, and Book 1 shows both sides of the animal.

**Carrion.** In the first lines of the poem the wrath of Achilles made the dead “the spoil of dogs and of every bird” ({{ref:Iliad 1.4-5}}). To lie unburied, food for dogs and [[birds|birds]], is the worst fate a warrior can fear (see [[cremation|cremation]]).

{{quote:0f38ffeb-58e9-5305-966c-031eba176b01}}

**Plague.** Apollo's arrows strike first the [[mules|mules]] and “the swift dogs” of the camp, then the men ({{ref:Iliad 1.50-52}}).

**Insult.** Achilles calls Agamemnon κυνῶπα, “dog-face” ({{ref:Iliad 1.159}}; see [[dog-face]]), and a man “with the eyes of a dog and the heart of a deer” ({{ref:Iliad 1.225}}; see [[dog-eyes-deer-heart]], [[eyes|eyes]] and [[deer|deer]]). The dog stands for shamelessness ([[anaideia]]): Helen calls herself “dog-faced” (Iliad 3.180; Odyssey 4.145), and Hera calls Artemis a “shameless dog” (Iliad 21.481).

## Outside Homer

Thucydides observed that in the Athenian plague the dogs, because they lived with people, showed its effects most clearly (2.50). The Cynic philosophers took their name from the dog (κύων) and made its shamelessness a principle.
