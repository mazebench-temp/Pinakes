## In the traces

{{quote:63de869a-83d3-5ee3-a487-005fd665b21e}}

ἐν δὲ παρηορίῃσιν ἀμύμονα Πήδασον ἵει, / τόν ῥά ποτʼ Ἠετίωνος ἑλὼν πόλιν ἤγαγʼ Ἀχιλλεύς, / ὃς καὶ θνητὸς ἐὼν ἕπεθʼ ἵπποις ἀθανάτοισι: “And in the trace horse's harness he put the noble Pedasus, / whom Achilles once brought back when he took the city of Eetion, / and who, mortal though he was, kept pace with the immortal horses” (16.152–154). The παρηορίαι are the traces of the παρήορος, the third horse that runs beside the two under the yoke, drawing by traces and not by the pole. Automedon yokes Xanthus and Balius, the immortal horses born of the Harpy Podarge to the West Wind, and puts Pedasus beside them (16.148–154; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]] and [[automedon]]). ἀμύμων, “noble, blameless”, is usually said of men.

Πήδασος is also the name of places and of a man: the town of Altes, king of the Leleges, on the river Satnioeis, which Achilles sacked with Lyrnessus (6.35, 20.92, 21.86–87; see [[pedasus-on-the-satnioeis]]), “vine-rich Pedasus” in Messenia, one of the seven cities that Agamemnon offers Achilles (9.152; see [[pedasus-in-messenia]]), and a son of Bucolion killed by Euryalus (6.21; see [[pedasus-son-of-bucolion]]). The horse came from another sack, that of Thebe, “the city of Eetion” (see [[sack-of-thebe]] and [[eetion]]). From Thebe came Chryseis, chosen from the spoils for Agamemnon (1.366–369), the lyre on which Achilles sings the famous deeds of men (9.186–189), and the lump of iron that Eetion used to throw, which Achilles sets as a prize at the funeral games (23.826–829).

## His death

{{quote:4eb16fcc-b791-526a-a36e-f9d8aa31b27d}}

Patroclus has killed Sarpedon's attendant Thrasymelus, and Sarpedon, rushing in second, misses him; “but he stabbed the horse Pedasus / in the right shoulder with his spear; and it screamed, gasping out its life, / and fell down in the dust with a cry, and its spirit flew away” (16.467–469). The death is told in the words of a warrior's. ἔβραχε, “screamed”, is said of bronze armor ringing when it is struck (4.420) and of wounded Ares (5.859); θυμὸν ἀΐσθων, “gasping out its life”, is said of men breathing their last: Hippodamas “gasped out his life and bellowed” (20.403). The verse of the fall, κὰδ δʼ ἔπεσʼ ἐν κονίῃσι μακών, ἀπὸ δʼ ἔπτατο θυμός, is used in the Odyssey for the great stag that Odysseus kills on Circe's island and for the boar that wounded him as a boy on Parnassus, which he too struck in the right shoulder (*Odyssey* 10.158–163, 19.449–454).

The dead trace horse throws the team into confusion: the two immortal horses start apart, the yoke creaks, and the reins are tangled, “since the trace horse lay in the dust”. Automedon draws his long-edged sword, darts in and cuts the trace horse loose, “and did not fail”, and the two are set straight and pulled taut in the reins (16.470–475). Nestor did the same on the second day of battle, when Paris' arrow struck his trace horse in the head and he cut the traces with his sword while Hector's horses came on (8.80–91). Pedasus is the first on Patroclus' side to die in Book 16; the next is the Myrmidon Epeigeus, killed by Hector over Sarpedon's body (16.570–580).

Walter Leaf reports that some critics would cut out both passages about Pedasus, holding that a third horse is not Homeric, since the trace horse appears elsewhere only in Nestor's chariot (8.87); he kept them, and suggested that the trace horse may have served to kick and bite rather than to draw, and as a reserve if a yoke horse were killed (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.152). He notes too that οὔτασεν, elsewhere a verb of thrusting, is used here of a spear that is thrown, which troubled the school of Aristarchus, and that according to Didymus the text of Philemon read ἤλασεν, “struck” (on 16.467).
