στρουθός is a small bird, usually identified with the sparrow. Later Greek also used the word loosely of small birds, and called the ostrich “the big στρουθός” (Xenophon, *Anabasis* 1.5.2). Its young are νεοσσοί (“nestlings”, from νέος, “young”).

Homer names the sparrow in a single passage, the sign at [[aulis|Aulis]] that [[Odysseus]] recalls in Book 2. There the sparrow is a victim, a mother with eight nestlings devoured by a [[serpent]] that Zeus has sent, and [[Calchas]] turns their number into the years of the war (see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]).

{{notes}}

## Elsewhere in Homer

Homer's other parent birds are images of care and loss. Achilles compares his toil for the Achaeans to a bird that brings its unfledged nestlings (ἀπτῆσι νεοσσοῖσι) every morsel it finds, and fares badly itself (Iliad 9.323–324). Odysseus and Telemachus, reunited, weep more shrilly than sea eagles or vultures whose young the country people have taken from the nest before they could fly (Odyssey 16.216–218).

## Outside Homer

In Sappho's prayer to [[aphrodite|Aphrodite]], “beautiful swift sparrows” draw the goddess' chariot down from heaven (fragment 1.9–10). In [[aeschylus|Aeschylus]]' *Agamemnon* the victims of the sign at Aulis are a pregnant hare and her unborn young (104–159; see [[aeschylus-aulis]]). [[Cicero]] translated the passage of the Iliad into Latin verse, and then asked what kind of augury it was that turned sparrows into years rather than months or days (*On Divination* 2.63–65; see [[cicero-aulis-sparrows]]).
