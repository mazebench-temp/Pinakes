## In the Iliad

The first fish of the Iliad is in the simile of the fisherman. Patroclus drags Thestor over the rail of his chariot on his spear “as when a man / sitting on a jutting rock drags a sacred fish / out of the sea onto the shore with line and gleaming bronze” (16.406–408; see [[the-fisherman]]). Why the fish is “sacred” is not said (see the translation's note on 16.407). The sea is “fish-filled” in Patroclus' jest a few hundred verses later, when he says that Cebriones, tumbling from his chariot, would make a fine diver for oysters (16.746; see [[fish-filled]] and [[like-a-diver]]).

In the later books fish are eaters of the dead. The boar on which Agamemnon swears his oath is thrown into the sea as food for the fish (19.266–268). Achilles throws the body of Lycaon into the Scamander and tells him to lie there among the fish, which will lick the blood from his wound, and a fish will rise to eat his white fat (21.120–127); eels and fish tear at the body of Asteropaeus on the sand (21.201–204), and when Hephaestus sets the river on fire the eels and fish are in torment, tumbling through the streams (21.352–355). The Trojans cowering in the river are like small fish fleeing a dolphin (21.22–26), and Euryalus, struck by the fist of [[epeius|Epeius]], leaps up as a fish leaps on a weedy beach under the ruffling of the North Wind (23.692–694). Iris plunges into the sea like a lead weight on the horn of an ox, which goes down “bringing doom to the fishes that eat raw flesh” (24.80–82; see [[the-lead-weight]]).

## In the Odyssey

Menelaus' men on Pharos and Odysseus' men on the island of the Sun fish with bent hooks because hunger is wearing them down (*Odyssey* 4.368–369, 12.330–332). Fishing is a likeness for killing: the Laestrygonians spear Odysseus' men like fish (*Odyssey* 10.124), Scylla lifts six of them as an angler on a headland flings a fish ashore (*Odyssey* 12.251–255), and the dead suitors lie heaped like fish that fishermen have drawn out on the beach in a net (*Odyssey* 22.383–388).

## Outside Homer

An ancient note on the oysters of Patroclus' jest (16.747), reported by Walter Leaf, answers those who gave the Iliad and the Odyssey to different poets because the heroes of the Iliad never eat fish: they know such food, since Patroclus names it, and the poet left it out as mean (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.747). Leaf himself saw in the “sacred” fish of the simile a trace of a taboo on eating fish, which Homer's men break only under the stress of hunger (on 16.407). Plato's Socrates draws a rule for soldiers' diet from Homer's silence: at the feasts of the heroes on campaign Homer does not feast them on fish, though they are by the sea at the Hellespont, nor on boiled meat, but only on roast, which is the easiest for soldiers to come by (*Republic* 3.404b–c).
