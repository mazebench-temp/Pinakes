αἰετός is the epic form of ἀετός, the eagle. Homer gives it the epithets of the hunter and of height: it is ὑψιπέτης, “flying high” (12.201, 13.822), μέλας θηρητήρ, “the black hunter” (21.252), and αἴθων, “tawny” or “fiery” (15.690). In Book 24 it is μόρφνος θηρητήρ, “the dark hunter, whom men also call the dusky one” (περκνός, 24.316); which bird these names describe is not certain. Three superlatives set it above the other birds. It is τελειότατος πετεηνῶν, “surest in omen of winged birds”, the bird whose sign comes true (8.247 = 24.315; see [[surest-of-birds]]); “the sharpest-sighted of the birds under heaven”, as men say (17.674–675); and “the strongest and swiftest of birds” (21.253).

**The bird of Zeus.** Zeus sends the eagle twice in the Iliad, in the same verse (8.247 = 24.315), and the sign it brings is his: the serpent that an eagle drops among the Trojans is “the portent of aegis-bearing Zeus” (12.209). When Priam asks Zeus for a sign before he goes to Achilles, he asks for “a bird of omen, a swift messenger, the one that is dearest to you of birds, and whose strength is greatest” (24.310–311, in Hecuba's words at 24.292–293), and Zeus sends the eagle at once, its wings as wide as the door of a rich man's chamber (24.314–321). A bird on the right is a good sign and one on the left a bad one. The eagle with a fawn in its talons that Zeus sends after Agamemnon's prayer in Book 8 drops its prey by his altar, and the Achaeans take heart (8.245–252). In Book 12 an eagle flies on the left of the Trojans at the ditch with a blood-red serpent in its talons; the serpent bites it, and it drops its prey among them and flies off screaming; Polydamas reads it as a warning not to attack the ships, and Hector refuses to heed birds (12.200–250). After Ajax's challenge to Hector in Book 13 an eagle flies on the right, and the Achaeans shout (13.821–823). See [[birds]], [[teras]] and [[divination]].

**The hunter.** In the similes the eagle swoops from a height on prey. Hector darts at the ships as a tawny eagle swoops on a flock of geese or cranes or long-necked swans feeding by a river (15.690–692; see [[geese]], [[cranes]] and [[swans]]). Menelaus, looking for Antilochus, looks about him like the eagle that sees from on high a hare crouching under a bush (17.674–678). Achilles springs away from the river with the swoop of the black eagle, the hunter (21.251–253). Hector, drawing his sword for his last charge, swoops like a high-flying eagle that goes down through the dark clouds to the plain to seize a tender lamb or a cowering hare (22.306–310; see [[lambs]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Odyssey has four eagles of omen, all of them read. Zeus sends two eagles from a mountain peak over the assembly of Ithaca; they wheel over the crowd, tear each other's cheeks and necks, and fly away to the right, and the old seer Halitherses foretells the return of Odysseus (*Odyssey* 2.146–176). As Telemachus leaves Sparta an eagle flies by on the right with a tame white goose in its talons, and Helen reads it as Odysseus coming home to take revenge (15.160–178). In Penelope's dream an eagle from the mountain breaks the necks of her twenty geese and then speaks to her: the geese are the suitors, and he is her husband (19.535–553). An eagle on the left with a dove in its talons turns the suitors from their plot to kill Telemachus (20.240–246). When Odysseus swoops on the suitors' kinsmen at the end, he does so like a high-flying eagle, in the line of Hector's last charge (*Odyssey* 24.538 = *Iliad* 22.308).

## Outside Homer

In [[hesiod|Hesiod]] Zeus sends a long-winged eagle against Prometheus, bound to a pillar, to eat his immortal liver, which grows back each night, until Heracles kills the bird (*Theogony* 521–531). [[pindar|Pindar]] makes the eagle sleep on the scepter of Zeus, its swift wings folded, lulled by the music of the lyre (*Pythian* 1.6–10).
