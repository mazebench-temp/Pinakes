## The bird

ἀλκυών is a bird of the sea and the shore, known for its lament. Homer names it only once. Later Greek often wrote it with a rough breathing, ἁλκυών, as if it came from ἅλς, “sea”; the origin of the word is unknown. Aristotle describes the halcyon as not much bigger than a sparrow, blue and green and purplish all over, with a long, thin beak; it lives on fish, goes up rivers, and builds a nest like a ball of sea foam (*History of Animals* 9.14). He knows two kinds, both with blue backs, a smaller one that sings sitting on the reeds and a larger one that is silent (8.3), and he calls it the rarest of birds to see (5.9). The description fits the kingfisher, and that is the usual identification. For the poets the halcyon is above all a bird that mourns.

## In Homer

{{quote:d844dee2-9468-56b5-8a4f-1fafbb8fc92f}}

μήτηρ ἀλκυόνος πολυπενθέος οἶτον ἔχουσα / κλαῖεν: Cleopatra's mother, “having the lot of the halcyon bird of many sorrows”, wept because Apollo had snatched her away (9.563–564). οἶτος is a lot or fate, usually an evil one (κακὸν οἶτον, 3.417, 8.34), and πολυπενθής, “of many sorrows”, is formed from πένθος, “grief” (see [[of-many-sorrows]]). The mother is Marpessa, daughter of Evenus, for whom Idas drew his bow against Apollo (9.557–560; see [[marpessa]], [[idas]] and [[idas-and-apollo]]). Her parents called their daughter Ἀλκυόνη, Alcyone, after the bird whose lot the mother shared (9.561–562; see [[cleopatra]]). Homer does not say why the halcyon mourns. In later poetry it laments a lost mate, and that fits a wife carried off from her husband.

The bird is part of Phoenix's story of Meleager, who lies beside his wife Cleopatra nursing his anger (9.555–565; see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). The daughter named for her mother's lament is the one whose weeping at last moves him: she tells him, “weeping”, of all the sorrows that come to men whose city is taken (ὀδυρομένη, 9.590–596).

## Outside Homer

The lament of the halcyon was a theme of tragedy. The chorus of captive Greek women in Euripides' *Iphigenia in Tauris* sings to the bird that “by the rocky ridges of the sea” sings its lament, a cry clear to those who understand, that it always mourns its mate in song; they compare their own laments to hers, “a bird without wings” (1089–1095). Even the word for the halcyon's lament is Homer's, οἶτον (1091).

Aristotle reports that the halcyon breeds at the winter solstice, and that when the solstice is calm, the seven days before it and the seven after are called “halcyon days” (ἁλκυόνειοι ἡμέραι). He quotes Simonides on the fourteen days of winter that Zeus lulls, which men call the windless season, the holy nurse of the young of the dappled halcyon (*History of Animals* 5.8, 542b).

The bird was also given a myth. Alcyone, daughter of Aeolus, married Ceyx, son of the Morning Star; because they called each other Zeus and Hera, Zeus turned them into birds, her into a halcyon and him into a κήυξ, another sea bird (Apollodorus, *Library* 1.7.4). Ovid tells the story at length, with Ceyx drowned at sea and the two changed into birds, and Alcyone brooding on her floating nest through calm days in winter (*Metamorphoses* 11). “Halcyon days” passed into English for a time of peace.
