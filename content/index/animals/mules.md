οὐρεύς (also ἡμίονος, “half-ass”) is the mule, the draft animal of the Homeric world. Mules pull wagons, haul timber, and plow better than oxen (Iliad 10.351–353; 23.110–122; 24.265–280).

When Apollo comes down to shoot, he goes first after the mules and the swift [[dogs|dogs]], then turns his arrows on the men ({{ref:Iliad 1.50-52}}). The plague begins with the animals of the camp and moves to men (see [[wrath-of-apollo]]).

## Outside Homer

[[Aristotle]] treats the line as a problem to be solved: why should the god begin with the mules? He offers a solution by rare word (γλῶττα): “perhaps he does not mean the mules but the guards” (*Poetics* 25, 1461a). The idea rests on οὖρος, “watcher, guardian”, which Homer uses of Nestor, “guardian of the Achaeans” (Iliad 8.80). Most readers since have kept the mules (see [[aristotle-mules]]).
