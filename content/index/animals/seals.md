φώκη is the seal. Homer names it seven times, all in the Odyssey: six times in Menelaus' tale of Proteus (*Odyssey* 4.404–450), and once in the swineherd's story of his childhood, where the Phoenician woman who stole him from his father's house dies on the voyage and is thrown overboard to become the prey of seals and fishes (*Odyssey* 15.477–481, a plain rendering; see [[fish]] and [[eumaeus]]).

## Descriptions

The seals of the Odyssey are a flock of the sea, and the words for them are few and rare. They are φῶκαι νέποδες καλῆς ἁλοσύδνης, “the seals, the brood of the lovely daughter of the sea” (*Odyssey* 4.404; see [[daughter-of-the-sea]]). νέποδες occurs only here, and its sense was disputed in antiquity. W. W. Merry reports that Apollonius and the *Etymologicum Magnum* took it as “swim-footed”, that is, “web-footed”, from νέω, “swim”; that other grammarians took it as “footless”; and that Eustathius says νέπους meant “descendant” in some dialect, though Apollonius called that sense a misunderstanding of the later poets. Merry himself refers the word to the root of Latin *nepos* and of ἀνεψιός, “cousin”, cites later poets who use it of descendants, and renders it “brood” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.404). The translation follows him (see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 4.404).

They are ἁλιοτρεφέων, “sea-nurtured” (*Odyssey* 4.442), a word found only here in Homer, and ζατρεφέας, “well-fed” (*Odyssey* 4.451), the word for beasts in their prime, bulls, hogs and goats (see [[well-fed]]). Homer also calls them κήτεα, “beasts”, the word for any great creature of the sea (*Odyssey* 4.443, 4.446, 4.452; see [[sea-beasts]]). What he says of them most is their smell: they breathe out “the bitter smell of the deep sea” (*Odyssey* 4.406), and their smell is “most baneful” (ὀλοώτατος ὀδμή, *Odyssey* 4.442). Elsewhere in Homer ὀδμή is the smell of sulfur where Zeus' stroke has felled an oak (*Iliad* 14.414–415), or a fragrance, of cedar burning on Calypso's hearth or of Maron's wine (*Odyssey* 5.59–61, 9.210).

## Outside Homer

[[Aristotle]] counts the seal among the animals that live both ways. It does not take in water, but it breathes and sleeps and gives birth on land, near the shore, like a land animal, while it spends most of its time in the sea and feeds there. It bears one or two young, at most three, suckles them, and brings them down to the sea when they are about twelve days old, many times a day, to accustom them to it little by little; it is hard to kill except by a blow on the temple; and it gives a cry like an ox (*History of Animals* 6.12). He calls it a kind of maimed four-footed beast: its fore feet, set just below the shoulder blade, are like hands, and its hind feet are shaped like the tails of fish (*History of Animals* 2.1; compare 1.1, where its feet are “docked”). Merry finds in these passages a hint of the ancient explanation of νέποδες as “footless” (on 4.404).

Merry also reports that Aelian has seals sleeping out of the sea at midday, as they sleep around Proteus at that hour in Eidothea's account (*Odyssey* 4.400–405), and, from Erhard's *Fauna der Cycladen* by way of Buchholz, that one species of seal is common in the Cyclades and that the islanders call the holes where the seals hide φωκότρυπαι (on 4.450).
