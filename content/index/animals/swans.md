κύκνος is the swan; Latin *cycnus* or *cygnus* is borrowed from it. Homer mentions swans in a single line, which he uses twice, “geese or cranes or long-necked swans” (Iliad 2.460, 15.692). Their epithet δουλιχόδειρος (“long-necked”, from δολιχός, “long”, and δειρή, “neck”) is theirs alone. In both passages they are wild birds of the river meadows, feeding and flying in flocks.

## Outside Homer

Homer's swans cry with the rest of the flock; the singing swan is later. A short hymn to Apollo among the Homeric Hymns begins with the swan that sings of the god with a clear voice as it alights on the bank of the eddying [[peneius|Peneius]] (*Homeric Hymn* 21.1–3). [[Plato]]'s Socrates says that swans sing most and most beautifully when they are about to die, for joy that they are going to the god whose servants they are, since they belong to Apollo (*Phaedo* 84e–85b). In [[aeschylus|Aeschylus]]' *Agamemnon*, Clytemnestra says that Cassandra died “like a swan”, having sung her last lament (1444–1445). Later tradition told that Zeus came to Leda, the mother of [[Helen]], in the form of a swan ([[euripides|Euripides]], *Helen* 17–21). [[Virgil]] turned Homer's birds of the Asian meadow into swans that sing on the wing, so that the river and the Asian marsh resound (*Aeneid* 7.699–702).
