ἐρῳδιός occurs only once in Homer, in Book 10, and the poem says nothing of the bird's look. It is usually taken as the heron, the bird that later Greek calls by this name (see the translation's note on 10.274–276). What the poem gives is its cry. In the dark the two men “did not see it with their eyes … but they heard it cry” (κλάγξαντος ἄκουσαν, 10.276). κλάζω and κλαγγή are the words for the harsh cries of birds: the cranes that fly crying over Oceanus (3.3–5; see [[cranes]]), and the eagle that shrieks as it drops its serpent and flies off down the wind (12.207; see [[eagle]]). At the end of the night the same noun gives the cry of the Trojans when they find the Thracians dead, “a clamor and an untold tumult” (10.523).

## The sign

{{quote:0d6200f6-05d1-51cc-84da-b9cf38bded24}}

Athena sends the bird, ἧκεν, as Zeus sends his eagles (8.247; 24.315), and she sends it δεξιόν, “on the right”, and close to the path. The right is the side of good omens: a bird on the right is the sign that Priam asks of Zeus (24.310–312), the eagle that answers Ajax flies on the right (13.821–823), and Hector, who scorns such signs, speaks of birds that fly to the right, toward the dawn and the sun, or to the left, toward the murky dark (12.239–240; see [[divination]] and [[birds]]). The great omen-bird of the Iliad is Zeus' eagle; here the sign is Athena's own, as in the Odyssey a hawk is “Apollo's swift messenger” (*Odyssey* 15.525–526). Odysseus “rejoiced at the bird” and prayed to her for glory and a safe return, and Diomedes prayed second, and she heard them both (10.277–295; see [[athena]] and [[prayer]]).

The heron is a sign for the ear. When Priam is about to set out for the Achaean ships, a journey made in the dark, Hecuba bids him ask Zeus for a bird on the right, so that he may see it “with his own eyes” and go to the ships trusting in it, and Zeus sends an eagle that the whole household sees (24.290–321). Odysseus and Diomedes go out into the dark and take their sign by hearing, in a book where the night is followed by ear: the dogs of the simile hear the beast in the woods, the sentries listen toward the plain, Dolon stops at the sound of feet behind him, and Nestor is the first to hear the horses coming home (10.183–189, 10.354, 10.532–535; see [[the-watchdogs]] and [[the-doloneia]]).
