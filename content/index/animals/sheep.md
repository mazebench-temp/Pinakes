ὄις (in Homer also ὄϊς) is the sheep, ewe or ram. The word is inherited: Latin *ovis* and English *ewe* are its cousins. A flock of sheep is a πῶϋ, and “a great flock of sheep” (πῶϋ μέγʼ οἰῶν) is a formula (11.696, 15.323; Odyssey 12.299). With goats, sheep make up the μῆλα, the small livestock of the farm, beside the cattle that measure a rich man's wealth. Homer's sheep are “white” (ἀργεννός, 3.198, 6.424, 18.588) and “woolly” (εἰροπόκος, 5.137; Odyssey 9.443). The male is the [[ram]], and the young are [[lambs]].

Sheep are wealth. Achilles killed Andromache's seven brothers among their cattle and white sheep (6.421–424). The shield of Achilles shows a great pasture of white sheep in a fair glen, with farmsteads, huts and pens (18.587–589). The Cyclops lives by his flocks of sheep and goats (Odyssey 9.183–186), and Odysseus' men escape from his cave bound under his rams (9.425–436). They are also victims: many sheep are slaughtered for the funeral feast of Patroclus (23.30–31), and a black ewe is offered to the dead (Odyssey 10.524–527).

In Book 3 Priam, on the wall, likens Odysseus to a thick-fleeced ram going through “a great flock of white sheep” (3.196–198; see [[ram-among-the-sheep]]). Aphrodite comes to Helen in the likeness of an old wool-comber, who used to work fine wool for her in Lacedaemon (3.386–388; see [[helen-and-aphrodite]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the similes the flock is always at risk. A lion leaps into a fold of woolly sheep (5.136–142) or falls on flocks without a shepherd (10.485–486), and wolves carry off lambs and kids that have strayed from the flock through a shepherd's carelessness (16.352–355). The Trojans advancing to battle are like ewes bleating in a rich man's fold, a sound without order (4.433–436). And the flock follows its leader: the Trojans follow Aeneas as sheep follow the ram to water (13.491–494). The image lies behind the Homeric title of a king, “shepherd of the people” (see [[shepherd-of-the-people]]).
