λύκος is the wolf. The word is inherited: Latin *lupus* and English *wolf* are its cousins. Homer's wolves are gray (πολιός, 10.334), they live in the mountains, and they are ὠμοφάγοι, “eaters of raw flesh” (16.157). They hunt together.

In the Iliad's similes wolves stand for groups of fighters; the single hero is more often a lion. The Myrmidons arm like wolves that have killed a great stag in the mountains and go in a pack to drink at a dark spring, their jaws red with blood (16.156–163). The Danaans fall on the Trojans like wolves on lambs or kids that a careless shepherd has let scatter (16.352–355). In the first battle of the poem, over the body of Elephenor, Trojans and Achaeans “like wolves leapt upon one another” (4.471–472; see [[like-wolves]]), and in Book 11 both armies rush on “like wolves” again (11.72–73). The wolf is the enemy of the flock. There is no agreement, Achilles tells Hector, between lions and men, and wolves and lambs have no like-minded heart (22.262–263; see [[lambs]] and [[sheep]]).

Dolon goes out to spy on the Achaean camp wearing the skin of a gray wolf (10.334). On Circe's island Odysseus' men find mountain wolves and lions, bewitched by her evil drugs, that fawn on them like dogs (Odyssey 10.212–219).
