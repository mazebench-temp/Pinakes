γύψ is the vulture. The fear of the Iliad's warriors is to lie unburied, the spoil of dogs and birds, as the proem says of the dead of Achilles' wrath (1.4–5; see [[dogs]] and [[birds]]). The vulture is the bird that gives that fear a name. It is spoken of more than it is seen.

The first vultures of the poem are in a curse. Going through the army, Agamemnon tells the eager that father Zeus will be no helper to liars, and that those who first did harm in defiance of the oaths will have their tender flesh eaten by vultures, while their wives and children are carried off in the ships (4.235–239; see [[the-review-of-the-army]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]). The curse spoken over the lambs of the oath in Book 3 promised the same two punishments, death for the breakers and their wives given to others (3.298–301; see [[like-this-wine]]).

The later vultures are threats and pictures of the dead. The charioteers killed by Agamemnon lie on the plain, “dearer to the vultures than to their wives” (11.161–162). Hector tells the dying Patroclus that the vultures will eat him (16.836), Polydamas warns that dogs and vultures will eat many Trojans if they stay on the plain (18.271–272), and Priam wishes that dogs and vultures would eat Achilles (22.42–43). In the underworld two vultures tear at the liver of Tityus, who assaulted Leto (Odyssey 11.576–581).

αἰγυπιός, another large bird of prey usually rendered “vulture”, belongs to the similes. Athena and Apollo sit on the oak of Zeus in the likeness of vultures to watch the armies (7.58–61); Sarpedon and Patroclus rush at each other like two vultures fighting on a high rock (16.428–430); and Automedon drives among the Trojans like a vulture among geese (17.460).
