πάρδαλις, genitive παρδάλιος, is the leopard or panther; the skin is παρδαλέη (3.17, 10.29; see [[leopard-skin]]). Homer gives the beast no epithet; only its skin is called ποικίλη, “dappled” (10.30).

## In Homer

The beast itself is first named in Poseidon's taunt, among the beasts whose prey the Trojans used to be: “like deer quick to flee, which through the forest are the prey of jackals and leopards and wolves” (13.102–103; see [[timid-deer]]). It is a beast of strength and spirit. Menelaus, mocking the sons of Panthous, says that neither leopard nor lion nor wild boar has so much might as they think they have (17.20–23). In Book 21 Agenor goes out against Achilles as a leopard comes out of a deep thicket to face a hunter, and has no fear when it hears the baying of the hounds; even when it is struck or pierced with a spear it does not give up its courage until it closes with him or is killed (21.573–578). In the Odyssey Proteus, held by Menelaus, turns into a lion, a serpent, a leopard and a great boar (*Odyssey* 4.456–458).

Its skin is worn as a cloak. Paris comes out in front of the Trojans with a leopard skin on his shoulders and his curved bow (3.17), and Menelaus puts on a dappled leopard skin when he rises in the night (10.29–30; see [[paris]] and [[menelaus]]). See also [[deer]], [[jackal]] and [[wolves]].
