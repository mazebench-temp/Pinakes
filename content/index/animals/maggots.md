# εὐλαί (22.509, 24.414).
grc: [εὐλὰς, εὐλαί]
en: [worms]
---

εὐλή is the maggot, the grub that hatches in a corpse; the earthworm is another word, σκώληξ (13.654; see [[worm]]). The translation renders εὐλάς as “worms” (19.26). Homer uses εὐλή only of the dead, and only of two bodies. In *Iliad* 19 Achilles fears that while he fights, flies will make their way into the wounds the bronze has struck in Patroclus, “and breed worms, and defile the body” (εὐλὰς ἐγγείνωνται, 19.26; see [[flies]]). Andromache, lamenting Hector, says that now by the ships, far from his parents, wriggling worms will eat him naked, when the dogs have had their fill (αἰόλαι εὐλαὶ ἔδονται, 22.508–510). And Hermes, in the likeness of a Myrmidon, tells Priam that his son has lain twelve days by Achilles' ship, and that his flesh does not rot and the worms that feed on men slain in battle do not eat him (24.411–415).

The half-verse of *Iliad* 24, αἵ ῥά τε φῶτας ἀρηϊφάτους κατέδουσιν, “which feed on men slain in battle”, is the verse that Thetis says of the flies in Book 19 (19.31). Both bodies are kept from the worms by the gods. Thetis drips ambrosia and red nectar into the nostrils of Patroclus, so that his flesh may be sound (19.38–39; see [[ambrosia]] and [[nectar]]), and Hermes tells Priam that the blessed gods care for his son, dead though he is (24.422–423), as Apollo keeps all disfigurement from Hector's skin and covers him with the golden aegis while Achilles drags him (24.18–21).

αἰόλος, the epithet of the worms in Andromache's lament, is quick and glancing movement: it is said of a snake and of wasps, and of Xanthus, “the horse with flashing feet” (12.208, 12.167, 19.404; see [[flashing]]).
