## In Homer

**Whiter than snow.** Dolon, offering the Thracians to his captors, describes the king's horses first: “His are the finest horses I have seen, and the biggest: / whiter than snow, and like the winds in running” (10.436–437; see [[whiter-than-snow]] and [[rhesus]]). No other white horses are named in Homer. Achilles' horses fly with the breezes too (16.149; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). The horses are what Odysseus asks Athena for when he dedicates Dolon's spoils, “guide us to the horses and the beds of the Thracian men” (10.464), and what he takes for his own share of the work: “Untie the horses; / or else you kill the men, and the horses will be my care” (10.480–481).

**Taken.** They stand beside the sleeping king, “tied by straps to the end of the chariot rail” (10.475). While Diomedes kills, Odysseus drags the dead out of the way so that the horses will not shy at treading on corpses, “for they were not yet used to them” (10.491–493). Then he unties them, ties them together, and drives them out of the throng, striking them with his bow, since he had not thought to take the whip from the chariot, and whistles to Diomedes (10.498–502). When Athena warns Diomedes to go, he “mounted the horses”, and Odysseus struck them with his bow, “and they flew toward the swift ships of the Achaeans” (10.513–514). The two ride them back, the only riding in the Iliad outside a simile (15.679–684; see the translation's note on 10.513, 10.529 and [[horses]]). Behind them Hippocoon wakes to see “the place empty where the swift horses had stood” (10.520; see [[hippocoon]]).

**At the ships.** Diomedes whips them on from the place where Dolon died, “and the pair flew on not unwilling / toward the hollow ships; for there their hearts were glad to go” (10.530–531, verses that return at 11.519–520 of Nestor's horses). Nestor hears “the sound of swift-footed horses” before he sees them (10.535; see [[nestor]]). Seeing them, he asks whether some god gave them, for “they are terribly like the rays of the sun”, and he has never seen horses like them (10.545–551; see [[like-the-sun]]). Odysseus answers that a god could easily give better, and that these are Thracian and newly come (10.556–559). He drives them across the ditch, laughing aloud, and they are tied at Diomedes' manger beside his own horses, which are eating honey-sweet wheat (10.564–569; see [[diomedes]] and [[odysseus]]).

They are not mentioned again. In the chariot race of Book 23 Diomedes drives the horses of Tros that he took from Aeneas (23.290–292; see [[tros]]). Analysts took the silence as a sign that Book 10 was added to the poem; others point to all that ties the book to the night of Book 9 and to the books that follow (see [[the-doloneia-question]]).

## Outside Homer

In the *Rhesus*, handed down among the plays of Euripides, the shepherd who reports Rhesus' arrival saw a golden yoke on the necks of colts “brighter than snow” (*Rhesus* 303–304). Athena tells Odysseus and Diomedes that white mares are tied by the Thracian chariots, gleaming in the night like the wing of a river swan, and bids them kill the master and take them, “the finest spoil” (616–621). Rhesus' charioteer dreams that wolves have mounted the horses and are driving them, and wakes to find his master dying (780–791), and he accuses Hector of killing his allies for love of the horses (839–840; see [[euripides-rhesus]]).

Virgil adds what Homer does not tell. Among the pictures of the war that Aeneas sees at Carthage, Diomedes, bloody from the slaughter, turns the fiery horses away to the Greek camp, “before they could taste the fodder of Troy and drink the Xanthus” (*Aeneid* 1.469–473; see [[virgil-rhesus-and-dolon]]). Servius explains that the fate of Troy hung on these horses: if they had grazed in the Trojan plain or drunk from the Xanthus, Troy could not have been taken (on *Aeneid* 1.469).
