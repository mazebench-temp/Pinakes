σῦς and ὗς are two forms of one word, the pig, wild or tame. Latin *sus* and English *swine* and *sow* are related to it. κάπρος is the boar, and it is often joined to σῦς: the Achaeans around Diomedes stand like lions “or wild boars” (ἢ συσὶ κάπροισιν, 5.783). In the Iliad the animal is nearly always the wild boar (σῦς ἄγριος), hunted with dogs and spears in the mountains and thickets.

The boar is the beast that turns on its hunters. In the similes it bristles its back, its eyes blaze, and it whets its white tusks against dogs and men; it waits for them instead of running (11.414–420, 13.470–477). Idomeneus among the champions is “like a boar in courage” (4.253; see [[like-a-boar]]), and he waits for Aeneas like a boar at bay in Book 13. Ajax, Odysseus and the two Lapiths at the gate are all given the image (17.281–285, 11.414–420, 12.146–152). The lion is its match: Hector turns among his enemies like a boar or a lion (12.41–48), and a lion overcomes a boar at a spring when Hector kills Patroclus (16.823–826).

Outside the similes, a wild boar sent by Artemis ravaged the orchards of Oeneus at Calydon, and the fight over its head and hide began the war of the Curetes and Aetolians (9.538–549; see [[the-death-of-meleager]]). Meriones gives Odysseus a leather helmet set with rows of a boar's white tusks (10.261–265). When Agamemnon swears to Achilles in Book 19, the victim of the oath is a boar, and the herald throws its body into the sea (19.250–268). In the Odyssey Odysseus got his scar from a boar's tusk while hunting on Parnassus (19.428–458), Circe turns his men into swine (10.237–243), and the swineherd Eumaeus keeps his master's pigs in twelve sties (14.13–20). Artemis delights in boars and swift deer (Odyssey 6.102–104).
