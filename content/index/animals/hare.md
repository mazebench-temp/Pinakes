λαγωός is the hare. Homer has another name for it, πτώξ, “the cowerer”, from πτήσσω, “cower, crouch”, and once he joins the two, πτῶκα λαγωόν, “a cowering hare” (22.310). Speed and fear are its marks: the hare is πόδας ταχύς, “swift of foot”, and it lies hidden under a leafy bush (17.676–677).

In the Iliad it is always the quarry of a simile. In the chase of Dolon two sharp-toothed hounds, skilled in the hunt, press a fawn or a hare through wooded country, and it runs on ahead, crying out (10.360–364; see [[two-hounds]] and [[deer]]). The other two hares are the eagle's prey. Menelaus, looking for Antilochus among his companions, turns his eyes everywhere like the eagle, sharpest-sighted of birds, from which even on high the swift-footed hare cannot hide under its bush, and which swoops down and takes its life (17.674–681). Hector, drawing his sword for his last charge at Achilles, swoops like a high-flying eagle that comes down to the plain through the dark clouds to snatch a tender lamb or a cowering hare (22.306–311; see [[eagle]]). Hunted by dogs or by birds, the hare is the weakest of the animals in the similes, with the fawn and the lamb. In the Odyssey the young men used to take Odysseus' dog Argos out after wild goats, deer and hares (*Odyssey* 17.294–295).

## Outside Homer

The eagle and the hare became an omen in Aeschylus' *Agamemnon*. When the army set out, two eagles, the king of birds, one black and one white behind, appeared near the palace on the side of the spear hand, feasting on a pregnant hare and her unborn young, and the army's prophet knew the two “hare-feasters” (λαγοδαίτας) for the two sons of Atreus (*Agamemnon* 109–125).
