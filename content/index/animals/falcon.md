κίρκος is a bird of prey of the hawk family. Which bird the word named is not certain; it is rendered “falcon” here to keep it apart from the ἴρηξ, the usual Homeric word for the hawk (see [[hawk]]). Homer gives it three marks. It kills smaller birds: it is the bird “which brings slaughter to small birds” (ὅ τε σμικρῇσι φόνον φέρει ὀρνίθεσσιν, 17.757). It hunts the dove (πέλεια), in the Iliad and in the Odyssey alike (22.139–142; *Odyssey* 15.525–528). And it is the swiftest of birds, ἐλαφρότατος πετεηνῶν, “the nimblest of winged birds” (22.139; *Odyssey* 13.87). In the Odyssey the two words stand together, ἴρηξ κίρκος, “a hawk, a falcon”, as if the κίρκος were a kind of hawk: not even it could keep pace with the Phaeacian ship that carries Odysseus home (*Odyssey* 13.86–87).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the Odyssey a falcon is a sign. As Telemachus lands on Ithaca, it flies by on the right, “Apollo's swift messenger”, with a dove in its talons, plucking her and scattering her feathers on the ground between the ship and Telemachus himself; the seer Theoclymenus tells him that no other house in Ithaca is more kingly than his (*Odyssey* 15.525–534; see [[birds]] and [[divination]]).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle counts the κίρκος among the hawks: of the hawks the strongest is the triorchis, the second the aesalon, the third the κίρκος (*History of Animals* 9.36). In Aeschylus the falcon chasing the dove is the image of the sons of Aegyptus pursuing their cousins the Danaids. Danaus tells his daughters to sit at the altar like a swarm of doves “in fear of falcons of the same feather” (κίρκων τῶν ὁμοπτέρων φόβῳ), kinsmen who are enemies (*Suppliants* 223–225), and in the *Prometheus Bound* the suitors come on “falcons not far behind doves” (κίρκοι πελειῶν οὐ μακρὰν λελειμμένοι, *Prometheus Bound* 857).
