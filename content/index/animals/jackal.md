θώς, plural θῶες, is usually identified as the jackal, the golden jackal of Greece and the Near East, though Homer gives no description that would settle it. In Homer the θῶες are scavengers and lesser hunters of the mountains. They hunt in a pack, eat raw flesh, and run when a stronger beast comes.

Their one scene is a simile in Book 11. Trojans close around the wounded Odysseus “as blood-red jackals in the mountains” press around a horned stag that a hunter has hit with an arrow. The stag runs while its blood is warm; when it is beaten down, “the jackals that eat raw flesh devour it in the mountains in a shady glade”; then a god brings a lion, “and the jackals scatter in fear, and he devours it”. Ajax is the lion, and the Trojans scatter as he comes (11.473–486; see [[the-jackals-and-the-stag]]). δαφοινοί, “blood-red”, may describe their tawny coat or their bloody work (see the translation's note on 11.474), and ὠμοφάγοι, “eating raw flesh”, is the word for lions and for wolves too (ὠμοφάγοισιν 5.782, ὠμοφάγοι 16.157).

The jackal is named once more. Poseidon, rallying the Achaeans at the ships, says that the Trojans used to be like timid deer in a wood, the food of jackals, leopards and wolves, wandering without courage (θώων παρδαλίων τε λύκων τʼ ἤϊα, 13.101–104). Both passages set the jackal among the hunters of deer, and both rank it below the stronger beasts.

## Outside Homer

Aristotle describes the θώς in the *History of Animals*. Its insides are like the wolf's (2.17); it bears blind young, two to four in a litter, is long in the body and short in height, and is swift and leaps far though its legs are short (6.35). Lion and jackal are enemies, “for both eat raw flesh and live on the same food” (*History of Animals* 9.1). Jackals are friendly to men and neither harm them nor fear them much, but they fight dogs and lions, and so are not found in the same place (9.44). The enmity of jackal and lion is the turn of Homer's simile.
