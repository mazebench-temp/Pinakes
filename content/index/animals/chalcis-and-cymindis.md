Homer names this bird once, and gives it two names: ὄρνιθι λιγυρῇ ἐναλίγκιος, ἥν τʼ ἐν ὄρεσσι / χαλκίδα κικλήσκουσι θεοί, ἄνδρες δὲ κύμινδιν, “like a shrill-voiced bird, which in the mountains the gods call chalcis, and men cymindis” (14.290–291). It is one of the few things for which the poems report the language of the gods, and, as with the Hundred-Hander whom the gods call Briareus and men Aegaeon, the gods' name comes first (1.403–404; see [[language-of-gods-and-men]]). λιγυρός, “shrill”, is the sound of the winds' blasts (5.526, 13.590), of the whip (11.532) and of the Sirens' song (*Odyssey* 12.44, 12.183). The bird lives “in the mountains”, and Ida is a mountain of forests and wild beasts (14.283–285). What bird it was the poem does not say, and it cannot now be determined (see the translation's note on 14.290–291).

## Sleep in the fir

{{quote:6a31c5e6-b3fb-59a9-b1c1-0761023e834f}}

Hera has won Sleep's help with the promise of one of the younger Graces, Pasithea, and has sworn the oath he asked for (14.263–280; see [[sleep]] and [[hera]]). The two leave Lemnos and Imbros clothed in mist, come to Ida at Lectum, where they first leave the sea, and go on over the dry land, “and the treetops of the forest shook beneath their feet” (14.281–285; see [[ida]]). There Sleep stays back, out of the sight of Zeus, and climbs the tallest fir on Ida, which reaches up through the mist into the upper air; he sits hidden among its branches, and there he is like the bird (14.286–291; see [[fir-trees]]). Hera goes on alone up to the peak of Gargarus, where Zeus sees her (14.292–293; see [[gargarus]]).

Sleep has reason to stay out of sight. Once before he lulled Zeus to sleep for Hera, so that she could drive Heracles over the sea to Cos, and Zeus woke in a rage, flung the gods about his house and looked for Sleep above all, and would have thrown him from the upper air into the sea had Night not saved him (14.249–262; see [[hera-drives-heracles-to-cos]]). In the fir he waits unseen until the god is asleep, and then goes running to the ships to tell Poseidon (14.354–360).

ὄρνιθι … ἐναλίγκιος, “like a bird”: whether Sleep takes the shape of the bird or only sits in the branches as such a bird would, the poem leaves open. The same question is asked of Athena and Apollo, who sit on the oak of Zeus “like birds, like vultures” to watch the duel of Book 7 (7.58–61; see [[like-vultures]]). Elsewhere gods plainly go in the shape of birds, as when Athena leaves Nestor's company at Pylos in the likeness of a bird of prey, and all who see it are amazed (*Odyssey* 3.371–372).

## Outside Homer

[[Aristotle]] describes the bird in the *History of Animals*. The cymindis is seldom seen, for it lives in the mountains; it is black, the size of the hawk called the dove-killer, and long and slender in form. The Ionians call it cymindis, he says, and he quotes Homer's line (*History of Animals* 9.12; see [[hawk]]). By his account the men's name was the name in use in Ionia.

Plato's Socrates takes the bird, with the river that the gods call Xanthus and men Scamander, as evidence that Homer distinguishes the names that gods and men give the same things, and that the gods surely call things by their correct names; he asks how much more correct it is for the bird to be called chalcis than cymindis (*Cratylus* 391d–392a; see [[plato-chalcis-and-cymindis]]).

Proclus, commenting on the dialogue, finds the gods' names smoother and shorter than men's. The chalcis, he says, was named for its clear, ringing voice, like the sound of bronze (χαλκός), and the Chaldaeans call it so, having heard the name from the gods; the cymindis was named for the bird's smallness (*On the Cratylus* 71; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]]). Aristotle's bird is the size of a hawk. The Byzantine lexicon called the Suda glosses χαλκίς as “a kind of bird; the owl” (χ 45), and κυμίνδιος, also spelled κύμινδος, as “the eagle” (κ 2686). The later guesses disagree.
