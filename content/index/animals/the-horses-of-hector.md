**The names.** Hector names four horses in one verse (8.185). Ξάνθος, Xanthus, is from ξανθός, “yellow, fair”: a bay or chestnut. Πόδαργος, Podargus, joins πούς, “foot”, and ἀργός, which is both “shining white” and “swift” (the “swift dogs” of 1.50): “White-foot” or “Swift-foot”. Αἴθων, Aethon, is “Blaze”, from αἴθω, “burn” (see [[tawny]]). Λάμπος, Lampus, “Bright”, from λάμπω, “shine”, is called δῖος, “brilliant” (see [[brilliant]]). The same names go to other horses. Achilles' immortal pair are Xanthus and Balius, foaled by the Harpy Podarge (16.148–151), and he too calls them by name before he drives out (19.400). In the chariot race Menelaus drives his own Podargus beside Agamemnon's mare Aethe (23.295).

**Their keep.** Hector asks them to repay (ἀποτίνετον) the care (κομιδή) that Andromache gave them. She set honey-sweet wheat before them and mixed them wine to drink whenever they wished, “before me, who boast that I am her husband in my prime” (8.188–190; see [[andromache]] and [[wine]]). Wine for horses occurs nowhere else in Homer. Elsewhere horses eat white barley and spelt, as the Trojans' horses do at the end of this book (8.564; 5.195–196). He speaks of their keep as a debt, as the poem speaks of the return a son owes the parents who reared him (θρέπτρα, 4.477–478; see [[threptra]]). Then he sets them their task: to carry him to Nestor's golden shield and Diomedes' corselet, “If we could take these two, I would hope to make the Achaeans board their swift ships this very night” (8.191–197; see [[shield]] and [[corselet]]). Diomedes began with the same words when he set his heart on the horses of Aeneas: “If we could take these two, we would win noble fame” (5.273; see [[tros]]).

**Four horses.** The verse that names them is reported to have been rejected in antiquity. Homer's chariots run with a pair, sometimes with a trace horse beside it (8.87; 16.152), and a team of four appears only in Nestor's story of the prize horses his father sent to race in Elis (11.699) and in a simile in the Odyssey (Od. 13.81). Hector speaks to the four in the dual (ἀποτίνετον, 8.186; ἐφομαρτεῖτον and σπεύδετον, 8.191), with a plural between (ὑμῖν, 8.188), and earlier in the book his team is a pair: “not for long were the two horses without a driver” (ἵππω, 8.127). The dual is not strict in Homer, however. In this book the fates of the Achaeans, a plural, take a dual verb (ἑζέσθην, 8.73). The translation renders the verse as transmitted.

**In the battle.** They bring Hector into the book “through the onslaught” (8.88–89). Twice they shy back when his charioteer is killed, Eniopeus by Diomedes and Archeptolemus by Teucer, in the same two verses (8.122–123 = 8.314–315; see [[eniopeus]] and [[archeptolemus]]), and his brother Cebriones takes their reins (8.318–319; see [[cebriones]]). Hector boasts that “the horses will easily leap over the ditch they have dug” (8.179), and when the Achaeans have fled behind it he wheels them this way and that before the ships (8.348). In Book 12 they stand neighing at the edge of the ditch and will not leap it (12.50–59; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). In Book 11 Cebriones drives them over corpses and shields until the axle is spattered with blood (11.531–537; see [[hector]] and [[horses]]).

## Outside Homer

The names were given again to famous horses. In the *Aeneid* Pallas' warhorse Aethon, stripped of his trappings, walks weeping in his master's funeral procession (Virgil, *Aeneid* 11.89–90), and Ovid names Aethon among the four horses of the Sun (*Metamorphoses* 2.153–154; see [[virgil]] and [[ovid]]).
