βοῦς is the ox or cow. Cattle are the measure of wealth in Homer: a woman skilled in handiwork is valued at four oxen and a tripod at twelve (Iliad 23.702–705), and Glaucus trades gold armor worth a hundred oxen for bronze worth nine (Iliad 6.234–236). The [[hecatomb|hecatomb]] is named for them.

Driving off a neighbor's cattle was a common cause of war. Achilles has no such grievance against the Trojans: “They never drove off my cattle or my horses” ({{ref:Iliad 1.154}}; see [[raiding|raiding]] and [[horses|horses]]).

{{quote:6b30e14b-7e22-501d-8c5e-7c93953347e2}}

Hera's epithet βοῶπις ({{ref:Iliad 1.551}}, {{ref:Iliad 1.568}}) is rendered “ox-eyed”, that is, large-eyed. Homer gives it to mortal women too (Iliad 3.144). Some modern scholars have seen in it a memory of a goddess imagined as a cow, but in the poems it is simply praise.
