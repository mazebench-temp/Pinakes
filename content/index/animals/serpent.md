δράκων is Homer's word for a large snake. The name is usually derived from δέρκομαι (“look, glare”), aorist ἔδρακον: the δράκων is the creature with the fixed and terrible stare. Homer puts the two words side by side when Hector waits for Achilles like a snake at its hole that “glares terribly” (σμερδαλέον δὲ δέδορκεν, Iliad 22.95). ὄφις, the usual word for a snake in later Greek, occurs in Homer only once (Iliad 12.208).

The δράκων lives in the mountains, in a glen or at the mouth of its hole (Iliad 3.33–34, 22.93). It is red or dark: the snake at Aulis is “blood-red” (δαφοινός), the one the eagle carries at the Achaean wall is “blood-red” too (φοινήεις, Iliad 12.202), and the snakes on Agamemnon's armor are dark blue (κυάνεοι, Iliad 11.26). It is sudden and deadly; the snake of Book 22 has fed on poisons (βεβρωκὼς κακὰ φάρμακʼ, Iliad 22.94). Twice in the Iliad a snake is a portent sent by Zeus.

## Elsewhere in Homer

At the Achaean wall, as the Trojans are about to cross the ditch, an eagle flying on the left carries a huge blood-red snake, still alive. The snake bites the eagle on the breast, the eagle drops it among the Trojans and flies off screaming, and the Trojans shudder at “the portent of aegis-bearing Zeus” (Iliad 12.200–209). Polydamas reads it as a warning not to attack the ships (Iliad 12.210–229), and Hector will not be ruled by birds (Iliad 12.243). The sign at Aulis promised the Achaeans victory in the tenth year; this one warns the Trojans, and is ignored.

In the similes the snake is the sudden danger that makes a man recoil. Paris, seeing Menelaus, shrinks back into the Trojan ranks like a man who comes upon a snake in a mountain glen and springs away, trembling and pale (Iliad 3.33–37). Hector waits for Achilles like a snake at its hole, full of poison and anger, coiling and glaring (Iliad 22.93–97).

On Agamemnon's armor snakes are ornament and threat. Three dark-blue snakes on each side of his corselet stretch toward the neck, like the rainbows Zeus sets in the clouds as a sign for mortals, and a dark-blue snake with three heads coils on the strap of his shield (Iliad 11.26–28, 11.39–40). The Chimaera that Bellerophon killed was “a lion in front, a serpent behind, and in the middle a she-goat”, breathing fire (Iliad 6.179–183). In the Odyssey, Proteus turns himself into a snake among his other shapes (Odyssey 4.456–457).

## Outside Homer

The *Cypria*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, told of the sign of the snake and the sparrows at the gathering of the fleet at Aulis, and of Calchas' prophecy (see [[the-portent-at-aulis]]). In [[aeschylus|Aeschylus]]' *Agamemnon* the sign at Aulis is a different one, two eagles tearing a pregnant hare (104–159; see [[aeschylus-aulis]]). [[Cicero]] asked why Calchas read the sparrows, in which there was nothing portentous, and said nothing of the serpent, which is said to have been turned to stone, a thing that cannot happen (*On Divination* 2.65; see [[cicero-aulis-sparrows]]). [[Ovid]] retells Homer's version: his snake is dark blue, it climbs a plane tree to a nest of eight birds, seizes the mother as she flies around her loss, and becomes a stone in the shape of a snake (*Metamorphoses* 12.11–23).

[[Hesiod]]'s monsters are part snake. His Chimaera has three heads, a lion's, a goat's and a snake's (*Theogony* 319–322), and [[typhoeus|Typhoeus]] has a hundred heads of “a dread serpent”, flickering dark tongues (*Theogony* 824–826).

Latin borrowed the word as *draco*, and English *dragon* comes from it.
