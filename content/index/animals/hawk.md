ἴρηξ is the epic form of ἱέραξ, the hawk or falcon. Homer also names the κίρκος, a kind of hawk, “the lightest of winged creatures” (ἐλαφρότατος πετεηνῶν, 22.139; *Odyssey* 13.87), and once puts the two names together, ἴρηξ κίρκος (*Odyssey* 13.86–87). The hawk's epithets are of speed and of killing: ὠκύπτερος, “swift-winged” (13.62), ὠκύς, “swift”, φασσοφόνος, “dove-killer”, and ὤκιστος πετεηνῶν, “the swiftest of birds” (15.237–238).

## In Homer

The hawk is a hunter of smaller birds. Its prey in the similes is the dove, which Artemis is like when she flees from Hera into a hollow rock (21.493–495) and which Achilles' hawk swoops on (22.139–142), and the jackdaws and starlings that scatter before it (16.582–583, 17.755–757).

Its speed makes it a likeness for gods. Poseidon leaves the two Ajaxes “as a swift-winged hawk flies, that rises from a sheer and towering rock, and darts across the plain to chase some other bird” (13.62–65; see [[the-hawk-from-the-rock]]). Apollo goes down from the mountains of Ida like a swift hawk, the dove-killer (15.237–238), and Thetis leaps down from snowy Olympus like a hawk, carrying the armor Hephaestus made (18.616–617). It is a likeness for heroes in their onset: Patroclus charges the Lycians like a swift hawk (16.582–584), and Achilles swoops on Hector like a mountain hawk on a dove (22.139–142). And it is the measure of speed. Ajax tells Hector that the time is near when, in flight, he will pray that his fine-maned horses “may be swifter than hawks” (13.817–820), and in the Odyssey not even a hawk could keep pace with the ship of the Phaeacians (*Odyssey* 13.86–88).

In the Odyssey the hawk is also a bird of omen. As Telemachus speaks to Theoclymenus, a bird flies by on the right, “a hawk, Apollo's swift messenger”, tearing a dove in its talons and shedding its feathers on the ground between the ship and Telemachus, and Theoclymenus reads it as a sign that no family in Ithaca is more kingly than his, and that his house will always be powerful (*Odyssey* 15.525–534). Hawks roost with owls and sea crows in the trees around Calypso's cave (*Odyssey* 5.65–67). See [[birds]] and [[eagle]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells the kings a fable of the hawk and the nightingale. A hawk carries a nightingale high in the clouds in its talons and, when she cries, tells her that one far stronger holds her, and that she will go where he takes her, singer though she is; “foolish is he who would set himself against the stronger” (*Works and Days* 202–212; see [[hesiod]]).
