τέττιξ is the cicada, the large insect whose males sing in the trees through the hot months. To Greek ears the song was music. Hesiod marks the height of summer by it: when the thistle blooms, “the chirping cicada, sitting on a tree, pours down its clear song thick from under its wings” (*Works and Days* 582–584). In the *Shield* it sits on a green branch and sings to men all day from dawn, and its food and drink is the dew (393–397).

Homer names the cicada only once. The Trojan elders at the Scaean gates, too old for war but excellent speakers, sit on the tower “like the cicadas that in the woods sit on a tree and send out their lily-soft voice” (3.151–152; see [[like-cicadas]] and [[trojan-elders]]). The comparison brings together age, frailty and endless talk. Three of the elders, Lampus, Clytius and Hicetaon, are brothers of Priam and of Tithonus (20.237–238).

## Outside Homer

In the *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* Tithonus is given immortality without youth; he withers in Dawn's house until he cannot move, and only his voice flows on without end (218–238). The story that he shriveled into a cicada is later than the Hymn.

In Plato's *Phaedrus* the cicadas sing overhead in the midday heat while Socrates and Phaedrus talk by the Ilissus. Socrates tells their story: they were once men who, when the Muses were born and song first appeared, were so delighted by it that they forgot to eat and drink and died singing; from them came the cicadas, who need no food, sing from birth to death, and report to the Muses who honors them (258e–259d). Thucydides says that the older men of the wealthy Athenian families had only lately given up wearing their long hair fastened with golden cicadas (1.6.3).
