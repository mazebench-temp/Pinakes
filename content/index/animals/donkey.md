ὄνος, the donkey or ass, occurs once in Homer, at Iliad 11.558. Its epithet there, νωθής, “sluggish”, occurs only in the same verse. The animal itself is everywhere behind the poems, for the mule, ἡμίονος, the draft animal that pulls wagons, hauls timber and plows, is the offspring of a donkey and a mare, and its name means “half-donkey” (ἡμι-, “half”, and ὄνος; see [[mules]]).

The donkey of the simile is a farm animal with a mind of its own. Going past a field, it gets the better of the boys who guard it, goes in and crops the deep grain; many cudgels have been broken on its sides, and the boys beat it with more, “but their strength is childish”, and they drive it out with effort only “once he has had his fill of fodder” (11.558–562). So the Trojans and their allies follow Ajax, stabbing at his great shield, and cannot hurry him (11.563–565; see [[the-donkey-in-the-field]] and [[ajax]]). The traits the simile picks out are slowness, stubbornness, appetite, and a hide that blows do not reach. It comes directly after a lion simile for the same retreat, and the lion and the donkey give two sides of one man (see [[lion-driven-from-the-farmyard]]).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus tells how the donkeys of the Persian army helped Darius against the Scythians. Scythia, he says, is too cold for donkeys and mules, and the Scythian horses, which had never heard a donkey's bray or seen a mule, were thrown into confusion by them, pricking up their ears and turning back (Herodotus 4.129; compare 4.28). When Darius slipped away from the Scythians by night, he left the donkeys tied in the camp so that their braying would make the enemy think the army was still there (4.134–135).
