μυῖα is the fly. Homer's flies do three things. They swarm, and a cloud of them around the milk pails in spring gives the measure of a crowd of men (Iliad 2.469–473, 16.641–643). They come back however often they are brushed away: Athena puts into Menelaus “the daring of a fly”, which keeps trying to bite, “and human blood is sweet to it” (Iliad 17.570–572). And they feed on the dead. Achilles fears that flies will get into Patroclus' wounds and breed worms, and Thetis promises to keep away “the savage tribes, the flies that eat men slain in battle” (Iliad 19.23–31).

## Elsewhere in Homer

One fly is only a nuisance. Athena turns aside the arrow Pandarus shoots at Menelaus “as a mother brushes a fly from her child, when he lies in sweet sleep” (Iliad 4.130–131).

κυνάμυια (“dog-fly”, from κύων, “dog”, and μυῖα) is an insult the gods throw at one another: Ares at Athena, and Hera at Aphrodite (Iliad 21.394, 21.421). It joins the dog's shamelessness to the fly's persistence (see [[dogs]] and [[anaideia]]).

## Outside Homer

Lucian's playful *Praise of the Fly* calls Homer to witness for the insect's courage, since the poet gave Menelaus “the daring of a fly”.
