## Family

The Iliad names him only as the father of Alcestis, “most beautiful in form of the daughters of Pelias”, the wife of Admetus and mother of Eumelus (2.713–715; see [[alcestis]], [[admetus]] and [[eumelus]]). The Odyssey tells his birth: Tyro, the daughter of Salmoneus, lay with Poseidon, who had taken the likeness of the river Enipeus, “and she conceived and bore Pelias and Neleus” (*Odyssey* 11.254; see [[tyro]], [[poseidon]] and [[neleus]]). Aeson, Pheres and Amythaon, her sons by Cretheus, were his half-brothers (*Odyssey* 11.258–259; see [[aeson]], [[pheres]], [[amythaon]] and [[cretheus]]), so that Eumelus, grandson of Pheres and of Pelias, descends from Tyro on both sides.

## Descriptions

πολύρρηνος, “rich in flocks” (*Odyssey* 11.257; see [[rich-in-flocks]]). W. W. Merry derives it from a stem meaning “sheep”, and reports from La Roche that Aristarchus read πολύρηνος (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.257, and his critical note).

## Outside Homer

In the *Theogony* Pelias is “the great, overbearing king, / violent Pelias, reckless and harsh in deed”, who laid the many grievous labors on Jason, son of Aeson, before he could bring Aeetes' daughter home to Iolcus (*Theogony* 992–999; see [[hesiod]], [[jason]], [[aeetes]] and [[medea]]). Strabo says that it was from Iolcus that Pelias sent out Jason and the Argo (*Geography* 9.5.15; see [[strabo]]), and in Pindar Pelias holds the throne of Iolcus and sends his nephew Jason to fetch the Golden Fleece (*Pythian* 4). W. W. Merry tells the later story: after the death of Cretheus, Pelias refused his half-brother Aeson any share in the kingdom, drove out his brother Neleus and made himself king in Iolcus, then sent Aeson's son Jason after the golden fleece for fear that he would claim his rights, and murdered Aeson while Jason was away (on *Odyssey* 11.259). When Jason came back, Medea tricked Pelias' daughters into killing their father: Euripides' nurse says that Medea would never have come to Corinth had she not persuaded “the daughters of Pelias to kill their father”, and Medea herself reminds Jason that she killed Pelias “at the hands of his own children, the most painful way to die” (*Medea* 9–10, 486–487; see [[euripides]]). In Apollodorus and Ovid she persuades them to cut him up and boil him, on the promise that he would be made young again (Apollodorus 1.9.27; Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 7.297–349). His funeral games were a famous subject of early poetry and art.
