**At the elbow.** Deucalion is the eighth man Achilles kills after Apollo has saved Hector, after Echeclus (20.474–477; see [[echeclus-son-of-agenor]] and [[achilles]]). The spear goes “where the sinews of the elbow / hold together”: Walter Leaf takes this as the place where the muscles of the forearm join the elbow, and χείρ here as the arm, not the hand (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.478). His arm weighed down, he waits for Achilles: ὃ δέ μιν μένε, “he awaited him”, πρόσθʼ ὁρόων θάνατον, “seeing death before him” (20.480–481). Leaf thinks it probable that Zenodotus wrote προσορόων, a compound that does not occur in Homer; either way, he notes, the phrase is unique (on 20.481).

**The head.** Achilles strikes his neck with the sword and sends his head flying far, αὐτῇ πήληκι, “helmet and all”, and the marrow spurts from the vertebrae (20.481–483; see [[sword]] and [[helmet]]). The spear stops the man and the sword ends him, as with Demuchus a little before (20.458–459; see [[demuchus]]). So Peneleos, after his spear had gone through Ilioneus' eye, cut off his head with the sword and dashed it to the ground, “the head, helmet and all” (14.493–498; see [[ilioneus]] and [[peneleos]]). σφονδύλιοι, “vertebrae”, occurs only here in Homer, and μυελός, “marrow”, is found elsewhere in the Iliad only in Andromache's lament, of the marrow that Astyanax ate on his father's knees (22.500–501). Leaf takes ἔκπαλθʼ, “spurted”, as “throbbed forth”, perhaps by some confusion with the spurting of blood from a severed artery (on 20.483).

**The name.** Homer's other Deucalion is the son of Minos and father of Idomeneus, who tells his own descent from Zeus through Minos and Deucalion (13.449–453; see [[deucalion]] and [[idomeneus]]); Odysseus, posing as a Cretan, claims him as a father in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 19.178–181). The Deucalion of the flood, son of Prometheus, belongs to later tradition (see [[deucalion]]). The Trojan is a third man.
