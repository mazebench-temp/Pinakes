# Πολύκτωρ (24.397), the only Polyctor in the Iliad. The same form at Od. 17.207 is another man, one of the three
# who built the fountain of the town of Ithaca: except it there when the Odyssey is indexed.
grc: [Πολύκτωρ]

Μυρμιδόνων δʼ ἔξειμι, πατὴρ δέ μοί ἐστι Πολύκτωρ, “I am one of the Myrmidons, and my father is Polyctor” (24.397). Zeus has sent Hermes to bring Priam unseen to Achilles (24.331–338), and the god has met the old man by the river in the likeness of a young prince (24.347–361). When Priam asks him who he is and of what parents (24.387), he answers with a tale (see [[hermes]], [[myrmidons]] and [[truth-and-deception]]):

{{quote:f54d45b5-2d25-548f-bc4d-ad4c7f4fd403}}

The invented father is made in Priam's likeness. He is “wealthy, and an old man, just as you are” (24.398; see [[wealthy]]), and he has many sons, as Priam had fifty (24.495; see [[sons-of-priam]]); the god had already told Priam “you look to me like my dear father” (24.371). Of his seven sons one goes to the war, chosen by lot among his brothers (24.399–400; see [[lots]]). Walter Leaf reports a variant of that verse in the T scholia, which presses the obvious objection to the tale: if the speaker is now a youth “with the first down on his lip” (24.348), he must have been a child when Achilles sailed (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.400; see the translation's note on 24.400). Priam does not question it. He asks the attendant of Achilles for the truth about his son, and the god tells it (24.406–423; see [[hector]]).

The name seems to be formed from πολύς, “much”, and the root of κτάομαι, “acquire”, like πολυκτήμων, “rich in possessions” (5.613): a fit name for a wealthy father. The Odyssey has another Polyctor in Ithaca, who built the fountain of the town with Ithacus and Neritus (*Odyssey* 17.207), and the suitor Pisander is called the son of Polyctor (Πολυκτορίδης, *Odyssey* 18.299, 22.243).
