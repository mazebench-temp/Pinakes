## Etymology

He has two names, and the translation keeps them apart: Ἀλέξανδρος is "Alexander" and Πάρις is "Paris". Homer uses Alexander far more often; Book 3 has it twenty-two times, and Paris twice (3.325, 3.437).

Ἀλέξανδρος is a Greek compound of ἀλέξω ("ward off, defend") and ἀνήρ ("man"): "defender of men". A treaty of the early thirteenth century BC between the Hittite king Muwatalli II and Alaksandu, king of Wilusa, has led many scholars to connect Alaksandu with Alexandros and Wilusa with Ilios (see [[troy]]), but neither identification is certain. Πάρις has no convincing Greek etymology and may be an Anatolian name. Hector turns it into an insult, Δύσπαρι, "Evil Paris" (3.39): δυσ- ("ill, bad") + Πάρις (see [[evil-paris]]).

## Family

{{quote:1aa648d2-641f-53dc-95f6-b1cbe1ff6a62}}

Son of [[Priam]], who cannot bear to watch "my dear son" fight. Menelaus' spear strikes "the son of Priam" (Πριαμίδαο, 3.356). Later writers make Hecuba his mother.

{{quote:971c64db-808e-5bea-9938-b5f24e434ba6}}

Brother (κασίγνητος) of [[Hector]] and of [[lycaon-son-of-priam|Lycaon]], whose corselet he wears in the duel.

{{quote:8b86fe54-564c-59c4-8269-af605b8336b8}}

Husband of [[Helen]]. "Brilliant Alexander, the husband of Helen of the lovely hair" (Ἑλένης πόσις ἠϋκόμοιο) is a formula that names him by his wife; it returns in later books (7.355, 11.369; see [[husband-of-helen]]).

## Descriptions

{{quote:553b8de8-3760-5a18-a4d2-79fd88e6b536}}

θεοειδής = θεός ("god") + εἶδος ("form, looks"): "godlike in looks". It is his standing epithet in Book 3, used six times, and it names the one thing everyone grants him. He first appears with an archer's leopard skin (παρδαλέη; see [[leopard-skin]]) and curved bow (καμπύλα τόξα), and with a spearman's two spears.

{{quote:450c586e-307a-5d7b-8b64-fee70366ca71}}

Hector's insults, in one line: Δύσπαρι, "Evil Paris"; εἶδος ἄριστε, "best in looks" ([[best-in-looks]]); γυναιμανής = γυνή ("woman") + μαίνομαι ("be mad"): "mad for women"; ἠπεροπευτής, "seducer", from ἠπεροπεύω ("beguile"; see [[eperopeuo]]). Hector repeats the line in Book 13 (13.769). See [[eidos]] and [[beauty-and-its-price]].

{{quote:d10cf66b-61cf-5306-8071-257cfecad967}}

His graces as his brother lists them: the lyre (κίθαρις), his hair (κόμη) and his looks, "the gifts of Aphrodite". Diomedes, wounded by his arrow, calls him “Archer, insulter, glorying in your bow, ogler of girls” (11.385).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Iliad tells his past in allusions: the ships that Phereclus built for him, "the beginning of evils" (5.59–64), and the voyage home with Helen by way of Sidon (6.289–292). In Book 6 Hector finds him at home handling his armor and rebukes him again, and he answers with the line he used in Book 3 (6.313–341; 6.333 = 3.59). In Book 7 he refuses to give up Helen, though he will give back the treasure (7.354–364). His arrows wound Diomedes, Machaon and Eurypylus (11.369–383, 11.505–507, 11.581–584). The dying Hector foretells that Paris and Apollo will kill Achilles at the Scaean gates (22.359–360), and in the last book Priam counts him among the surviving sons he calls "liars and dancers" (24.249–262).

The same book gives the only allusion in Homer to the Judgment of Paris. Hera, Poseidon and Athena keep their hatred of Troy "because of the blind folly of Alexander, who insulted the goddesses when they came to his steading, and praised the one who gave him grievous lust" (24.25–30). [[Aristarchus]] rejected these lines, holding that Homer did not know the story.

## Outside Homer

The *Cypria*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, told the Judgment on Ida and the abduction. Aphrodite promised him Helen; Menelaus entertained him at Sparta and then sailed for Crete; the lovers left by night with the treasure, and a storm drove Paris to Sidon before he reached Troy. Herodotus says that in the *Cypria* the voyage took three days (2.117). In the *Little Iliad* Philoctetes kills him in single combat, and Deiphobus marries Helen. [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] tells of Hecuba's dream that she bore a firebrand, of the child exposed on Ida and raised by a herdsman, and of the name Alexander, won by defending the flocks against robbers (*Library* 3.12.5).

Another tradition kept Helen out of Troy: Stesichorus' palinode (Plato, *Phaedrus* 243a–b, *Republic* 9.586c; see [[stesichorus-palinode]]) and the story Herodotus heard in Egypt (2.112–120; see [[helen-in-egypt]]). Euripides' lost *Alexandros* (415 BC) told how the exposed child came home and was recognized, and Ovid gave Paris and Helen an exchange of letters (*Heroides* 16–17; see [[ovid-heroides]]).
