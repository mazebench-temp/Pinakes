## Family

Wife of [[Antenor]] (5.70–71; 6.299) and daughter of [[Cisseus]], who brought up her son Iphidamas in Thrace: he was Iphidamas' “mother's father, who fathered fair-cheeked Theano” (11.221–224). Agamemnon kills Iphidamas, and then his elder brother Coon, who tries to avenge him (11.221–263). Of Antenor's many other sons, the Iliad does not say which were hers. It does say that she brought up one who was not:

{{quote:956d6071-3d2b-55f1-9d7b-e8283d194078}}

A νόθος is a son born to a man by a woman who is not his wife (see [[nothos]] and [[pedaeus]]). πύκα, “with care”, and ἶσα φίλοισι τέκεσσι, “like her own dear children”, are praise; χαριζομένη πόσεϊ ᾧ, “to please her husband”, gives the reason. The narrator tells it in the moment of the young man's death, when Meges drives his spear through the back of Pedaeus' head (5.72–75; see [[meges]]). Her epithets are δῖα, “brilliant” (5.70; see [[brilliant]]), and καλλιπάρῃος, “fair-cheeked” (6.298, 6.302, 11.224).

{{notes}}

## In Homer

She is the only woman Homer calls a priestess (ἱέρεια, 6.300). In Book 6 she opens Athena's temple on the citadel to Hecuba and the old women of Troy, lays their robe on the goddess's knees, and prays for the city, and Athena shakes her head (6.297–311; see [[the-robe-for-athena]], [[priesthood]] and [[temple]]).

## Outside Homer

In Bacchylides' dithyramb on the embassy to Troy, Theano receives Odysseus and Menelaus when they come to ask for Helen (Bacchylides 15; see [[bacchylides-embassy]] and [[the-embassy-to-troy]]). Euripides makes Cisseus the father of Hecuba (*Hecuba* 3), whose father in the Iliad is Dymas (16.718).
