## The name

Homer names him once, in the genitive that makes the Laestrygonians' city his: Λάμου αἰπὺ πτολίεθρον, “the steep citadel of Lamus” (*Odyssey* 10.81; see [[telepylus]]). The poem says nothing else of him. Fäsi, as W. W. Merry reports, took Ἀντιφάτης and Λάμος for “the double title of the murderous king of the land”, the first from φένω, πέφαται, “slay”, the second from λαμός, λαιμός, “gullet”, and Merry compares Λαμία, the child-devouring ogress (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 10.81; see [[antiphates-the-laestrygonian]]). Some commentators took Lamus for the name of the town, comparing the city of Ilios (*Iliad* 5.642); Merry takes him for the king, as the scholia did, which make him a son of Poseidon (on *Odyssey* 10.81). Elsewhere in Homer the genitive before αἰπὺ πτολίεθρον is the name of a town, Dium or Pylos (*Iliad* 2.538; *Odyssey* 3.485).

## Outside Homer

The Roman poets made Lamus a founder. Horace greets Aelius Lamia as “noble from ancient Lamus”, from whom the earlier Lamiae took their name, and who is said to have been the first to hold the walls of Formiae and the Liris flooding Marica's shores (*Odes* 3.17.1–8; see [[horace]]). Ovid's Macareus comes to “the ancient city of Lamus the Laestrygonian”, where Antiphates now ruled (*Metamorphoses* 14.233–234; see [[ovid]]), and Silius Italicus names Caieta, “ruled by Lamus”, beside “the house of Antiphates” (*Punica* 8.529–530). Cicero, writing from Formiae, calls the town “this Telepylus of the Laestrygonians” (*Letters to Atticus* 2.13.2; see [[cicero]]), but does not name Lamus, though Merry cites the letter for him.
