## The name

Εὐρυμέδων joins εὐρύ, “widely”, and μέδων, “ruling”: “wide-ruling”, a name that fits a king. Homer gives the same name to two servants in the Iliad, Agamemnon's charioteer, the son of Ptolemaeus (*Iliad* 4.228; see [[eurymedon]]), and Nestor's attendant (*Iliad* 8.114, 11.620; see [[eurymedon-attendant-of-nestor]]); nothing joins either of them to the king of the Giants.

## In Homer

He is named once, when Athena tells Odysseus the descent of Arete and Alcinous:

{{quote:af1d5655-cc0e-59b0-95a2-d04e6647fa80}}

His youngest daughter, Periboea, “most beautiful in form of women”, bore Nausithous to Poseidon, and Nausithous was the father of Rhexenor and Alcinous (*Odyssey* 7.56–63). So the king of the Giants is the great-grandfather of Alcinous, and of Arete through her father Rhexenor (see [[periboea-mother-of-nausithous]], [[nausithous]], [[alcinous]], [[rhexenor]], [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[giants]]). The poem says nothing else of him.

## Outside Homer

Pausanias, as Merry quotes him, found in these verses the plainest proof that Homer's Giants were mortal men and not a divine race (Pausanias 8.29.2, in Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 7.59; see [[pausanias]] and [[giants]]).
