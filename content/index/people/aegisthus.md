## Family

Son of [[Thyestes]]: Θυεστιάδης Αἴγισθος, “Aegisthus, son of Thyestes”, lived in the house where Thyestes had lived before him, at the far edge of the land (*Odyssey* 4.517–518). The renderings of books of the Odyssey other than the first three are plain renderings, not the translation.

## Descriptions

- ἀμύμων, “noble” (*Odyssey* 1.29), which Merry takes as almost titular, with no moral sense (see [[blameless]]).
- δολόμητις, “scheming” (*Odyssey* 1.300; 3.198, 3.250, 3.308; 4.525; see [[schemer]]).
- ἄναλκις, “the coward”, in Nestor's account of his funeral feast (*Odyssey* 3.310), as Cassandra calls him a cowardly lion in Aeschylus (*Agamemnon* 1224).

## Mythology

While the Achaeans were at Troy he seduced Clytemnestra, though the gods had sent Hermes to warn him; when Agamemnon came home he killed him at a feast, ruled Mycenae for seven years, and in the eighth was killed by Orestes (*Odyssey* 1.35–43; 3.262–275, 3.303–310; 4.512–537; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]] and [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]]).

## Outside Homer

In Aeschylus' *Agamemnon* he claims the murder as just revenge for his father Thyestes, whom Atreus had fed on his own children, and calls himself the one who stitched the plot together from outside the house (*Agamemnon* 1577–1611; see [[aeschylus]] and [[the-feast-of-thyestes]]). Aristotle uses him to show that the happy ending belongs to comedy: “there, those who are the bitterest enemies in the story, such as Orestes and Aegisthus, go off at the end as friends, and nobody is killed by anybody” (*Poetics* 1453a36–39; see [[the-double-plot]]).
