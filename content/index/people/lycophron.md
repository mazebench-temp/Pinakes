**The attendant.** Λυκόφρονα Μάστορος υἱὸν / Αἴαντος θεράποντα Κυθήριον, “Lycophron, son of Mastor, / the attendant of Ajax, from Cythera” (15.430–431; see [[mastor]], [[ajax]] and [[cythera]]). A θεράπων is the companion who serves a chief and fights at his side, as Meriones serves Idomeneus and Patroclus Achilles. He had come to Ajax as an exile: “who lived with him, / since he had killed a man in holy Cythera” (15.431–432). Medon, whose death the same book has told a hundred verses earlier, lived in Phylace for the same reason (15.333–336), and Patroclus, who as a boy killed the son of Amphidamas in a quarrel over knucklebones, was brought from Opus to Peleus, who reared him and named him Achilles' attendant (σὸν θεράποντʼ ὀνόμηνεν, 23.85–90; see [[exile-and-refuge]], [[medon]] and [[patroclus]]). The killer becomes the attendant of the house that received him. Whom Lycophron killed, and how a man of Cythera came to Telamon's sons, the poem does not say.

**His death.** Hector has thrown at Ajax to save the body of his cousin Caletor, whom Ajax has just speared as he brought fire to the ship (15.419–429; see [[hector]] and [[caletor]]). He misses, and the spear strikes Lycophron “on the head above the ear” as he stands near Ajax; he falls on his back “from the stern of the ship to the ground” (15.433–435). He was fighting from the ship, as the Achaeans fight high on the ships with their long spears while the Trojans come at the sterns in their chariots (15.385–389; see [[stern]] and [[ships]]). A spear meant for one man that kills the man beside him is a pattern of the battle at the ships: Hector's spear thrown at Teucer killed Amphimachus (13.183–187), Deiphobus' thrown at Idomeneus killed Hypsenor (13.402–412), and Ajax's thrown at Polydamas killed Archelochus (14.459–468; see [[the-battle-at-the-ships]]).

**Ajax's grief.** “And Ajax shuddered, and spoke to his brother: / ‘Teucer, my friend, our trusted companion has been killed, / the son of Mastor, who came from Cythera and lived in our house, / and whom we honored in our halls like our own dear parents’” (15.436–439; see [[teucer]], [[telamon]] and [[trusted-companion]]). The dual νῶϊ, “we two”, makes the brothers one household, and the honor is a son's honor to his parents. The same book gives the Trojan side a match. Melanippus, son of Hicetaon, came back to Ilios when the Achaean ships arrived, “and lived beside Priam; and Priam honored him as he honored his children” (15.550–551 = 13.175–176, where the verses were said of Imbrius, Priam's son-in-law; see [[melanippus-son-of-hicetaon]] and [[imbrius]]). Both men are honored like kin in another man's house, and both die at the ships, Lycophron by Hector's spear and Melanippus by Antilochus'. Ajax's grief turns at once to the bow: “Where now are your arrows / of swift death, and the bow that Phoebus Apollo gave you?” (15.440–441), and Teucer's first arrow kills Cleitus, the charioteer of Polydamas (15.445–453; see [[cleitus]]).

**The name.** Λυκόφρων joins λύκος, “wolf”, and φρήν, “mind”. He is the only Lycophron in Homer.

## Outside Homer

The name was borne by historical men. Aristotle cites a sophist Lycophron for his frigid style and names a Lycophron of Pherae in a speech made in court (*Rhetoric* 1405b36, 1406a7, 1410a18; see [[lycophron-the-sophist]] and [[lycophron-of-pherae]]), and Herodotus tells of Lycophron, the son of Periander of Corinth, who would not speak to the father who had killed his mother (3.50.3, 3.52.6).
