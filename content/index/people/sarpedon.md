## Descriptions

{{quote:5ea519a8-cd28-560d-8c06-98a8618dbf0c}}

The Trojan catalogue ends with its farthest and most important allies, “from far-off Lycia, from the eddying Xanthus” (2.877). Sarpedon is named first and Glaucus second, as they are throughout the poem. The phrase τηλόθεν ἐκ Λυκίης, “from far-off Lycia”, is one Sarpedon himself takes up in *Iliad* 5: “Lycia is far away, on the eddying Xanthus, / where I left my dear wife and my infant son” (5.479–480). See [[lycia]] and [[xanthus-of-lycia]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

Sarpedon is the son of Zeus and of Laodameia, the daughter of Bellerophon; Glaucus is his cousin (6.196–206). In *Iliad* 5 he rebukes Hector: the allies do the fighting while Hector's brothers-in-law and brothers hang back (5.471–492). Tlepolemus, a son of Heracles, wounds him in the thigh, and he kills Tlepolemus; “but his father still kept ruin away” (5.628–662). At the Achaean wall he tells Glaucus why the Lycians honor them like gods, with the best seats, meat and full cups, and why they must therefore fight in the front line: if they could escape the war and be ageless and immortal, he would not fight, “but now, since in any case the fates of death stand over us, / countless, which no mortal can flee or escape, / let us go” (12.310–328). Then he pulls down a battlement (12.397–399).

In *Iliad* 16 Patroclus kills him. Zeus, grieving, thinks of snatching his son out of the battle. Hera answers that the other gods would not approve, and Zeus lets him die, raining drops of blood on the earth in honor of his son (16.431–461). After the fight over his body, Apollo washes and anoints it, and Sleep and Death carry it home to Lycia, where his kinsmen will bury him with a tomb and a pillar, “for that is the prize of the dead” (16.666–683).

## Outside Homer

Later tradition made Sarpedon a son of Zeus and [[europa|Europa]] and a brother of Minos; the scholia on 12.292 tell the story of Europa and the bull and say that it is in Hesiod and Bacchylides (scholium A). In a papyrus fragment of Aeschylus' *Carians* (or *Europa*), Europa tells how Zeus carried her off and she bore him three sons, Minos, Rhadamanthys and Sarpedon, and fears for Sarpedon, now that the Greeks have come to sack Troy (fr. 99 Nauck; much of the text is restored). Herodotus says that he led settlers from Crete to Lycia (Herodotus 1.173). Strabo, telling how the Cretan Sarpedon, brother of Minos and Rhadamanthys, brought the Termilae to the land later called Lycia, observes that the poet makes his Sarpedon a native of the country (*Geography* 12.8.5; see [[strabo]]). Mythographers reconciled the two versions by giving him a life of three generations (Apollodorus 3.1.2). Sleep and Death carrying his body is a favorite subject of Athenian vase painting, most famously on the calyx krater painted by Euphronios around 515 BC.

Plato's Socrates quotes Zeus' lament, “αἲ αἲ ἐγών …” (16.433–434), with his grief for Hector (22.168–169), as verses in which the poets make the greatest of the gods lament so unlike himself; if the young took such lines seriously, they would sing dirges over small sufferings (*Republic* 3.388c–d; see [[plato]] and [[plato-laments-of-gods-and-heroes]]). In the *Aeneid* Jupiter consoles Hercules, who weeps for Pallas, with the death of his own son: so many sons of gods fell beneath the high walls of Troy, and with them “Sarpedon, mea progenies”, “Sarpedon, my own offspring” (*Aeneid* 10.464–472; see [[virgil]] and [[jupiter-and-sarpedon]]).
