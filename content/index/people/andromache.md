## Etymology

Ἀνδρομάχη = ἀνήρ (“man”) + μάχη (“battle”): “she who fights with men”, or “battle of men”, a warrior's name like her son's.

## Family

{{quote:3764cc28-e031-539d-9faa-2cb5029e9531}}

She is the daughter of [[Eetion]], king of the [[Cilicians]] at [[Thebe]] under [[Placus]]; “daughter of great-hearted Eetion” is her formula, and Hector uses it again to his horses (8.187). Achilles killed her father and her seven brothers on one day; her mother, taken captive and ransomed, was struck down by Artemis “in her father's halls” (6.413–428; see [[sack-of-thebe]]). Hector took her from the house of Eetion, “after giving a countless bride-price” (22.470–472; see [[marriage]]). Their son is [[Astyanax]] (6.399–403). At Troy her kin are Hector's sisters and his brothers' wives (6.378, 22.473; see [[in-laws]]).

## Descriptions

λευκώλενος, “white-armed” (6.371, 6.377, 24.723), is also the epithet of Hera and of Helen (see [[white-armed]]). πολύδωρος (6.394, 22.88; see [[richly-dowered]]) is “richly dowered”, bringing many gifts to her husband's house, or “won with many gifts”.

## Elsewhere in Homer

Hector reminds his horses that Andromache fed them honey-sweet wheat and mixed wine for them before she served him, “who boast that I am her husband in my prime” (8.185–190). When he puts on Achilles' armor, Zeus pities him: Andromache will never take those arms from his hands (17.201–208).

In Book 22 she is at her loom, with a bath heating for his return, when she hears the wailing from the tower; she runs to the wall, sees him dragged toward the ships, faints, and laments him and the orphan their son will be (22.437–515; see [[astyanax]]).

Over his body in the last book she leads the first of the three laments, holding his head, and foresees the sack of the city, her own captivity and her son's death (24.723–746; see [[the-laments-for-hector]]).

## Outside Homer

Sappho sang of her wedding: Hector and his companions bring her by sea “from holy Thebe and ever-flowing Placia”, and Troy comes out to meet them (fr. 44; see [[sappho-wedding-of-hector-and-andromache]]). The epic cycle made her a slave. In the *Little Iliad* Neoptolemus takes her to the ships and throws her son from the tower, of his own will, Pausanias notes, and not by the Greeks' decision (10.25.9); in the *Iliupersis*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, Neoptolemus takes her as his prize (see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). In Euripides' *Trojan Women* (415 BC) the Greeks take her son from her (see [[euripides-astyanax]]); in his *Andromache* she is Neoptolemus' concubine in Phthia and the mother of his son, persecuted by his wife Hermione, until Thetis sends her to marry Helenus (see [[euripides-andromache]] and [[helenus]]). Pausanias names her three sons by Neoptolemus, Molossus, Pielus and Pergamus, and knew a shrine of Andromache at Pergamum (1.11.1–2). Virgil's Aeneas finds her at Buthrotum, the wife of Helenus, and she sees her son in young Ascanius: *o mihi sola mei super Astyanactis imago*, “the only image left to me of my Astyanax” (*Aeneid* 3.294–505, 3.489; see [[virgils-andromache]]). Sophocles' Tecmessa pleads with Ajax in words modeled on hers (see [[sophocles-tecmessa]]), and Racine made her the heroine of *Andromaque* (1667; see [[racine-andromaque]]). See also [[porcia-and-andromache]] and [[andromaches-dream]].
