**The spear meant for Idomeneus.** Deiphobus, grieving for Asius, throws at Idomeneus, who crouches under his round shield; the spear flies over and grazes the shield with a dry ring (13.402–410; see [[deiphobus]] and [[idomeneus]]).

{{quote:63153acd-36b6-5a53-8e28-cddcce4b481e}}

ποιμὴν λαῶν, “shepherd of the people”, is the title of kings and leaders (see [[shepherd-of-the-people]]). Deiphobus boasts that Asius, on his way to the house of Hades, will be glad, “since I have given him an escort” (13.414–416; see [[asius]] and [[gatekeeper]]).

**Carried from the field.** The Argives grieve at the boast, Antilochus most of all, but “even in his grief he did not neglect his companion”: he runs and stands over him and covers him with his shield, and two trusty companions, Mecisteus, son of Echius, and brilliant Alastor, stoop under him and carry him, groaning heavily, to the hollow ships (13.417–423; see [[antilochus]], [[mecisteus-son-of-echius]] and [[alastor-who-carries-teucer]]). The last four verses are those that carried the wounded Teucer from the field in Book 8, with Antilochus in Ajax's place (13.420–423 = 8.331–334; see [[teucer]]).

**Dead or dying.** “At once loosened his knees” is the verse of a killing: it ends Apisaon son of Phausius and Apisaon son of Hippasus, the second after a verse built like 13.411 (11.578–579, 17.348–349; see [[apisaon]]). Deiphobus speaks of an escort to Hades. Yet in the text as printed the man carried off groans (στενάχοντα, 13.423); Aristarchus read the dual, στενάχοντε, which gives the groaning to the two bearers. Either he is dying, or the verses of Teucer's rescue have brought their groaning with them. He is not named again (see the translation's note on 13.412–423).

The name seems to join ὕψι, “on high”, to the -ήνωρ of Agenor and Elephenor, from ἀνήρ, “man”. The Trojan Hypsenor, son of the priest Dolopion, is another man (5.76–83; see [[hypsenor]]).
