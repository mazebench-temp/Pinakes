**The hunter.** αἵμονα θήρης, “skilled in the hunt”: αἵμων, “skilled, eager”, is found only here, and its exact sense is disputed. He was “a good hunter”, for “Artemis herself had taught him to shoot all the wild things that the forest nurtures in the mountains” (5.51–52). This is the first time Artemis is named in the Iliad (see [[artemis]]). The hunter's skill is named with the archer god's word: ἑκηβολίαι, “far-shooting”, found only here, is the noun of ἑκηβόλος, the epithet of her brother Apollo (see [[hekatebolos]]).

**A gift that does not save.** “But Artemis of the showering arrows was no help to him then, nor his far-shooting, in which he had excelled before” (5.53–54). The Iliad's obituaries often name a man's gift at the moment it fails him. Of the augur Ennomus the Catalogue says that “his birds did not save him from black doom” (2.859), and the sons of the seer Merops went to war against their father's warning (2.831–834; see [[the-sons-of-merops]]). In this book the builder Phereclus, whom Athena loved, dies in flight (5.59–68; see [[phereclus]]), and the old dream-reader Eurydamas reads no dreams for his sons (5.149–150).

**The death.** A man who shot from afar is killed at close range, from behind, as he flees. Menelaus stabs him “in the back as he fled before him, between the shoulders” (5.56–57); the second verse is the one just used of Agamemnon's first kill (5.41). He falls face down (πρηνής, 5.58). See [[menelaus]] and [[androktasia]].

**The name.** Σκαμάνδριος, “of the Scamander”, is a Trojan name taken from the river, as Simoeisius was named for the Simoeis (4.473–477; see [[simoeisius]]). Hector called his own son Scamandrius, though the Trojans called the boy Astyanax (6.402–403). See [[scamander]].
