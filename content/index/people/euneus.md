## Etymology

Εὔνηος (Eunēos) looks like a compound of εὖ, “well”, and νηῦς, “ship”: “of the good ships”. The name suits the son of the captain of the Argo, and a man whose ships carry wine to the Achaean camp. Homer also calls him Ἰησονίδης, “son of Jason” (7.468, 7.471; 23.747).

## Family

{{quote:9e295d6b-7ab0-5acc-81d5-db760bb71c25}}

His father is [[Jason]], “shepherd of the people” (7.469; see [[shepherd-of-the-people]]), and his mother [[Hypsipyle]], a woman of Lemnos. The line takes for granted the story that later poets told at length: the Argo put in at Lemnos, and Jason lay with Hypsipyle (see [[the-lemnian-women]]). It is the Iliad's only allusion to the Argonauts.

**Wine for the camp.** When the wall is finished and the Achaeans have eaten, “ships from Lemnos stood by, bringing wine, many of them, which Euneus, son of Jason, had sent” (7.467–468). To the sons of Atreus he gives a thousand measures of wine “apart” (7.470–471; see [[atreidae]]), and the rest the Achaeans buy with bronze, iron, hides, cattle and slaves (7.472–475; see [[trade]], [[wine]] and [[lemnos]]). He honors the commanders with a gift and sells to the army.

## Elsewhere in Homer

**Lycaon.** Euneus appears twice more, both times through Lycaon, a son of Priam (see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). Achilles caught Lycaon by night in his father's orchard and sold him on Lemnos, “and the son of Jason paid the price” (υἱὸς Ἰήσονος ὦνον ἔδωκε, 21.41). A guest-friend, Eetion of Imbros, ransomed him from there, and he escaped home, only to fall into Achilles' hands again at the river twelve days later (21.34–48).

**The silver bowl.** At the funeral games for Patroclus, Achilles sets out as the first prize for the footrace a silver mixing bowl of six measures, the most beautiful on earth. Sidonians made it, and Phoenician traders carried it over the sea and gave it to Thoas; “and as the price of Lycaon, son of Priam, Euneus, son of Jason, gave it to the hero Patroclus” (23.740–747; see [[mixing-bowl]], [[sidonians]] and [[patroclus]]). Odysseus wins it (23.778). Thoas is presumably the king of Lemnos, “the city of godlike Thoas” (14.230), whom later poets make Hypsipyle's father; the bowl would then have come to Euneus from his grandfather.

## Outside Homer

In Euripides' *Hypsipyle*, a play known from papyrus fragments, Hypsipyle is a slave at Nemea, and her two sons by Jason, Euneus and Thoas, come in search of her and recognize her (see [[hypsipyle]] and [[euripides-hypsipyle]]). Apollodorus names the sons she bore to Jason as Euneus and Nebrophonus (*Library* 1.9.17). An Athenian family of lyre-singers who served at sacred rites was called the Euneidae (Suda ε 3592), and its name is usually connected with his.
