## Etymology

Ἀστεροπαῖος is formed from ἀστεροπή, “lightning” (11.184), the word in Zeus' title ἀστεροπητής, “lord of the lightning” (12.275; see [[lord-of-the-lightning]]).

## Family

His father is Pelegon, whom the broad-flowing Axius fathered on Periboea, the eldest of the daughters of Acessamenus (21.141–143). When he tells his lineage to Achilles he begins from the river, in the words with which the Catalogue praised it (21.157–158 ≈ 2.849–850; see [[axius]]).

## Descriptions

ἀρήϊος, “warlike” (12.102, 17.352; see [[warlike]]); ἥρως, “the hero” (21.163; see [[heros]]); and Πηλεγόνος … φαίδιμος υἱός, “the shining son of Pelegon” (21.152; see [[phaidimos]]).

## In Homer

**At the wall.** He is first named among the leaders of the five columns that attack the Achaean wall on foot. Sarpedon, who leads the allies, picks him and Glaucus as the best of the rest after himself:

{{quote:76087caf-0c52-57c8-9e75-397bce1ecd75}}

Book 12 gives him nothing more to do (see [[sarpedon]], [[glaucus]] and [[trojan-allies]]). The Catalogue does not name him: there Pyraechmes leads the Paeonians, “with their curved bows” (2.848; see [[paeonians]] and [[pyraechmes]]).

**Over Patroclus.** Hector names him among the allies he urges on in Book 17 (17.217). When Lycomedes kills Apisaon, son of Hippasus, a Paeonian who “after Asteropaeus was the best at fighting”, “warlike Asteropaeus” pities him and charges, but cannot break through the shields the Achaeans hold around Patroclus' body (17.348–355; see [[lycomedes]]).

**Against Achilles.** In Book 21 Achilles, killing Trojans in the river, leaps at him. He stands up out of the water holding two spears, and the river Xanthus, angry for the young men slaughtered in its stream, puts might in his heart (21.139–147; see [[scamander]] and [[achilles]]). Achilles asks who he is, with Diomedes' threat to Glaucus, “It is the sons of unhappy men who meet my might” (21.151 = 6.127; see [[the-sons-of-unhappy-men]]). He answers with Glaucus' question to Diomedes, “why do you ask about my lineage?” (21.153 ≈ 6.145; see [[genealogy-in-battle]] and [[genee]]): he comes from Paeonia, far away, leading the Paeonians “with long spears”; this is the eleventh dawn since he came to Ilios; and his line goes back to the Axius (21.154–160).

He is περιδέξιος (21.163), a word Homer uses only here, “skilled with both hands”, and he throws both spears at once. One strikes Achilles' shield and is stopped by the gold, the god's gift; the other grazes his right forearm, and the dark blood spurts (21.164–168). No one else in the Iliad draws Achilles' blood. Achilles' spear misses and sticks in the bank. Three times Asteropaeus tries to pull it out, and the fourth time to bend and break it, before Achilles kills him with the sword (21.169–181; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]). Leaping on his chest, Achilles strips him and boasts that it is hard for one born of a river to contend with the children of Zeus, for Zeus is stronger than rivers, and even Ocean fears his thunderbolt (21.182–199). He leaves him in the sand for the eels and fish, and routs the Paeonians, who had fled when they saw their best man fall (21.200–210; see [[aristos]]).

**The spoils.** At Patroclus' games Achilles gives Eumelus the bronze corselet he took from Asteropaeus, with a casting of shining tin around it (23.560–562; see [[eumelus]]). The silver-studded Thracian sword is the prize of the armed combat, and Achilles gives it to Diomedes when the Achaeans stop the fight (23.807–808, 23.824–825; see [[silver-studded]], [[sword]] and [[contests]]). The other prize of that contest is the armor of Sarpedon (23.798–800), the leader who chose Asteropaeus at the wall.

## Outside Homer

[[Strabo]], reporting that the Paeonians were once called Pelagonians, finds it “not unreasonable” that Asteropaeus, one of the Paeonian leaders at Ilios, is called the son of Pelegon (*Geography* 7, fr. 38, fr. 39). The Suda lists among the books of Ptolemy Pindarion of Alexandria, a pupil of Aristarchus, one *On the Asteropaeus Mentioned by Homer* (π 3034).
