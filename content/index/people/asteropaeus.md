## Etymology

Ἀστεροπαῖος is formed from ἀστεροπή, “lightning” (11.184), the word in Zeus' title ἀστεροπητής, “lord of the lightning” (12.275; see [[lord-of-the-lightning]]).

## Family

His father is Pelegon, whom the broad-flowing Axius fathered on Periboea, the eldest of the daughters of Acessamenus (21.141–143; see [[pelegon]], [[periboea]] and [[acessamenus]]). When he tells his lineage to Achilles he begins from the river, in the words with which the Catalogue praised it (21.157–158 ≈ 2.849–850; see [[axius]]).

## Descriptions

ἀρήϊος, “warlike” (12.102, 17.352; see [[warlike]]); ἥρως, “the hero” (21.163; see [[heros]]); Πηλεγόνος … φαίδιμος υἱός, “the shining son of Pelegon” (21.152; see [[phaidimos]]); and περιδέξιος, said of no one else in Homer, which the translation renders “he could use either hand” (21.163; see [[ambidextrous]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Sarpedon chooses him with Glaucus to lead the allies against the Achaean wall (12.101–104; see [[sarpedon]] and [[glaucus]]). In the fight over Patroclus Hector names him among the allied leaders he urges on, and when Lycomedes kills the Paeonian Apisaon, second only to him in the fighting, he charges to avenge him but cannot break through the shields around the body (17.217, 17.348–355; see [[apisaon-son-of-hippasus]]). In the river he stands against Achilles with two spears, grazes his arm, and dies by his sword (21.139–204; see [[achilles]]).

**The spoils.** At Patroclus' games Achilles gives Eumelus the bronze corselet he took from Asteropaeus, with a casting of shining tin around it (23.560–562; see [[eumelus]]). The silver-studded Thracian sword is the prize of the armed combat, and Achilles gives it to Diomedes when the Achaeans stop the fight (23.807–808, 23.824–825; see [[silver-studded]], [[sword]] and [[contests]]). The other prize of that contest is the armor of Sarpedon (23.798–800), the leader who chose Asteropaeus at the wall. Walter Leaf observes that the fight in the river says nothing of a corselet, though if he wore one Achilles' sword must have gone through it when it struck him in the belly (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.560; 21.180).

## Outside Homer

[[Strabo]], reporting that the Paeonians were once called Pelagonians, finds it “not unreasonable” that Asteropaeus, one of the Paeonian leaders at Ilios, is called the son of Pelegon (*Geography* 7, fr. 38, fr. 39). The Suda lists among the books of Ptolemy Pindarion of Alexandria, a pupil of Aristarchus, one *On the Asteropaeus Mentioned by Homer* (π 3034).
