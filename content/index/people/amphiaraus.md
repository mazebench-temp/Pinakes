## Family

Ἀντιφάτης μὲν ἔτικτεν Ὀϊκλῆα μεγάθυμον, / αὐτὰρ Ὀϊκλείης λαοσσόον Ἀμφιάραον, “Antiphates begot great-hearted Oicles, / and Oicles Amphiaraus, who rouses armies” (*Odyssey* 15.243–244): he is the son of Oicles, the grandson of Antiphates and the great-grandson of the seer Melampus, and “His sons were Alcmaeon and Amphilochus” (*Odyssey* 15.248; see [[oicles]], [[antiphates-son-of-melampus]], [[melampus]], [[alcmaeon]] and [[amphilochus]]). His wife Eriphyle, whom the Odyssey names among the heroines in the land of the dead, “took precious gold as the price of her own husband” (*Odyssey* 11.326–327; see [[eriphyle]]). The seer Theoclymenus is of the other line of Melampus (see [[theoclymenus]]).

## Descriptions

- λαοσσόος, “who rouses armies” (*Odyssey* 15.244), the epithet of Athena, Ares, Eris and Apollo, and of no other mortal in Homer (*Iliad* 13.128, 17.398, 20.48, 20.79; see [[who-rouses-armies]]).
- The man “whom aegis-bearing Zeus and Apollo loved with all their hearts / with every kind of love” (*Odyssey* 15.245–246; see [[zeus]] and [[apollo]]).

## Outside Homer

**The seer at Thebes.** As the Seven fled, Zeus split the earth with his thunderbolt and swallowed him with his chariot and horses (Pindar, *Nemean* 9.24–27; see [[the-seven-against-thebes]]). D. B. Monro observes that the leading figure of the epic *Thebaid*, “also known as the ‘expedition of Amphiaraus’”, was Amphiaraus, who stood to Adrastus somewhat as Achilles to Agamemnon, fighting in a cause not his own and knowing his fate, but a hero “of a new and very different type, one in which valour was united with prophetic wisdom”; the scholia take from that poem Adrastus' praise of him in Pindar, “both a good seer and good at fighting with the spear”, and his death is connected with the foundation of an oracle, of a kind that the Iliad does not know (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix III §10). In Aeschylus' *Seven against Thebes* the messenger names the sixth champion “a man most temperate, best in valor, a seer, mighty Amphiaraus”, who reviles Tydeus and Polynices, foretells that he himself will “enrich this earth, a seer buried beneath enemy soil”, and carries a shield with no device, “for he wishes not to seem the best but to be it”; Eteocles calls him “a temperate, just, good, pious man, a great prophet”, dragged down with the impious (568–592, 609–614; see [[aeschylus-seven]]). In Euripides' *Suppliant Women* Adrastus went to war “against the will of Amphiaraus” (158), and Theseus says that the gods snatched “the noble son of Oicles” alive into the depths of the earth with his four-horse chariot (925–927; see [[euripides]] and [[oicles]]).

**The oracle.** Croesus tested his oracle with the others, judged it truthful, and, “having learned of his excellence and his suffering”, dedicated to him a golden shield and a spear all of gold, which in Herodotus' day lay at Thebes in the temple of Ismenian Apollo (Herodotus 1.46.2, 1.49.1, 1.52.1). No Theban might consult him, because he bade the Thebans choose him as their seer or as their ally, and they chose him as their ally (Herodotus 8.134.2; see [[herodotus]]). Strabo places near Oropus the Amphiareion, “once an honored oracle”, where, as Sophocles says, “the Theban dust broke open and received him, with his arms and his four-horse chariot”; it had been moved there from Cnopia in the Theban land, and Harma, a village in the territory of Tanagra, was named after his chariot (*Geography* 9.1.22, 9.2.10, 9.2.11; see [[strabo]]). Cicero names him among the augurs whose fame proved their art (*On the Laws* 2.33): Greece so honored him “that he was held a god, and oracles were sought from the ground in which he was buried” (*On Divination* 1.88); and in *On the Nature of the Gods* Cotta asks, “Will Amphiaraus be a god, and Trophonius?”, recalling that the Roman tax-farmers denied that anyone who had once been a man was immortal, when lands in Boeotia belonging to the gods were exempted from the censors' lease (3.49; see [[cicero]]).

**In Aristotle.** In the *Poetics* Carcinus' play failed because “Amphiaraus was coming back up from the temple”, an incongruity the poet missed by not visualizing the scene (1455a26–29; see [[carcinus]] and [[before-the-eyes]]).
