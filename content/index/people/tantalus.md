## The name

Plato's Socrates finds the name rightly given, “if what is said of him is true”: many dreadful misfortunes befell him while he lived, and at the end of them his whole country was overturned, and in Hades there is the swaying of the stone over his head (ἡ ὑπὲρ τῆς κεφαλῆς τοῦ λίθου ταλαντεία), wonderfully in accord with the name, as though someone wishing to call him ταλάντατος, “most wretched”, had disguised the word and said Τάνταλος instead (*Cratylus* 395d–e, plain renderings; see [[plato]]). W. W. Merry thinks the name more likely connected with the root of τλῆναι and τολμᾶν, “the daring man”. He finds the close connection of the name with τάλαντον, “balance”, in a proverb, and thinks it may account for the two forms of the punishment: one hangs him balanced over the water, the other, which the tragedians and lyric poets mostly follow, sets a rock over his head, like the sword of Damocles (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.582). The Suda has the proverb, Ταντάλου τάλαντα τανταλίζεται, which plays on the name and on τάλαντα, “talents”, and explains that Tantalus was so famous for his wealth that he passed into a proverb (Suda τ 81).

## Descriptions

- ὁ γέρων, “the old man” (*Odyssey* 11.585, 11.591). Homer gives him no father, no country and no crime.
- κρατέρʼ ἄλγεʼ ἔχοντα, “suffering bitter pains” (*Odyssey* 11.582), the half-verse that introduces Sisyphus too (*Odyssey* 11.593; see [[sisyphus]]).

## Outside Homer

**His house.** Euripides' Electra opens the *Orestes* with him: “the blessed one (and I do not reproach his fortunes), / born of Zeus, as they say, Tantalus”, the father of Pelops, whose son was Atreus, the father of Agamemnon and Menelaus (*Orestes* 4–18, plain renderings; see [[orestes-play]], [[pelops]] and [[atreus]]). Plato's Socrates, explaining the names of the same house, goes up from Agamemnon through Atreus and Pelops to Tantalus, and from him to “his reputed father, Zeus” (*Cratylus* 395a–396a). Homer knows Pelops, to whom Hermes gave the scepter of Zeus and who gave it to Atreus (*Iliad* 2.104–105), but never names his father.

**The rock and the crime.** Euripides' Tantalus “flits in the air, fearing the rock that towers over his head”, and pays this penalty, “as they say, because, a man sharing the honor of a common table with the gods, / he had an unbridled tongue, a most shameful disease” (*Orestes* 6–10). Cicero says that the poets hang a rock over Tantalus in the underworld “for his crimes, his lack of self-control and his arrogant talk”, as the image of the man who is always fearing some evil to come (*Tusculan Disputations* 4.35, a plain rendering; see [[cicero]]), though elsewhere, listing the terrors of the underworld, he keeps Homer's picture, “Tantalus, touching the top of the water with his chin, perishing of thirst” (*Tusculan Disputations* 1.10). Lucretius makes the rock the image of the empty fear of the gods that weighs on the living (*On the Nature of Things* 3.980–983; see [[lucretius-the-punishments-of-acheron]]). The Suda joins the two punishments: Tantalus, a son of Zeus admitted to the gods' table, made their mysteries public, and is punished under the earth with a rock hanging over him and much water and fruit-bearing shoots below; whenever he wished to drink, the stone fell on him (Suda τ 79). Merry quotes Pindar's reason, that he could not digest his great happiness (*Olympian* 1.55, as Merry quotes it), and sums up the stories: whether he revealed the secrets of the gods, to whose company he had been admitted, or stole nectar and ambrosia from their table, all agree that his punishment came for the crimes, the lack of self-control and the arrogant talk of which Cicero speaks (Merry on *Odyssey* 11.582).

**Plato.** Socrates in the *Gorgias* names Tantalus first of the kings and potentates whom Homer shows punished forever in Hades, with Sisyphus and Tityus (*Gorgias* 525d–e; see [[plato-thersites]] and [[tityus]]).

**Proverbs.** “Tantalean punishments” were said of those who have good things and are not allowed to enjoy them, and the orator Isaeus, asked which fish and birds were best to eat, said that he had given up such pursuits, having understood that he was “plucking the gardens of Tantalus”, since all pleasures are shadows and dreams (Suda τ 78, τ 80).

**Virgil.** Virgil gives the two punishments to others: over the Lapiths, Ixion and Peirithous a black rock hangs, ever about to fall, while a royal feast is set before them which the eldest of the Furies will not let them touch (*Aeneid* 6.601–607; see [[virgil-the-punished-in-tartarus]]).
