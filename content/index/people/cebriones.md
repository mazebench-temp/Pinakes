## Family

Hector turns to Cebriones, “his brother, who was close by” (ἀδελφεὸν ἐγγὺς ἐόντα, 8.318). Book 16 names his birth: Κεβριόνην νόθον υἱὸν ἀγακλῆος Πριάμοιο, “Cebriones, bastard son of glorious Priam” (16.738; see [[nothos]] and [[sons-of-priam]]). His mother is not named. Priam's bastard sons fight beside their brothers, and some of them, like Cebriones, drive for them: Isus, “the bastard”, held the reins in the chariot in which his brother Antiphus rode when Agamemnon killed them both (11.101–104).

## Hector's charioteer

Twice in the afternoon Hector loses a charioteer. Diomedes' spear kills Eniopeus, and Hector finds Archeptolemus to take his place (8.118–129; see [[eniopeus]] and [[archeptolemus]]). Then Teucer's arrow, aimed at Hector, kills Archeptolemus, and the verses of Hector's grief are repeated (8.124–125 = 8.316–317; see [[teucer]]). This time he does not go looking for a driver. He tells Cebriones, who is close by, to take the reins, “and he heard and did not disobey” (8.318–319), and leaps down from the chariot with a stone to strike Teucer (8.320–329; see [[hector]]).

On the next day of fighting he stands beside Hector in the chariot, sees the Trojans thrown into confusion where Telamonian Ajax is driving them, “I know him well, for he has the broad shield around his shoulders”, and turns the horses there (11.521–530; see [[ajax]]). The horses trample corpses and shields, and the axle and the rails are spattered with blood from the hooves and the wheels (11.531–537), in verses used again of Achilles' chariot (20.499–502). When the Trojans leave their chariots at the ditch of the Achaean wall, he goes on foot as the third leader of the largest company, with Hector and Polydamas, and Hector leaves a lesser man with the chariot (12.88–92). At the ships the fighting is thickest around him and Polydamas (13.789–790).

## His death

When Patroclus drives the Trojans back toward the city, Hector tells Cebriones to whip the horses into the battle and makes straight for Patroclus (16.727–732). Patroclus leaps from his chariot with a jagged stone and strikes “Hector's charioteer, Cebriones, bastard son of glorious Priam” on the forehead as he holds the reins. The stone crushes both brows, his eyes fall in the dust before his feet, and he drops from the chariot “like a diver” (16.733–743). Patroclus mocks the fall: a man who dives so lightly from a chariot could feed many with the oysters he would bring up from a ship at sea, “so there are tumblers among the Trojans too” (16.744–750). Hector and Patroclus fight over the body like two lions over a slain deer, Hector holding the head and Patroclus a foot, while the armies close around them (16.751–771). “And he lay in the whirl of dust, great in his greatness, forgetful of his horsemanship” (16.775–776), words that the Odyssey gives to the dead Achilles (Od. 24.39–40). The battle is timed in the verses that timed the day of Book 8. The missiles strike home on both sides as long as the sun stands astride the middle of the sky, and when it turns toward the time for unyoking oxen, the Achaeans prevail beyond their portion (16.777–780; compare 8.66–69, where at midday Zeus lifts his scales). They drag Cebriones out from among the missiles and strip his armor (16.781–782), and Patroclus charges on to his own death (16.783–857; see [[patroclus]]).

He comes into the poem as Hector leaps from the chariot with a jagged stone to strike Teucer (λίθῳ ὀκριόεντι, 8.320–327), and he leaves it when Patroclus leaps from his chariot with a jagged stone to strike him (μάρμαρον ὀκριόεντα, 16.733–735; see [[stone-throwing]]). The verse-end Ἕκτορος ἡνιοχῆα, “Hector's charioteer”, marks the death of Archeptolemus and his own (8.312, 16.737).

## Outside Homer

Demetrius of Scepsis, as Strabo reports, connected the name with Cebrenia, the district of the Troad below Dardania, or rather, as he thought more likely, with its city, Cebrene (Strabo 13.1.33; see [[strabo]]).
