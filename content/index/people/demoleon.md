**The defender.** Demoleon is the second of the men Achilles kills when he comes back to the battle, after Iphition (20.381–394; see [[iphition]] and [[achilles]]). ἐσθλὸν ἀλεξητῆρα μάχης, “a brave defender against battle” (20.396): ἀλεξητήρ, from ἀλέξω, “ward off”, is found only here in Homer; Walter Leaf compares Phoenix's words to Achilles, πόλεμόν περ ἀλαλκών, “though you turn back the war” (9.605; *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.396; see [[brave]]). Ἀντήνορος υἱὸν, “the son of Antenor”, names his father in the words used of Pedaeus, the first of Antenor's sons to die (5.69; see [[son-of-antenor]], [[antenor]] and [[pedaeus]]).

**Through the helmet.** Achilles stabbed him “in the temple, through the bronze-cheeked helmet”, and the point broke the bone (20.397–400; see [[bronze-cheeked]] and [[helmet]]). The verses are, with a few words changed, those in which Polypoetes killed Damasus at the Achaean wall (12.183–186; see [[damasus]] and [[polypoetes]]); the last of them, “and he beat him down for all his eagerness”, ended the death of Bianor's companion Oileus at Agamemnon's hands (11.98; see [[oileus-the-trojan]]). Leaf notes the parallels and would remove the borrowed verses with the Iphition episode (on 20.381 and 20.397; see the translation's note on 20.381–395). The verses that follow are borrowed too: Hippodamas, the next man, dies in a verse from Odysseus' killings and another from Menelaus' (20.401–402; see [[hippodamas]]).

**Antenor's sons.** He is the last of the sons of Antenor killed in the Iliad, after Pedaeus, killed by Meges (5.69–75), Iphidamas and Coon, killed by Agamemnon (11.221–263), Archelochus, killed by Ajax' spear meant for Polydamas (14.459–464), Laodamas, killed by Ajax (15.516–517), and Acamas, if the Acamas whom Meriones kills is his son (16.342–344); Agenor, whom Apollo carries away from Achilles in the next book, survives (21.595–611; see [[iphidamas]], [[coon]], [[archelochus]], [[laodamas]], [[acamas]] and [[agenor]]).

**The name.** Δημολέων seems to join δῆμος, “people”, and λέων, “lion”.
