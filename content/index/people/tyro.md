## Etymology

Τυρώ looks like τυρός, “cheese”. A scholion that W. W. Merry quotes says that she was so called for her whiteness, and he renders the name “fair-faced”, literally “curd-white” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.235; see [[cheese]]).

## Family

She is the daughter of Salmoneus and the wife of Cretheus, son of Aeolus (*Odyssey* 11.236–237; see [[salmoneus]], [[cretheus]] and [[aeolus-father-of-sisyphus]]). To Poseidon, who lay with her in the likeness of the river Enipeus, she bore Pelias and Neleus, and to Cretheus “Aeson and Pheres and Amythaon, who fought from his chariot” (*Odyssey* 11.241–259; see [[poseidon]], [[enipeus]], [[pelias]], [[neleus]], [[aeson]], [[pheres]] and [[amythaon]]). Merry makes Cretheus and Salmoneus brothers, so that Tyro, like Arete, married her uncle (on *Odyssey* 11.235; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). Through Neleus she is the grandmother of Nestor, and through Pelias of Alcestis (see [[nestor]] and [[alcestis]]).

## Descriptions

- εὐπατέρεια, “daughter of a noble father” (*Odyssey* 11.235; see [[daughter-of-a-noble-father]]), the epithet of Helen in the Iliad and the Odyssey (*Iliad* 6.292; *Odyssey* 22.227).
- βασίλεια γυναικῶν, “the queen among women” (*Odyssey* 11.258; see [[basileus]]).

## Outside Homer

The Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* counted Salmoneus among the sons of Aeolus (fr. 4 Evelyn-White), and a scholion on *Odyssey* 12.69, printed among its fragments, gives the whole family: Tyro, the daughter of Salmoneus, had two sons by Poseidon, Neleus and Pelias, then married Cretheus and bore him Aeson, Pheres and Amythaon (fr. 13 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]). Strabo, describing Salmone in Elis, near the spring from which the Enipeus flows to the Alpheus, says that it was this river that Tyro loved, quoting the verse of the Odyssey, since her father Salmoneus was king there, as Euripides says in the *Aeolus* (*Geography* 8.3.32; see [[strabo]]); Merry notes that Salmoneus had homes in both countries, Thessaly and Elis, each with its Enipeus, and that the Thessalian river falls into the Peneus, not the sea, so that its “mouth” is loosely said (on *Odyssey* 11.235, 11.242). Sophocles wrote two plays named *Tyro*, which told how she exposed the twins and how they were recognized by the little boat in which she had set them adrift (see [[tyro-play]], [[the-boat-of-tyro]] and [[tyro-and-her-sons]]).
