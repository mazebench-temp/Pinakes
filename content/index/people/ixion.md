## In Homer

Homer names him once, and only through his wife: Ἰξιονίης ἀλόχοιο, “the wife of Ixion”, the first of the loves that Zeus names to Hera, “who bore Peirithous, a counselor equal to the gods” (14.317–318; see [[the-loves-of-zeus]] and [[peirithous]]). He gives her no name; later mythographers called her Dia (Leaf on 14.317).

## Outside Homer

Pindar tells his story as a warning against ingratitude. Bound on his winged wheel, Ixion tells mortals to repay a benefactor with kindness. He had been the first to stain men with a kinsman's blood; received among the gods, he desired Hera, lay with a cloud in her likeness, and so fathered Centaurus, the ancestor of the Centaurs (*Pythian* 2.21–48; see [[ixion-on-the-wheel]] and [[centaurs]]). In Aeschylus' *Eumenides* Apollo asks whether Zeus too erred when he accepted “Ixion's supplication after the first murder”, and Athena calls Orestes, sitting at her image, “a holy suppliant in Ixion's manner” (*Eumenides* 441, 717–718; see [[aeschylus]]). For the plays named after him see [[ixion-plays]].
