Κοπρῆος φίλον υἱόν, ὃς Εὐρυσθῆος ἄνακτος / ἀγγελίης οἴχνεσκε βίῃ Ἡρακληείῃ, “the dear son of Copreus, who used to go for lord Eurystheus / with messages to mighty Heracles” (15.639–640). The Iliad names him only as the father of Periphetes of Mycenae, whom Hector kills at the ships, and it judges him in passing: “From him, a far worse father, was born a better son” (15.641; see [[periphetes-of-mycenae]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). In what he was worse the poem does not say. οἴχνεσκε, “used to go”, an iterative form, makes the errand a habit, one message for each labor (see [[eurystheus]], [[heracles]], [[might-of-heracles]] and [[heralds]]). Elsewhere in the Iliad Eurystheus sets the labors and Heracles is “worn out by the labors that Eurystheus set him” (8.363; again 19.133); here the poem names the man who went between them. His name looks like κόπρος, “dung” (24.164, 24.640), but the poem makes nothing of it.

## Outside Homer

[[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] tells how Eurystheus, amazed at Heracles' strength when he brought back the Nemean lion, forbade him to enter the city, had a bronze jar made to hide in under the earth, and sent him his commands for the labors through a herald, Copreus, son of Pelops of Elis. Copreus had killed Iphitus and fled to Mycenae, where Eurystheus purified him, and he made his home there (*Library* 2.5.1). In this account he was himself a killer received in another man's house, like the exiles of the Iliad (see [[exile-and-refuge]]).
