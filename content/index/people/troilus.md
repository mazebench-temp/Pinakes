καὶ Τρωΐλον ἱππιοχάρμην, “and Troilus, who fought from his chariot” (24.257). Priam, rebuking the nine sons who are left, names three of the best whom Ares has destroyed, Mestor, Troilus and Hector (24.255–260; see [[sons-of-priam]], [[mestor]] and [[hector]]). ἱππιοχάρμης = ἵππος (“horse”; in the plural, a chariot team) + χάρμη (“battle”; see [[charme]]): in the Iliad only here, and in the Odyssey once more, of Amythaon, son of Tyro (*Odyssey* 11.259; see [[hippiocharmes]]). Walter Leaf notes that Troilus is not mentioned again in Homer, though he is one of the favorite figures of the later epic cycle and of the vase painters (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.257).

## Outside Homer

Leaf reports a scholion that the later poets, because Homer called Troilus ἱππιοχάρμης, made him pursued on horseback, and that some made him a boy, though Homer by the epithet shows him a grown man (on 24.257). Apollodorus names him last among the sons of Hecuba, adding that he is said to have been fathered by Apollo, and tells that Achilles lay in ambush for him and killed him in the sanctuary of Thymbraean Apollo (*Library* 3.12.5; *Epitome* 3.32; see [[apollodorus]] and [[thymbre]]). Among the pictures of the war on the temple at Carthage, Aeneas sees Troilus fleeing with his arms lost, *infelix puer atque impar congressus Achilli*, “an unhappy boy, no match for Achilles”, carried along by his horses and clinging, thrown on his back, to the empty chariot, still holding the reins, while his neck and hair are dragged over the ground (*Aeneid* 1.474–478; see [[virgil]]). In medieval romance he became the lover of Briseida, later Criseida and Cressida (see [[from-briseis-to-cressida]]).
