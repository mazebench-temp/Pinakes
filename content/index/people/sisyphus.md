κέρδιστος, “the craftiest” (see [[craftiest]]), is the superlative of κέρδος, “gain”; in the plural κέρδεα are wiles, the cunning of those who know how to profit. Athena and Odysseus “both know / crafty ways” (εἰδότες ἄμφω κέρδεα, *Odyssey* 13.296–297). Αἰολίδης, “son of Aeolus”, is his patronymic (see [[aeolus-father-of-sisyphus]]). He heads the lineage that Glaucus recites: Sisyphus, [[glaucus-son-of-sisyphus|Glaucus]], [[Bellerophon]], [[Hippolochus]], and Glaucus himself (see [[ephyra-in-argos]]).

## The name

Curtius, as W. W. Merry reports him, explains Σίσυφος as a reduplicated form of σοφός, “wise, clever”, with the Aeolic υ for ο, a name that suits “the craftiest of men” of the Iliad (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.593). The Suda says the same of the word: the poets handed Sisyphus down as shrewd and a rogue, having learned it from one word in Homer, κέρδιστος (Suda σ 490).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Among the dead Odysseus sees him pushing a huge stone up a hill, which rolls back down to the plain whenever he is about to heave it over the top, so that he must push it up again (*Odyssey* 11.593–600; see [[shameless-stone]] and [[tantalus]]). The Odyssey does not say what he had done.

## Outside Homer

The Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* counts him among the sons of Aeolus, “Sisyphus of shifting counsel” (αἰολόμητις, perhaps a play on his father's name; fr. 4 Evelyn-White). [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] says that he founded Ephyra, “now called Corinth”, married Merope, a daughter of Atlas, and was punished for telling the river Asopus that Zeus had carried off his daughter Aegina (*Library* 1.9.3). Theognis tells how he came back even from Hades, persuading Persephone with wily words (702–704). Tragedy made him the true father of Odysseus: “the seed of Sisyphus” (Euripides, *Iphigenia at Aulis* 524; *Cyclops* 104; see [[laertes]]). The Suda says the same, that Odysseus traced his descent from Sisyphus, king of Corinth (Suda σ 490). Aeschylus wrote a *Sisyphus the Stone-Roller*, of which one line survives, “He is an Aetnaean beetle in the violence of his labors” (fr. 233 Nauck, a plain rendering; see [[aeschylus]]); Merry names it with a satyr play, *Sisyphus the Runaway*, and another satyr play ascribed to Euripides (on *Odyssey* 11.593). Strabo saw the ruins of the Sisypheion, a temple or palace of white stone, below the spring Peirene on Acrocorinth (*Geography* 8.6.21; see [[strabo]]).

Plato's Socrates names him with Tantalus and Tityus among the kings whom Homer shows punished forever in Hades (*Gorgias* 525e), and looks forward to examining, after his own death, the leader of the great army against Troy, or Odysseus, or Sisyphus (*Apology* 41b–c; see [[plato]] and [[plato-the-choice-of-lives]]). Lucretius made his stone the image of the man who keeps seeking office and keeps failing (*On the Nature of Things* 3.995–1002; see [[lucretius-the-punishments-of-acheron]]), and Albert Camus made him the hero of the absurd (*The Myth of Sisyphus*, 1942).
