κέρδιστος, “the craftiest” (see [[craftiest]]), is the superlative of κέρδος, “gain”; in the plural κέρδεα are wiles, the cunning of those who know how to profit. Athena and Odysseus are “both knowing wiles” (εἰδότες ἄμφω κέρδεα, Od. 13.296–297). Αἰολίδης, “son of Aeolus”, is his patronymic (see [[aeolus-father-of-sisyphus]]). He heads the lineage that Glaucus recites: Sisyphus, [[glaucus-son-of-sisyphus|Glaucus]], [[Bellerophon]], [[Hippolochus]], and Glaucus himself (see [[ephyra-in-argos]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Odysseus sees him among the sinners punished in the underworld, with Tityus and Tantalus. He is pushing a monstrous stone up a hill with his hands and feet; when he is about to heave it over the top, its weight turns it back, and “the shameless stone” rolls down to the plain, and he pushes it up again, sweat pouring from his limbs and dust rising from his head (Od. 11.593–600; see [[shameless-stone]]). The Odyssey does not say what he had done. Plato's Socrates names him among the kings whom Homer shows punished forever in Hades (*Gorgias* 525e), and looks forward to questioning him after death (*Apology* 41c).

## Outside Homer

The Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* counts him among the sons of Aeolus, “Sisyphus of shifting counsel” (αἰολόμητις, perhaps a play on his father's name; fr. 4 Evelyn-White). [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] says that he founded Ephyra, “now called Corinth”, married Merope, a daughter of Atlas, and was punished for telling the river Asopus that Zeus had carried off his daughter Aegina (*Library* 1.9.3). Theognis tells how he came back even from Hades, persuading Persephone with wily words (702–704). Tragedy made him the true father of Odysseus: “the seed of Sisyphus” (Euripides, *Iphigenia at Aulis* 524; *Cyclops* 104; see [[laertes]]). Strabo saw the ruins of the Sisypheion, a temple or palace of white stone, below the spring Peirene on Acrocorinth (8.6.21). Lucretius made his stone the image of the man who keeps seeking office and keeps failing (3.995–1002), and Albert Camus made him the hero of the absurd (*The Myth of Sisyphus*, 1942).
