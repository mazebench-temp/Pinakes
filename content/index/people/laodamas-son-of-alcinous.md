## Descriptions

- ἀγαπήνωρ, “manly” (*Odyssey* 7.170), an epithet of warriors in the Iliad, given in the Odyssey to him alone (see [[manly]]).
- ἀμύμων, “blameless” (*Odyssey* 8.117; see [[blameless]]), and ἀγαθὸς πάϊς Ἀλκινόοιο, “the good son of Alcinous” (*Odyssey* 8.130, 8.143).

He is one of the five sons of Alcinous, two of them married and three still unmarried (*Odyssey* 6.62–63), and he stands up for the games with two of his brothers, Halius and Clytoneus (*Odyssey* 8.118–119): probably the three unmarried ones, W. W. Merry suggests (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 8.204; see [[alcinous]] and [[nausicaa]]). He shares his name with a Trojan, the son of Antenor whom Ajax kills at the ships (*Iliad* 15.516–517; see [[laodamas]]).

## Later in the Odyssey

The renderings here are plain renderings, not the translation. At the games on the next day, Euryalus is named as the best of all the Phaeacians in looks and build “after blameless Laodamas”, and the three sons of Alcinous stand up, “Laodamas and Halius and godlike Clytoneus”; in boxing Laodamas is the best (*Odyssey* 8.115–130; see [[contests]]). When the young men have had their fill of the contests, he proposes to ask the stranger whether he knows any contest, for he is not ill built, but the sea has broken him; Euryalus bids him go and challenge him himself, and he does: there is no greater glory for a man while he lives than what he achieves with his feet and his hands (*Odyssey* 8.131–151). Odysseus answers him by name, “Laodamas, why do you bid me this in mockery?”, since his sorrows weigh on him more than contests (*Odyssey* 8.152–157), and it is Euryalus' taunt that drives him to throw the discus. Then he challenges all the Phaeacians except Laodamas, “for he is my host: who would fight with one who welcomes him?” (*Odyssey* 8.204–211). Laodamas had made the challenge, so that if guestship barred even him from the lists, it was understood to bar the other two (Merry on *Odyssey* 8.204). At the king's bidding he and Halius dance alone, since no one could vie with them, with a purple ball that Polybus had made, one throwing it toward the clouds and the other leaping to catch it before his feet touched the ground (*Odyssey* 8.370–380; see [[ball]] and [[dance]]).
