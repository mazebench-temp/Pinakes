## Etymology

Αὐτομέδων (Automedōn) joins αὐτός, “self”, and μέδων, “ruler”, the word in ἡγήτορες ἠδὲ μέδοντες, “leaders and rulers” (9.17). Names in -μέδων are common in Homer: Laomedon, Eurymedon, and Alcimedon, who takes the reins from Automedon in Book 17.

## Family

He is “the brave son of Diores” (Διώρεος ἄλκιμος υἱός, 17.429; 17.474). This Diores is not the Epeian leader of that name (2.622, 4.517; see [[diores]]), and the poem tells nothing more of him.

## In Homer

He is first named on the night of the embassy. Achilles carves the meat for the envoys, “Automedon held them for him” (9.209), and Patroclus makes the fire (see [[achilles]], [[patroclus]] and [[feast-type-scene]]). He says nothing, and the poem does not yet call him a charioteer.

In Book 16 Patroclus has him yoke the horses. He was the man “whom he honored most after Achilles, breaker of men, and who was most to be trusted to await his call in battle” (16.145–147). He yokes Xanthus and Balius, the immortal horses born to the West Wind, with the mortal trace horse Pedasus, taken at the sack of Eetion's city (16.148–154; see [[sack-of-thebe]]). When Sarpedon's spear kills Pedasus, he cuts the trace horse loose with his sword and frees the team (16.466–476). After Patroclus falls, Hector goes after him, “the godlike attendant of the swift-footed grandson of Aeacus”, but the horses carry him away (16.864–867).

In Book 17 the horses stand weeping for Patroclus and will not move, until Zeus pities them and puts strength in them (17.426–458). Automedon cannot both drive and fight, and gives the reins to Alcimedon. He prays to Zeus, kills Aretus, dodges Hector's spear, and strips the body, saying that he has eased his heart a little of its grief for the dead son of Menoetius, “though I have killed a lesser man” (17.459–542). In Book 19 he and Alcimus yoke the horses, and he mounts as charioteer with Achilles armed behind him (19.392–399). Achilles sends him for the corselet he gives to Eumelus (23.563–565). When Priam comes, Automedon and Alcimus are the two companions waiting on Achilles, the men he honored most “after the dead Patroclus” (24.473–476, 24.573–576). At the meal Automedon sets out the bread in baskets while Achilles serves the meat, in the verses that gave the task to Patroclus on the night of the embassy (24.625–626 ≈ 9.216–217).

## Outside Homer

In Virgil he stands with Neoptolemus at the doors of Priam's palace, “the driver of the horses of Achilles, the armor-bearer Automedon” (*Aeneid* 2.476–477). His name became a byword for a driver: Cicero calls the man sent off by night in a carriage with the news of a murder “that Automedon” (*Pro Roscio Amerino* 98).
