ἀλλʼ ἦ τοι Κλεῖτον χρυσόθρονος ἥρπασεν Ἠὼς / κάλλεος εἵνεκα οἷο, ἵνʼ ἀθανάτοισι μετείη, “but Cleitus was carried off by Dawn of the golden throne / for the sake of his beauty, so that he might dwell among the immortals” (*Odyssey* 15.250–251; see [[eos]] and [[golden-throned]]). He is a son of Mantius and the brother of the seer Polypheides, in the pedigree of the seer Theoclymenus (*Odyssey* 15.249; see [[mantius]], [[polypheides]] and [[theoclymenus]]), and the poem tells nothing else of him.

## The verses

His taking is told in the formulas of Dawn's other loves and of Ganymede. The second verse is the Iliad's verse of Ganymede, “the most beautiful of mortal men”, whom “the gods snatched up to pour wine for Zeus, / for the sake of his beauty, so that he might dwell among the immortals” (*Iliad* 20.233–235; see [[ganymede]]), and Aristarchus obelized it here, as taken from that passage (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, critical note on *Odyssey* 15.251). The Homeric *Hymn to Aphrodite* has the words of both verses in its account of the beautiful men of the Trojan royal house: Zeus snatched fair-haired Ganymede “for his beauty, so that he might dwell among the immortals”, ἥρπασε ὃν διὰ κάλλος, ἵνʼ ἀθανάτοισι μετείη, and “so again Dawn of the golden throne carried off Tithonus”, ὣς δʼ αὖ Τιθωνὸν χρυσόθρονος ἥρπασεν Ἠώς (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 202–203, 218; see [[tithonus]]). In Homer Tithonus is Dawn's husband (*Iliad* 11.1), and she took Orion for herself until Artemis killed him (*Odyssey* 5.121–124; see [[dawn-and-orion]]); Cleitus, unlike either, is taken so that he may live with the gods (see [[immortality]]).

Homer's other Cleitus is the Trojan son of Peisenor, the charioteer of Polydamas, whom Teucer shoots (*Iliad* 15.445–453; see [[cleitus]]).
