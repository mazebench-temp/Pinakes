## The name

Ἀντίκλεια joins ἀντι-, “against, matching”, and κλέος, “fame”: the man's name Ἄντικλος is formed in the same way, that of the Achaean in the wooden horse who alone wanted to answer Helen's voice until Odysseus pressed his mouth shut (*Odyssey* 4.285–288).

## Family

Αὐτολύκου θυγάτηρ μεγαλήτορος Ἀντίκλεια, “Anticleia, daughter of great-hearted Autolycus” (*Odyssey* 11.85; see [[autolycus]] and [[great-hearted]]): her father is the grandfather who gave Odysseus his name, and her mother is Amphithea, who embraced the boy when he came to Parnassus (*Odyssey* 19.399–409, 19.416, Greek only). She is the wife of Laertes, whom Autolycus calls his son-in-law when he names her child, γαμβρὸς ἐμὸς θυγάτηρ τε, “my son-in-law and my daughter” (*Odyssey* 19.406, a plain rendering; see [[laertes]]), and the mother of Odysseus (*Odyssey* 11.84–86; see [[odysseus]]) and of a daughter, Ctimene, “the youngest she bore of her children”, whom she brought up together with the swineherd Eumaeus until Ctimene was given in marriage to Same (*Odyssey* 15.361–367, a plain rendering; see [[eumaeus]]).

## Descriptions

- πότνια μήτηρ, “my lady mother”, in her son's tale (*Odyssey* 11.180, 11.215; see [[queenly]]), and μητρὸς κατατεθνηυίης, “my dead mother” (*Odyssey* 11.84, 11.141, 11.205).
- κεδνὴ ἄλοχος, Laertes' “devoted wife”, whose anger he shunned (*Odyssey* 1.432–433; see [[devoted]]), and κουριδίη ἄλοχος δαΐφρων, his “wise-hearted wedded wife”, in the swineherd's mouth (*Odyssey* 15.356, a plain rendering; see [[wedded]] and [[wise-hearted]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

When the disguised Odysseus asks the swineherd about the mother and the father of Odysseus, whether they are still alive, Eumaeus answers that Laertes lives, but prays to Zeus that his spirit may waste from his limbs in his halls, grieving terribly for his son who is gone and for his wedded wife, whose death grieved him most of all and set him in a raw old age; “and she died of grief for her glorious son, / a wretched death” (*Odyssey* 15.347–360, a plain rendering). While she lived, though she grieved, he liked to ask after her, because she had reared him with her daughter; when Ctimene married, she clothed him in a fine cloak and tunic, gave him sandals and sent him to the fields, and she loved him dearly; now he has lost all that, and from his mistress there is no kind word or deed, since evil has fallen on the house (*Odyssey* 15.361–375, a plain rendering). The death she tells her son of in the land of the dead is the same (*Odyssey* 11.202–203).

## Outside Homer

W. W. Merry reports a later story in which she hanged herself when Nauplius falsely reported that Odysseus was dead, referring to Hyginus, *Fabulae* 243 (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.85). Hyginus' own summary of the Odyssey has Ulysses speak in the underworld with his mother Anticlia “about the end of his wandering”, *de fine errationis suae* (*Fabulae* 125), the subject of Teiresias' prophecy in Homer. For the story that Sisyphus lay with her before she married Laertes, see [[autolycus]].
