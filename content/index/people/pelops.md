The origin of the name Πέλοψ (Pelops) is unknown.

{{quote:778cced3-e225-5437-8786-4ee584223e25}}

πλήξιππος = πλήσσω (“strike”) + ἵππος (“horse”): “driver of horses”, a man who lashes on his team. The epithet fits the chariot race of later myth, which Homer does not tell. See [[driver-of-horses]].

This is the only place Homer names him. He does not say that Pelops was the father of Atreus, and he says nothing of the crimes of the house. In these lines the scepter passes from hand to hand in peace, from Zeus to the kings of Argos. See [[the-scepter-of-agamemnon]] and [[agamemnon]].

## Outside Homer

Pelops was the son of Tantalus, who served him to the gods at a feast; the gods restored him to life with a shoulder of ivory. Pindar rejects that story and says instead that Poseidon carried the boy off for love (*Olympian* 1). To win Hippodameia, Pelops had to beat her father Oenomaus in a chariot race. In Pindar he wins with a golden chariot and winged horses from Poseidon (*Olympian* 1); in another, Oenomaus' charioteer Myrtilus betrays his master, and Pelops afterward kills him (Apollodorus, *Epitome* 2.4–9). The start of the race was carved on the east pediment of the temple of Zeus at Olympia (Pausanias 5.10.6–7).

Thucydides says that Pelops came from Asia to the Peloponnese with great wealth and won power there, so that the land was named after him although he was a stranger (1.9.2). The name Πελοπόννησος (“island of Pelops”) does not occur in Homer.
