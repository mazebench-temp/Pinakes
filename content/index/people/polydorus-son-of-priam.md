**The youngest son.** Achilles has already killed Iphition, Demoleon and Hippodamas when he goes after ἀντίθεον Πολύδωρον, “godlike Polydorus” (20.407; see [[achilles]], [[antitheos]] and [[son-of-priam]]). The narrator tells of his father's care: τὸν δʼ οὔ τι πατὴρ εἴασκε μάχεσθαι, “his father would not let him fight at all”, the imperfect of a refusal made again and again (20.408), because he was the youngest and the dearest of Priam's sons. So the seer Merops of Percote “would not let his sons / go into man-destroying war”, and they did not obey him (2.831–834 = 11.329–332; see [[merops]]). Walter Leaf notes that νεώτατος γόνοιο, “the youngest of his offspring”, uses γόνος of the whole brood, as Homer apparently does nowhere else (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.409). He outran all his brothers, and he dies of his gift. νηπιέῃσι, “in his childishness”, is the word for the play of the boy who builds walls of sand by the sea and knocks them down again (15.362–364; see [[the-boy-and-the-sand]] and [[nepios]]), and ποδῶν ἀρετὴν ἀναφαίνων, “showing off the prowess of his feet”, makes the prowess a runner's (see [[arete]]). He “rushed through the champions, until he lost his dear life” (20.412).

{{quote:f19e3ba9-12cc-5b23-8ee8-e62c8edaba63}}

**The blow.** The man who surpassed his brothers “in speed of foot” is caught by “brilliant swift-footed Achilles” as he darts past (20.410, 20.413; see [[podarkes]]). The two verses on the belt and the double corselet are those in which Athena guided Pandarus' arrow to Menelaus' belt (20.414–415 = 4.132–133; see [[belt]], [[corselet]] and [[double]]); there the arrow went through belt and corselet and only grazed Menelaus' skin (4.134–139), and here the spear goes straight through to the navel. “He fell to his knees with a groan” is the fall of Phereclus, whom Meriones killed, with a dark cloud in place of death (20.417 ≈ 5.68; see [[phereclus]]), as “the black cloud of death” enfolded Erymas (16.350; see [[erymas-killed-by-idomeneus]]). He is still alive, sinking to the ground and holding his bowels in his hands, when Hector sees him (20.418–420; see [[hector]]).

**His brother.** Ἕκτωρ δʼ ὡς ἐνόησε κασίγνητον Πολύδωρον, “But when Hector saw his brother Polydorus” (20.419; see [[kasignetos]]): Apollo had sent Hector back into the crowd, but at the sight of his brother he could no longer bear to keep away, and came against Achilles (20.375–380, 20.421–423). The two were sons of different mothers. Polydorus' mother was Laothoe, daughter of Altes, king of the Leleges at Pedasus, and his full brother was Lycaon (21.84–91, 22.46–48; see [[laothoe]], [[altes]], [[lycaon-son-of-priam]], [[leleges]] and [[pedasus-on-the-satnioeis]]), who begs Achilles in the next book to spare him “since I am not of the same womb as Hector” (21.95).

**Remembered.** In Book 21 his brother Lycaon, caught by Achilles at the river, names him as he begs for his own life (21.88–91; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]] and [[laothoe]]). In Book 22 Priam, on the wall, looks for him and Lycaon among the Trojans who have fled into the city and hopes to ransom them, not knowing that both are dead (22.46–53; see [[priam]] and [[apoina]]).

## Outside Homer

[[Strabo]], counting the dynasties of the Troad, says that Priam had Lycaon and Polydorus by the daughter of Altes, king of the Leleges (*Geography* 13.1.7; see [[altes]]).

Later poets told another story. In [[euripides|Euripides]]' *Hecuba* the ghost of Polydorus speaks the prologue: he is the son of Priam and of Hecuba, daughter of Cisseus; he was the youngest of Priam's sons, too young to carry arms, and his father sent him secretly out of Troy with much gold to his Thracian guest-friend Polymestor, so that his surviving children should not want; when Troy fell, Polymestor killed him for the gold and threw his body into the sea, and he lies unburied on the shore (*Hecuba* 1–30; see [[hecuba]] and [[cisseus]]). His mother is to see his body on the day her daughter Polyxena dies (*Hecuba* 43–46), and she takes her revenge: she blinds Polymestor and kills his two sons (*Hecuba* 1035–1051). In his words νεώτατος δʼ ἦ Πριαμιδῶν, “I was the youngest of Priam's sons” (*Hecuba* 13), Euripides keeps the Iliad's word for him, though his Polydorus never fought. Apollodorus too counts him among the sons of Hecuba, and Lycaon among the sons whom Priam had by other women (*Library* 3.12.5; see [[apollodorus]]).

[[Virgil]] gives the story to Aeneas. Landing in Thrace, he tears shoots from a mound and they drip black blood, and a voice from the mound tells him, *nam Polydorus ego*, “for I am Polydorus”: the spears that pierced him have grown into a thicket over his body. Priam had sent him with a great weight of gold to the Thracian king, who, when Troy's fortune failed, killed him and took the gold, *auri sacra fames*, and the Trojans give him a second funeral (*Aeneid* 3.13–68). [[Ovid]] tells it in the same way: Priam sent him secretly to Polymestor, removing him from the Phrygian war, and when Troy fell the king cut his throat and threw the body from a cliff into the sea (*Metamorphoses* 13.429–438).
