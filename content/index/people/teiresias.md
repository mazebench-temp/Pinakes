## The name

W. W. Merry derives the name either from τέρας, “portent”, as the interpreter of portents, or from τείρεα, “the signs of heaven”, the constellations (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 10.492): on the shield of Achilles Hephaestus makes “all the constellations with which the sky is crowned” (τὰ τείρεα πάντα, *Iliad* 18.485).

## Descriptions

{{quote:2905de1b-f92a-5ff5-b1c5-a39269cff5ce}}

- Θηβαῖος, “Theban” (*Odyssey* 10.492, 10.565; again 11.90, 11.165, 12.267, 23.323; see [[thebes]]). In the Iliad the word is a man's name (see [[thebaeus]]).
- μάντις ἀλαός, “the blind seer”, τοῦ τε φρένες ἔμπεδοί εἰσι, “whose wits are steadfast” (*Odyssey* 10.493; again 12.267; see [[the-blind-seer]] and [[phrenes]]). In *Odyssey* 11 he is “the noble seer” and “lord Teiresias”, and he comes “holding a golden scepter” (*Odyssey* 11.99, 11.151, 11.91, plain renderings; see [[scepter]]).
- τῷ καὶ τεθνηῶτι νόον πόρε Περσεφόνεια, / οἴῳ πεπνῦσθαι, “to him even in death Persephone has given a mind, / that he alone should be wise” (*Odyssey* 10.494–495; see [[persephone]], [[noos]] and [[wise]]). Merry renders, “to whom even after his death did Persephone grant sense, for him alone of all men to have wisdom”; the rest of the dead, not invisible but without substance, flit like shadows, and it was a special privilege to keep, as Teiresias did, even the φρένες (Merry on *Odyssey* 10.494, 10.495). He compares Achilles' cry over the soul of Patroclus: “Ah, truly even in the house of Hades there is some / soul and phantom, but there is no mind in it at all” (*Iliad* 23.103–104; see [[psyche]] and [[eidolon]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

**The prophecy.** At the pit, after the souls of Elpenor and of Anticleia, Odysseus' mother, “there came the soul of Theban Teiresias, / holding a golden scepter, and he knew me and spoke to me”, asking why he had left the light of the sun “to see the dead and this joyless place”, and bidding him draw back from the pit and put away his sword, “so that I may drink of the blood and tell you truly” (*Odyssey* 11.90–96, a plain rendering; see [[elpenor]] and [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]). He tells him that a god will make his homecoming grievous, for the shaker of the earth is angry because he blinded his dear son; that he may still come home if he restrains his own heart and his companions' when they find the cattle and fat sheep of the Sun on Thrinacia; that if they harm them, he foretells destruction for the ship and the companions, and Odysseus, if he escapes, “will come home late and in evil plight, having lost all your companions, / on someone else's ship, and find bane in your house”, overbearing men wooing his wife; that he will take vengeance on them; and that he must then take an oar and go until he comes to men who do not know the sea, and when a wayfarer calls it a winnowing fan, fix it in the ground and sacrifice to Poseidon, and go home and offer hecatombs to all the gods; and death will come to him “away from the sea”, a very gentle death in an old age of ease, with his people prosperous around him (*Odyssey* 11.100–137, a plain rendering; see [[poseidon]], [[the-cattle-of-helios]], [[the-suitors]], [[winnowing]] and [[telegonus-kills-odysseus]]). He tells him how the dead may speak with him, if he lets them drink the blood, and goes back into the house of Hades, “the soul of lord Teiresias, when he had told the oracles” (*Odyssey* 11.146–151, a plain rendering).

**The prophecy remembered.** Odysseus tells Achilles that he came “to consult Teiresias, in case he might tell me some plan / how I might come to rocky Ithaca” (*Odyssey* 11.479–480, a plain rendering). Off the island of the Sun he hears the cattle lowing and remembers the word “of the blind seer, Theban Teiresias, / and of Circe of Aeaea”, who had told him to shun the island, and he repeats it to his men (*Odyssey* 12.264–276, a plain rendering; see [[circe]]). In Ithaca he tells Penelope that his trials are not yet over, “for so the soul of Teiresias prophesied to me / on the day when I went down into the house of Hades”, and tells her of the oar and the land that does not know the sea; and in the summary of his wanderings he tells how he went to the house of Hades “to consult the soul of Theban Teiresias” (*Odyssey* 23.248–284, 23.322–323, a plain rendering). The renderings of Books 12 and 23 here are plain renderings, not the translation.

## Outside Homer

**Theban legend.** The *Melampodia*, ascribed to Hesiod, made him live for seven generations and has him complain to Zeus that he would rather have had a shorter life and only the wisdom of other men (fr. 2 Evelyn-White); it told how, seeing two snakes mating on Cithaeron, he killed the female and became a woman, and later killed the male and became a man again, so that when Zeus and Hera disputed which takes more pleasure in love, the man or the woman, they chose him as judge; he answered that the man enjoys one part of ten and the woman all ten, and Hera in anger blinded him, while Zeus gave him the art of prophecy (fr. 3 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]). Merry adds that others blamed Athena for his blindness, and that there was an oracle of Teiresias at Orchomenus (Merry on *Odyssey* 10.492). Strabo shows his tomb by the spring Tilphossa, under Mount Tilphossium near Haliartus and Alalcomenae, where, it was said, he died when the Thebans fled their city in the war of the Epigoni (*Geography* 9.2.27, 9.2.36; see [[strabo]] and [[the-epigonoi]]), and he tells, after Hesiod, of the contest at Claros between Calchas and the seer Mopsus, son of Manto, Teiresias' daughter (*Geography* 14.1.27; see [[calchas]]).

**In tragedy.** Euripides brings him on the stage as the old seer of Thebes. In the *Bacchae* he comes to the door of Cadmus, his fellow in age, with whom he has agreed to carry the thyrsus, wear the fawnskin and crown his head with ivy for the new god, and he defends Dionysus against Pentheus (*Bacchae* 170–190, 266–327; see [[cadmus]], [[pentheus]] and [[dionysus]]); in the *Phoenician Women* his daughter leads him in, “an eye to my blind foot”, and he tells Creon that to save the city he must sacrifice his own son Menoeceus (*Phoenician Women* 834–840, 911–914; see [[creon]] and [[euripides]]). For his part in the story of Oedipus see [[the-discovery-of-oedipus]].

**The philosophers.** Plato's Socrates, striking out of the poets the verses that make the underworld dreadful, quotes Circe's verse on him, “that he alone should be wise; but the others flit as shadows”, in the form ταὶ δὲ σκιαὶ ἀΐσσουσι (*Republic* 386d; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]] and the Odyssey translation's note on 10.495). Cicero sets him beside Amphiaraus among the eminent seers of old, “of whom Homer says that even among the dead he alone was wise, and the rest wandered like shadows” (*On Divination* 1.88), and, in the second book, reports Carneades' question whether a diviner blind as Teiresias was could tell white from black (*On Divination* 2.9; see [[cicero]], [[amphiaraus]] and [[cicero-polyphemus-and-the-ram]]). For Horace's Tiresias see [[horace-on-the-suitors]].
