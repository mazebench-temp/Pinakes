**The narrator's turn.** σοὶ Μενέλαε δαμῆναι, “to be beaten down by you, Menelaus” (13.603): the narrator turns to address Menelaus in the second person, as he does six other times in the poem (4.127, 4.146, 7.104, 17.679, 17.702, 23.600; see [[menelaus]]). μοῖρα κακή, “an evil fate”, leads Peisander to his death before the fight begins (see [[moira]]).

**The axe.** Menelaus' spear turns aside; Peisander's strikes the broad shield and breaks at the socket, “and he rejoiced in his heart and hoped for victory” (13.604–609; see [[spear]] and [[shield]]). Menelaus draws his silver-studded sword, and Peisander takes from under his shield “a fine / axe of good bronze, on a handle of olive wood, / long and well polished” (13.611–613; see [[axe]] and [[silver-studded]]). ἀξίνη, “axe”, is named only here and in the fighting at the ships in Book 15, beside πέλεκυς, another word for an axe (15.711). They strike at the same moment. The axe hits the ridge of Menelaus' helmet under the crest; the sword hits Peisander's forehead above the base of the nose, the bones crack, and his eyes fall bloody in the dust at his feet, as Cebriones' eyes will fall under Patroclus' stone (13.614–618; 16.740–742; see [[cebriones]]).

**The speech over him.** Menelaus sets his heel on his chest, strips his armor and speaks, not to the dead man but to all the Trojans: they stole his wedded wife after they had been welcomed as guests, they did not fear the wrath of Zeus, god of guests, and they are insatiable of battle (13.618–639; see [[menelaus]], [[xenia]] and [[satiety-of-all-things]]). He gives the bloody armor to his companions (13.640–641).

The name, from πείθω, “persuade”, and ἀνήρ, “man”, is borne by three other men in Homer: the son of Antimachus whom Agamemnon kills (11.122–147; see [[peisander-son-of-antimachus]]), a leader of the Myrmidons (16.193) and a suitor of Penelope (Od. 18.299, 22.243, 22.268).
