## Etymology

Ἑκάβη (Hekabē). The name has no agreed etymology. Hecuba is its Latin form.

## Family

{{quote:7d252193-6f88-5d8f-8c22-418b5aa8bdff}}

She is the mother of [[Hector]], who calls her “lady mother” (πότνια μήτηρ, 6.264), and of [[Laodice]], “the most beautiful in form of her daughters” (6.252). Helenus calls her “your mother and mine” (6.87; see [[helenus]]), and Hector names Deiphobus as the dearest “of my brothers, the sons whom Hecuba and Priam bore” (22.233–234). Priam says that nineteen of his fifty sons were born “of one womb” (24.495–497); he does not name the mother, but she is usually taken to be Hecuba (see [[sons-of-priam]]).

Her own family was Phrygian. Apollo rouses Hector in the likeness of [[asius-son-of-dymas|Asius]], his uncle, “own brother of Hecuba, and son of Dymas, who lived in Phrygia by the streams of the Sangarius” (16.715–720; see [[dymas]] and [[sangarius]]); the Greek leaves it open whether the one who lived there was Dymas or Asius. He is not the Asius son of Hyrtacus who leads the men of Arisbe ([[asius]]).

## Descriptions

ἠπιόδωρος (6.251), found only here in Homer, = ἤπιος (“gentle, kind”) + δῶρον (“gift”): “generous” (see [[generous]]). It is her epithet as she comes to meet her son, and her first act is to offer him wine.

## Elsewhere in Homer

In *Iliad* 22 she begs Hector from the wall, holding up the breast that nursed him, and when he is dead and dragged she leads the Trojan women's lament (22.79–91, 22.405–436).

In *Iliad* 24 she tries to keep Priam from going to Achilles, whose liver she wishes she could eat, then brings him wine to pour to Zeus before he goes, and over Hector's body she speaks the second of the three laments (24.200–216, 24.283–305, 24.747–760; see [[bereaved-parents]], [[libation]] and [[the-laments-for-hector]]).

## Outside Homer

Later writers disagreed about her father. [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] names Dymas, or Cisseus, or the river Sangarius and Metope, makes her Priam's second wife, and tells how before Paris was born she dreamed that she gave birth to a firebrand (*Library* 3.12.5; see [[paris]]). Euripides calls her “Hecuba, daughter of Cisseus” (*Hecuba* 3), and Virgil calls her *Cisseis* (*Aeneid* 7.320, 10.705; see [[cisseus]]).

After the fall of Troy she was a slave. In Euripides' *Trojan Women* she is allotted to Odysseus, answers Helen's defense before Menelaus, and buries Astyanax in Hector's shield (see [[euripides-trojan-women]] and [[euripides-astyanax]]). In his *Hecuba* her daughter Polyxena is sacrificed at Achilles' tomb, and the body of her youngest son Polydorus, murdered for his gold by the Thracian king Polymestor, is washed ashore. In the Iliad Polydorus, Priam's youngest son, is the son of Laothoe (22.46–48; see [[polydorus-son-of-priam]]). She blinds Polymestor and kills his sons, and he foretells that she will become a dog with fiery eyes and that her tomb will be called the Dog's Tomb, “a mark for sailors” (1265–1273). Ovid tells the same story (*Metamorphoses* 13.404–575; see [[ovid]]). In Stesichorus' *Sack of Troy*, Pausanias reports, Apollo carried her away to Lycia (10.27.2; see [[stesichorus]]). Her grief became a byword: “What's Hecuba to him, or he to Hecuba, / That he should weep for her?” (Shakespeare, *Hamlet* 2.2).
