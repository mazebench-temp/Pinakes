## Etymology

Ἑκάβη (Hekabē). The name has no agreed etymology. Hecuba is its Latin form.

## Family

{{quote:7d252193-6f88-5d8f-8c22-418b5aa8bdff}}

She is the mother of [[Hector]], who calls her “lady mother” (πότνια μήτηρ, 6.264), and of [[Laodice]], “the most beautiful in form of her daughters” (6.252). Helenus calls her “your mother and mine” (6.87; see [[helenus]]), and Hector names Deiphobus as the dearest of “the brothers whom Hecuba and Priam bore” (22.233–234). Priam says that nineteen of his fifty sons were born “from one womb” (24.495–497); he does not name the mother, but she is usually taken to be Hecuba (see [[sons-of-priam]]).

Her own family was Phrygian. Apollo rouses Hector in the likeness of Asius, his uncle, “own brother of Hecuba, and son of Dymas, who lived in Phrygia by the streams of the Sangarius” (16.715–720; see [[sangarius]]); the Greek leaves it open whether the one who lived there was Dymas or Asius. He is not the Asius son of Hyrtacus who leads the men of Arisbe ([[asius]]).

## Descriptions

ἠπιόδωρος (6.251), found only here in Homer, = ἤπιος (“gentle, kind”) + δῶρον (“gift”): “generous” (see [[generous]]). It is her epithet as she comes to meet her son, and her first act is to offer him wine.

## Elsewhere in Homer

In Book 22 she stands on the wall beside Priam, loosens the fold of her robe and holds out her breast to Hector, begging him to come inside: if Achilles kills him, neither she nor his “richly dowered wife” will weep over him on a bier (22.79–89). When she sees his body dragged, she tears her hair and flings away her veil (22.405–407), and she begins the Trojan women's lament (22.430–436).

In Book 24 she tries to keep Priam from going to Achilles, a savage, faithless man whose liver she wishes she could eat (24.200–216; see [[bereaved-parents]]). Yet she brings him wine in a golden cup to pour to Zeus. The wine that Hector refused in Book 6 Priam accepts: he washes his hands, pours and prays, and Zeus sends an eagle (24.283–321; see [[libation]]). Her lament over the body comes second of three, after Andromache's: Achilles sold her other sons across the sea, but Hector he dragged around the tomb of Patroclus, and now he lies fresh, “like one whom Apollo has slain with his gentle arrows” (24.747–759).

## Outside Homer

Later writers disagreed about her father. [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] names Dymas, or Cisseus, or the river Sangarius and Metope, makes her Priam's second wife, and tells how before Paris was born she dreamed that she gave birth to a firebrand (*Library* 3.12.5; see [[paris]]). Euripides calls her “Hecuba, daughter of Cisseus” (*Hecuba* 3), and Virgil calls her *Cisseis* (*Aeneid* 7.320, 10.705; see [[cisseus]]).

After the fall of Troy she was a slave. In Euripides' *Trojan Women* she is allotted to Odysseus, answers Helen's defense before Menelaus, and buries Astyanax in Hector's shield (see [[euripides-trojan-women]] and [[euripides-astyanax]]). In his *Hecuba* her daughter Polyxena is sacrificed at Achilles' tomb, and the body of her youngest son Polydorus, murdered for his gold by the Thracian king Polymestor, is washed ashore. She blinds Polymestor and kills his sons, and he foretells that she will become a dog with fiery eyes and that her tomb will be called the Dog's Tomb, “a mark for sailors” (1265–1273). Ovid tells the same story (*Metamorphoses* 13.404–575; see [[ovid]]). In Stesichorus' *Sack of Troy*, Pausanias reports, Apollo carried her away to Lycia (10.27.2; see [[stesichorus]]). Her grief became a byword: “What's Hecuba to him, or he to Hecuba, / That he should weep for her?” (Shakespeare, *Hamlet* 2.2).
