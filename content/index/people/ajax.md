## Etymology

Αἴας (Aias), genitive Αἴαντος. The name has no agreed etymology, and the Greeks explained it in two ways. A scholion on Pindar reports that in the Hesiodic *Great Eoiae* Heracles, a guest in Telamon's house, stood on his lion skin and prayed, and Zeus sent an eagle (αἰετός), from which the child took his name (fr. 3 Evelyn-White); Pindar tells the story in the sixth *Isthmian*. In Sophocles, Ajax hears his own name in the cry of grief αἰαῖ (*Ajax* 430–431).

## Family

Son of [[Telamon]]: Τελαμώνιος and Τελαμωνιάδης, “son of Telamon”, are his usual titles (see [[telamonian]]), and Odysseus greets his shade as “son of noble Telamon” (Od. 11.553). His half-brother [[Teucer]] is a bastard whom Telamon raised in his own house (8.281–284), “his brother by the same father” (κασίγνητος καὶ ὄπατρος, 12.371). Teucer shoots from behind Ajax's shield, and Ajax covers him with it when he falls (8.266–272, 8.330–331).

Homer names no mother, wife or child for him, and does not make Telamon a son of Aeacus. Later writers did. Pindar and Sophocles call his mother Eriboea; Telamon became the brother of Peleus, and Ajax the cousin of Achilles (see [[telamon]] and [[aeacus]]); and Sophocles gives him a captive wife, Tecmessa, and a son, Eurysaces. The Athenian family of Miltiades traced its descent to Philaeus, “the son of Ajax” (Herodotus 6.35).

## Descriptions

{{quote:e12d7b62-92de-51f2-86ac-41170cbc3c37}}

πελώριος, “huge”, is from πέλωρ, “monster, prodigy”, and ἕρκος, “fence, wall”, gives ἕρκος Ἀχαιῶν, “the bulwark of the Achaeans”. The narrator repeats Helen's words (6.5, 7.211; see [[huge]] and [[bulwark-of-the-achaeans]]). μέγας, “great”, sets him apart from the other Ajax, who is μείων, “lesser” (2.528; see [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]). With the son of Oileus he makes a pair, “the two Ajaxes”, who fight side by side like two oxen at the plough (13.701–708) and call themselves ὁμώνυμοι, “of one name” (17.720).

{{quote:7a36300d-342c-59c6-9f8f-a6b668b1f744}}

Elsewhere he is the best after Achilles “in form and in deeds” (17.279–280; Od. 11.550–551; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]), and Idomeneus says that in a standing fight he would not give way even to Achilles (13.321–325). Odysseus calls him a “tower” (πύργος, Od. 11.556), and his shield is “like a tower” (see [[the-shield-of-ajax]]). He is a man of few words: his speech in the embassy of Book 9 is the shortest of the three (9.624–642), and Hector calls him ἁμαρτοεπής, “blundering in speech”, and βουγάϊος, a word of uncertain sense, perhaps “braggart” (13.824).

## Elsewhere in Homer

**The line and the embassy.** His ships are drawn up at one end of the camp and Achilles' at the other (8.222–226 = 11.5–9). He is one of the three envoys to Achilles in Book 9, and his short, blunt speech wins the only concession Achilles makes (9.624–655; see [[the-embassy-to-achilles]]).

**The wall and the ships.** He rescues Odysseus, cut off among the Trojans (11.465–491), and when Zeus puts fear in him he gives ground step by step, like a lion driven from the cattle or a donkey that boys can hardly beat out of the grain, with spears standing in his shield (11.544–574). He holds the wall with the other Ajax and with Teucer, and drives Sarpedon back from the battlement (12.265–270, 12.370–407). He fells Hector with a stone (14.409–420). At the ships he strides from deck to deck with a pike twenty-two cubits long, tells the army that no wall and no city stands behind them, “so the light is in our hands”, and wounds twelve men who come with fire (15.674–688, 15.733–746). He gives way only when Hector lops the head from his spear and he sees the work of Zeus, and the first ship burns (16.114–123).

**Patroclus.** He stands over the body of Patroclus like a lion over its cubs (17.128–137). When Zeus hides the field in mist he prays: “Father Zeus, rescue the sons of the Achaeans from the mist … and in the light destroy us, since that is your pleasure”, and Zeus, pitying him, scatters the mist (17.645–650). With the other Ajax he holds back the Trojans while Menelaus and Meriones carry the body away (17.715–753).

**The games.** At the funeral games he wins no contest outright. He wrestles Odysseus to a draw (23.708–739); the Achaeans, fearing for him, stop his armed fight with Diomedes (23.811–825); and Polypoetes outthrows him with the iron weight (23.836–849; see [[contests]]). The wrestling match has been read as a glance at his later contest with Odysseus for Achilles' arms.

**The Odyssey.** Nestor says that Ajax lies at Troy with Achilles and Patroclus (Od. 3.109). In the underworld his shade alone stands apart, still angry over the arms of Achilles, which Odysseus won from him in a judgment by the ships, “and the sons of the Trojans and Pallas Athena were the judges” (Od. 11.543–547). Odysseus wishes he had never won them and begs him to let his anger go; Ajax gives no answer and walks away into Erebus (Od. 11.548–564). The Odyssey says that the earth covered him because of the arms (Od. 11.549), but not how he died.

## Outside Homer

**The arms.** In the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* he is one of Helen's suitors, promising to round up the cattle and sheep of his neighbors, since he was outstanding with the long spear (fr. 68 Evelyn-White). In the *Aethiopis*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, he carried the body of Achilles to the ships while Odysseus held off the Trojans, and the two quarreled over the dead man's arms. In the *Little Iliad* Odysseus won them by the will of Athena, and Ajax went mad, ravaged the Achaeans' plunder and killed himself. Pindar blamed the Danaans' secret votes and Odysseus' shifting lies (*Nemean* 8), and Homer's sweet words, which made Odysseus' story greater than his sufferings (*Nemean* 7; see [[pindar]]).

**Tragedy and after.** In Sophocles' *Ajax* Athena turns his madness on the army's herds, which he slaughters, thinking them the sons of Atreus and Odysseus. Come to himself, he takes leave of Tecmessa and his son (see [[sophocles-tecmessa]]) and falls on the sword that Hector gave him (see [[sophocles-sword-and-belt]]); Odysseus persuades Agamemnon to allow his burial. In Ovid the judgment becomes a contest of speeches, and from Ajax's blood springs a flower marked with the letters of his name (*Metamorphoses* 13.1–398; see [[ovid]]). In Plato's myth of Er his soul, remembering the judgment of the arms, chooses the life of a lion (*Republic* 10.620b; see [[plato]]). The author of *On the Sublime* found his silence in the underworld greater than any speech (9.2; see [[longinus]]).

**Cult.** Before the battle of Salamis in 480 BC the Greeks prayed to the gods and “called on Ajax and Telamon from Salamis itself”, and after it they dedicated a captured Phoenician trireme to Ajax on the island (Herodotus 8.64, 8.121). Cleisthenes named one of the ten Athenian tribes, Aiantis, after him, adding him “as a neighbor and ally”, though he was a foreigner (Herodotus 5.66; see [[herodotus]]). Pausanias saw his sanctuary on Salamis, with a statue of ebony (1.35.3). At Rhoeteum, on the shore of the Hellespont, visitors were shown his tomb, a temple and a statue, which Antony carried off to Egypt and Augustus gave back (Strabo 13.1.30; see [[strabo]]).
