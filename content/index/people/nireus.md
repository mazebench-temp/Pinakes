## The three lines

Each of the first three lines begins with his name, and each adds something: his ships, his parents, his beauty. The figure is [[anaphora]], and it is unusual in the Catalogue, where a leader is normally named once. It gives the smallest contingent in the army a monument.

The praise is qualified at once. Nireus is the most handsome man who came to Ilios "after the noble son of Peleus", the same measure the Catalogue will use for the best of the fighters (2.768–770; see [[best-of-the-achaeans]]). Then comes the reversal: he was ἀλαπαδνός, "weak, feeble", and few men followed him.

The line about his beauty has a twin earlier in the book. Thersites is αἴσχιστος δὲ ἀνὴρ ὑπὸ Ἴλιον ἦλθε, "the ugliest man who came to Ilios" (2.216); Nireus is ὃς κάλλιστος ἀνὴρ ὑπὸ Ἴλιον ἦλθε, "the most handsome man who came to Ilios". The book has both extremes, and neither man counts for anything in battle. See [[thersites]].

## Outside Homer

Aristotle cites the passage to show how repetition makes a small subject seem great: Homer mentions Nireus once and never again, and yet makes him memorable (*Rhetoric* 3.12, 1414a2–7; see [[aristotle-nireus]]). Nireus became a byword for beauty in Latin poetry (Horace, *Odes* 3.20.15, *Epodes* 15.22). In Lucian's *Dialogues of the Dead* he and Thersites argue over which of them is the handsomer, and Menippus tells them that among the dead there is no difference. In the *Posthomerica* of Quintus of Smyrna he is killed by Eurypylus, the son of Telephus (book 6).
