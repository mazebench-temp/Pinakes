Hector has thrown at Idomeneus, who stands in his chariot, and missed him by a little (17.608–609; see [[hector]] and [[idomeneus]]). The spear finds the driver. Coeranus is named only as he dies: Meriones' ὀπάων and ἡνίοχος, his “squire and charioteer” (17.610; see [[meriones]] and [[chariot]]), who had followed him from Lyctus in Crete (see [[lyctus]] and [[cretans]]). ὀπάων is the word for Meriones himself at Idomeneus' side (7.165, 8.263, 10.58, 17.258), so that the squire has a squire of his own.

A parenthesis tells why Idomeneus had need of him. He had left the ships on foot, “and he would have put great power in the Trojans' hands, / had not Coeranus quickly driven up the swift-footed horses” (17.613–614; see the translation's note on 17.612–616 and [[swift-footed]]). “And he came as a light to him and kept off the pitiless day” (17.615): φάος, “light”, is the light of rescue (see the translation's note on 15.741), and νηλεὲς ἦμαρ, “the pitiless day”, is the day of death (see [[pitiless]]). The man who saved the leader dies in his place, “at the hands of man-slaying Hector” (17.616; see [[man-slaying]]). The spear strikes “under the jaw and the ear”, where Paris struck Euchenor and Meriones struck Laogonus (17.617 = 13.671 = 16.606; see [[euchenor]] and [[laogonus]]), and knocks out his teeth and cuts through his tongue; he falls from the chariot and the reins pour to the ground (17.617–619).

Meriones stoops to pick up the reins from the plain and tells Idomeneus to whip the horses to the ships, “you know yourself that the power is no longer with the Achaeans”, and Idomeneus flees in fear (17.620–625).

Another Coeranus, a Lycian, is the first of the seven men whom Odysseus kills in Book 5 (5.677; see [[coeranus-of-lycia]]). The name is the common noun κοίρανος, “lord, commander” (see [[koiranos]]).
