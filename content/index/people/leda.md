## The name

W. W. Merry suggests that Λήδη may be the same word as *lada*, “wife”, which is found in Lycian inscriptions (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.298).

## Family

Homer gives her one verse of family: she is “the wife of Tyndareus”, τὴν Τυνδαρέου παράκοιτιν, and she bore to Tyndareus two fierce-hearted sons, Castor and Polydeuces (*Odyssey* 11.298–300; see [[tyndareus]], [[castor]], [[polydeuces]] and [[fierce-hearted]]). The poems never say that she was the mother of Helen. Helen is “born of Zeus”, Διὸς ἐκγεγαυῖα (*Iliad* 3.199, 3.418; *Odyssey* 4.184, 4.219), and the brothers she looks for from the wall of Troy are her own, “whom one mother bore with me” (*Iliad* 3.238); the Iliad does not name that mother, and the Odyssey, which names the brothers' mother, does not call her Helen's (see [[helen]]). Clytemnestra is “the daughter of Tyndareus” in the last book of the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 24.199, a plain rendering; see [[clytemnestra]]).

Merry gives the later genealogy: Leda was the daughter of Thestius, king in Aetolia, and the mother of four children, Castor and Polydeuces, Helen and Clytemnestra, though their fathers are variously given; later forms of the story make Clytemnestra and Castor the children of Tyndareus, and Helen and Polydeuces the children of Zeus, so that Castor is mortal and Polydeuces immortal (on *Odyssey* 11.298; see [[the-death-of-the-dioscuri]]).

## Outside Homer

**The mother of the sons of Zeus.** The Homeric Hymns to the Dioscuri call Castor and Polydeuces both Τυνδαρίδαι, “sons of Tyndareus”, and sons of Zeus. In the shorter hymn lady Leda, πότνια Λήδη, bore them under the peaks of Taygetus, secretly overcome by the dark-clouded son of Cronus (*Hymn* 17.2–4); in the longer they are “the splendid children of Leda of the lovely ankles”, Λήδης καλλισφύρου ἀγλαὰ τέκνα, whom she bore to the son of Cronus under the summit of Taygetus, to save men and swift ships when storms rush over the sea (*Hymn* 33.1–8; see [[taygetus]]). In the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, as a scholiast on Pindar reports it, both brothers are sons of Zeus, and Helen is the child neither of Leda nor of Nemesis, but of a daughter of Oceanus and Zeus (fr. 66 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]).

**The daughter of Thestius.** Strabo, writing of the Lacedaemonians who settled in Acarnania, reports that Tyndareus and his brother Icarius, driven from home by Hippocoon, went to Thestius, ruler of the Pleuronians, and helped him win much of the land beyond the Achelous; Tyndareus went home again, having married Leda, the daughter of Thestius, while Icarius stayed on in Acarnania, where Penelope was born to him (*Geography* 10.2.24; see [[strabo]] and [[icarius]]). In Euripides' *Iphigenia at Aulis* Agamemnon begins his story of the war with her: “There were born to Leda, daughter of Thestius, three maidens, / Phoebe, and Clytemnestra, my wife, / and Helen” (49–51; see [[iphigenia-at-aulis]]).

**The swan.** In Euripides' *Helen* Helen names Sparta as her country and Tyndareus as her father, and adds: “there is a story that Zeus flew to my mother Leda, taking the form of a bird, a swan, and accomplished a treacherous union, fleeing from the pursuit of an eagle, if the story is true” (*Helen* 16–21; see [[euripides]]). In the *Orestes* Orestes remembers how Tyndareus carried him in his arms as a child, with Leda beside him, both honoring him no less than the Dioscuri (*Orestes* 462–465; see [[orestes-play]]).
