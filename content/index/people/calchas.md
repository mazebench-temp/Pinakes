## Etymology

Κάλχας (Kalchas). The name has been linked with κάλχη (the purple murex, and a purple dye) and with καλχαίνω ("ponder darkly"), but its origin is unknown.

## In Book 1

{{quote:826b195b-6f5c-551d-9e2c-0d1f5b52dc7f}}

οἰωνοπόλος = οἰωνός (bird of omen) + -πόλος (one who busies himself with): a bird-seer, an augur. His knowledge spans all time: "the things that are, the things to come, and the things that were before". Hesiod uses the same line of the Muses. See [[calchas-and-the-muses]].

Agamemnon's reply shows an old grievance: "Prophet of evils, never yet have you told me anything good" (1.106). See [[calchas-evil-prophecies]].

## Outside Homer

In Iliad 2.299–332 Odysseus recalls how, at Aulis, Calchas read the omen of the snake and the nine sparrows as nine years of war and victory in the tenth. In the *Cypria* it was he who declared that Iphigenia must be sacrificed. A later story, attributed to Hesiod by Strabo (14.1.27), has him die of grief after losing a contest of prophecy to the seer Mopsus.
