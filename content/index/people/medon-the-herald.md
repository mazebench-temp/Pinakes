## The name

Μέδων is the participle of μέδω, “rule, take thought for”, made a name: μέδοντες are the “rulers” of the Argives in the Iliad (*Iliad* 2.79), and Phorcys is ἁλὸς ἀτρυγέτοιο μέδοντος, the one “who rules over the barren sea” (*Odyssey* 1.72; see [[phorcys-the-sea-god]]). The Iliad gives the name to the bastard son of Oileus, the half-brother of the Locrian Ajax, who led Philoctetes' men while he lay on Lemnos (*Iliad* 2.727; see [[medon]]), and to an ally of the Trojans (*Iliad* 17.216; see [[medon-ally-of-the-trojans]]). Nothing joins them to the herald of Ithaca.

## Descriptions

- κῆρυξ, “herald” (*Odyssey* 4.677; see [[heralds]]).
- πεπνυμένα εἰδώς, “who had wise understanding” (*Odyssey* 4.696, 4.711; again 22.361, 24.442), the end of the verse in which he speaks (see [[wise]]).

## Later in the Odyssey

Medon is the herald who waits on the suitors: when Telemachus counts them for his father, the young men from Dulichium, Same, Zacynthus and Ithaca have with them “Medon the herald and the divine singer and two attendants, skilled in carving” (*Odyssey* 16.247–253, a plain rendering). Of all the heralds he pleased them most and was present at their feasts, and it is he who calls them in from their games to dinner (*Odyssey* 17.170–176). His warning to Penelope is recalled when she comes down to rebuke Antinous: she had heard of the plot against her son, “for the herald Medon told her, who heard their counsels” (*Odyssey* 16.412 = 4.677).

When Odysseus kills the suitors, Medon hides under a chair, wrapped in a freshly flayed oxhide. Telemachus asks his father to spare him, “who always cared for me in our house when I was a child”; Medon throws off the hide and clasps Telemachus' knees, and Odysseus, smiling, tells him to take heart and to tell others “how much better good deeds are than evil deeds”, and sends him out with the singer Phemius to sit by the altar of Zeus in the courtyard (*Odyssey* 22.354–380, plain renderings; see [[phemius]] and [[telemachus]]). In the last book he comes from the house to the assembly of the suitors' kinsmen and tells them that Odysseus did not do these things against the will of the gods: he himself saw an immortal god standing beside him, in all things like Mentor (*Odyssey* 24.439–449; see [[mentor]]).
