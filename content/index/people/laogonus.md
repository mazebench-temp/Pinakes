**The priest's son.** “Then in turn Meriones killed a helmeted man of the Trojans, / Laogonus, the bold son of Onetor, who was the priest of Zeus / of Ida, and was honored by the people like a god” (16.603–605; see [[meriones]] and [[helmeted]]). The relative clause belongs to the father: Onetor is the priest (see [[onetor]] and [[honored-like-a-god]]). So Walter Leaf takes it, comparing Hypsenor, son of Dolopion, priest of the Scamander, whose father is described in the same words (5.76–78): “Here also ὅς refers to the non-combatant father” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.604–605; see [[hypsenor]] and [[dolopion]]). The fight around Sarpedon's body has just cost the Achaeans Bathycles, the wealthiest of the Myrmidons, and Meriones answers with the priest's son (16.593–607; see [[bathycles]]).

**His death.** Meriones strikes him “under the jaw and the ear; and quickly his spirit / went from his limbs, and hateful darkness seized him” (16.606–607; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). The two verses are those of the death of Euchenor, son of the Corinthian seer Polyidus, whom Paris killed in Book 13 (13.671–672; see [[euchenor]] and [[polyidus-the-seer]]): the son of a seer and the son of a priest die in the same words. Aeneas then throws his spear at Meriones and misses, and the two exchange taunts until Patroclus rebukes Meriones (16.608–631; see [[aeneas]]).

**Another Laogonus.** Laogonus, son of Bias, falls to Achilles with his brother Dardanus (20.460–462; see [[laogonus-son-of-bias]]). The name joins λαός, “people”, and γόνος, “offspring”.
