He is named only here, in the story of the mare that Menelaus yokes beside his own horse for the chariot race at Patroclus' funeral games (23.293–300; see [[aethe-and-podargus]] and [[menelaus]]). Ἐχέπωλος joins ἔχω (“have, hold”) and πῶλος (“foal, horse”): “owner of horses”, a fitting name for a man who pays with a mare. Ἀγχισιάδης, “son of Anchises”, is elsewhere Aeneas (17.754, 20.160; see [[aeneas]]); this Anchises is another man, of whom the poem says nothing more. Sicyon, where Echepolus lived on the wealth that Zeus had given him, is one of the towns of Agamemnon's kingdom in the Catalogue, “and Sicyon, where Adrastus was king at first” (2.569–572; see [[sicyon]] and [[adrastus]]).

The mare was given in place of service. Euchenor of Corinth, another rich man of Agamemnon's kingdom, chose to sail to Troy knowing that he would die there, and so avoided “the grievous fine of the Achaeans” and a death by sickness at home (13.663–670; see [[euchenor]]). Echepolus paid, so that he “might stay at home and take his pleasure” (23.298). Walter Leaf refers to the fine for this payment in place of personal service (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.297, citing his note on 13.669).

The name is also that of the Trojan whom Antilochus kills, the first man to die in the battles of the Iliad (4.457–462; see [[echepolus]]). In the chariot race it is Antilochus again who forces his way past the mare of this Echepolus, where the road narrows (23.415–437; see [[antilochus]]).

## Outside Homer

Leaf reports the dry remark of the scholia, after Plutarch, that Agamemnon thought it more useful to take a war horse than a man who would not serve (on 23.297).
