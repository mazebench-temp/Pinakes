Homer names him only as a father, in the genitive Πιτθῆος (“of Pittheus”). The patronymic is the one detail that sets Aethra apart from the other servants in the poem.

## Outside Homer

Plutarch makes Pittheus a son of Pelops and the king of Troezen, a small city, but a man with the greatest name for wisdom in his time. When Aegeus of Athens came to him with an oracle he could not understand, Pittheus understood it and brought it about that Aegeus lay with his daughter Aethra. Their son was Theseus, whom Pittheus brought up at Troezen (Plutarch, *Theseus* 3–4). Euripides' Hippolytus, the son of Theseus, is “the pupil of holy Pittheus” (*Hippolytus* 11).
