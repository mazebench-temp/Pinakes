**The dancer.** χορῷ καλὴ Πολυμήλη, “Polymele, beautiful in the dance” (16.180): χορός is both the dance and the band of dancers, and her epithet names the place where her story begins. Hermes saw her “with his eyes among the girls singing and dancing / in the dance of Artemis of the golden distaff, the clamorous” (16.182–183): a band of girls singing and dancing for Artemis (see [[artemis]] and [[dance]]). In the Odyssey Nausicaa playing among her maids is likened to Artemis among her nymphs (*Odyssey* 6.102–109). He fell in love with her, ἠράσατʼ (16.182), the verb of Zeus' list of his loves (14.317) and of the North Wind's love for the mares of Erichthonius (20.223; see [[eros]]).

**The secret union.** “At once he went up to her upper room and lay with her in secret” (εἰς ὑπερῷʼ ἀναβὰς παρελέξατο λάθρῃ, 16.184; see [[hermes]]). The words are those of Ares and Astyoche in the Catalogue: the maiden “went up to the upper room” of her father's house, and the god “lay with her in secret” (2.514–515; see [[astyoche]] and [[the-birth-of-ascalaphus-and-ialmenus]]). The upper room is the women's part of the house, where Penelope keeps herself in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 1.328–330, 18.206). Polymele's son is παρθένιος, “a maiden's son” (16.180), born to a girl not yet married (see [[eudorus]]).

**The marriage.** After Eileithyia had brought the child into the light, “the strong might of Echecles, son of Actor, took her / to his house, after giving a countless bride-price” (16.189–190; see [[echecles]] and [[bride-price]]). ἐπεὶ πόρε μυρία ἕδνα is the formula of a marriage made: so Hector led Andromache from the house of Eetion (22.472), and so Neleus married Chloris in the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 11.282). Polymele goes to her husband's house, and her son stays in her father's: the old man Phylas reared him as if he were his own son (16.191–192; see [[phylas]] and [[the-birth-of-eudorus]]).

**The name.** Πολυμήλη is the feminine of πολύμηλος, “rich in flocks”, an epithet of a town and of wealthy men (2.605, 2.705, 14.490; see [[rich-in-flocks]]). One of those men is the Trojan Phorbas, “whom most / of the Trojans Hermes loved, and granted him wealth” (14.490–491). The Trojan Polymelus, son of Argeas, whom Patroclus kills in the same book as Polymele's story, bears the masculine as a name (16.417).

## Outside Homer

The chorus of Artemis is the scene of Hermes' snatching in the Homeric *Hymn to Aphrodite*. Aphrodite, disguised as a Phrygian princess, tells Anchises that the slayer of Argus carried her off “from the chorus of Artemis of the golden distaff, the clamorous”, where many nymphs and maidens danced, and brought her to be Anchises' wedded wife (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 117–127; see [[aphrodite-and-anchises]]). The verse ends with the words of Iliad 16.183.
