Glaucus names Proetus only as Bellerophon's persecutor and does not name his city. His people are the Argives, and Bellerophon's Ephyra lay “in a corner of horse-pasturing Argos” (6.152; see [[ephyra-in-argos]]). The sentence is read in more than one way. The translation makes Proetus “far the mightier among the Argives”, and the Argives the people whom Zeus had made subject to his scepter. Others take “of the Argives” with “the land” (“drove him from the land of the Argives”) and make Bellerophon the man whom Zeus had put under Proetus' scepter, and some make Bellerophon the one who was “far the better”.

When Anteia accuses Bellerophon, “anger seized the lord at what he heard” (6.166). But he “shrank from killing him, for his heart held back in awe from that” (6.167; see [[sebas]]). Homer does not say what he held in awe. In later versions Bellerophon had come to him as an exile and been purified by him (Apollodorus 2.3.1), so the man he wanted dead was his guest. He sends Bellerophon instead to his πενθερός, his “father-in-law” (6.170), the king of Lycia, who calls Proetus his γαμβρός, “son-in-law” (6.177–178; see [[in-laws]]). The killing he will not do is to be done by his wife's father, on the word of the signs on the tablet (see [[writing]] and [[bellerophon]]).

## Outside Homer

[[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] makes Proetus and Acrisius twin sons of Abas, who quarreled already in the womb. Driven from Argos, Proetus went to Lycia, married the daughter of king Iobates, whom Homer calls Anteia and the tragedians Stheneboea, and came back with a Lycian army. He took Tiryns, which the Cyclopes walled for him, and the brothers divided the Argolid, Acrisius ruling at Argos (*Library* 2.2.1). His daughters went mad, “as Hesiod says, because they would not accept the rites of Dionysus”, and the seer Melampus cured them (*Library* 2.2.2). In the Catalogue of Ships Tiryns belongs to Diomedes (2.559; see [[tiryns]]).
