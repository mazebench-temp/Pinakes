Erylaus is the third man Patroclus kills when he pens the Trojans between the ships, the river and the wall, after Pronous and Thestor, and he is killed with a stone (16.394–414; see [[patroclus]], [[pronous]], [[thestor-son-of-enops]] and [[stone-throwing]]): “Then he struck Erylaus with a stone as he rushed on, / full on the head; and it was all split in two / in the heavy helmet; and he fell face down on the ground, / and death that destroys the spirit was poured around him” (16.411–414; see [[helmet]] and [[that-destroys-the-spirit]]). Nine more follow in three verses (16.415–418; see [[erymas-killed-by-patroclus]]).

**Twice told.** The same words return some hundred and sixty verses later, when Hector kills the Myrmidon Epeigeus with a stone as he takes hold of Sarpedon's body: “and it was all split in two / in the heavy helmet; and he fell face down on the body, / and death that destroys the spirit was poured around him” (16.578–580; see [[epeigeus]] and [[hector]]). The Trojan falls “on the ground”, the Achaean “on the body”; the rest is the same. Patroclus answers Epeigeus' death with a stone of his own, which kills Sthenelaus (16.586–587; see [[sthenelaus]]).

**The passage.** Walter Leaf judged 16.411–417 “an insignificant and mechanical production” that could well be dispensed with, made of names and of verses found elsewhere (16.412 = 20.387; 16.418 = 12.194). He also noted that Patroclus, who throws a stone here, must be on foot, though he was driving his chariot at 16.378 and leaps down from it at 16.427, and took this for an instance of the quick mounting and dismounting of Homer's fighters (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.411).
