**The bearer.** When Hector's stone has felled Teucer and Ajax covers him with his shield, “two trusty companions stooped under him, Mecisteus, son of Echius, and brilliant Alastor, and carried him, groaning heavily, to the hollow ships” (8.332–334; see [[teucer]], [[ajax]] and [[mecisteus-son-of-echius]]). In Book 13 the same pair carries Hypsenor, son of Hippasus, Antilochus' companion, whom Deiphobus has killed (13.411–423; see [[antilochus]]). δῖος, “brilliant”, is all the description he is given (see [[brilliant]]).

**One Alastor or two?** The Iliad has at least four men of the name: one of the five captains around whom Nestor marshals the Pylians (4.295; see [[alastor]]), a Lycian whom Odysseus kills (5.677; see [[alastor-of-lycia]]), this bearer, and the father of the Trojan Tros, whom Achilles kills (20.463). The bearer could be the Pylian captain. In Book 13 the man he carries is Antilochus' own companion (ἑοῦ … ἑταίρου, 13.419), and Antilochus is Nestor's son, so the bearers there may well be Pylians. But in Book 8 they carry Teucer, Ajax's brother, as his “trusty companions”, and the poem never connects Alastor with Pylos, nor gives him a father or a home. The rescue is told in a block of four verses, with both names inside it, that serves for both men (8.331–334 = 13.420–423), and the same poet gives the pair of names Alastor and Chromius both to Nestor's captains and to Lycians (4.295, 5.677). The index therefore keeps the bearer apart from the captain, without ruling out that they are one man. On the name, ἀλάστωρ, later the avenging spirit of tragedy, see [[alastor]].
