## The name

Εὐρύαλος is a name of the Iliad too (see below). Among the Phaeacians it stands with the names of the young men who rise for the games before him, Ὠκύαλος, Ἀγχίαλος and Ἀμφίαλος (*Odyssey* 8.111–114), which seem to end, as his does, in ἅλς, “sea”. Almost all Phaeacian names, W. W. Merry observes, are taken from circumstances of seafaring life, and he excepts only Alcinous, Arete, Laodamas, Polybus and Rhexenor (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 8.111; see [[phaeacians]]).

## Family

He is Ναυβολίδης, “the son of Naubolus” (*Odyssey* 8.116), a father known only through the patronymic (see [[naubolus-father-of-euryalus]]). The common reading Ναυβολίδης θʼ would make “the son of Naubolus” another competitor; the text, with La Roche, Bekker and Nauck, omits θʼ, and La Roche argued that Homer does not call obscure men by their father's name alone, but only men known to his hearers from elsewhere, as the sons of Peleus, Atreus, Tydeus and Menoetius (Merry's critical note on *Odyssey* 8.116; see the Odyssey translation's note on 8.116).

## Descriptions

- βροτολοιγῷ ἶσος Ἄρηϊ, “the equal of Ares, ruin of mortals” (*Odyssey* 8.115), the verse-end that the Iliad gives twice to Hector (*Iliad* 11.295, 13.802; see [[ruin-of-mortals]] and [[ares]]).
- ὃς ἄριστος ἔην εἶδός τε δέμας τε / πάντων Φαιήκων μετʼ ἀμύμονα Λαοδάμαντα, “who was the best in looks and form / of all the Phaeacians after noble Laodamas” (*Odyssey* 8.116–117; see [[laodamas-son-of-alcinous]] and [[eidos]]). The verses are those with which the Odyssey praises Ajax, best in looks and form of the other Danaans “after the noble son of Peleus”, μετʼ ἀμύμονα Πηλεΐωνα (*Odyssey* 11.469–470 = 24.17–18, Greek only), the verse-end of Nireus, the handsomest of the Danaans who came to Ilios (*Iliad* 2.673–674; see [[ajax]] and [[nireus]]): the young Phaeacian stands to the king's son as Ajax to Achilles. Odysseus grants him his looks and denies him a mind: εἶδος μὲν ἀριπρεπές, “your looks are outstanding” (*Odyssey* 8.176; see [[different-gifts]]).

Homer's other Euryalus is the Argive [[euryalus|son of Mecisteus]], “a man equal to the gods”, the third leader of the men of Argos and Tiryns, who boxes against Epeius at the funeral games of Patroclus and is knocked senseless (*Iliad* 2.565–566, 23.677–699). Both men of the name are athletes, the Argive a boxer and the Phaeacian a wrestler (*Odyssey* 8.127).
