Ἀγκαῖον δὲ πάλῃ Πλευρώνιον, ὅς μοι ἀνέστη, “and in wrestling Ancaeus of Pleuron, who stood up against me” (23.635): the second of the four victories that Nestor claims at the games of Amarynceus, and all that Homer tells of him (see [[the-funeral-games-of-amarynceus]] and [[nestor]]). Πλευρώνιος, “of Pleuron”, makes him an Aetolian, for Pleuron is one of the towns of the Aetolians in the Catalogue (2.639; see [[pleuron]] and [[aetolians]]), and the Aetolians are among the peoples whose champions Nestor says he outdid (23.633).

ὅς μοι ἀνέστη, “who stood up against me”: the verb with a dative is the challenger's, as when Euryalus alone “stood up against” Epeius in the boxing (Εὐρύαλος δέ οἱ οἶος ἀνίστατο, 23.677; see [[euryalus]]), and when Melantho warns the beggar Odysseus, who has just beaten Irus with his fists, that a better man than Irus may soon stand up against him (*Odyssey* 18.333–336). Walter Leaf, who compares both places, thinks the phrase “evidently technical” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.635).

The Arcadian Ancaeus, whose son Agapenor leads the Arcadians at Troy (2.609), is another man (see [[ancaeus]] and [[agapenor]]).
