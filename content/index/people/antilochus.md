## Etymology

Ἀντίλοχος joins ἀντί (“against, facing”) and λόχος (“ambush”, and the armed band that lies in one). Other names in the poem are formed on λόχος in the same way, such as Ἀρχέλοχος, one of Antenor's sons (2.823).

## Family

Book 4 names him without his father. From Book 5 on he is “the son of great-hearted Nestor” (μεγαθύμου Νέστορος υἱός, 5.565, 13.400, 17.653, 23.541; see [[great-hearted]]), or simply Νεστορίδης, “son of Nestor” (6.33, 15.589). He fights beside his brother Thrasymedes; together they are “the sons of Nestor” (Νεστορίδαι, 16.317). The Odyssey names Nestor's wife, Eurydice (Od. 3.451–452), and his sons at home in Pylos, among them Thrasymedes and Peisistratus (Od. 3.412–415), but not Antilochus' mother.

## Descriptions

- μενεχάρμης = μένω (“stand fast”) + χάρμη (“battle”; see [[charme]]): “steadfast in battle” (13.396, 15.582, 23.419).
- Young and swift. Menelaus tells him that no other Achaean is younger than he, or swifter of foot, or as brave in the fight (15.569–570). He runs to Achilles πόδας ταχύς, “swift of foot” (18.2), and at the games “he beat all the young men in running” (23.756). The Odyssey remembers him as πέρι μὲν θείειν ταχὺς ἠδὲ μαχητής, “surpassing in speed of foot, and a fighter” (Od. 3.112, 4.202).
- πεπνυμένος, “sensible, wise” (23.570, 23.586), and ἀμύμων, “noble”, in the Iliad (23.522) and in the Odyssey (Od. 4.187, 11.468, 24.16).

## Elsewhere in Homer

**In the Iliad.** He fights in most of the great battles. He kills Ablerus (6.32–33), and in the battle at the ships kills the charioteer of Asius, stands over his fallen companion Hypsenor, and strips Thoon while Poseidon keeps the spears from him (13.394–401, 13.417–423, 13.545–565). He kills Melanippus, leaping on him like a dog on a wounded fawn, and then runs from Hector like a beast that has done harm (15.568–591).

When Patroclus falls, Menelaus sends him to Achilles with the news. He is struck dumb, his eyes fill with tears, and he runs to the ships (17.679–701). He tells Achilles that Patroclus lies dead, and holds his hands as he groans, afraid that he will cut his throat (18.16–34).

At the funeral games he drives Nestor's horses. He forces past Menelaus where the road narrows and comes in second “by trickery, not by speed” (23.402–447, 23.515). When Achilles proposes to give his prize to Eumelus, he refuses, and Achilles smiles, “rejoicing in Antilochus, because he was his dear companion” (23.555–556). Menelaus accuses him of foul driving; Antilochus gives way to the older man, and Menelaus lets him keep the mare (23.566–611). He comes in last in the footrace, says that the gods honor older men, and wins half a talent of gold from Achilles for his compliment (23.785–797).

**In the Odyssey.** Nestor counts him among the best who died at Troy: “my own dear son, strong and noble, Antilochus” (Od. 3.111–112). His brother Peisistratus, who never met him, weeps for him as the man “whom the shining son of bright Dawn killed” (Od. 4.186–202), that is, Memnon, whom the Odyssey names elsewhere (Od. 11.522). His ghost comes with those of Achilles, Patroclus and Ajax (Od. 11.467–469, 24.15–17). His bones lie in the great mound on the Hellespont, apart from those of Achilles and Patroclus, which are mixed in one golden jar; after Patroclus, Achilles honored him above all his companions (Od. 24.76–84).

## Outside Homer

In Proclus' summary of the *Aethiopis*, Memnon, son of the Dawn, comes to help the Trojans in armor made by Hephaestus; he kills Antilochus, and Achilles then kills him. Pindar tells the death as the model of a son's devotion. Paris' arrow wounded one of Nestor's horses and stopped his chariot, Memnon came on, and Antilochus, called by his father, stood his ground and bought his father's life with his own (*Pythian* 6.28–42). Quintus of Smyrna retells the story in the second book of his *Posthomerica*. Apollodorus counts him among the seven sons of Nestor by Anaxibia, daughter of Cratieus (*Library* 1.9.9).

A friend of Achilles killed by a Trojan champion and avenged by Achilles: the story of the *Aethiopis* has the shape of the story of Patroclus and Hector. The Neoanalysts argued that the Iliad was modeled on an older poem about Memnon (Heinrich Pestalozzi, *Die Achilleis als Quelle der Ilias*, 1945; Wolfgang Schadewaldt); others hold that the *Aethiopis* imitated the Iliad. The question is still disputed.
