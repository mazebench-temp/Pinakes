## Etymology

Εὐρυσθεύς is formed from εὐρύς, "wide", and σθένος, "strength", the same elements as εὐρυσθενής, "wide in strength", an epithet of Poseidon (8.201; see [[wide-in-strength]]).

## Family

Son of Sthenelus, the son of Perseus: "Eurystheus, son of Sthenelus, son of Perseus, of your race" (Εὐρυσθεὺς Σθενέλοιο πάϊς Περσηϊάδαο, 19.123). Through Perseus he is of the blood of Zeus, like Heracles. His father is not the Sthenelus of the Iliad, Diomedes' charioteer (see [[sthenelus]]).

## In Homer

Book 8 names him first, in Athena's complaint against her father. Zeus forgets "how very often I saved his son / when he was worn out by the labors that Eurystheus set him" (8.362–363), and she names one of them: "when Eurystheus sent him to the house of Hades the gatekeeper / to bring from Erebus the hound of hateful Hades" (8.367–368; see [[heracles]], [[athena]] and [[heracles-and-cerberus]]). The Greek of 8.367 does not name the sender; the translation supplies him from 8.363.

Agamemnon tells the whole story of his birth in Book 19, as an example of ἄτη, the blind folly that deceived even Zeus (19.95–133; see [[ate]]). On the day Alcmene was to bear Heracles in Thebes, Zeus announced to the gods that a man of his blood would be born that day who would rule all his neighbors. Hera made him swear to it, went down to Achaean Argos, brought the wife of Sthenelus to birth in her seventh month, and held back Alcmene's labor. Then she told Zeus that a noble man was born to rule the Argives, Eurystheus. Zeus seized Ate by the hair and flung her out of heaven, but his oath stood, and he groaned whenever he saw his son doing shameful labor "under the labors of Eurystheus" (ὑπʼ Εὐρυσθῆος ἀέθλων, 19.133, the words of 8.363). Zeus' announcement opens with the address of his ban in Book 8 (19.101–102 ≈ 8.5–6).

Eurystheus did not deal with Heracles in person. His herald Copreus "used to go with his messages to mighty Heracles", and Copreus' son Periphetes of Mycenae, a far better man than his father, is killed by Hector (15.638–641). In the Odyssey the ghost of Heracles tells Odysseus, without naming him, that he was subject to "a far worse man", who laid hard labors on him and once sent him down to bring back the hound (*Odyssey* 11.620–626).

## Outside Homer

In the Hesiodic *Shield*, Heracles' brother Iphicles leaves his home and parents "to honor sinful Eurystheus" (89–92), and the *Hymn to Heracles* has the hero wandering over land and sea "under the commands of lord Eurystheus" (15.4–5). In Euripides' *Heracles* the hero's last labor, at Eurystheus' command, is to bring up the three-headed dog of Hades (22–25, 1276–1278). [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] tells the twelve labors in order. After the Nemean lion Eurystheus, terrified, had a bronze jar sunk in the earth to hide in, and sent his orders through the herald Copreus (*Library* 2.5.1).

After Heracles' death Eurystheus pursued his children. In Euripides' *Children of Heracles* he sends his herald from Mycenae to demand them (136), makes war on Athens for sheltering them, is captured by Iolaus at the Scironian rocks, and is brought to Alcmene, who has him killed (859–862, 928–929, 1045–1052). Before he dies he reveals an old oracle of Apollo: buried in Attica, he will protect Athens against the descendants of the children when they invade (1026–1036). Apollodorus has Hyllus kill him in flight and Alcmene gouge out the eyes of his severed head (*Library* 2.8.1).
