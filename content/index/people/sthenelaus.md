**Stone for stone.** Hector has just killed the Myrmidon Epeigeus with a stone as he took hold of Sarpedon's body (16.577–580; see [[epeigeus]] and [[hector]]). “And grief came upon Patroclus for his fallen companion” (16.581), and he makes straight through the champions “like a hawk, / a swift one, which puts jackdaws and starlings to flight” (16.582–583; see [[like-a-hawk]]). The narrator turns to him: “so straight at the Lycians, Patroclus, urger of horses, / you rushed, and at the Trojans, and your heart was angered for your companion” (16.584–585; see [[apostrophe]] and [[patroclus]]). He answers Hector's stone with a stone: “And he struck Sthenelaus, the dear son of Ithaemenes, / on the neck with a stone, and broke the tendons in it” (16.586–587; see [[stone-throwing]] and [[ithaemenes]]). The champions and Hector give ground as far as a javelin carries (16.588–592).

**The name.** Σθενέλαος joins σθένος, “strength”, and λαός, “people”. Walter Leaf reports Fick's remark that the first syllable of Ἰθαιμένεος cannot be short, and concludes that the verse should read Σθένελον, “Sthenelus”, for Σθενέλαον (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.586). Homer's Sthenelus is Diomedes' companion, the son of Capaneus (see [[sthenelus]]).
