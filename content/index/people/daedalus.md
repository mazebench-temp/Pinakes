## Etymology

Δαίδαλος is a name made from the words of craft. A δαίδαλον is a piece of intricate work, like the jewelry that Hephaestus forged for Thetis and Eurynome and the designs he puts on the shield, δαίδαλα πολλά (18.400, 18.482); δαιδάλεος is “intricately worked” (18.390, 18.612), δαιδάλλω “work intricately” (πάντοσε δαιδάλλων, 18.479), and πολυδαίδαλος “richly wrought” (see [[intricately-worked]]). The Greeks heard the craft in the name. Plato's Socrates says that the sculptors of his day held that Daedalus, if he were born now and made such works as those from which he got his name, would be laughed at (*Hippias Major* 282a; see [[plato]]).

## In Homer

{{quote:f4defbd0-1497-598b-b7e0-228f39bb423e}}

This is Daedalus' only appearance in Homer. He works at Cnossus, the city of Minos, for Minos' daughter (see [[cnossus]], [[ariadne]] and [[minos]]), and his work is the measure of the god's: Hephaestus inlays on the shield a dancing floor like the one Daedalus fashioned (see [[the-dancing-floor-of-ariadne]] and [[the-shield-of-achilles]]). The verb, ἤσκησεν, “fashioned”, is used of other skilled makers: the craftsman who worked in horn and fitted Pandarus' bow (4.110), Athena, who smoothed and worked Hera's robe and set many δαίδαλα in it (14.178–179), and the Sidonians, πολυδαίδαλοι, who made the silver mixing bowl that Achilles sets as a prize (23.741–743).

## Outside Homer

**Family.** Apollodorus makes him an Athenian, son of Eupalamus, the son of Metion, and of Alcippe (*Library* 3.15.8; see [[apollodorus]]), and in Plato's *Ion* Socrates names “Daedalus son of Metion” first among the sculptors (533a). In the version that Plutarch reports from Cleidemus he was a kinsman of Theseus, through his mother Merope, daughter of Erechtheus (*Theseus* 19; see [[plutarch]] and [[theseus]]). Plato's Socrates calls Daedalus “our ancestor” (*Euthyphro* 11b), and traces his own line back to Daedalus, and Daedalus' to Hephaestus son of Zeus (*Alcibiades I* 121a; see [[hephaestus]]).

**Athens and Crete.** Apollodorus calls him the best of architects and the first inventor of statues. He fled Athens after he had thrown his sister's son Talos, his pupil, from the Acropolis, for fear that the boy's talent would surpass his own; condemned by the Areopagus, he went to Minos. In Crete he made a wooden cow for Pasiphae, who had fallen in love with the bull of Poseidon, and built the labyrinth in which the Minotaur was kept, and to which the Athenians sent seven young men and seven girls every year (*Library* 3.15.8; see [[minos]]). When Theseus came, Ariadne asked Daedalus for the way out of the labyrinth, and at his suggestion she gave Theseus a thread (*Epitome* 1.8–9; see [[ariadne]]).

**The flight.** When Theseus escaped, Minos shut Daedalus in the labyrinth with his son Icarus. Daedalus made wings for them both, and told Icarus to fly neither high, lest the sun melt the glue, nor near the sea, lest the damp loosen the feathers; Icarus flew higher and higher, fell into the sea that is named after him, and was drowned, and Daedalus came safe to Camicus in Sicily (*Epitome* 1.12–13). Strabo tells the same of the island Icaria, from which the Icarian sea takes its name (*Geography* 14.1.19; see [[icarian-sea]] and [[strabo]]). Minos pursued him with a spiral shell, promising a reward to anyone who could pass a thread through it. At Camicus king Cocalus took the shell and gave it to Daedalus, who tied the thread to an ant and let it through; Minos knew then that he had found him and demanded him, and was killed after his bath by the daughters of Cocalus (*Epitome* 1.14–15; see [[sicily]]). Herodotus already knew that Minos, coming to Sicily in search of Daedalus, died a violent death there (Herodotus 7.169.2–170.1; see [[herodotus]]).

**The works.** His statues were said to move. Socrates tells Meno that the statues of Daedalus run away unless they are tied down (*Meno* 97d–e), and jokes with Euthyphro that their arguments, like the works of his ancestor Daedalus, will not stay where they are put (*Euthyphro* 11b–e, 15b). Aristotle sets the works of Daedalus beside the tripods of Hephaestus, which go of themselves to the assembly of the gods (18.376), as tools that would do their own work (*Politics* 1.4, 1253b33–1254a1; see [[aristotle-the-tripods-of-hephaestus]]). Pausanias names the wooden images of Daedalus that survived in his day: a Heracles at Thebes and the Trophonius at Lebadea in Boeotia, a Britomartis at Olus and an Athena at Cnossus, where he also places the dance of Ariadne carved on white stone, and a small Aphrodite at Delos, which he believed Ariadne had from Daedalus and took with her when she followed Theseus (Pausanias 9.40.3–4; see [[the-dancing-floor-of-ariadne]] and [[pausanias-the-dance-of-ariadne]]).
