## Etymology

Ἰφιδάμας joins ἶφι, “by might”, to the stem of δάμνημι, “subdue”, as Polydamas and Chersidamas do in the same book (see [[polydamas]] and [[chersidamas]]).

## Family

{{quote:c2bdca6e-e58a-5357-baae-ff452b66f0ba}}

He is Ἀντηνορίδης, “son of Antenor” (see [[son-of-antenor]]), and his mother is Theano: Cisseus is his μητροπάτωρ, “his mother's father, who fathered fair-cheeked Theano” (11.224; see [[theano]] and [[cisseus]]). μητροπάτωρ occurs only here. Cisseus brought him up in Thrace, and when he came of age “kept him there, and gave him his daughter” (11.226; see [[thrace]]): his bride was his mother's sister. The verse is the one used of the Lycian king who kept Bellerophon and gave him his daughter (6.192 = 11.226; see [[bellerophon]]), and the poem does not remark on the marriage (see [[marriage]]). Coon is his elder brother, “his brother by the same father” (11.257; see [[coon]]).

## In Homer

**The first to face Agamemnon.** The poet stops to ask the Muses “who was the first to come against Agamemnon, / whether of the Trojans themselves or of their famous allies” (11.219–220; see [[invocation-of-the-muses]]). The answer is both: a son of a Trojan elder, who came to the war from Thrace as the allies came (see [[trojan-allies]]). ἠΰς τε μέγας τε, “tall and brave” (11.221), is the praise Priam gives Agamemnon and Ajax on the wall (3.167, 3.226).

**The bridegroom.** “But newly married he came from the bridal chamber at the report of the Achaeans, / with twelve curved ships that followed him” (11.227–228). He left the ships at Percote, on the Hellespont, and came on foot to Ilios (11.229–230; see [[percote]]). The report, κλέος, is the news of the expedition that reached Cinyras in Cyprus (11.21), and Othryoneus came to Troy at the report of war as he did (13.364).

**The fight.** Agamemnon misses. Iphidamas stabs him at the waist below the corselet and leans his weight on the spear, but the point meets the silver of the belt and bends “like lead” (11.233–237; see [[belt]], [[corselet]] and [[like-lead]]). Agamemnon pulls the spear out of his hand, “eager as a lion”, and strikes him on the neck with his sword (11.238–240; see [[agamemnon]]).

{{quote:4ca3fb72-7bc6-5f3d-a3f6-8cd6c393667b}}

χάλκεον ὕπνον, “the sleep of bronze”, occurs only here (see [[hypnos]] and [[death-in-battle]]). The narrator counts his death from the bride's side: he had given a hundred cattle and promised a thousand goats and sheep for a wife from whom “he had seen no joy” (11.243–245; see [[wedded]] and [[cattle]]). The Achaean Protesilaus, the first man killed at Troy, also left a new wife, “her cheeks torn in mourning”, and a house half-finished (2.700–701; see [[protesilaus]]). Agamemnon strips him and carries his armor into the throng of the Achaeans (11.246–247; see [[stripping-the-dead]]).

**His brother.** Coon, seeing him fall, wounds Agamemnon through the forearm and is beheaded as he drags the body away by the foot, “over Iphidamas” (11.248–261; see [[coon]] and [[fighting-over-the-body]]). “There the sons of Antenor, at the hands of the king, the son of Atreus, / fulfilled their fate and went down into the house of Hades” (11.262–263). His few lines of life belong with the Iliad's short obituaries, like that of Simoeisius, who did not live to repay his parents for his rearing (4.473–479; see [[simoeisius]]). The poem names him nowhere else.
