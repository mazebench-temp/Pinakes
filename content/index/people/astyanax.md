## His names

{{quote:915a8bbc-80e3-5a16-9ff0-d4082a5abbf6}}

Σκαμάνδριος is “of the Scamander”, the river of Troy (see [[scamander]]), as Simoeisius was named for the Simoeis, on whose banks he was born (4.474–477; see [[simoeisius]]). A Trojan hunter killed in Book 5 has the same name (see [[scamandrius]]). Ἀστυάναξ = ἄστυ (“town”) + ἄναξ (“lord”): “lord of the city”. The name is his father's glory, not his own, and the poet explains it: “for Hector alone was guarding Ilios” (6.403). When Hector is dead Andromache gives the same reason: the Trojans call him Astyanax, “for you alone guarded their gates and their long walls” (22.506–507). Ἑκτορίδης, “son of Hector”, is a patronymic found only here (6.401).

## Descriptions

He is “an infant of tender mind, quite small” (ἀταλάφρονα νήπιον αὔτως, 6.400). ἀταλάφρων = ἀταλός (“tender, young”) + φρήν (“mind”), found only here (see [[of-tender-mind]]); νήπιος αὔτως returns of him in both of his mother's laments (22.484, 24.726; see [[nepios]]). He is his father's “beloved” son (ἀγαπητός; see [[agapetos]]), “like a lovely star” (6.401; see [[like-a-star]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Andromache's lament in Book 22 imagines his life as an orphan. Other men will take his lands; he will go among his father's companions, tugging at a cloak or a tunic, until one of them holds a cup to his lips for a moment; and a boy whose parents are living will drive him from the feast: “Get out! Your father does not dine with us” (22.487–499). Before, on his father's knees, he ate only marrow and the rich fat of sheep, and slept in his nurse's arms (22.500–504; see [[handmaids]]).

In Book 24 she fears that he will not live to be a man. He will follow her into slavery, or some Achaean will take him by the arm and hurl him from the tower to a grim death, in anger for a brother or a father or a son whom Hector killed (24.726–739; see [[the-tower-of-ilios]]).

## Outside Homer

In the *Little Iliad* Neoptolemus took him from his nurse's bosom and threw him from the tower; Pausanias notes that the poet made it Neoptolemus' own act, not a decision of the Greeks (10.25.9). In the *Iliupersis*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, Odysseus killed him (see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). In Euripides' *Trojan Women* the Greeks, persuaded by Odysseus, will not let the son of so great a father grow up and throw him from the walls, and Hecuba buries him in Hector's shield (see [[euripides-astyanax]] and [[hecuba]]). In Seneca's *Troades* Andromache hides him in Hector's tomb, and he leaps from the tower of his own will.

Plato's Socrates asks which of his two names Homer thought the more correct, and argues for Astyanax: the Trojan men gave it, and men name more wisely than women; and the son of the city's defender is rightly called its lord (*Cratylus* 392b–393b; see [[plato-astyanax]]). Virgil's Andromache sees him in young Ascanius (*Aeneid* 3.489; see [[virgils-andromache]]). Later poets let him live: Racine's Pyrrhus holds him hostage for his mother's hand (see [[racine-andromaque]]), and in Ronsard's *Franciade* (1572) he escapes the sack and, as Francus, becomes the ancestor of the kings of France.
