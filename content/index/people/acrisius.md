## In Homer

Ἀκρισιώνη, “daughter of Acrisius”, is all that Homer says of him: Zeus names “Danae of the lovely ankles, daughter of Acrisius” among his loves, and her son Perseus (14.319–320; see [[danae]], [[perseus]] and [[the-loves-of-zeus]]). The woman's patronymic is formed like Εὐηνίνη, “daughter of Evenus”, and Ἀδρηστίνη, “daughter of Adrastus” (9.557, 5.412; Leaf on 14.319). The Hesiodic *Shield* calls Alcmene in the same way, and with an epithet of the ankles, ἐυσφύρου Ἠλεκτρυώνης, “the fair-ankled daughter of Electryon” (*Shield* 16; see [[alcmene]]).

## Outside Homer

**Argos and Tiryns.** [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] makes Acrisius and Proetus twin sons of Abas, son of Lynceus, who quarreled already in the womb and, when grown, fought for the kingdom, inventing shields in the war. Acrisius drove Proetus out, Proetus came back with a Lycian army and took Tiryns, and the brothers divided the Argolid between them, Acrisius ruling at Argos (*Library* 2.2.1; see [[proetus]] and [[argos]]). His wife was Eurydice, daughter of Lacedaemon, and Danae was their daughter (*Library* 2.2.2).

**The oracle.** When Acrisius asked how he might have male children, the god answered that his daughter would bear a son who would kill him. He built a bronze chamber under the ground and kept Danae in it, but she bore Perseus, to Zeus, who came to her as gold through the roof, or, as some said, to Proetus. Acrisius would not believe that the child was Zeus', and he set mother and son adrift in a chest (*Library* 2.4.1).

**The discus.** When Perseus came to Argos with Danae and Andromeda, Acrisius, still afraid of the oracle, withdrew to the Pelasgian land. At Larissa, at the funeral games that King Teutamides held for his father, Perseus competed in the pentathlon, and his discus struck Acrisius on the foot and killed him at once. Ashamed to claim the inheritance of the man he had killed, Perseus exchanged Argos for Tiryns with Proetus' son Megapenthes (*Library* 2.4.4; see [[discus]]). In Pausanias' version Acrisius had gone to Larisa on the Peneus, and Perseus came there wanting to greet his mother's father with fair words and deeds; proud of the discus, which he had invented, he was showing his skill when Acrisius stepped unseen into its path (Pausanias 2.16.2–3).
