**The eldest son.** Bucolion was Laomedon's firstborn, πρεσβύτατος γενεῇ, “the eldest by birth”, but σκότιον δέ ἑ γείνατο μήτηρ, “his mother bore him in secret” (6.24). σκότιος, from σκότος (“darkness”), occurs only here in Homer: he was born out of wedlock, and his mother is not named (see [[nothos]]). He does not appear in the line of Troy that Aeneas recites in Book 20, where Laomedon's sons are Tithonus, Priam, Lampus, Clytius and Hicetaon (20.237–238). Though he was the eldest, it was Priam who became king.

**The herdsman.** His name is formed on βουκόλος (“cowherd”), and the poem finds him with sheep, ποιμαίνων ἐπʼ ὄεσσι, “tending his sheep” (6.25). Trojan princes herd on the slopes of Ida in the Iliad. Anchises was tending cattle when Aphrodite bore him Aeneas (5.313), and Achilles caught two sons of Priam on Ida, ποιμαίνοντʼ ἐπʼ ὄεσσι, in the same words (11.104–106). Naiads bear sons to mortal men in two later passages: Satnius, born to Enops as he tended cattle by the Satnioeis (14.443–445), and Iphition, born to Otrynteus, sacker of cities, under snowy Tmolus (20.383–385). See [[abarbarea]] and [[ida]].

**The twins.** The story of the parents is told between the verb that sends Euryalus after the sons (6.21) and the verse that kills them (6.27–28). They are διδυμάονε, “twins”, the word for the sons of Diocles whom Aeneas killed at the end of Book 5 (5.548; see [[crethon]] and [[orsilochus-son-of-diocles]]). Book 5 lost a pair of Achaean twins, and Book 6 opens with the loss of a pair of Trojan ones, the grandsons of a king.
