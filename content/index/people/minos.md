## Etymology

Μίνως is not a Greek word, and its origin is unknown. Arthur Evans named the Bronze Age civilization of Crete “Minoan” after him (see [[crete]] and [[cnossus]]).

## Family

{{quote:0867d904-6ed6-5d4f-8f46-6c7e228e7a77}}

Boasting over Alcathous, Idomeneus tells Deiphobus his line (see [[idomeneus]], [[deucalion]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]). Zeus names the mother among the women he has loved: “the daughter of far-famed Phoenix, who bore me Minos and godlike Rhadamanthys” (14.321–322); later writers call her Europa. In the Odyssey, Ariadne is “the daughter of baneful-minded Minos”, whom Theseus was taking from Crete to Athens when Artemis killed her on Dia (Od. 11.321–325; see [[theseus]]), and the disguised Odysseus makes himself Minos' grandson and a younger brother of Idomeneus (Od. 19.178–184).

## Descriptions

- Κρήτῃ ἐπίουρον, “a guardian over Crete” (13.450). ἐπίουρος is a warden or overseer; Eumaeus is ὑῶν ἐπίουρος, “overseer of the swine” (Od. 13.405, 15.39; see [[guardian-of-the-achaeans]]).
- Διὸς ἀγλαὸς υἱός, “the splendid son of Zeus” (Od. 11.568).
- Διὸς μεγάλου ὀαριστής, “the familiar of great Zeus” (Od. 19.179). ὀαριστής, one who shares intimate talk, occurs only here (see [[oaristys]]). In the same verse he ἐννέωρος βασίλευε at Cnossus. ἐννέωρος is elsewhere “nine years old” (18.351; Od. 10.19, 11.311), and its sense here is disputed: that he was king at nine, or that he reigned in spells of nine years, going up to Zeus every ninth year, as Plato and Strabo took it.
- ὀλοόφρων, “baneful-minded” (Od. 11.322), the word for Atlas and Aeetes (Od. 1.52, 10.137) and for a lion and a boar (15.630, 17.21).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Among the dead Odysseus saw Minos “holding a golden scepter, giving judgments to the dead, seated, while they asked the lord for justice around him” (Od. 11.568–571). The stranger in Eumaeus' hut claims to be a guest-friend of Odysseus' father, living “in Crete, where the race of Minos is” (Od. 17.522–523).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod makes Ariadne, “daughter of Minos”, the wife of Dionysus (*Theogony* 947–949). The Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* told how Zeus carried Europa, daughter of Phoenix, to Crete, where she bore Minos, Sarpedon and Rhadamanthys (fr. 19 Evelyn-White; see [[sarpedon]]).

For Herodotus, Minos was the first master of the sea: the Carians, who then held the islands, were his subjects and manned his ships (1.171.2), and Polycrates was the first Greek to plan an empire of the sea, “apart from Minos of Cnossus” (Herodotus 3.122.2). He drove his brother Sarpedon out of Crete (1.173.2) and died a violent death in Sicily, where he had gone in search of Daedalus; the Trojan war came “in the third generation after Minos died” (Herodotus 7.170.1, 7.171.1). Aristotle repeats that he held the sea and died near Camicus, and says that the Cretans still used his laws (*Politics* 2.10, 1271b30–40).

In Plato's *Laws* the Athenian asks whether Minos, as Homer says, went every ninth year to consort with his father and made laws by his sayings, and the Cretan agrees (624a–b). The *Minos*, transmitted with Plato's dialogues, takes ὀαριστής as Zeus' pupil in conversation, and sets Homer's Minos against the cruel king of Athenian tragedy (318d–321b). In the *Gorgias* he judges the dead with Rhadamanthys and Aeacus (523e–524a, 526c–d; see [[aeacus]]). Strabo reports from Ephorus the visits to the cave of Zeus every nine years, and also a story of a violent king who took tribute (10.4.8); of the two views, that Minos was a stranger in Crete or a native, he thinks Idomeneus' verse favors the second (10.4.9). His son Glaucus, drowned in a jar of honey, was brought back to life by the seer Polyidus (Apollodorus 3.3.1–2; see [[polyidus-the-seer]]).
