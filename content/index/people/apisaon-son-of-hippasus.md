**Revenge for a companion.** Aeneas has just killed Leiocritus, son of Arisbas, the companion of Lycomedes (17.344–345; see [[leiocritus]] and [[aeneas]]). “And Lycomedes, dear to Ares, pitied him as he fell, / and came up close and stood and threw his shining spear” (17.346–347; see [[lycomedes]], [[dear-to-ares]] and [[pity]]): the same two verses, with one fallen man for two and another avenger, tell how great Telamonian Ajax avenged Menesthes and Anchialus on Amphius in Book 5 (5.610–611; see [[amphius-son-of-selagus]]). The spear strikes Apisaon “in the liver beneath the midriff, and at once loosened his knees” (17.349), the verse of the deaths of Apisaon, son of Phausius, and of Hypsenor, son of Hippasus (11.579, 13.412; see [[apisaon]], [[hypsenor-son-of-hippasus]] and [[loosened-his-limbs]]). Only the patronymic in the verse before tells the two Apisaons apart (see [[hippasus-the-paeonian]]).

**From Paeonia.** His obituary is two verses: he “had come from deep-soiled Paeonia, / and after Asteropaeus he was the best in the fighting” (17.350–351; see [[paeonia]], [[paeonians]] and [[deep-soiled]]). ποιμὴν λαῶν, “shepherd of the people”, is the title of kings and leaders (17.348; see [[shepherd-of-the-people]]). The pity passes to the other side in the same words: “And warlike Asteropaeus pitied him as he fell” (17.352; see [[asteropaeus]]). Asteropaeus makes straight for the Danaans, but they stand fenced with their shields around Patroclus, as Ajax has ordered, and he can do nothing (17.353–359; see [[ajax]]).
