{{quote:86d72ed2-83d4-56a8-876f-238ae3b36382}}

Homer names Aethra here and nowhere else. She is one of two ἀμφίπολοι (“handmaids”) who walk with their mistress to the Scaean gates; when Helen comes home from the wall, the handmaids go “quickly to their tasks” (3.422). The poem gives her a father, [[Pittheus]], and nothing more.

## Etymology

αἴθρη is a Greek word for the clear, bright sky, from αἴθω (“burn, shine”), like αἰθήρ (“the bright upper air”). Homer also uses it as a common noun: Ajax begs Zeus to “make clear sky” (ποίησον δʼ αἴθρην, 17.646).

## Outside Homer

In later myth Aethra, daughter of Pittheus of [[Troezen]], is the mother of [[Theseus]]. Pittheus brought it about that the childless Aegeus of Athens lay with her (Plutarch, *Theseus* 3; Apollodorus 3.15.6). When Theseus carried off the young Helen to Aphidnae, Castor and Polydeuces took the town, brought their sister home, and led away Theseus' mother as a captive (Apollodorus 3.10.7). She went with Helen to Troy, and at the sack her grandsons Demophon and Acamas found her and took her with them (the *Iliupersis*, in [[proclus|Proclus]]' summary). See [[helen-and-theseus]].

If the Iliad's Aethra is Theseus' mother, the line alludes to a story the poem never tells, and the hero of Athens is present in the Iliad only through his mother's captivity. Ancient readers disagreed. Plutarch cites the line as Homer's evidence that Aethra went to Troy with Helen, and reports that others rejected it (*Theseus* 34; see [[aethra-line]]). Modern readers still disagree whether the line alludes to the Theseus story, and if it does, whether it belongs to the original poem.
