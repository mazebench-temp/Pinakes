Σκῦρον ἑλὼν αἰπεῖαν Ἐνυῆος πτολίεθρον, “when he took steep Scyros, the citadel of Enyeus” (9.668). Achilles gave Patroclus the captive Iphis from its spoil (9.666–667; see [[iphis]], [[scyros]] and [[achilles]]). Homer says nothing more of Enyeus: not his family, and not whether he died when his city fell. The genitive of possession, “the citadel of Enyeus”, is the way the poem names other towns by their lords, as Thebe is “the city of Eetion” (9.188) and Lyrnessus “the city of divine Mynes” (19.296; see [[eetion]] and [[mynes]]).

The name resembles Ἐνυώ, Enyo, the goddess of battle (5.333, 5.592), and Ἐνυάλιος, Enyalius, a name of the war god (2.651; see [[enyo]] and [[enyalius]]), but a connection is not certain. In later tradition the king of Scyros is Lycomedes, at whose court Achilles was hidden among the king's daughters (Apollodorus, *Library* 3.13.8; see [[scyros]]). Homer does not name him, and he is not [[lycomedes|the Achaean Lycomedes]], son of Creon, a captain of the sentries in this book (9.84).
