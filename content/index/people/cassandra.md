## The name

Κασσάνδρη (Kassandrē) is the epic form; the Attic poets write Κασάνδρα, with one sigma, and Strabo, quoting Homer, writes Κασσάνδρην in the verse and Κασάνδρας in his own prose (*Geography* 13.1.40). The origin of the name is unknown.

## Family

She is a daughter of Priam: “the most beautiful in form of the daughters of Priam” (13.365; see [[priam]]), and in the Odyssey Agamemnon calls her Πριάμοιο θυγατρός, “the daughter of Priam” (Od. 11.421). Homer does not name her mother. The formula of her beauty is given also to her sister Laodice (3.124, 6.252; see [[laodice]] and [[best-in-looks]]), and Idomeneus turns it on the daughters of Agamemnon (13.378; see [[laodice-daughter-of-agamemnon]]).

## In Homer

**The suitor.** Othryoneus of Cabesus came to Troy at the report of the war and asked Priam for her ἀνάεδνον, “without bride-price” (13.366), promising instead to drive the Achaeans from Troy; Priam promised and nodded his assent, and Idomeneus kills Othryoneus at the ships and mocks the bargain (13.363–384; see [[othryoneus]] and [[bride-price]]).

**Hector's return.** In the last book she is the first of all the Trojans, men and women, to see Priam coming home with Hector's body. Like golden Aphrodite, she has climbed Pergamus; she sees her father standing in the chariot with the herald, and Hector lying on the bier in the mule cart, and she cries out through the city for the Trojans and the Trojan women to come and look on Hector, if ever they rejoiced when he came back alive from battle (24.697–706; see [[pergamus]] and [[golden]]).

**Her death.** In the underworld Agamemnon tells Odysseus that the most pitiful voice he heard as he died was that of Cassandra, daughter of Priam, whom Clytemnestra killed beside him (Od. 11.421–423; see [[clytemnestra]] and [[clytemnestra-and-the-return]]). The Odyssey does not say how she came to Mycenae. Homer never makes her a seer, and he says nothing of Apollo's love or of Ajax.

## Outside Homer

[[Strabo]] quotes the verses on Othryoneus (13.363–366) to show that Homer knew Cassandra as a maiden at this point in the war and knew nothing of her violation (φθορά) at the sack, nor of any violence; the yearly sending of the Locrian maidens to Ilium, he adds, is not Homeric either (*Geography* 13.1.40; see [[strabo-on-cassandra]]).

In [[aeschylus|Aeschylus]]' *Agamemnon* she comes home with the king, “the chosen flower of many possessions, the army's gift” (954–955), and he asks his wife to receive the stranger kindly (950–951). She tells the chorus that Apollo made her a seer and wooed her; she consented and then broke her word to him, and from then on she persuaded no one of anything she foretold (1202–1212). Clytemnestra kills her with Agamemnon and boasts over the bodies of “this captive, this reader of portents”, who sang her last lament “like a swan” (1440–1446).

In Euripides' *Trojan Women* Poseidon says that Agamemnon, heedless of the god's claim and of piety, takes the maiden Cassandra to his bed by force (41–44), and he recalls the outrage that turned Athena against the Greeks, “when Ajax dragged Cassandra by force” (69–70). Hecuba calls her “the maiden of Phoebus”, to whom the god gave an unwedded life, and the herald Talthybius answers that Agamemnon has taken her as his chosen prize, for love of the inspired girl (249–255; see [[euripides-trojan-women]]). The Suda quotes Aelian for the oracle of Apollo that the Locrians' troubles would not end until they sent two maidens every year to Athena at Ilium, “the penalty for Cassandra” (π 3092; see [[locrians]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).
