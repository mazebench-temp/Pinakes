Phoenix names her in passing, in the family of Meleager's wife: Cleopatra was the “daughter of Marpessa of the lovely ankles, daughter of Evenus” (9.557). The verse is built like Δανάης καλλισφύρου Ἀκρισιώνης, “Danae of the lovely ankles, daughter of Acrisius” (14.319): a name, the epithet καλλίσφυρος (see [[of-the-lovely-ankles]]), and a woman's patronymic (see [[evenus-father-of-marpessa]]). Her name has been connected with μάρπτω, “seize”, which would suit a woman carried off by a god; the derivation is uncertain.

She was Idas' bride (νύμφη) when Apollo “snatched her away” and Idas took up his bow against the god “for the sake of the bride of the lovely ankles” (9.559–564; see [[idas]] and [[idas-and-apollo]]). Her grief is told through a bird, the halcyon, whose “lot” she had (9.563; see [[halcyon]]). Homer does not say how she came back to Idas, but she did, since Cleopatra is the child of both, and her parents named the girl Alcyone after her mother's lament (see [[cleopatra]]).

## Outside Homer

In [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] Zeus parts Idas and Apollo and lets her choose between them, and she chooses Idas, fearing that the god would leave her when she grew old (*Library* 1.7.8–9). On the chest of Cypselus at Olympia a verse named her with Homer's epithet and Homer's verb: Ἴδας Μάρπησσαν καλλίσφυρον, ἃν οἱ Ἀπόλλων / ἅρπασε, τὰν Εὐανοῦ ἄγει πάλιν οὐκ ἀέκουσαν, “Idas leads back Marpessa of the lovely ankles, the daughter of Evenus, whom Apollo snatched from him; she comes not unwilling” (Pausanias 5.18.2). Pausanias counts her the first of three women in a line, with her daughter Cleopatra and her granddaughter Polydora, who killed themselves when their husbands died (Pausanias 4.2.7).
