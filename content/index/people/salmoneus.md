## Family

Homer knows him only as Tyro's father: she “said that she was the offspring of noble Salmoneus” (*Odyssey* 11.236; see [[tyro]]). The poem names no wife and no other child. W. W. Merry makes him the brother of Cretheus, Tyro's husband, both of them sons of Aeolus by Laodice, so that Tyro married her uncle, as Arete married hers (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.235; see [[cretheus]], [[aeolus-father-of-sisyphus]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]).

## Descriptions

ἀμύμων, “noble” (*Odyssey* 11.236; see [[blameless]]). Some ancient copies read ἀτασθάλου, “reckless”, instead, as a scholion reports. Merry refers to his note on Aegisthus, whom the Odyssey also calls ἀμύμων: the word marks some excellence of birth or looks, almost a title, and need carry no moral sense (Merry's critical note on *Odyssey* 11.236; Merry on *Odyssey* 1.29).

## Outside Homer

In the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* the sons of Aeolus are “kings who dealt out judgments, / Cretheus and Athamas and Sisyphus of shifting counsel, / and unjust Salmoneus and high-hearted Perieres” (fr. 4 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]): ἄδικος, “unjust”, where the Odyssey has ἀμύμων. Strabo puts him in Elis: Salmoneus is said to have been king in the Pisatis, and Salmone, one of its eight cities, lies near the spring of the same name from which the Enipeus flows, the river that Tyro loved; her father was king there, he adds, as Euripides says in the *Aeolus* (*Geography* 8.3.31–32; see [[strabo]] and [[euripides]]). Merry gives the same account: Salmoneus lived first in Thessaly and later moved to Elis, where he built Salmone, and the river on which it stood was also called Enipeus (on *Odyssey* 11.235).

Virgil's Sibyl saw him in Tartarus paying cruel penalties “while he imitates the fires of Jupiter and the sounds of Olympus”: he had driven through the peoples of the Greeks and the city in the middle of Elis with a four-horse team, brandishing a torch and claiming the honor of the gods, a madman who would counterfeit the storm clouds and the thunderbolt that cannot be imitated with bronze and the beat of horses' hooves, until the almighty father hurled a real bolt and drove him headlong (*Aeneid* 6.585–594; see [[virgil]]).
