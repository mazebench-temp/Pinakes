Κλεοπάτρη means “glory of the father”, from κλέος, “fame”, and πατήρ, “father”. Phoenix names her once, as Meleager's “wedded wife, the beautiful Cleopatra” (9.556), and turns aside to her family: she was the daughter of Marpessa and of Idas, who drew his bow against Apollo for his bride, and her parents called her Alcyone because her mother wept like the halcyon when Apollo carried her off (9.557–564; see [[idas-and-apollo]] and [[halcyon]]). The byname comes from her mother's grief, not her own. μνηστὴ ἄλοχος, “wedded wife”, is a wife won by courtship (μνάομαι, “woo”), not a captive (see [[marriage]]). Meleager lies beside her in his anger (παρκατέλεκτο, 9.565); the verb returns once in Homer, at the end of the book, when Achilles lies down with Diomede, a woman he had brought from Lesbos (9.664; see [[diomede]]).

She comes last, when elders and priests, father, sisters, mother and companions have failed and the Curetes are on the towers. Her plea is reported, not spoken: the men killed, the city burned, the children and the women led away (9.591–594), the fate Hector foresees for Andromache (6.454–465; see [[women-and-war]] and [[captive-women]]). Her name has the two parts of Patroclus' name in reverse order, and as she moves Meleager, Patroclus will move Achilles; the likeness is often noted, though Homer does not point to it (see [[patroclus]] and [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]).

## Outside Homer

[[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] names Meleager's wife “Cleopatra, daughter of Idas and Marpessa”, and says that after Meleager's death she and Althaea hanged themselves (*Library* 1.8.2–3). Pausanias adds that the *Cypria* made Polydora, the wife of Protesilaus, a daughter of Meleager (Pausanias 4.2.7; see [[protesilaus]] and [[marpessa]]).
