## The name

Two men in Homer are called Laerces: the goldsmith of Pylos, named once (*Odyssey* 3.425), and the father of the Myrmidon Alcimedon, who is named only in his son's title, “son of Laerces” (*Iliad* 16.197, 17.467; see [[laerces]] and [[alcimedon]]). Nothing joins the two.

## Descriptions

- χρυσοχόος, “goldsmith” (*Odyssey* 3.425), the only use of the word in Homer: χρυσός, “gold”, and χέω, “pour”, the verb of the gilding, χρυσὸν κέρασιν περιχεύῃ, “that he may pour gold around the heifer's horns” (*Odyssey* 3.426).
- χαλκεύς, “the smith” (*Odyssey* 3.432), with his χαλκήια, “smith's tools” (*Odyssey* 3.433). The word for a worker in bronze is the word for any smith: W. W. Merry explains that the goldsmith is called χαλκεύς from the metal most generally worked (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.425; see [[forge]]).

## A craftsman called from outside

Laerces does not belong to Nestor's household. He is sent for: one of Nestor's sons is to “call” him to come (κελέσθω, *Odyssey* 3.425). Merry quotes Bothe: there were already in that age craftsmen (δημιουργοί), free men who practiced a craft, among them carpenters and smiths, and this goldsmith does not live in Nestor's house, though kings' houses had metal in plenty and Odysseus' house is said to have had a smithy for everyday use (*Odyssey* 18.328; Merry on 3.425). Such men are the ones a house calls in from outside. Eumaeus asks who would go and call a stranger from elsewhere unless he were one of the δημιοεργοί, “workers for the people”, a seer, a healer of ills, a builder in wood or an inspired singer, “for these are called among mortals over the boundless earth” (*Odyssey* 17.382–386, a plain rendering; see [[heralds]] for the heralds, whom the poem also calls δημιοεργοί, *Odyssey* 19.135).
