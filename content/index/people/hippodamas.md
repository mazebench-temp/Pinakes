**In flight.** Hippodamas is the third man Achilles kills when he returns to the battle, after Iphition and Demoleon (20.381–400; see [[iphition]], [[demoleon]] and [[achilles]]). He has leapt down from his chariot and is running when the spear takes him “in the back as he fled before him” (20.402). Both verses of his death come from earlier killings: the first is that of Chersidamas, killed by Odysseus, with his name in Chersidamas' place (11.423), and the second that of Scamandrius, killed by Menelaus (5.56; see [[scamandrius]]). Walter Leaf, who notes the borrowing, would remove these verses with the Iphition episode before them (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.381 and 20.401; see the translation's note on 20.381–395).

**The bellow.** He alone of Achilles' victims in the book is given a simile. “He gasped out his life and bellowed” (θυμὸν ἄϊσθε καὶ ἤρυγεν, 20.403) like a bull that young men drag around the Heliconian lord, Poseidon, who is glad of them, “so as he bellowed, his proud spirit left his bones” (20.406): the death of a fighting man is told as a sacrifice (see [[the-bull-of-the-heliconian-lord]], [[heliconian-poseidon]] and [[sacrifice]]). θυμὸν ἀΐσθων, “gasping out its life”, is said elsewhere only of Achilles' trace horse Pedasus under Sarpedon's spear (16.468; see [[pedasus-the-horse]]).

**The name.** Ἱπποδάμας, from ἵππος, “horse”, and δαμάζω, “tame”, has the root of ἱππόδαμος, “tamer of horses”, the Trojans' epithet, and of the name of Hippodamus, whom Odysseus kills in *Iliad* 11 (11.335; see [[tamer-of-horses]] and [[hippodamus]]).

## Outside Homer

Whether the Heliconian lord of the simile is Poseidon of Helice or of the Ionians' Panionia was already disputed in antiquity (Strabo, *Geography* 8.7.2; see [[heliconian-poseidon]] and [[helice-or-the-panionia]]).
