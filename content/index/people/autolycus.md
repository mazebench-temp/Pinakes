## Etymology

Αὐτόλυκος (Autolykos) is αὐτός (“self, the very”) and λύκος (“wolf”): “the wolf himself”. Neither poem comments on the name, though the Odyssey has Autolycus explain the name he gives his grandson (*Odyssey* 19.406–409; see [[odysseus]]).

## Family

In the Odyssey he is the father of Anticleia, Odysseus' mother, “Anticleia, daughter of great-hearted Autolycus” (*Odyssey* 11.85), and he calls Laertes γαμβρὸς ἐμός, “my son-in-law” (*Odyssey* 19.406; see [[laertes]]). His wife is Amphithea, and he has sons, who are not named (19.413–418). His house is on Parnassus. In Homer Hermes is his patron, not his father; later writers make him Hermes' son and give him another grandson, Jason (see below).

## In Homer

**The Iliad.** The poem names him once, in the history of the helmet that Meriones gives Odysseus for the night raid:

{{quote:c1004c2c-a396-5bee-99a8-43069d2053b3}}

ἀντιτορήσας, “boring through, breaking into”, is the burglar's verb. In Homer it is used of a house only here; in the *Homeric Hymn to Hermes* the newborn god threatens to break into Apollo's great house at Pytho, and Apollo foresees that he will often break into men's houses by night (178, 282–285). The Iliad does not say why Autolycus gave the helmet away, or that he was Odysseus' grandfather (see [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]], [[amyntor]] and [[amphidamas]]).

**The Odyssey.** When Eurycleia knows Odysseus by his scar, the poem tells how he got it (*Odyssey* 19.392–466). He had gone to Parnassus to visit “Autolycus and his sons, / his mother's noble father, who surpassed all men / in thievery and the oath; the god himself had given it to him, / Hermes” (*Odyssey* 19.394–397), to whom Autolycus burned the thighs of lambs and kids. Hermes practices both himself in the *Hymn to Hermes*, where he steals Apollo's cattle and denies it with a great oath (274–277, 383–384). Autolycus had named the baby when he came to Ithaca for the birth: “since I have come here angered (ὀδυσσάμενος) with many … let his name be Odysseus” (*Odyssey* 19.399–409). When the boy came of age to claim the gifts he had been promised, he hunted on Parnassus with Autolycus' sons, and a boar gashed his leg above the knee before he killed it; the sons bound the wound and stopped the blood with a charm, and sent him home with gifts (19.410–466). The scar proves Odysseus to his herdsmen and to his father (21.217–221, 24.331–335).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates teases Polemarchus that by his definition the just man turns out to be a kind of thief: “you seem to have learned it from Homer, for he loves Autolycus, Odysseus' grandfather on his mother's side, and says that he surpassed all men in thievery and the oath” (*Republic* 1.334a–b; see [[plato]]).

[[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] counts Autolycus, son of Hermes, among the Argonauts, beside Laertes, and makes his daughter Polymede the mother of Jason (*Library* 1.9.16; see [[jason]]). He taught Heracles to wrestle (2.4.9), and when he stole cattle from Euboea, Eurytus blamed Heracles (2.6.2; see [[heracles]] and [[eurytus]]). In Ovid his mother is Chione, who bore him to Mercury: he was “skilled at every theft” and could make white things black and black things white (*Metamorphoses* 11.301–317; see [[ovid]]). Hyginus tells how Sisyphus, whose cattle he stole, found them out by marks on their hooves and lay with Anticleia in Autolycus' house before she married Laertes, so that some called Ulysses the son of Sisyphus (*Fabulae* 201; see [[sisyphus]] and [[laertes]]).
