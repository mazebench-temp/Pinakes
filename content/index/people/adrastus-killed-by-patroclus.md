**Nine names.** Patroclus goes after the Trojans and Lycians, “and was greatly blinded, / the fool”: had he kept Achilles' word, he would have escaped death (16.684–687; see [[patroclus]]). The narrator turns to him with a question, “Then whom first, whom last did you slay, / Patroclus, when the gods called you to your death?” (16.692–693; see [[whom-first-whom-last]] and [[apostrophe]]). The answer is nine names in three verses, and “the others each turned their minds to flight” (16.697). Adrastus is the first: Ἄδρηστον μὲν πρῶτα καὶ Αὐτόνοον καὶ Ἔχεκλον, “Adrastus first, and Autonous and Echeclus” (16.694; see [[autonous-killed-by-patroclus]] and [[echeclus]]). The verse is built like the first verse of Hector's nine victims in *Iliad* 11, Ἀσαῖον μὲν πρῶτα καὶ Αὐτόνοον καὶ Ὀπίτην, “Asaeus first, and Autonous and Opites” (11.301), and has the same second name. The others are Perimus, son of Megas, Epistor, Melanippus, Elasus, Mulius and Pylartes (16.695–696; see [[perimus]], [[epistor]], [[melanippus-killed-by-patroclus]], [[elasus]], [[mulius-killed-by-patroclus]] and [[pylartes-killed-by-patroclus]]). Then the Achaeans would have taken Troy at Patroclus' hands, had not Apollo stood on the tower (16.698–701; see [[apollo]]).

**Other men named Adrastus.** The Iliad has three more: Adrastus, king of Sicyon and later of Argos, the grandfather of Diomedes (2.572, 14.121; see [[adrastus]]); Adrestus, son of the seer Merops, a leader of the men of Adrasteia (2.828–834; see [[adrestus]]); and the Trojan whom Menelaus took alive and Agamemnon killed (6.37–65; see [[adrastus-the-trojan]]).
