Αἰγεΐδης: "son of Aegeus". ἐπιείκελος ἀθανάτοισιν: "like the immortals".

## The line

1.265 is missing from most of the medieval manuscripts, and it is identical to line 182 of the Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles*. Many editors think it was added to give Athens a hero in the Iliad; the Monro–Allen text prints it. See [[theseus-line]].

## In the Odyssey

Odysseus names him twice among the dead: as the man who was bringing Ariadne from Crete to Athens when Artemis killed her, and, with Peirithous, among the heroes of former days whom he hoped to see (*Odyssey* 11.321–325, 11.630–631; see [[ariadne]] and [[peirithous]]).

## Outside Homer

**Crete.** Plato's Phaedo explains why Socrates' death was put off: the ship that the Athenians send to Delos every year is the one “in which Theseus once went to Crete, taking the famous twice seven, and saved them and was saved himself”, and while it is away the city may put no one to death (*Phaedo* 58a–b, a plain rendering; see [[plato]]). Hesiod makes Ariadne, the daughter of Minos, the wife of Dionysus (*Theogony* 947–949), and W. W. Merry gives the common account, in which Theseus deserted her on Dia, that is Naxos, and Dionysus took her to wife (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.322; see [[minos]]).

**Peirithous and Hades.** In later tradition he was the friend of Peirithous and fought beside the Lapiths against the Centaurs, as the Iliad's line already has him do (see [[theseus-line]] and [[lapiths]]). The two went down to Hades together: Virgil's Charon says that they came to carry off the queen from the chamber of Dis, and in Virgil's Tartarus “unhappy Theseus sits, and will sit forever” (*Aeneid* 6.392–397, 6.617–618; see [[virgil-the-punished-in-tartarus]]). In Euripides' *Heracles* Heracles brings Theseus back from Hades (*Heracles* 619–621; see [[euripides]]). Aristotle cites his choice of Helen as proof of her worth (*Rhetoric* 1363a18, 1399a2; see [[helen-and-theseus]]).
