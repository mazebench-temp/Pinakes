**Lycaon's mother.** Laothoe is named twice in Homer, both times as the mother of two sons whom Achilles kills. Lycaon names her at the river, clasping Achilles' knees, as part of his plea for his life: he was born “to be short-lived”, μινυνθάδιον, the word Achilles used of himself to his own mother (21.84; 1.352; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]], [[short-lived]] and [[the-death-of-lycaon]]). Her other son is Polydorus, whom Achilles had already killed among the foremost fighters earlier that day (21.90–91; 20.407–418; see [[polydorus-son-of-priam]]). Lycaon's last argument turns on her too: “do not kill me, since I am not of the same womb as Hector” (21.95), Hecuba's son (see [[hecuba]]). Achilles throws Lycaon's body into the river and tells him that the fishes will lick his blood, and that his mother will not have his body to mourn: “nor will your mother / lay you on a bier and lament for you” (21.123–124; see [[lament]] and [[mothers-and-sons]]).

**A king's daughter.** Her father is Altes, “who rules over the war-loving Leleges, / holding steep Pedasus on the Satnioeis” (21.86–87; see [[altes]], [[leleges]] and [[pedasus-on-the-satnioeis]]). The verses that name her are built like those that name Andromache, another king's daughter of the southern Troad who married into the house of Priam: “Andromache, the daughter of great-hearted Eetion, / Eetion who lived beneath wooded Placus, / in Thebe under Placus, lord over the Cilician men; / it was his daughter who was the wife of Hector armed in bronze” (6.395–398; see [[andromache]] and [[eetion]]). Here: “bore me, Laothoe, daughter of the old man Altes, / Altes, who rules over the war-loving Leleges, / holding steep Pedasus on the Satnioeis. / His daughter Priam had for a wife, and many others too” (21.85–88).

**Priam's wives.** “and many others too” (21.88): Hecuba was not Priam's only wife. ἔχε, “had”, is imperfect, though Laothoe is still alive in the next book; Walter Leaf explains that it goes back to the time of her bearing him (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.88). Castianeira, a bride from Aesyme, was the mother of Gorgythion (8.302–305; see [[castianeira]]), and Priam tells Achilles that nineteen of his fifty sons were born to him from one womb and the rest by women in his halls (24.495–497; see [[sons-of-priam]]). In Book 22 he calls Laothoe κρείουσα γυναικῶν, “princess among women” (22.48). Leaf takes the word, found only there, to mean that she was a true wife and in no lower place: a plain case of polygamy, which the poem shows among the Trojans and nowhere among the Greeks (on 22.48).

**In Book 22.** Priam, on the wall, looks for her two sons among the Trojans who have fled into the city, and cannot see them. If they are still alive among the host, he will ransom them with bronze and gold, for there is store of it in the house: old Altes, “of famous name”, gave much with his daughter. If they are already dead and in the house of Hades, it is grief to him and to their mother (22.46–53; see [[priam]], [[apoina]] and [[bereaved-parents]]). Both are dead. Leaf thinks the gifts, if the line is genuine, show again that she held no inferior place; but he thinks van Leeuwen probably right to omit it as a late addition, since gifts given with a daughter at her marriage belong only to the end of the Homeric period, and Priam would hardly say that he counts on his wives' dowries to ransom his sons. He reports that Hoffmann rejected 22.46–55 and Naber 22.46–53 (on 22.51).

**The name.** Λαοθόη seems to join λαός, “people”, and θοός, “swift”, the second element of the men's names Hippothous and Alcathous (2.840, 12.93; see [[hippothous]] and [[alcathous]]).

## Outside Homer

Strabo, counting the dynasties of the Troad that were subject to Priam, makes the Leleges of Altes the third, after those of Mynes at Lyrnessus and of Eetion at Thebe, and adds that Priam begot Lycaon and Polydorus by Altes' daughter, whom he does not name (*Geography* 13.1.7; see [[strabo-on-the-city-of-mynes]]). Apollodorus does not name her either. He makes Polydorus a son of Hecuba, as Euripides had done (*Hecuba* 3–4), and lists Lycaon among the sons whom Priam had by other women (*Library* 3.12.5; see [[apollodorus]] and [[polydorus-son-of-priam]]).
