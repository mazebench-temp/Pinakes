κρατερὸς πάϊς Ἀμφιτρύωνος, “the mighty son of Amphitryon” (5.392): Heracles, who shot Hera in the breast (see [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]]). The same speech calls him “the son of aegis-bearing Zeus” (5.396), and Homer gives Heracles both fathers without explaining how. The Iliad says that Alcmene bore him to Zeus in Thebes (14.323–324, 19.98–99). In the Odyssey Odysseus sees Alcmene, “the wife of Amphitryon”, who bore Heracles in the arms of Zeus (Od. 11.266–268), and Megara, the wife of “the son of Amphitryon” (Od. 11.269–270). Amphitryon himself never appears.

## Outside Homer

The *Shield of Heracles* opens with his story. Amphitryon had killed Alcmene's father, Electryon, and fled from Argos to Thebes. On the night he came home from war, Zeus lay with Alcmene, and she bore twins, Heracles to Zeus and Iphicles to Amphitryon ([Hesiod], *Shield* 1–56). [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] tells the same story at length (*Library* 2.4.6–8). The god who took the husband's shape made Amphitryon a figure of comedy, in Plautus' *Amphitruo* and in the plays of Molière (1668), Kleist (1807) and Giraudoux (*Amphitryon 38*, 1929).
