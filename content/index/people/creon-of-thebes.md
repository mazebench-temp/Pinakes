## In Homer

Homer names a Creon of the heroic past once, in the catalogue of heroines: Odysseus saw “Megara, the daughter of high-hearted Creon, / whom the son of Amphitryon, ever unyielding in his might, had as wife” (*Odyssey* 11.269–270; see [[megara-wife-of-heracles]] and [[heracles]]). The poem does not say where he ruled or whose son he was, and it says nothing of the Creon of the Theban tragedies. Two lines later it tells the story of Epicaste, Jocasta's Homeric name, without her brother (*Odyssey* 11.271–280; see [[jocasta]] and [[oedipus]]). ὑπέρθυμος, “high-hearted” (*Odyssey* 11.269; see [[high-hearted]]).

Whether the two are one man is disputed. Euripides gives them the same name and father: in the *Heracles* Megara's father is “Creon, the son of Menoeceus”, lord of Thebes (*Heracles* 8–9), and in the *Phoenician Women* Jocasta, “daughter of Menoeceus”, names Creon her brother by the same mother (*Phoenician Women* 10–11; see [[euripides]]). W. W. Merry says that this Creon “is not identical with the brother-in-law of Oedipus” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.269; see the Odyssey translation's note on 11.269). The index follows Euripides and keeps both in one entry.

He is not the Creon of the Iliad, the father of Lycomedes (*Iliad* 9.84; see [[creon]]).

## Outside Homer

In tragedy Creon is the Theban king in Sophocles' *Antigone* and *Oedipus the King*, and the regent in Euripides' *Phoenician Women*. For the threat of his son see [[haemon-threatens-creon]]. In Euripides' *Heracles*, Lycus, a newcomer from Euboea, has killed Creon and taken the city, and means to kill Heracles' sons and their mother, Creon's daughter Megara, so that they may never avenge their mother's kin (*Heracles* 31–43).
