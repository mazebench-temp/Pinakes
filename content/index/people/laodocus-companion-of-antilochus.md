βῆ δὲ θέειν, τὰ δὲ τεύχεʼ ἀμύμονι δῶκεν ἑταίρῳ / Λαοδόκῳ, ὅς οἱ σχεδὸν ἔστρεφε μώνυχας ἵππους, “but set off running, and gave his armor to his noble companion / Laodocus, who was wheeling his single-hoofed horses close by” (17.698–699; see [[antilochus]], [[blameless]] and [[single-hoofed]]). He is named only here. ἔστρεφε, “was wheeling”, is a charioteer's work: Walter Leaf explains it as wheeling round “so as to follow all his movements” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 17.699), that is, keeping the chariot near Antilochus wherever he went.

Menelaus has just told Antilochus that Patroclus is dead and asked him to run to Achilles (17.685–693; see [[menelaus]]). Antilochus stands speechless, his eyes full of tears, but does not neglect the command; he leaves his armor with Laodocus and goes on foot, weeping (17.694–701). The narrator does not say why he leaves the armor. When he reaches Achilles in the next book he is πόδας ταχὺς ἄγγελος, “a messenger swift of foot” (18.2).

Another Laodocus is a son of Antenor, whose likeness Athena takes in Book 4 when she goes to find Pandarus (4.86–88; see [[laodocus]]).
