**Sarpedon's attendant.** ὅς ῥʼ ἠῢς θεράπων Σαρπηδόνος ἦεν ἄνακτος, “who was the good attendant of lord Sarpedon” (16.464; see [[sarpedon]]). The poem gives him no father, no land and no deed, and does not say whether he drove Sarpedon's chariot, from which Sarpedon has just leapt to meet Patroclus (16.426–427). Later in the book the same words, ἠῢς θεράπων, name Patroclus himself, “the good attendant of Achilles, son of Peleus”, as Zeus decides that he will drive the Trojans to the city before he dies (16.653; see [[patroclus]]). His epithet, ἀγακλειτός, “famous” (16.463), is the one given to the allies whom Sarpedon led against the Achaean wall (ἀγακλειτῶν ἐπικούρων, 12.101; see [[trojan-allies]]).

**The first blow.** Zeus has just let his son go to his death (16.431–461), and the duel begins with a loss on each side. Patroclus kills Sarpedon's attendant; Sarpedon, rushing in second, misses Patroclus and kills his trace horse Pedasus (16.466–469; see [[pedasus-the-horse]]). The wound, νείαιραν κατὰ γαστέρα, “in the lower belly” (16.465), is named in these words only here, and λῦσε δὲ γυῖα, “and loosened his limbs”, ends the line (see [[loosened-his-limbs]]).

The name is formed with θρασύς, “bold”, as is that of Thrasymedes, son of Nestor, who kills Maris earlier in the book (16.321–325; see [[thrasymedes]]).
