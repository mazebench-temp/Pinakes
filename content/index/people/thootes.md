Menestheus cannot make himself heard over the din at the gates (12.337–341), so he sends a runner, “brilliant Thootes” (12.343; see [[brilliant]] and [[heralds]]). The name seems to be formed from θοός, “swift”; an ancient note calls it a name proper to a herald, “from running” (scholia A and T on 12.342). It suits the errand: the herald “went running along the wall of the bronze-armored Achaeans” (12.352).

He gives the message in Menestheus' words, changed only as delivery requires (see [[message-repeated]]). He opens with an address, Agamemnon's verse from the review (4.285), and names the sender by his father: “Ajaxes, leaders of the bronze-armored Argives, / the dear son of Peteos, whom Zeus has nurtured, bids you / go there” (12.354–356; see [[the-two-ajaxes]] and [[peteos]]). “Here” and “there” change places (12.345, 12.358; 12.348, 12.361), and “toil and quarrel” becomes “war and quarrel”; the rest is repeated exactly. Telamonian Ajax obeys (12.364), and the herald is not mentioned again.
