## In Homer

{{quote:5969c907-d704-59a1-af96-11db2ca6c422}}

Λυκόοργος (Lykoorgos) is the epic form of the name later written Λυκοῦργος; the genitive here is Λυκούργου (6.134). He is κρατερός, “mighty” (6.130), and ἀνδροφόνος, “man-slaying” (6.134; see [[man-slaying]]), the standing epithet of Hector. His crime is an attack on the god's nurses (τιθῆναι) on the holy mountain (see [[nysa]]): they drop their θύσθλα, the sacred things of the rite, as he strikes them with a βουπλήξ, an ox-goad or an axe (see [[dionysus]]). The god himself flees into the sea, where [[Thetis]] takes him to her bosom. The punishment comes from all the gods together: “the gods who live at ease were angry with him” (6.138; see [[who-live-at-ease]]), and Zeus, “the son of Cronus”, blinded him. The poem does not say where he ruled or how he died.

Diomedes tells the story to explain why he will not fight a god (6.128–129, 6.141; see [[diomedes]] and [[mythological-paradigm]]). Its moral, that the man who fought the gods “did not live long” (δὴν ἦν, 6.131, 6.139–140), is Dione's warning about Diomedes himself in Book 5 (5.407; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]). The same words turn Glaucus' story of Bellerophon toward its end: he too “was hated by all the gods” (6.200; see [[bellerophon]]). It is the oldest surviving Greek story of a man who resisted Dionysus. See [[lycurgus-and-dionysus]]. Another Lycurgus, an Arcadian, killed Areithous the mace-man (7.142–149; see [[lycurgus-of-arcadia]]).

## Outside Homer

Aeschylus told the story in a lost tetralogy, the *Lycurgeia*; fragments of its *Edonians* survive. In Sophocles the son of Dryas, king of the Edonians, is walled up in a rocky prison by Dionysus for his mocking rage (*Antigone* 955–965). [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] makes him king of the Edonians by the river Strymon: when he drove out Dionysus, the god fled into the sea to Thetis, and Lycurgus, driven mad, killed his own son Dryas with an axe, thinking he was pruning a vine; the land became barren, and the Edonians, obeying an oracle, took him to Mount Pangaeum, where he was destroyed by horses (*Library* 3.5.1). The Arcadian Lycurgus of Nestor's story, who killed Areithous by a trick, is another man (7.142–149).
