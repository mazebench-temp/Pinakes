**The suppliant.** The Trojans make the first push in the fight over Sarpedon's body, “for a man was hit who was by no means the worst among the Myrmidons” (16.569–570; see [[myrmidons]] and [[litotes]]). The narrator stops to give the fallen man a past in five verses. He had been a king, “lord in well-peopled Budeum” (see [[budeum]]), until he killed “a brave cousin of his” (ἐσθλὸν ἀνεψιόν). Then he came as a suppliant (ἱκέτευσε) to Peleus and to silver-footed Thetis, and they sent him to follow Achilles to Ilios (see [[peleus]], [[thetis]], [[supplication]] and [[exile-and-refuge]]). The poem tells the same of others who found refuge in Peleus' house. Phoenix, fleeing his father's anger, came to Phthia “to lord Peleus; and he received me gladly”, and Peleus made him lord of the Dolopians and sent him to Troy with Achilles (9.478–484, 9.438–443; see [[phoenix]]). Patroclus came there as a boy after killing a playmate, and Peleus reared him (23.85–90; see [[patroclus]]). The killer of a kinsman who leaves his country is a figure the Iliad meets often: Tlepolemus killed his father's uncle Licymnius and fled over the sea to Rhodes (2.661–667), Medon killed a kinsman of his stepmother and lived in Phylace (13.694–697), and Lycophron killed a man in Cythera and lived with Ajax (15.430–432; see [[tlepolemus]], [[medon]] and [[lycophron]]). The verse names Thetis beside Peleus as the one who received him, in Peleus' house, where Achilles remembers hearing her (1.396–397); in the Iliad's present she sits “in the depths of the sea beside her aged father” (1.358).

**His death.**

{{quote:efec1326-c46c-582e-b140-277445997f09}}

He is struck “as he took hold of the corpse” (ἁπτόμενον νέκυος), doing what Patroclus had just urged the two Ajaxes to do, to seize Sarpedon's body and strip it (16.558–561; see [[sarpedon]] and [[fighting-over-the-body]]). The three verses of his fall are those in which Patroclus killed Erylaus with a stone a little earlier, except that Epeigeus falls “face down on the body” where Erylaus fell “face down on the ground” (16.578–580 ≈ 16.412–414; see [[erylaus]] and [[hector]]). Patroclus answers at once: “And grief came upon Patroclus for his fallen companion, / and he made straight through the champions like a hawk” (16.581–582; see [[like-a-hawk]]), and his own stone breaks the neck of Sthenelaus (16.586–587; see [[sthenelaus]]). He is named only here.

**The name.** Ἐπειγεύς looks like a formation from the verb ἐπείγω, “press hard, press on”; the poem does not play on it. The narrator gives him the epithet δῖος, “brilliant” (16.571; see [[brilliant]]), and his father the epithet μεγάθυμος, “great-hearted” (see [[great-hearted]] and [[agacles]]).
