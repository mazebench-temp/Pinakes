παῖδες Ἀλωῆος, “the sons of Aloeus” (5.386). Homer names him only through his sons and his wives. Dione calls Eeriboea their μητρυιή, “stepmother” (5.389), so she was his wife but not their mother (see [[eeriboea]]). The Odyssey names their mother, Iphimedeia, “the wife of Aloeus” (Ἀλωῆος παράκοιτιν, *Odyssey* 11.305), who claimed that she had lain with Poseidon (see [[iphimedeia]]). After him the brothers were later called the Aloadae. The name looks like a derivative of ἀλωή, “threshing floor” or “orchard” (5.90, 5.499). W. W. Merry takes him for “the man of the threshing-floor”, and the names of his sons for the treading out and the winnowing of the grain, so that the family commemorates the early glories of agriculture (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.307; see [[otus]] and [[ephialtes]]).

## Outside Homer

The Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, as a scholiast on Apollonius reports it, made Otus and Ephialtes the sons of Aloeus and Iphimedeia in name only, and in truth of Poseidon and Iphimedeia, and said that their father founded Halus, a city in Aetolia (fr. 6 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]).

[[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] makes Aloeus a son of Poseidon and Canace, daughter of Aeolus, and the husband of Iphimedeia, daughter of Triops. Iphimedeia fell in love with Poseidon and bore him the two giants (*Library* 1.7.4). Merry gives the same genealogy and makes Aloeus the uncle as well as the husband of Iphimedeia (on *Odyssey* 11.305).
