**From Percote to Priam's house.** The narrator stops before Hector's rebuke to give his history. The verses that bring him back to Ilios and to Priam's house were said of Imbrius, Priam's son-in-law, in *Iliad* 13 (15.549–551 = 13.174–176; see [[imbrius]] and [[priam]]): each lived away from the city until the Achaeans came, Melanippus with his cattle at Percote, Imbrius at Pedaeum (13.172; see [[percote]] and [[pedaeum]]). His father Hicetaon is Priam's brother (20.237–238; see [[hicetaon]]), so that he is Hector's first cousin, and so is Dolops, son of Lampus, whose death Hector holds up to him (15.553–555; see [[dolops-son-of-lampus]]).

**Rebuked.** “Are we to hold back like this, Melanippus? Is your dear heart / not moved at all, now that your cousin is killed?” (15.553–554). They can no longer fight the Argives from a distance, Hector tells him, until either they kill them or the Argives take Ilios (15.556–558; see [[hector]]). He follows, “a man equal to the gods” (15.559).

**His death.** Menelaus spurs on Antilochus, who leaps out from the champions and hits Melanippus “in the chest beside the nipple” as he goes into the war (15.568–577; see [[antilochus]] and [[menelaus]]); “He fell with a thud, and darkness covered his eyes” (15.578; see [[fell-with-a-thud]] and [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). Antilochus springs on him like a dog on a wounded fawn (see [[the-dog-and-the-fawn]]), and the narrator turns to address him: “so at you, Melanippus, Antilochus, steadfast in battle, leapt / to strip your armor” (15.582–583). So the narrator addresses Apollo at the wall in this book (15.365), and Menelaus and Patroclus elsewhere (4.127, 16.20). Hector comes running through the combat, and Antilochus flees without the armor, like a beast that has done some harm (15.583–591; see [[the-beast-that-has-done-harm]]).

## Outside Homer

[[Strabo]] quotes the lines on his cattle at Percote (15.546–548) with those on Democoon, Priam's bastard son, who came to the war from his horses at Abydos (4.499–500). Each, he argues, grazed herds that were his family's, not another's, and so the coast from Abydos to Adrasteia was Trojan land under Priam (*Geography* 13.1.7; see [[percote]] and [[democoon]]).
