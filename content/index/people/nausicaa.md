## The name

Ναυσικάα begins with ναῦς, “ship”, like many names of her seafaring people: her grandfather Nausithous (*Odyssey* 6.7; see [[nausithous]]) and the young men who stand up for the games, among them Nauteus, Prymneus, Eretmeus, Ponteus and Proreus, names of the sailor, the stern, the oar, the sea and the prow (*Odyssey* 8.111–113; see [[phaeacians]]). The Suda finds the name fitting for the land, since the Phaeacians, the most seafaring of peoples, burned pitch onto their ships for safety (ἐπέκαιον ταῖς ναυσὶ πίσσαν, Suda ν 70), as though the name joined ναῦς and καίω, “burn”.

## Family

She is θυγάτηρ μεγαλήτορος Ἀλκινόοιο, “the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous” (*Odyssey* 6.17, 6.196, 6.213; 8.464), the verse-end the Iliad gives Andromache, “the daughter of great-hearted Eetion” (*Iliad* 6.395; see [[alcinous]] and [[andromache]]). Her mother is the queen whose knees Nausicaa tells Odysseus to clasp at the hearth (*Odyssey* 6.303–315), named Arete in the next book (*Odyssey* 7.53–55, 7.141–142; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]). Arete is the daughter of Alcinous' brother Rhexenor, so that both her parents descend from Nausithous, the son of Poseidon (*Odyssey* 7.56–66; see [[nausithous]] and [[poseidon]]).

She is the only daughter of the house (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 6.65), with five brothers, two married and three still unmarried (*Odyssey* 6.62–65). Three of them, Laodamas, Halius and Clytoneus, stand up at the games (*Odyssey* 8.118–119), and when she comes home her brothers, “like the immortals”, stand around her, unyoke the mules and carry the clothes inside (*Odyssey* 7.4–6, a plain rendering). The old chamber-woman Eurymedusa, brought by ship from Apeire and chosen as a prize for Alcinous, nursed her, and lights her fire and makes ready her supper (*Odyssey* 7.7–13).

## Descriptions

- λευκώλενος, “white-armed” (*Odyssey* 6.101, 6.186, 6.251; 7.12), above all an epithet of Hera, given in the Odyssey also to her mother Arete and to her handmaids (*Odyssey* 7.233; 6.239; see [[white-armed]]).
- ἐύπεπλος, “well-robed” (*Odyssey* 6.49; see [[well-robed]]).
- ἐυῶπις, “fair-faced”, of her alone in Homer (ἐυώπιδα κούρην, *Odyssey* 6.113, 6.142; see [[fair-faced]]).
- παρθένος ἀδμής, “a maiden unwed” (*Odyssey* 6.109, 6.228): ἀδμής, “unbroken”, is said elsewhere of a heifer that no man has yet led under the yoke, of a mare and of young mules (*Iliad* 10.293, 23.266; *Odyssey* 4.637; see [[unbroken]]).
- βασίλεια, “the princess” (*Odyssey* 6.115), elsewhere the word for a queen (see [[basileus]]). Odysseus calls her ἄνασσα, “queen” (*Odyssey* 6.149, 6.175), a word Homer otherwise gives only to Athena, whom Nestor addresses so (*Odyssey* 3.380), and to Demeter (*Iliad* 14.326), so that there is a special compliment in giving it to a woman (Merry on *Odyssey* 6.149; see [[anax]]); and γύναι, “lady” (*Odyssey* 6.168).
- κούρη, “the girl”, the narrator's word for her through her book (*Odyssey* 6.15, 6.74, 6.78, 6.113, 6.237), and θάλος, “scion”, in Odysseus' praise (*Odyssey* 6.157).
- ἀθανάτῃσι φυὴν καὶ εἶδος ὁμοίη, “like the immortals in stature and in looks” (*Odyssey* 6.16), and at her farewell θεῶν ἄπο κάλλος ἔχουσα, “with beauty from the gods” (*Odyssey* 8.457, a plain rendering). The narrator likens her among her handmaids to Artemis among the nymphs, and Odysseus likens her to Artemis when he first speaks to her (*Odyssey* 6.102–109, 6.151–152; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]] and [[artemis]]).

## Later in the Odyssey

For the whole episode, see [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]]. The renderings of the later books here are plain renderings, not the translation. She drives home ahead of Odysseus, and her brothers unyoke the mules and carry the clothes in (*Odyssey* 7.1–6). In the palace Odysseus tells her parents how he found her among her handmaids on the shore, “like the goddesses”, and how she did not fail in good sense, as one would not expect of someone so young (*Odyssey* 7.290–296). When Alcinous blames her for not bringing the stranger home with her women, Odysseus defends “the blameless girl”: she bade him follow with her handmaids, he says, but he would not, for fear and shame (*Odyssey* 7.298–307), though in her own words she had told him to wait in Athena's grove before the city (*Odyssey* 6.295–299). Alcinous wishes that such a man might have his daughter and be called his son-in-law (*Odyssey* 7.311–315).

On the evening of the games, as Odysseus comes from the bath to the feast at which he will tell his story, she stands by the doorpost of the hall, marvels at him and speaks for the last time in the poem: “Farewell, stranger, so that even in your native land you may remember me, since to me first you owe the price of your life”; and he answers that if Zeus grants him his homecoming he will pray to her there as to a god all his days, “for you gave me my life, girl” (*Odyssey* 8.457–468). She is not named again.

## Outside Homer

**Telemachus' wife.** Later writers gave her a husband. Eustathius, on *Odyssey* 16.118, reports that Aristotle, in the *Constitution of the Ithacans*, and Hellanicus married Telemachus to Nausicaa, daughter of Alcinous, and made her the mother of the son whom Hesiod gives to Polycaste, Nestor's youngest daughter (Aristotle, *Constitution of the Ithacans* fr. 512 Gigon; see [[aristotle]], [[telemachus]] and [[polycaste]]). The Suda, citing Hellanicus, makes the orator Andocides a descendant of Telemachus and Nausicaa (Suda α 2148).

**The ball game.** A woman grammarian of Corcyra, Agallis, gave Nausicaa the invention of the game of ball, as a favor to her countrywoman (Athenaeus 1.25, as Merry quotes him on *Odyssey* 6.101; see [[athenaeus]] and [[ball]]); the Suda, which calls her Anagallis, repeats it (Suda α 1817, σ 1720). Corcyra was the later Greeks' Scheria (see [[scheria]]).

**Plays.** Eustathius records that Sophocles wrote a satyr play, the *Washerwomen* (Πλύντριαι), in which he himself played Nausicaa (as Merry reports him on *Odyssey* 6.115; see [[sophocles]]), and the Suda lists a *Washerwoman, or Nausicaa* among the comedies of Philyllius, a poet of Old Comedy (Suda φ 457). For the painting of the scene by Polygnotus and for Plutarch's question why she washed in the river and not in the sea, see [[the-meeting-of-odysseus-and-nausicaa]]; for the judgments of her wish to have such a husband, [[nausicaas-wish]]; for Virgil's Dido, likened to Diana as Nausicaa is to Artemis, [[virgils-dido-as-diana]].

**A lesson for husbands and wives.** The *Economics* ascribed to Aristotle, whose third book survives only in Latin, takes the words Odysseus speaks to her at *Odyssey* 6.168, which its Latin gives as *te mulier valde miror et timeo*, as an example of the love joined with modest fear that Homer thought husband and wife should feel for each other, and his wish for her, a husband, a home and a noble oneness of mind (*Odyssey* 6.180–185), as proof that Homer honored above all the chaste partnership of marriage (*Economics* 3, in Rose's Latin text, fr. 184, pp. 145–146; see [[husbands-and-wives]] and [[nothing-better-than-one-mind-in-a-house]]).
