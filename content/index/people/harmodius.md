tags: [athenian]
summary: The young Athenian who with Aristogeiton killed Hipparchus, brother of the tyrant Hippias, in 514 BCE; the pair were honored as the tyrant-slayers, with the first statues set up in the agora.
aliases: [the tyrant-slayers, the tyrannicides]
---

Thucydides tells the story to correct the Athenians' own version. The motive was a love affair: Hipparchus had tried to win Harmodius from his lover Aristogeiton and then insulted his sister. The man they killed, at the Panathenaea, was Hipparchus and not the tyrant Hippias, and the tyranny lasted four more years (6.54–59; compare Herodotus 5.55–56). Harmodius was killed on the spot and Aristogeiton soon after. The bronze statues of the pair by Antenor stood in the agora; Xerxes carried them off, and they were later sent back, while a second group, by Critius and Nesiotes, had taken their place (Pausanias 1.8.5).

The *Rhetoric* returns to them twice in its second book: Iphicrates answered a descendant of Harmodius who reproached him with his low birth (2.23, 1397b30–32, 1398a17–22; see [[iphicrates-against-harmodius]]), and their love, which put down the tyranny, is an example of a fallacious argument that lovers are useful to cities (2.24, 1401b10–12; see [[hipparchus]]).
