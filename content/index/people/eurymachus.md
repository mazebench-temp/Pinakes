# Εὐρύμαχος, Εὐρύμαχʼ (Od. 1.399, 1.413) and, in later books, Εὐρύμαχον, Εὐρυμάχου, Εὐρυμάχῳ: only the suitor,

## Etymology

Εὐρύμαχος = εὐρύς (“wide”) + μάχη (“battle”), a compound of the same kind as Τηλέμαχος, τῆλε (“far off”) + μάχη (see [[telemachus]]).

## Family

He is introduced by his father's name, τὸν δʼ αὖτʼ Εὐρύμαχος Πολύβου πάϊς ἀντίον ηὔδα, “Then Eurymachus, son of Polybus, answered him face to face” (*Odyssey* 1.399 = 2.177, and the patronymic again at 16.345, 16.434, 18.349, 20.359, 21.320; see [[polybus-father-of-eurymachus]]). Telemachus calls him “the splendid son of wise-hearted Polybus” (*Odyssey* 15.519).

## Descriptions

He is θεοειδής, “godlike” (*Odyssey* 4.628, 21.186; see [[godlike]]), and with Antinous one of the “leaders of the suitors, and in excellence they were by far the best” (*Odyssey* 4.629 = 21.187; see [[antinous]] and [[the-suitors]]). Telemachus says that the Ithacans now look on him as on a god, that he is by far the best man, and that he is most eager to marry Penelope and to have Odysseus' honor (*Odyssey* 15.519–522); according to Athena, Penelope's father and brothers urge her to marry him, since he outdoes all the suitors in gifts and has raised his bride-price (*Odyssey* 15.16–18; see [[icarius]]). The renderings of later books here are plain renderings, not the translation.

## Elsewhere in Homer

When Penelope learns of the plot against her son, he swears that no man shall lay hands on Telemachus while he lives, since Odysseus used to set him on his knees as a child and give him meat and wine: “so he spoke, encouraging her, but he himself was planning his death” (*Odyssey* 16.434–448).

He courts Penelope with praise of her beauty and her mind (*Odyssey* 18.244–249) and gives her a golden necklace strung with amber (*Odyssey* 18.295–296). The maid Melantho is his lover (*Odyssey* 18.325), and the goatherd Melanthius sits facing him at the feast, “for he loved him most” (*Odyssey* 17.256–257). He mocks the beggar Odysseus for the light that shines from his bald head, and the footstool he throws at him strikes the cupbearer's hand (*Odyssey* 18.349–398; see [[footstool]]). He mocks the seer Theoclymenus too (*Odyssey* 20.359–362). At the contest he warms the bow at the fire and still cannot string it; it is not the marriage he grieves for, he says, since there are many other Achaean women, but the shame for men to come of being so far beneath godlike Odysseus in strength (*Odyssey* 21.245–255).

When Odysseus has killed Antinous and revealed himself, Eurymachus alone answers. He grants that the suitors have done many reckless things, lays the blame on Antinous, “who now lies dead”, and promises that each of them will pay the worth of twenty oxen in bronze and gold (*Odyssey* 22.44–59; see [[atasthalie]]). Odysseus refuses (*Odyssey* 22.60–64). Then Eurymachus draws his sword and rushes at him with a terrible cry, and Odysseus' arrow strikes him in the breast and fixes in his liver; he falls over a table, scattering the food and the cup, and beats the ground with his forehead (*Odyssey* 22.69–88).

## Outside Homer

Merry quotes Athenaeus' description of a game said to have been played by the suitors, in which each cast a piece at a piece called Penelope; Eurymachus, the account ends, had won most often and had high hopes of the marriage (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.107; see [[the-game-of-penelope]] and [[athenaeus]]).
