Homer names Polynices only here, in Agamemnon's story of Tydeus. Tydeus came to Mycenae “not in war”, a guest together with Polynices, “gathering an army” for a war already begun against “the sacred walls of Thebes”. The Mycenaeans were willing to give them allies, but Zeus turned them back with ill-omened signs. ἀντίθεος (“godlike”; [[antitheos]]) is a standing epithet of heroes. Homer does not say whose son Polynices is, why he is making war on Thebes, or how the war ended for him. Of the Theban royal house the Iliad knows only that Oedipus fell and was honored with funeral games at Thebes (23.679–680), and the Odyssey tells of Oedipus and his mother Epicaste without naming their children (Od. 11.271–280). See [[tydeus]] and [[thebes]].

## Etymology

Πολυνείκης = πολύς (“much”) + νεῖκος (“quarrel, strife”; see [[neikos]]): “much strife”. The tragedians heard the name as a prophecy; in Euripides a speaker tells him that his father named him for strife “by divine foresight” (*Phoenician Women* 636–637).

## Outside Homer

The lost *Thebaid* told of the curse that set the brothers against each other. Athenaeus quotes the lines in which Polynices sets before Oedipus the silver table of Cadmus and a golden cup, and the old man, seeing his own father's treasures, curses both his sons: they will never share their inheritance in friendship, but always in war (11.465e). In the later story Polynices is the son of Oedipus and Jocasta. Driven out by his brother [[Eteocles]], he went to Argos, married a daughter of [[Adrastus]] as Tydeus married another, and led the Seven against Thebes, where the brothers killed each other (Apollodorus 3.5.9–3.6.8; Aeschylus, *Seven against Thebes*). His sister Antigone buried him against Creon's decree (Sophocles, *Antigone*). See [[the-seven-against-thebes]].
