## Etymology

Εὐπείθης is formed from εὖ (“well”) and the root of πείθω (“persuade”; in the middle, “obey”). When the kinsmen arm themselves, the poem sets the name beside the verb: ἀλλʼ Εὐπείθει / πείθοντʼ, “but they obeyed Eupeithes” (*Odyssey* 24.465–466).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Penelope, rebuking Antinous for plotting her son's death, reminds him of his father's debt to Odysseus. Eupeithes once came to Odysseus' house as a fugitive, in fear of the people, who were angry because he had joined Taphian pirates in harrying the Thesprotians, the allies of Ithaca; they wanted to kill him and devour his great wealth, but Odysseus held them back (*Odyssey* 16.424–430). “His house you now devour without payment, and woo his wife, and would kill his son” (*Odyssey* 16.431–432; see [[antinous]]).

After the slaughter of the suitors he rises in the assembly of Ithaca, grieving for his son, “Antinous, the first whom brilliant Odysseus killed”. Odysseus, he says, lost the ships and the men he led to Troy and has now killed the best of the Cephallenians at home, and they must take vengeance before he escapes to Pylos or to Elis, or be shamed for ever (*Odyssey* 24.422–437; see [[cephallenians]]). More than half of the people follow him; he leads them “in their folly”, thinking to avenge his son's murder, “but he was not to come home again” (*Odyssey* 24.463–471). Athena breathes might into Laertes; he prays to the daughter of Zeus and throws, and the old man's spear strikes Eupeithes through his bronze-cheeked helmet (*Odyssey* 24.513–525; see [[laertes]]). The renderings here are plain renderings, not the translation.
