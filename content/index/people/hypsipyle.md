Homer names her once, as the mother of Euneus, whom “Hypsipyle bore to Jason, shepherd of the people” (7.469; see [[euneus]] and [[jason]]). ὑπʼ Ἰήσονι, literally “under Jason”, is the idiom for a woman who bears a child to a man, as Alcestis bore Eumelus “to Admetus” (τὸν ὑπʼ Ἀδμήτῳ τέκε, 2.714; see [[alcestis]]). Her name is a compound of ὕψι, “high”, and πύλη, “gate”: “High Gate”. The adjective formed in the same way, ὑψίπυλος, “high-gated”, is an epithet of walled cities (6.416; see [[high-gated]]).

Homer does not tell her story, or say why Jason came to Lemnos. The Iliad knows a king of the island, “godlike Thoas” (14.230), and a silver bowl that passed from Thoas to Euneus (23.745–747); later poets make Thoas her father.

## Outside Homer

**The Lemnian women.** The women of Lemnos had neglected Aphrodite, and their husbands turned from them to captive women carried off from Thrace. The wives killed all the men of the island, and Hypsipyle alone spared her father Thoas, the king; in Apollonius she sets him adrift in a chest. When the Argo put in, the women, now ruled by Hypsipyle, received the Argonauts, and she took Jason as her lover; she offered him her father's kingdom, but he sailed on (Apollonius, *Argonautica* 1.609–909; Apollodorus, *Library* 1.9.17; see [[the-lemnian-women]], [[apollonius-lemnos]] and [[pindar-lemnian-women]]). Herodotus says that because of this crime, and of a later murder on the same island, all cruel deeds in Greece came to be called “Lemnian” (Herodotus 6.138.4), and Aeschylus' chorus sets the Lemnian crime first among the crimes of story (*Libation Bearers* 631–634). Pindar tells how Erginus won the race in armor among the Lemnian women and spoke to Hypsipyle as he went to take his crown (*Olympian* 4), and how the Argonauts, on their way home, lay with the “man-slaying” women of Lemnos (*Pythian* 4). Aeschylus wrote a *Hypsipyle* and Sophocles a *Lemnian Women*; both are lost.

**At Nemea.** Euripides' *Hypsipyle*, known from papyrus fragments (P.Oxy. 852, published in 1908), tells what became of her. Sold into slavery, she is nurse to Opheltes, the infant son of Lycurgus of Nemea. When the Seven pass on their way to Thebes, she leaves the child to show them a spring, and a serpent kills him. She is condemned to die, Amphiaraus speaks for her, and her sons by Jason, Euneus and Thoas, find her and recognize her. The Nemean games were said to have been founded in honor of the child (see [[euripides-hypsipyle]]). Apollodorus tells the same story (*Library* 3.6.4), and Statius retells it at length (*Thebaid* 4–6; see [[the-seven-against-thebes]]). Ovid gives her a letter to Jason, reproaching him for leaving her and for Medea (*Heroides* 6; see [[ovid-heroides]]).
