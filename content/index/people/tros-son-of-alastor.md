**The hope.** Tros is the fifth man Achilles kills after Apollo has saved Hector, after Dryops, Demuchus and the two sons of Bias (20.455–462; see [[dryops]], [[demuchus]], [[laogonus-son-of-bias]] and [[dardanus-son-of-bias]]). Τρῶα δʼ Ἀλαστορίδην, “And Tros, son of Alastor” (20.463; see [[alastor-father-of-tros]]): the accusative is left hanging while his hope is told, and the verb that governs it comes six verses later, “but the other stabbed him with his sword in the liver” (20.469). Walter Leaf compares the broken construction at 6.510 (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.463). He came to meet Achilles “to clasp his knees” (20.463; see [[gounata]] and [[supplication]]), and the narrator tells what he hoped: that Achilles would take him alive, spare him and let him go, “pitying a man of his own age” (20.464–465). ὁμηλικίη is the word for one's age-mates (3.175; *Odyssey* 22.209). No ransom is mentioned, though Adrastus, Dolon and the sons of Antimachus had all offered one (6.46–50, 10.378–381, 11.131–135; see [[apoina]]). Tros' claim is to be a young man like Achilles.

**The narrator's judgment.** “The fool, and he did not know that the other would not be persuaded” (20.466; see [[nepios]]) is the verse Nestor uses in the Odyssey of Agamemnon, who stayed to sacrifice and did not know that Athena would not be persuaded (*Odyssey* 3.146): there it was a god whose mind does not turn, and here it is Achilles. The reason follows: “for he was no sweet-tempered man, nor gentle-hearted, / but in a great fury” (20.467–468). γλυκύθυμος and ἀγανόφρων occur only here in Homer (see [[sweet-tempered]] and [[gentle-hearted]]). Leaf suspects the five verses from ὃ μέν in 463 to ἐμμεμαώς in 468: the description of Achilles in the narrator's own words is, he says, wholly alien to the epic style, and γλυκύς is elsewhere said of things that give pleasure, never of the mind; the lines look to him like an expansion of 468–469 (on 20.463; see the translation's note on 20.463–468).

**The death.** He never reaches the knees: ἥπτετο, “he was trying to clasp”, is an attempt that fails, as Leaf, after Hentze, takes it (on 20.464; see [[gounata]]). “Eager to beg him”, he is stabbed in the liver, “and the liver slipped out of him, and the dark blood from it / filled his bosom” (20.469–471). Leaf explains that the edge of the liver came out through the wound, not the whole organ (on 20.470). “And darkness covered his eyes” (20.471; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). Every man who begs for his life on the battlefield of the Iliad is killed: Adrastus, Dolon, the sons of Antimachus, Tros and, in the next book, Lycaon (6.37–65, 10.454–457, 11.122–147, 21.64–119; see [[supplication-type-scene]]). The verb of Tros' hope, πεφίδοιτο, “spare” (20.464), returns when Achilles tells Lycaon that before Patroclus died it was dearer to him “to spare” Trojans (πεφιδέσθαι), and that he took many alive and sold them, but now none whom a god puts into his hands will escape death (21.99–105; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]); and in the last book Zeus sends word to Priam that Achilles “with great care … will spare a man who comes as a suppliant” (πεφιδήσεται, 24.158, 24.187; see [[priam]]).

**The name.** Τρώς is the name of the king, son of Erichthonius and father of Ilus, Assaracus and Ganymede, whom Aeneas named in his genealogy (20.230–232; see [[tros]] and [[trojans]]). The son of Alastor is another man.

## Outside Homer

Leaf notes that Aristarchus read ἐνέπρησεν, “puffed out” the loose breast of the tunic, for ἐνέπλησεν, “filled”, at 20.471, and that Virgil evidently read “filled”: in the death of Lausus the sword passes through the shield and the tunic his mother had woven, “and the blood filled the fold” (*implevitque sinum sanguis*, *Aeneid* 10.817–819; on 20.471; see [[virgil]]).
