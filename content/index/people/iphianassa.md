**The name.** Ἰφιάνασσα joins ἶφι (“with might”) and ἄνασσα (“queen, lady”): “mighty queen”. Iphigenia (Ἰφιγένεια) begins with the same word.

**In the Iliad.** She is named last of the three daughters in Agamemnon's offer and in Odysseus' repetition of it (9.145, 9.287; see [[chrysothemis]], [[laodice-daughter-of-agamemnon]] and [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). All three are at home in Agamemnon's hall, and any of them may be Achilles' bride (9.144–148). Homer never names Iphigenia and never tells of a sacrifice at Aulis; the only offerings he sets there are the hecatombs beside the spring under the plane tree (2.305–307; see [[aulis]]). Whether the poet knew the story and left it out, or the story was not yet told, cannot be decided.

## Outside Homer

The *Cypria*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, told how Agamemnon offended Artemis at Aulis and Iphigenia was brought to be sacrificed, and how the goddess snatched her away and put a deer on the altar (see [[aulis]] and [[calchas-evil-prophecies]]). Later writers took different views of Homer's Iphianassa. Lucretius uses her name for the girl sacrificed at Aulis, whose blood “the chosen leaders of the Danaans” shed on the altar of Artemis (*On the Nature of Things* 1.84–86). Sophocles keeps the two apart: his chorus names Chrysothemis and Iphianassa as Electra's sisters still living in the house (*Electra* 157), while the play tells of the sister whom Agamemnon sacrificed (see [[sophocles]]). In Euripides' *Orestes* the three daughters are Chrysothemis, Iphigenia and Electra (22–23; see [[euripides]]).

Another Iphianassa is a daughter of Proetus of Tiryns, one of the sisters who went mad and were healed by Melampus (Apollodorus, *Library* 2.2.2; see [[proetus]]).
