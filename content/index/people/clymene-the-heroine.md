## The name

κλύμενος is an old participle of κλύω, “hear”: “heard of, famous”. It is a common name in myth. The Iliad gives it to the ox-eyed handmaid who walks with Aethra behind Helen to the Scaean gates (3.144; see [[clymene]]) and to a Nereid (18.47; see [[clymene-the-nereid]]); nothing in the poems connects them with this Clymene. W. W. Merry notes that the name is common in Minyan legend, where there is a Clymene, daughter of a Minyas and mother of Atalanta, an Eteoclymene and a Periclymene in the same family, and a Clymenus, king of the Minyae (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.326).

## In Homer

She is named once, in the last verse of the catalogue of heroines: “And I saw Maera and Clymene and hateful Eriphyle” (*Odyssey* 11.326; see [[maera-the-heroine]] and [[eriphyle]]).

## Outside Homer

The scholion on this verse quotes the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* for her: Clymene, daughter of Minyas, son of Poseidon, and of Euryanassa, daughter of Hyperphas, married Phylacus, son of Deion, and bore Iphiclus, the swift-footed boy, who could race the winds and run over the ears of grain; in Hesiod's verses he ran over the topmost fruit of the stalks without breaking them, on the tips of the wheat, and did not harm the crop (fr. 84 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]], [[phylacus]] and [[iphiclus]]). Merry gives the same family: she was the daughter of Minyas, the wife of Phylacus and the mother of Iphiclus (on *Odyssey* 11.326). If she is that Clymene, she is the mother of the Iphiclus of Phylace whose cattle the seer had to drive off to win Pero (*Odyssey* 11.289–297), and the grandmother of Protesilaus and Podarces, the sons of “Iphiclus, rich in flocks, the son of Phylacus” (2.704–706; see [[protesilaus]] and [[podarces]]).
