**The gift.** Dolops, son of Lampus, stabs through the middle of Meges' shield, and the corselet stops the spear (15.525–529; see [[meges]], [[dolops-son-of-lampus]] and [[corselet]]). The poet gives its history in five verses. Phyleus brought it home from Ephyra, “for his guest-friend, Euphetes, lord of men, had given it to him / to wear into war, a defense against enemy men” (15.532–533; see [[phyleus]]). Euphetes is named only here. ξεῖνος, “guest-friend”, makes the corselet a guest-gift, given by a host to his guest and kept in the guest's house (see [[xenia]] and [[guest-gifts]]), as Cinyras of Cyprus gave Agamemnon his corselet (11.19–23; see [[cinyras]]). The last verse closes the story: ὅς οἱ καὶ τότε παιδὸς ἀπὸ χροὸς ἤρκεσʼ ὄλεθρον, “and then too it kept destruction from his son's body” (15.534). καὶ τότε, “then too”, lets the listener infer that it had saved the father before. The gift of one generation keeps the next alive. Armor in the Iliad carries such histories: the boar's-tusk helmet passed from hand to hand, by theft and by gift, before it came to Odysseus (10.261–271; see [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]]).

ἄναξ ἀνδρῶν, “lord of men”, is Agamemnon's title. The Iliad gives it to only a few others: Anchises, Aeneas, Augeas, Euphetes and, at the funeral games, Eumelus (5.268, 5.311, 11.701, 15.532, 23.288; see [[lord-of-men]]).

**Ephyra.** The half-verse of the place, ἐξ Ἐφύρης ποταμοῦ ἄπο Σελλήεντος, “from Ephyra, from the river Selleis” (15.531), is the one in which Heracles brought Astyocheia, the mother of Tlepolemus, from her home (2.659; see [[ephyra]], [[selleis-ephyra]], [[astyocheia]] and [[heracles]]). Where it lay the poem does not say.

## Outside Homer

Strabo puts this Ephyra in Elis, on the Selleis that flows from Pholoe, and takes from it both Tlepolemus' mother and Meges' corselet (*Geography* 8.3.5; see [[strabo]]). If he is right, Phyleus, an Elean prince, had his guest-friend in his own country.
