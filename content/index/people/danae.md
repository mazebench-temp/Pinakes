## In Homer

Homer names her once, in the list of loves that Zeus recites to Hera on Gargarus (see [[the-loves-of-zeus]]). Δανάης καλλισφύρου Ἀκρισιώνης, “Danae of the lovely ankles, daughter of Acrisius” (14.319; see [[of-the-lovely-ankles]] and [[acrisius]]). Ἀκρισιώνη is a feminine patronymic, like Εὐηνίνη, “daughter of Evenus”, and Ἀδρηστίνη, “daughter of Adrastus” (9.557, 5.412; Leaf on 14.319). The verse is built like the one that names Marpessa, Μαρπήσσης καλλισφύρου Εὐηνίνης, “Marpessa of the lovely ankles, daughter of Evenus” (9.557; see [[marpessa]]): the woman's name, the epithet of her ankles, and her father's name made into hers at the end of the line. Her son is “Perseus, preeminent among all men” (14.320; see [[perseus]]). Homer says nothing of how Zeus came to her, or of what became of her and her son.

## Outside Homer

**The shower of gold.** On the shield of Heracles in the Hesiodic *Shield*, Perseus is “the son of lovely-haired Danae, the horseman Perseus” (ἠυκόμου Δανάης τέκος, *Shield* 216). Pindar calls him “the son of Danae, who, we say, was born of gold that flowed of its own accord” (*Pythian* 12.17–18). Apollodorus gives the whole story. Her mother was Eurydice, daughter of Lacedaemon (*Library* 2.2.2). When Acrisius asked an oracle how he might have male children, the god said that his daughter would bear a son who would kill him. He built a bronze chamber under the ground and kept her there, but Zeus, “changed into gold”, poured through the roof into her lap; others said that Proetus, Acrisius' brother, had seduced her. Acrisius would not believe that the child was Zeus', and he put mother and son in a chest and cast it into the sea. It came ashore on Seriphos, where Dictys took up the boy and raised him (*Library* 2.4.1; see [[proetus]]).

**Seriphos.** Polydectes, the king of Seriphos and Dictys' brother, fell in love with Danae but could not come near her while her son was at her side, and by a trick he sent Perseus to fetch the Gorgon's head. When Perseus came back he found his mother and Dictys at the altars, where they had fled from Polydectes' violence, and he turned the king and his friends to stone (*Library* 2.4.2–3; see [[gorgon]] and [[perseus-and-the-graeae]]). Pindar speaks of “the long slavery” of Perseus' mother and of the bed that was forced on her, which her son repaid when he cut off Medusa's head (*Pythian* 12.14–16). Perseus then took his mother and Andromeda to Argos (*Library* 2.4.4).

**Danae in Herodotus.** Herodotus says that the Greeks rightly trace the Dorian kings back to “Perseus, son of Danae”, but no further, since Perseus had no mortal father to be named after, as Heracles had Amphitryon; from “Danae, daughter of Acrisius” upward, the kings' ancestors would turn out to be Egyptians (6.53.1–2; see [[herodotus]]).

Sophocles and Euripides wrote plays on her story (Frazer on Apollodorus, *Library* 2.4.1); fragments of Euripides' *Danae* survive.
