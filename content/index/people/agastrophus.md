**His blindness.** ἀάσατο δὲ μέγα θυμῷ, “he was greatly blinded in his heart” (11.340), is the verb of ἄτη, the folly that blinds (see [[ate]]). The narrator names the mistake: his attendant held the horses “some way off”, and he went on foot among the champions, so that when he was wounded there was no chariot to carry him away (11.340–342; see [[chariot]]). Other men are saved by their drivers in this book: Agamemnon, Diomedes and Nestor all leave the battle in chariots, and Odysseus is led out by the hand “until his attendant drove the horses near” (11.273–283, 11.399–400, 11.488, 11.517–520).

**His armor.** After Hector has escaped, Diomedes turns back to strip “the son of Paeon, famed for his spear” (11.368; see [[famed-for-his-spear]]). He is taking the gleaming corselet from Agastrophus' chest, the shield from his shoulders and the heavy helmet when Paris, leaning on a pillar of the tomb of Ilus, shoots him through the foot (11.369–378; see [[diomedes]], [[paris]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]). The spoils of Agastrophus cost Diomedes the battle.

Παιονίδης, “son of Paeon”, names a father otherwise unknown. The patronymic recalls the Paeonians, Troy's allies from the Axius (2.848–850; see [[paeonians]]), but the poem does not say that he was one of them. He is named nowhere else.
