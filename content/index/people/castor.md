Helen has named the Achaean leaders for Priam. Two men she looks for and cannot find:

{{quote:70e03702-ee22-558f-a91f-bbf93bc38989}}

She guesses that they stayed at home, or that they came and keep out of the fighting for shame at her disgrace. The narrator tells what she cannot know: they are dead, and buried in their own country. It is the only time the Iliad names them.

## Family

αὐτοκασιγνήτω (“my own brothers”, a dual) and μία … μήτηρ (“one mother”): Castor and [[Polydeuces]] are Helen's brothers by the same mother. The Iliad does not name her. The Odyssey does: Leda, the wife of Tyndareus, bore the two brothers to Tyndareus (*Odyssey* 11.298–300; see [[leda]] and [[tyndareus]]). Homer calls Helen a daughter of Zeus (3.199, 3.418), but never her brothers.

## Descriptions

ἱππόδαμος = ἵππος (“horse”) + δαμάζω (“tame”): “tamer of horses”, the epithet of the Trojans and of heroes who are horsemen (see [[tamer-of-horses]]). It makes Castor the horseman of the pair, as πὺξ ἀγαθός (“good with his fists”) makes Polydeuces the boxer. The Odyssey names the brothers in the same line (*Odyssey* 11.300 = 3.237). W. W. Merry derives Κάστωρ from the same root as Ἐπικάστη, Epicaste (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.300). The nominative Κάστωρ is found in Homer only once, and not of him: Castor, son of Hylax, is the rich Cretan whom the disguised Odysseus names as his father in a false tale (*Odyssey* 14.204; see [[castor-son-of-hylax]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]).

## Mythology

In the Iliad the brothers are simply dead, and the poem knows nothing of a life for them after death. κάτεχεν (“held fast”) is the verb the Catalogue used of the dead Protesilaus, whom “the black earth held him fast” (2.699), and the earth that holds them is φυσίζοος, “life-giving” (see [[life-giving-earth]]). The Odyssey repeats the line with a difference: the earth holds them fast “alive”, and they live and are dead on alternate days, with honor from Zeus like the gods' (*Odyssey* 11.301–304; see [[immortality]]). Merry thought the two accounts could be reconciled only by straining the words (on *Odyssey* 11.301).

## Outside Homer

The *Cypria*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, told how they died. While Paris was taking Helen to Troy, Castor and Polydeuces were caught stealing the cattle of Idas and Lynceus. Idas killed Castor, Polydeuces killed Lynceus and Idas, and Zeus gave the brothers immortality on alternate days. In that account they died after Helen had left Sparta. See [[the-death-of-the-dioscuri]].

Pindar makes Castor the mortal son of Tyndareus and Polydeuces the son of Zeus. When Idas kills Castor, Polydeuces gives up living forever on Olympus alone and shares his immortality with his brother: they spend one day with Zeus and the next under the earth at Therapne (*Nemean* 10.55–90; see [[polydeuces]]). The Homeric Hymns to the Dioscuri (17 and 33) call both of them sons of Zeus and Leda, born under Mount Taygetus, and the saviors of sailors in storms, and the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, as a scholiast on Pindar reports it, made both sons of Zeus (fr. 66 Evelyn-White; see [[leda]]). Their joint name, Διόσκουροι (“sons of Zeus”), is not Homeric.

John Ruskin took the lines on their death as the model of a poet who grieves without letting grief distort what he sees: “though Castor and Pollux be dead, yet the earth is our mother still” (*Modern Painters* III, 1856, ch. 12, “Of the Pathetic Fallacy”; see [[ruskin-life-giving-earth]]).
