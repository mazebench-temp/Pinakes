## Etymology

Ἕλενος (Helenos). The origin of the name is unknown. It looks like a masculine form of Helen's name, Ἑλένη, but no connection between the two is known.

## Family

{{quote:209819cb-3964-55cb-9930-b892569315c8}}

Πριαμίδης, “son of Priam” (6.76; see [[priam]]); in Book 7 he is Πριάμοιο φίλος παῖς, “the dear son of Priam” (7.44). He tells Hector to go to “your mother and mine” (μητέρι σῇ καὶ ἐμῇ, 6.87), so the two are sons of the same mother, [[Hecuba]], and he reminds Hector, κασίγνητος δέ τοί εἰμι, “I am your brother” (7.48; see [[kasignetos]]). With Deiphobus, “two sons of Priam”, he leads a company in the attack on the Achaean wall (12.94–95), and in the last book he is the first of the nine surviving sons whom Priam names in his anger (24.249).

## Descriptions

οἰωνοπόλων ὄχʼ ἄριστος, “by far the best of the bird-seers” (6.76). The half-line introduced Calchas on the Achaean side (1.69; see [[best-of-bird-seers]] and [[calchas]]): each army has its best reader of birds, and the poem gives both the same words. Helenus is also a fighter, who kills with the sword and shoots with the bow (13.576–600). The narrator names him as heroes are named, by their strength: βίη Ἑλένοιο ἄνακτος, “mighty lord Helenus” (13.758, 13.770, 13.781). See [[divination]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

**The archer.** In Book 13 he kills the Achaean Deipyrus with a great Thracian sword and shoots Menelaus in the breastplate, but the arrow glances off like beans from a winnowing shovel. Menelaus drives his spear through the hand that holds the bow, and Agenor binds the wound with a sling of wool (13.576–600; see [[agenor]]).

**A son left alive.** In the last book Priam, setting out to ransom Hector, rages at his nine remaining sons, Helenus first among them: “liars and dancers”, while the best are dead (24.249–262). Helenus does not appear in the Odyssey.

## Outside Homer

Proclus' summary of the *Cypria* says that when Paris built his ships for Sparta, Helenus prophesied to them what would come of it, and Cassandra too foretold the future (see [[proclus]]). In the *Little Iliad* Odysseus lies in ambush and captures Helenus, and on his prophecy about the taking of Troy Diomedes brings Philoctetes back from Lemnos (Proclus). In Sophocles' *Philoctetes* this is the prophecy that sends Odysseus and Neoptolemus to Lemnos: Troy will not fall until Philoctetes comes with the bow of Heracles (604–613; see [[philoctetes]]). Apollodorus adds that after Paris' death Helenus and Deiphobus quarreled over Helen; when Deiphobus won her, Helenus withdrew to Ida, and Odysseus captured him there and learned the conditions of Troy's fall: the bones of Pelops, the coming of Neoptolemus, and the theft of the Palladium (*Epitome* 5.9–10; see [[apollodorus]]).

He survived the sack. At the end of Euripides' *Andromache*, Thetis orders that Andromache go to the land of the Molossians and marry Helenus (1243–1245). Virgil's Aeneas finds him ruling a Greek city in Epirus, with Andromache as his wife, and Helenus foretells the course of Aeneas' voyage to Italy (*Aeneid* 3.294–505; the prophecy is 3.374–462; see [[virgil]]).
