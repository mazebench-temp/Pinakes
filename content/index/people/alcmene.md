## In Homer

**Zeus' list.** “Nor Alcmene in Thebes, / who bore Heracles, a fierce-hearted son” (14.323–324): she shares a verse with Semele, the other Theban woman whom Zeus loved, and her son is named before Semele's (see [[semele]], [[heracles]], [[thebes]] and [[the-loves-of-zeus]]).

**The day of the birth.** In Book 19 Agamemnon tells how even Zeus was deceived by Ate. On the day when Alcmene was to bear Heracles in Thebes, Zeus swore that a man of his blood born that day would rule all who dwell around him, and Hera brought Eurystheus to an early birth and stopped Alcmene's labor, holding back the goddesses of childbirth, so that Eurystheus, not Heracles, became lord of the Argives (19.95–124; see [[the-birth-of-heracles]], [[eurystheus]], [[eileithyia]] and [[ate]]). Thebes is ἐϋστέφανος there, “well-crowned” (19.99; see [[well-crowned]]), the only city in Homer with the epithet; Walter Leaf takes it as “well crowned with walls” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.99).

**The Odyssey.** Antinous says that Penelope's cunning surpasses that of the famous women of old, “Tyro and Alcmene and fair-crowned Mycene” (*Odyssey* 2.120). In the underworld Odysseus sees “Alcmene, the wife of Amphitryon, who bore Heracles, bold in endurance and lion-hearted, after she had lain in the arms of great Zeus” (*Odyssey* 11.266–268; see [[amphitryon]]).

## Outside Homer

**The Shield.** The Hesiodic *Shield* begins with her. Alcmene, “daughter of Electryon, the rouser of armies”, left her home and her country and came to Thebes with Amphitryon, who had killed her father in a quarrel over cattle; she surpassed all women in beauty and stature, and in mind. Amphitryon could not go to her bed until he had avenged her brothers' death on the Taphians and Teleboans. On the night of his return Zeus, who meant to beget a defender for gods and men, came down from Olympus and lay with her, and Amphitryon came home the same night; in seven-gated Thebes she bore twins, Heracles to Zeus and Iphicles to Amphitryon (*Shield* 1–56). The *Theogony* calls Heracles “the valiant son of fair-ankled Alcmene” (526, 950) and counts her among the women who bore children to Zeus (943–944; see [[hesiod]]), and the *Homeric Hymn to Heracles* says that she bore him “in Thebes of the lovely dancing places” (*Hymn* 15.1–3).

**The long night.** In [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] she is the daughter of Electryon, a son of Perseus, so that Danae was her great-grandmother. Her father betrothed her to Amphitryon, who killed Electryon by accident and took her to Thebes, where she promised to marry him when he had avenged her brothers. Before he came back from the war, Zeus came to her by night, made the one night three times as long, and lay with her in Amphitryon's likeness. She bore Heracles to Zeus and Iphicles, a night younger, to Amphitryon (*Library* 2.4.5–8; see [[danae]]). [[pindar|Pindar]] makes Thebes receive “the greatest of the gods”, snowing gold at midnight, when he stood in Amphitryon's doorway and went in to his wife to beget Heracles (*Isthmian* 7.5–7). Plautus put the story on the comic stage: in the *Amphitruo* Jupiter lies with Alcumena in her husband's shape, and the night is made longer for his pleasure (*Amphitruo* 97–115).

**After Amphitryon.** When Amphitryon fell fighting for Thebes, Rhadamanthys, son of Zeus, married Alcmene and lived with her in exile at Ocaleae in Boeotia (*Library* 2.4.11, 3.1.2; see [[rhadamanthus]]). Herodotus held that both of Heracles' parents, Amphitryon and Alcmene, were of Egyptian descent, and he dated Heracles, son of Alcmene, about nine hundred years before his own time (2.43.2, 2.145.4; see [[herodotus]]).
