δῖα, “brilliant” (see [[brilliant]]), is a standing honorific and says nothing of her character. ἐπεμήνατο, “was mad with longing”, occurs only here in Homer; it is the verb μαίνομαι, “rage, be mad”, with ἐπί, “for”. Her lie is the only direct speech inside Glaucus' story, and it leaves her husband no choice: “Either die, Proetus, or kill Bellerophon” (6.164). Proetus believes her, but will not do the killing himself (see [[proetus]] and [[bellerophon]]).

Homer calls the king of Lycia Proetus' “father-in-law” (6.170), so Anteia was presumably the king's daughter, and the princess whom Bellerophon married her sister (6.192). Nothing is said of her after the accusation.

## Outside Homer

Apollodorus calls her the daughter of the Lycian king Iobates and says that the tragedians named her Stheneboea (*Library* 2.2.1). Euripides' lost *Stheneboea* made her the heroine of a tragedy of illicit love; an ancient summary says that Bellerophon came back and punished her with a fall from Pegasus. In Aristophanes' *Frogs* Aeschylus boasts that he never made whores of Phaedras and Stheneboeas, as Euripides did (1043). The story of a married woman who is refused by a young man and accuses him is told also of Phaedra and Hippolytus, and of Potiphar's wife and Joseph (Genesis 39.7–20).
