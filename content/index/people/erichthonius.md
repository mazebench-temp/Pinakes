## Etymology

Ἐριχθόνιος joins the strengthening prefix ἐρι-, “very”, to χθόνιος, “of the earth” (χθών). The name suits the earth-born king of Athens of later legend; of the Trojan's birth Homer says only that Dardanus fathered him (20.219).

## Family

{{quote:63a3c9c2-c89b-53c0-964e-c719df811bd5}}

He is the second link in the line that Aeneas recites to Achilles, between [[Dardanus]], the son of Zeus who founded [[Dardania]], and [[Tros]], “lord of the Trojans”, whom he fathered (20.215–230; see [[genealogy-in-battle]]). Through Tros he is the ancestor of both branches of the royal house: Priam is his great-great-grandson through Ilus and Laomedon, and Anchises through Assaracus and Capys (20.231–240; see [[priam]], [[anchises]], [[assaracus]] and [[capys]]). Homer names no wife of his.

## Descriptions

βασιλεύς, “king” (20.219): he is the only one in the genealogy to be given the title, as Tros is the only one called “lord of the Trojans” (Τρώεσσιν ἄνακτα, 20.230; see [[basileus]] and [[anax]]).

ἀφνειότατος θνητῶν ἀνθρώπων, “the richest of mortal men” (20.220): the superlative of ἀφνειός, “wealthy”, found only here in Homer. The verse is built like the one that praises his grandson Ganymede: ὃς δὴ ἀφνειότατος γένετο θνητῶν ἀνθρώπων, “who was the richest of mortal men”, and ὃς δὴ κάλλιστος γένετο θνητῶν ἀνθρώπων, “who was the most beautiful of mortal men” (20.220, 20.233; see [[ganymede]]). His wealth is counted in horses: three thousand mares with their foals, grazing in the marshland, which the North Wind loved (20.221–229; see [[the-mares-of-erichthonius]] and [[horses]]).

## Outside Homer

In [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] Dardanus has two sons, Ilus, who dies childless, and Erichthonius, who succeeds to the kingdom, marries Astyoche, daughter of the river Simoeis, and fathers Tros (*Library* 3.12.2; see [[simoeis]]).

The name belongs above all to the earth-born king of Athens. In Euripides' *Ion* he is “earth-born Erichthonius”, the ancestor of Creusa's house, whom Athena lifted from the earth and gave to the daughters of Cecrops to keep unseen in a chest, which they opened (*Ion* 20–26, 267–274; see [[erechtheus]] and [[athena-and-erechtheus]]). [[Strabo]] reports that later writers, who said that a Teucer came to the Troad from the Attic deme of the Troes, now called Xypete, took an Erichthonius among the founders of both peoples as a sign of a bond between the Trojans and the Athenians (*Geography* 13.1.48; see [[smintheus-and-the-mice]]). Walter Leaf finds the Attic hero startling in a Trojan genealogy, and reports Fick's suggestion that the passage was made at Athens about 610 BC, when the Athenians were trying to gain a footing at Sigeum; Leaf himself thinks it conceivable that the whole genealogy is of a piece (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.219).
