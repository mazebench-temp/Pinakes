## In Homer

Homer names her once, in the last verse of the catalogue of heroines in the land of the dead, with an epithet and a crime: she is στυγερή, “hateful”, and she “took precious gold as the price of her own husband” (*Odyssey* 11.326–327; see [[hateful]] and [[amphiaraus]]). The Odyssey tells the husband's end in a later book, in the pedigree of the seer [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]]: Amphiaraus, whom Zeus and Apollo loved, did not reach the threshold of old age, but perished at Thebes “for the sake of gifts to a woman”, γυναίων εἵνεκα δώρων, and his sons were Alcmaeon and Amphilochus (*Odyssey* 15.244–248; see [[the-seven-against-thebes]], [[alcmaeon]] and [[amphilochus]]). The same words are said in this book of the Ceteians who died with Eurypylus (*Odyssey* 11.521).

## Outside Homer

The necklace was Harmonia's, given her by Polynices (see [[the-seven-against-thebes]] and [[polynices]]). W. W. Merry tells the story: Amphiaraus of Argos, knowing that the expedition against Thebes would be fatal to him, wished to take no part in it, but was overpersuaded by his wife, whom Polynices had bribed with the gift of a golden necklace; when he was about to be killed in the war, Zeus split the ground with his thunderbolt and swallowed him with his chariot and horses. Merry adds that the whole story was told in one of the poems of the Epic Cycle, the *Expulsion of Amphiaraus* (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.326). Her son Alcmaeon killed her for it (see [[alcmaeon]] and [[alcmaeon-kills-eriphyle]]).

Aristotle: “The stories that have been handed down cannot be undone, I mean such as the death of Clytemnestra at the hands of Orestes, and of Eriphyle at the hands of Alcmaeon” (*Poetics* 1453b22–24; see [[alcmaeon-kills-eriphyle]]). Plato's Socrates asks whether a man who enslaves the best part of himself to the worst for gold does not take a bribe “for a more terrible destruction than Eriphyle, when she took the necklace for her husband's life” (*Republic* 590a; see [[plato]]). In Virgil's Fields of Mourning, among the women whom cruel love has wasted, Aeneas sees “sorrowful Eriphyle, showing the wounds of her cruel son”, *maestamque Eriphylen / crudelis nati monstrantem vulnera* (*Aeneid* 6.445–446; see [[virgil]], [[phaedra]] and [[procris]]).
