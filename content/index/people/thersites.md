## Etymology

Θερσίτης (Thersitēs), from θέρσος, the Aeolic form of θάρσος (“boldness, daring”): “the Bold One”. In a man of his standing the boldness is impudence. The same stem appears in the heroic name Θερσίλοχος (Thersilochus), “bold in the ambush”.

## Descriptions

{{quote:88ad2396-b31f-5d7f-9d58-56787e854433}}

The narrator describes his speech before he lets him speak. He is ἀμετροεπής (“of unmeasured words”; see [[of-unmeasured-words]]), his words are ἄκοσμα (“disorderly”), he quarrels οὐ κατὰ κόσμον (“against all order”), and he says whatever will make the Argives laugh. [[Odysseus]] calls him ἀκριτόμυθος (“reckless in speech”; see [[reckless-in-speech]]) but also λιγὺς ἀγορητής (“a clear-voiced orator”, 2.246), the praise given to [[Nestor]] in Book 1 (1.248; see [[clear-voiced-orator]]). The army calls him λωβητὴρ ἐπεσβόλος (“a word-slinging slanderer”, 2.275).

{{quote:84632ffd-0ab5-5371-b980-4a4b1dabcf30}}

αἴσχιστος (“ugliest”) is the superlative of αἰσχρός (“ugly, shameful”; see [[aischros]]). The Iliad seldom describes a man's body; this portrait lists its defects one by one. φολκός (“bandy-legged”, or perhaps “squint-eyed”) and φοξός (“pointed”, of the head) occur only here, and the sense of φολκός is uncertain. The portrait makes his body match his standing: he is lame like [[Hephaestus]], hunched, with a scant stubble on a pointed head. In the same book the handsomest of the Achaeans after Achilles, Nireus, is a weakling with few men behind him (2.673–675; see [[nireus]]).

{{quote:8421a324-0b8f-50ee-88c2-425288f33fe7}}

His usual targets are [[Achilles]] and [[Odysseus]]. In Book 2 he turns on [[Agamemnon]] instead, in words borrowed from Achilles.

## Outside Homer

The *Aethiopis* told of his death. In [[proclus|Proclus]]' summary, Achilles kills the Amazon Penthesilea, and Thersites mocks him for his supposed love for her; Achilles kills him, the Achaeans quarrel over the killing, and Achilles sails to Lesbos, where Odysseus purifies him of the blood. Quintus of Smyrna tells the same story at the end of the first book of his *Posthomerica*. A later genealogy made him a son of Agrius, of the royal house of Calydon, and so a cousin of [[Diomedes]] (Apollodorus, *Library* 1.8.6).

In the myth of Er, the soul of Thersites the buffoon chooses to be reborn as an ape (Plato, *Republic* 10, 620c). See [[plato-thersites]]. He is the railing, scurrilous commentator of Shakespeare's *Troilus and Cressida*. See [[shakespeare-thersites]].

Modern readers disagree about what the scene means. Some take Thersites as the one voice the poem gives to the common soldier, raised only to be silenced. Others point out that he speaks like a king, echoes Achilles, and names no grievance of the ranks. Gregory Nagy read him as the embodiment of blame poetry, the opposite of the epic of praise (*The Best of the Achaeans*, 1979).
