She is named once in Homer, by a goddess, in a threat. Dione tells Aphrodite that the man who fights the immortals does not live long, and that “his children do not call him papa at his knees” (5.408; παππάζουσιν occurs only here in Homer). Then she names his wife, Aegialeia, who will wake the house with her wailing, “longing for her wedded husband, the best of the Achaeans” (5.414). She is περίφρων, “wise” (5.412), the Odyssey's standing epithet for Penelope. The threat is not fulfilled in Homer: the Odyssey brings Diomedes home to Argos (Od. 3.180–182). See [[husbands-and-wives]].

Ἀδρηστίνη is taken as “daughter of Adrastus”. Tydeus too married one of the daughters of Adrastus (14.121), so father and son took their wives from the same house. See [[adrastus]] and [[diomedes]].

## Outside Homer

Apollodorus lists Aegialeia among the daughters of Adrastus (*Library* 1.9.13). In the stories of the returns, Nauplius, avenging his son Palamedes, led the wives of the Greek leaders into adultery, and Aegialeia took Cometes, son of Sthenelus, as her lover (Apollodorus, *Epitome* 6.9). Virgil's exiled Diomedes complains that the gods begrudged him the wife he longed for (*Aeneid* 11.269–270).
