τῇσιν δʼ ἀμφοτέρῃσι πατὴρ ἐμὸς ἐμβασίλευε, / Κτήσιος Ὀρμενίδης, ἐπιείκελος ἀθανάτοισιν, “and over both of them my father was king, / Ctesius, son of Ormenus, like the immortals” (*Odyssey* 15.413–414): the king of Syrie, the island “above Ortygia” where the swineherd Eumaeus was born (*Odyssey* 15.403–412; see [[syrie]], [[eumaeus]] and [[ormenus-father-of-ctesius]]). His son, a slave in Ithaca, names him to the disguised Odysseus when he tells his own story: a Phoenician woman, a slave in his father's house, carried the boy off to a Phoenician ship, and the traders sold him to Laertes (*Odyssey* 15.415–484; see [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]], [[the-phoenician-nurse]] and [[laertes]]). The poem does not name his wife, “my lady mother” (*Odyssey* 15.385, 15.461).

## The name

Κτήσιος is formed from κτῆσις, “possessions”, whose genitive κτήσιος has the same letters (*Iliad* 15.663; *Odyssey* 19.534): a name for a rich king. It was also a title of Zeus as the guardian of household property, Ζεὺς Κτήσιος, whose image, the Suda says, people set up even in their storerooms, “as giver of wealth” (Suda ζ 40; κ 2522). In Aeschylus the king of Argos speaks of goods that may come again “by the grace of Zeus Ktesios” (*Suppliants* 445), and Clytemnestra bids Cassandra take her place among the slaves “near the altar of Ktesios” (*Agamemnon* 1038; see [[aeschylus]]).

## Descriptions

- ἐπιείκελος ἀθανάτοισιν, “like the immortals” (*Odyssey* 15.414; see [[like-the-immortals]]): a verse of name, patronymic and epithet, as the Iliad names Theseus, son of Aegeus, and Maeon, son of Haemon (*Iliad* 1.265, 4.394), and the Odyssey Iphitus, son of Eurytus (*Odyssey* 21.37).
