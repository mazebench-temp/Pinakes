## Etymology

The name has no clear Greek etymology. It recalls κινύρα, a stringed instrument (“a musical instrument, or a kithara”, Suda κ 1650), whose name is usually taken from a Semitic word for the lyre (Hebrew *kinnōr*), and the adjective κινυρός, “plaintive”, said of a cow lowing over her first calf (17.5). Later tradition made Cinyras a favorite of Apollo, which suits the lyre.

## In Homer

{{quote:3d3b6923-e62c-50cf-8b4b-b709a03a28ed}}

Homer says no more of him than these verses. The report of the expedition reached him in Cyprus, and he gave Agamemnon the corselet “to be a guest-gift” (ξεινήϊον εἶναι, 11.20), “to do the king a favor” (11.23; see [[guest-gifts]] and [[xenia]]). The poem does not call him a king, or say where in Cyprus he lived (see [[cyprus]]). The same κλέος, the “report” of the Achaeans' coming, moved men on the other side: Iphidamas left his bride in Thrace to fight them “at the report of the Achaeans” (11.227; see [[iphidamas]] and [[kleos]]).

The gift is the corselet that the king puts on second in the longest arming scene of the poem (see [[corselet]], [[arming-scene]] and [[agamemnon]]). Few things in the Iliad come with a history of this kind. Meges' corselet is another gift of guest-friendship, which his father once brought home from Ephyra (15.529–533; see [[meges]] and [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]]).

## Outside Homer

Strabo quotes the two verses, reading Κύπρονδε, “to Cyprus”, as an example of the gifts that kings and dynasts gave the sons of Atreus for their fame (1.2.32; see [[strabo]]). Apollodorus adds a broken promise: Menelaus, Odysseus and Talthybius went to Cinyras in Cyprus to win him as an ally; he gave the absent Agamemnon corselets and swore to send fifty ships, but sent one, under the son of Mygdalion, and molded the rest out of earth and launched them on the sea (*Epitome* 3.9; see [[apollodorus]]).

For Pindar he is the man “whom golden-haired Apollo loved”, the cherished priest of Aphrodite, whom the songs of the Cyprians often celebrate (*Pythian* 2.15–17), and his wealth was a byword: prosperity planted with a god's help, Pindar says, once weighed him down with riches in Cyprus by the sea (*Nemean* 8.17–18; see [[pindar]]). Plato's Athenian, reworking an elegy of Tyrtaeus, says that a man richer “than Cinyras and Midas” is wretched if he is unjust (*Laws* 2.660e; see [[plato]]). The Suda records a proverb about long life, “to grow older than Tithonus, richer than Cinyras, more luxurious than Sardanapalus”, and calls him a king of the Cyprians, outstanding in wealth (κ 497; see [[tithonus]]).

Apollodorus makes him the son of Sandocus and Pharnace: he came to Cyprus with a band of followers, founded Paphos, and married Metharme, daughter of Pygmalion, king of Cyprus, and their sons were Oxyporus and Adonis (3.14.3). In Ovid he is the son of Paphos, the child of Pygmalion and his statue, and his daughter Myrrha bears him Adonis (*Metamorphoses* 10.297–518; see [[ovid]]). Tacitus reports a more recent tradition that Cinyras consecrated the temple of Venus at Paphos, where in his day only a priest of the Cinyrad line was consulted (*Histories* 2.3). Strabo calls Byblos, in Phoenicia, the royal seat of Cinyras (16.2.18).
