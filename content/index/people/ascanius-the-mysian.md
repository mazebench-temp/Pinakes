Hector and Paris go where the fighting is thickest, around Cebriones and Polydamas and the men named with them (see [[hector]], [[paris]], [[cebriones]] and [[polydamas]]). The last three, Palmys, Ascanius and Morys, son of Hippotion, had come from Ascania ἀμοιβοί, “as relief”, a word found only here: fresh men in exchange for others (see the translation's note on 13.792–794 and [[ascania]]). They arrived “on the morning before”, the morning of the day of Book 8, and Zeus has now roused them to fight (13.793–794; see [[palmys]], [[morys]] and [[trojan-allies]]). Whether the relative clause takes in the three named before them, Phalces, Orthaeus and Polyphetes, is not clear (see [[phalces]]).

**Two men of the name.** An Ascanius already leads the Phrygians “from far-off Ascania” in the Trojan Catalogue (2.862–863; see [[ascanius]] and [[phrygians]]). Homer does not say whether this is the same man, newly come with fresh troops, or another. [[Strabo]] takes him as another. The land around the Ascanian lake, he says, had the lake's name, and part of it was Phrygian and part Mysian, the Phrygian part farther from Troy. So the Catalogue's “far-off Ascania” is the Phrygian one, and there is a nearer Mysian Ascania by the present Nicaea, which the poet means here. Strabo quotes the verses with a line that is not in our text after Morys' name, Μυσῶν ἀγχεμάχων ἡγήτορα, “leader of the Mysians who fight hand to hand”, with the epithet the Mysians have at the start of the book (13.5), and concludes that it is no wonder if the poet names a leader of the Phrygians Ascanius, come from Ascania, and a leader of the Mysians Ascanius, come from Ascania, for homonyms taken from rivers, lakes and places are common in him (*Geography* 12.4.5; see [[mysians]]).
