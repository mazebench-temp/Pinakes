summary: A name that Aristotle, beside Socrates, gives to any individual man; the most famous Callias of the time was Callias son of Hipponicus, the richest man in Athens and the host of the sophists in Plato's Protagoras.
---

In Aristotle's examples Callias and Socrates stand for particular men, as distinct from kinds: experience knows that this remedy helped Callias, sick with this disease, and Socrates, and many others one by one (*Metaphysics* A 1, 981a7–9). The name was a natural choice. Callias son of Hipponicus, of the family that held the office of torchbearer at the Mysteries of Eleusis, spent his inheritance on the sophists, and Plato sets the *Protagoras* in his house; in the *Rhetoric*'s third book Iphicrates calls him a begging priest rather than a torchbearer (*Rhetoric* 3.2, 1405a19–21; see [[callias-son-of-hipponicus]]).
