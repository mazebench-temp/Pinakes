ἔνθʼ ὅ γέ μʼ ἠνώγει πέμψαι βασιλῆϊ Ἀκάστῳ, “There he bade them take me to King Acastus” (*Odyssey* 14.336): in the false tale that Odysseus tells Eumaeus, Pheidon, king of the Thesprotians, puts the shipwrecked Cretan on a Thesprotian ship bound for Dulichium and bids the crew take him to its king, but the sailors plot to sell him as a slave (*Odyssey* 14.334–340; see [[pheidon]], [[thesprotians]], [[dulichium]] and [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]]). Homer names him nowhere else, and gives Dulichium other lords: in the Catalogue its men are led by Meges, son of Phyleus (*Iliad* 2.625–630; see [[meges]]), and in the Odyssey the suitor Amphinomus is the son of Nisus, lord of Dulichium (*Odyssey* 16.394–396, 18.126–127).

## The name

The Acastus of later poetry is another man, the son of Pelias, king of Iolcus. In Euripides' *Alcestis* Pheres tells Admetus that Acastus will avenge his sister Alcestis, who has died for her husband (*Alcestis* 732–733), and in the *Trojan Women* “Acastus, the offspring of Pelias”, has driven Peleus from his land (*Trojan Women* 1126–1128; see [[euripides]]).
