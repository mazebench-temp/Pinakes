## Etymology

Χρύσης (Chrysēs), "the man of Chryse", named from his town, which in turn is named from χρυσός, gold. His daughter's name, Χρυσηΐς, means "the daughter of Chryses".

## In Book 1

**The plea.** Chryses comes to the ships as a suppliant, carrying the fillets of Apollo on a golden scepter, the signs of his priesthood:

{{quote:269356fa-d33a-518f-8c99-240a0367bcb1}}

His speech is courteous and short. He wishes the Achaeans success against Troy, offers the ransom, and names the god. The whole army approves (1.22–23). Only Agamemnon refuses, with threats (1.26–32).

**The first prayer.** He walks away in silence along the shore of the loud-roaring sea (1.34). His prayer to Apollo lists his past services and asks for revenge in one line:

{{quote:51dc47df-7585-52b9-9a4c-8310d3b0f1d2}}

The logic is that of reciprocity with the gods: "if ever I roofed a temple… fulfill for me this wish". See [[reciprocity-with-the-gods]].

**The second prayer.** When Odysseus brings his daughter home, Chryses prays again in the same opening words, and asks for the opposite:

{{quote:668ec5b7-4bf6-5d0a-8529-971a4e97a059}}

Both prayers are answered in the same line: "So he spoke in prayer, and Phoebus Apollo heard him" (1.43 = 1.457). The repetition shows how completely the priest controls the god's anger in this book.

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates retells the Chryses episode (1.12–42) in plain prose to show the difference between narrative and imitation (*Republic* 3.392e–394a). See [[plato-chryses-diegesis]].
