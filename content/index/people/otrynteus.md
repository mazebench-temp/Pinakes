Ὀτρυντῆϊ πτολιπόρθῳ, “to Otrynteus, sacker of cities” (20.384). Homer gives the epithet to Odysseus, to Achilles, and to the war gods Ares and Enyo, and twice to a father of the older generation at the end of the verse in which his son's birth is told: Medon is the bastard son of Oileus, “whom Rhene bore to Oileus, sacker of cities” (2.727–728), and Iphition is the son whom a Naiad bore to Otrynteus (see [[sacker-of-cities]], [[oileus]], [[medon]] and [[iphition]]). The poem says nothing of the cities he sacked.

He is named nowhere else. The only other thing told of him is in Achilles' boast over his son: Iphition's birthplace is by the Gygaean lake, “where your father's domain is, / by the fish-filled Hyllus and the eddying Hermus” (20.391–392; see [[gygaean-lake]], [[hyllus]] and [[hermus]]). A τέμενος is land set apart for a king or a hero: the Lycians marked out for Bellerophon “a domain finer than all others” (6.194), the Aetolian elders bade Meleager choose “a beautiful domain” (9.578), and Sarpedon and Glaucus “hold a great domain by the banks of the Xanthus” (12.313; see [[bellerophon]], [[meleager]] and [[sarpedon]]). Otrynteus' lay by the rivers of Lydia.

The name is formed like ὀτρυντύς, “summons” (19.234–235), on ὀτρύνω, “urge on”. Walter Leaf noted that the first syllable of Ὀτρυντῆϊ and Ὀτρυντεΐδης is short, as it never is in ὀτρύνω, and counted it among the signs that the Iphition episode is late (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.381; see the translation's note on 20.381–395).
