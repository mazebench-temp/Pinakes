Ἀρηΐθοον θεράποντα, “Areithous, his attendant” (20.487): a θεράπων serves a warrior, drives or holds his horses and fights at his side (see [[hetairos]]). Rhigmus has fallen from the chariot with Achilles' javelin in his belly, and Areithous is turning the horses back when Achilles “stabbed in the back with his sharp spear, / and thrust him from the chariot; and his horses were thrown into confusion” (20.488–489; see [[rhigmus]], [[achilles]] and [[chariot]]). He is the last man Achilles kills by name in the book. A master and his attendant die together elsewhere: Diomedes killed Axylus and “his attendant Calesius, who at that time / drove his horses as his charioteer” (6.12–19), and Thymbraeus and Molion, “the godlike attendant of that lord”, fell to Diomedes and Odysseus together (11.320–322; see [[axylus]], [[calesius]], [[thymbraeus]] and [[molion]]). κυκήθησαν, “were thrown into confusion”, was said of the Trojans and their allies when Achilles shouted three times from the ditch (18.229).

**The name.** He shares it with Areithous the mace-man, the lord whom Lycurgus killed in a narrow road, father of Menesthius (7.8–10, 7.137–146; see [[areithous]]). The name is the adjective ἀρηΐθοος, “swift in war”, said of young fighters (ἀρηϊθόων αἰζηῶν, 8.298, 15.315), and given in this book to the young men of the lion simile (20.167; see [[swift-in-war]]).
