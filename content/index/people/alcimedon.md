πέμπτης δʼ Ἀλκιμέδων Λαέρκεος υἱὸς ἀμύμων, “and the fifth by Alcimedon, the noble son of Laerces” (16.197): Alcimedon is the last of the five leaders of the Myrmidons, named after the old horseman Phoenix (16.196; see [[myrmidons]], [[phoenix]] and [[laerces]]). Unlike Menesthius, Eudorus and Peisander, he returns in the next book, as the charioteer of Achilles' horses.

Ἀλκιμέδων joins ἀλκή, “courage, defense” (see [[alke]]), and μέδων, “ruler”, as Automedon's name joins αὐτός and μέδων. In Books 19 and 24 Automedon's fellow attendant is Ἄλκιμος, Alcimus (19.392, 24.474, 24.574); Walter Leaf cites Ἄλκιμος beside Ἀλκιμέδων as a familiar shortened form of a name (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.11), but the poem does not say whether Alcimus and Alcimedon are one man.

## Elsewhere in Homer

After Patroclus' death Automedon fights alone from the chariot of Achilles' immortal horses, rushing in and out of the Trojan throng like a vulture after geese, but he cannot both drive and strike with the spear. Alcimedon, “son of Laerces, son of Haemon”, sees him, stands behind the chariot and asks which god has taken away his wits, to fight the Trojans alone when his companion is dead and Hector wears the armor. Automedon answers that no other Achaean could match Alcimedon at holding in the immortal horses, except Patroclus while he lived, and gives him the whip and the reins; Alcimedon leaps onto the chariot and Automedon jumps down to fight (17.459–483; see [[automedon]] and [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). When Hector and Aeneas come against them, Automedon prays to Zeus and tells Alcimedon, his “trusted companion”, to keep the horses close, breathing on his back (17.498–506; see [[trusted-companion]]). The leader of a company serves here as a charioteer.

## Outside Homer

Strabo reports that those who take Homer's Messe (2.582) for Messene, shortened, cite other shortenings from the poet, κρῖ, δῶ and μάψ, and “the hero Automedon and Alcimus” (24.474), Ἄλκιμος “instead of Ἀλκιμέδων” (*Geography* 8.5.3; see [[strabo]]).
