The swineherd who keeps Odysseus' swine in a fold of his own building, away from the town. Athena sends Odysseus to him first when she has changed him into a beggar (*Odyssey* 13.404–411), and the poem names him when Odysseus comes to his hut (*Odyssey* 14.55). The disguised Odysseus spends his first nights on Ithaca with him, makes himself known to Telemachus in his hut while Eumaeus is away in the town, and goes to the town as a beggar under his guidance (*Odyssey* 14–17). Before the contest of the bow Odysseus shows him and the cowherd Philoetius his scar to prove who he is (*Odyssey* 21.205–225), and the two fight beside Odysseus and his son against the suitors (*Odyssey* 22.267, 22.284; see [[the-suitors]]).

## Family

He tells his own story to the stranger in the hut (*Odyssey* 15.390–484). He was born on Syrie, an island “above Ortygia” (*Odyssey* 15.404, a plain rendering), where his father Ctesius, son of Ormenus, was king of both its cities. A Phoenician woman, a slave in his father's house who nursed him, carried him off as a child to the ship of Phoenician traders; she died at sea, and the traders sold him in Ithaca to Laertes (*Odyssey* 15.403–484; see [[phoenicians]] and [[laertes]]). Odysseus' mother Anticleia reared him with her youngest daughter, Ctimene, and when Ctimene was married he was sent to the fields (*Odyssey* 15.363–370; see [[anticleia]]).

## Descriptions

- δῖος ὑφορβός, “the brilliant swineherd” (*Odyssey* 14.3, 14.48 and often; see [[brilliant]]).
- συβώτης, ὄρχαμος ἀνδρῶν, “the swineherd, leader of men” (*Odyssey* 14.22, 14.121, 15.351, 15.389, 16.36, 17.184), the title that the Iliad gives to Asius and to Achilles (*Iliad* 2.837, 6.99), and the Odyssey to Peisistratus, to Polites and to the cowherd Philoetius (*Odyssey* 3.400, 10.224, 20.185; see [[leader-of-men]]).
- τὸν δʼ ἀπαμειβόμενος προσέφης, Εὔμαιε συβῶτα, “Answering him, you said, swineherd Eumaeus” (*Odyssey* 14.55 and often): the poet turns to him in the second person fifteen times, and to no other character of the Odyssey. In the Iliad he speaks so to Patroclus and to Menelaus (*Iliad* 16.20, 16.744, 16.843; 4.127, 7.104, 17.702; see [[apostrophe]] and [[answering-him]]).
- Telemachus calls him ἄττα, “father” (*Odyssey* 16.31, 16.57, 16.130, 17.599, 21.369), Achilles' word for old Phoenix (*Iliad* 9.607; see [[phoenix]]).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle: Odysseus “was recognized through his scar in one way by the nurse and in another by the swineherds”, and recognitions made for the sake of proof are less artistic (*Poetics* 1454b26–29; see [[the-scar-of-odysseus]]). In his *Constitution of the Ithacans* Aristotle told that Telemachus freed Eumaeus' people and settled them among the citizens, and that the family of the Koliadai descended from Eumaeus, as the Boukoloi from the cowherd Philoetius (fr. 511 Gigon, from Plutarch's *Greek Questions*; see [[aristotle]]). In Ovid's letter of Penelope three servants are on her side, the keeper of the cattle, the old nurse, and, third, *inmundae cura fidelis harae*, “the faithful keeper of the filthy sty” (*Heroides* 1.103–104; see [[ovid-penelopes-letter]]). D. B. Monro, asking at what stage in the growth of the story each of its lesser characters came in, finds in some of them, “such as Nausicaa, Eumaeus, Eurycleia”, “no reason to see anything but the invention of a great poet” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix I §9).
