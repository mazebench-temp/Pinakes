**The priest of Idaean Zeus.** Λαόγονον θρασὺν υἱὸν Ὀνήτορος, ὃς Διὸς ἱρεὺς / Ἰδαίου ἐτέτυκτο, θεὸς δʼ ὣς τίετο δήμῳ, “Laogonus, the bold son of Onetor, who was the priest of Zeus / of Ida, and was honored by the people like a god” (16.604–605; see [[laogonus]] and [[priesthood]]). The relative clause follows the father's name and belongs to him, as Walter Leaf also takes it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 16.604–605). Ἰδαῖος, “of Ida”, is said of Zeus only here, but Zeus is often the god of the mountain: he is invoked as “Father Zeus, who rule from Ida” (3.276), he has a precinct and a fragrant altar on Gargarus (8.47–48), and Hector burned the thighs of many oxen to him on the peaks of Ida (22.170–171; see [[zeus]], [[ida]] and [[gargarus]]).

**Priests and their sons.** The verse-end θεὸς δʼ ὣς τίετο δήμῳ is said of another priest whose son is killed, Dolopion, priest of the Scamander, the father of Hypsenor (5.76–78; see [[dolopion]] and [[honored-like-a-god]]). Dares, priest of Hephaestus, loses one of his two sons to Diomedes, and the god saves the other (5.9–24; see [[dares]]). No god intervenes for Onetor's son (see [[bereaved-parents]]).

Homer's other Onetor is the father of Phrontis, the helmsman of Menelaus, whom Apollo killed at Sunium on the voyage home (Φρόντιν Ὀνητορίδην, Od. 3.278–283).
