## The name

Ἐλπήνωρ ends in -ήνωρ, from ἀνήρ, “man”, like Agenor and Antenor; the first part is the stem of ἔλπομαι, “hope”.

## Descriptions

Ἐλπήνωρ δέ τις ἔσκε νεώτατος, οὔτε τι λίην / ἄλκιμος ἐν πολέμῳ οὔτε φρεσὶν ᾗσιν ἀρηρώς, “There was one Elpenor, the youngest, not very / valiant in war nor sound in his wits” (*Odyssey* 10.552–553; see [[alke]] and [[phrenes]]). φρεσὶν … ἀρηρώς is “steady in mind”, like the later φρενήρης (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 10.553; see the Odyssey translation's key words).

## Elsewhere in Homer

**Among the dead.** At the pit, before Teiresias comes, “first came the soul of my companion Elpenor; / for he had not yet been buried beneath the wide-wayed earth; / for we had left his body behind in the hall of Circe, / unwept and unburied, since another toil was pressing us” (*Odyssey* 11.51–54, a plain rendering; see [[psyche]]). Odysseus weeps and asks him how he came “beneath the misty darkness”, faster on foot than he in his black ship; Elpenor answers that “an evil fate from a god led me into blind folly, and endless wine”, tells his fall in the words of Book 10, and begs Odysseus, by his wife, his father and Telemachus, not to leave him behind “unwept, unburied”, lest he become a cause of the gods' wrath against him, but to burn him with his armor, heap up a mound for him on the shore of the gray sea, “the mound of an unhappy man, even for men to come to hear of”, and fix on the tomb the oar with which he rowed among his companions (*Odyssey* 11.55–78, a plain rendering; see [[ate]], [[tomb]] and [[oars]]). Odysseus promises, and the two sit exchanging hateful words, he holding his sword over the blood, “and on the other the phantom of my companion, saying many things” (*Odyssey* 11.79–83, a plain rendering; see [[eidolon]]).

**The burial.** Back on Aeaea, at dawn, Odysseus sends companions to Circe's house to bring the body; they cut logs where the headland juts out furthest and bury him weeping, and when the body and his armor are burned they heap up a mound, drag a pillar onto it, and fix the well-shaped oar on the top of the mound (*Odyssey* 12.8–15, a plain rendering; see [[the-death-of-elpenor]], [[honoring-the-dead]] and [[cremation]]).

**The doubts.** Merry thought the meeting in *Odyssey* 11 hard to accept as genuine. With Ad. Hermann he finds it strange that so large a part goes to one humble companion when none of the others Odysseus lost is mentioned, and with Lauer that the interview with Teiresias, the purpose of the journey, is put off for a man described as the youngest and not sound in his wits. Odysseus, who knew of the death, asks him how he came there; Elpenor knows, no one says how, that Odysseus will put in again at Aeaea; and his claim to burial, lest he become a cause of the gods' wrath, Merry calls a later refinement. He takes the episode for an addition from a time when the book of the dead was recited as a separate rhapsody (Merry on *Odyssey* 11.51). He quotes with approval Eustathius' remark that Elpenor is not sound in his wits even in Hades, for he asks simply for cheap things, and for a mound on the shore with an oar set up on it that can serve no purpose, since, Merry adds, no one would see it on the remote island of Aeaea (Merry on *Odyssey* 11.51). The soul comes first, Merry explains, because, unburied, it could not pass within the gates of Hades, as the soul of Patroclus says to Achilles (*Iliad* 23.71–74; Merry on *Odyssey* 11.51).

## Outside Homer

Plutarch says that of the Homeric souls named in the book of the dead, Elpenor's, not yet mingled with those in Hades because its body was not buried, wanders as if on the borders (*Table Talk* 9.5.3, as Merry quotes it on *Odyssey* 11.51). In Ovid he is one of the men sent by lot to Circe's house, “Elpenor, too fond of wine” (*nimiique Elpenora vini*, *Metamorphoses* 14.252; see [[ovid]] and [[eurylochus]]).
