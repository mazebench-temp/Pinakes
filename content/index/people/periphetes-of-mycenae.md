**The only one.** Hector falls on the Achaeans like a lion on the cattle of a great marsh, and as the lion “devours a cow, and they all flee in fear”, so the Achaeans “fled in wondrous rout before Hector and father Zeus, / all of them; but he killed only Periphetes of Mycenae” (15.630–638; see [[hector]], [[zeus]] and [[lion-among-cattle]]). The rout of a whole army is paid for with one life, and the narrator stops to say whose.

**Father and son.** Copreus “used to go for lord Eurystheus / with messages to mighty Heracles” (15.639–640; see [[copreus]], [[eurystheus]] and [[might-of-heracles]]), and the son was the better man: “From him, a far worse father, was born a better son / in every kind of prowess, both in running and in fighting, / and in mind he was among the first of the Mycenaeans” (15.641–643; see [[arete]] and [[mycenae]]). In what the father was worse the poem does not say. The Iliad usually measures sons against fathers the other way: Agamemnon tells Diomedes that Tydeus fathered a son “worse than he in battle, though better in assembly” (4.399–400), and Athena that he “has begotten a son little like himself” (5.800), while Sthenelus boasts that the sons are “far better than our fathers” (4.405; see [[better-than-our-fathers]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). Here it is the narrator who judges, and the son is better in feet, hands and mind alike.

**The fall.** “He it was who then put the higher glory into Hector's hands” (15.644): the phrase is the one Hector used of Zeus a little earlier, who puts the higher glory into the hands of those he favors (κῦδος ὑπέρτερον ἐγγυαλίξῃ, 15.491; see [[kydos]]). Here a stumble gives it. Turning back, he tripped on the rim of the great shield “which he carried himself, reaching to his feet, a barrier against javelins”, fell on his back, and his helmet rang about his temples (15.645–648; see [[reaching-to-the-feet]], [[shield]] and [[helmet]]). The rim of Hector's own shield knocked against his ankles and his neck as he walked (6.117–118). The man who was among the first in running is brought down by his own armor. “But Hector was quick to see it, and ran and stood beside him, / and fixed his spear in his chest”, close to his companions, who could not help him, grieving though they were, “for they themselves feared brilliant Hector greatly” (15.649–652). The fall decides the battle: the Achaeans come face to face with their ships and give way from the first of them (15.653–656; see [[ships]]).

He is not the Trojan Periphetes whom Teucer killed in the rout of *Iliad* 14 (14.515; see [[periphetes]]).
