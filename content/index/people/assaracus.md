## Family

Homer names him twice, both times in the genealogy that Aeneas recites to Achilles: Tros had “three noble sons, / Ilus and Assaracus and godlike Ganymede” (20.231–232; see [[tros]], [[ilus]] and [[ganymede]]), and Assaracus begins the line that ends in Aeneas.

{{quote:89541ac7-fe81-5beb-9702-1303228c1094}}

The royal house divides at the sons of Tros. From Ilus come Laomedon, Priam and Hector; from Assaracus come Capys, Anchises and Aeneas (see [[laomedon]], [[priam]], [[hector]], [[capys]] and [[anchises]]). Hector and Aeneas are both great-great-grandsons of Tros, “third cousins”, as Walter Leaf puts it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.240). The two branches lead the two first contingents of the Trojan army: Hector the Trojans, and Aeneas the Dardanians (2.816–821; see [[dardanians]] and [[genealogy-in-battle]]).

## Outside Homer

In [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] Assaracus marries Hieromneme, daughter of the river Simoeis, and fathers Capys (*Library* 3.12.2). [[virgil|Virgil]] makes the house of Assaracus the line of Aeneas and of Rome. Jupiter foretells a time when “the house of Assaracus” will press Phthia and famous Mycenae into slavery and be lord over conquered Argos (*Aeneid* 1.284–285); in the underworld Aeneas sees the ancient race of Teucer, “Ilus and Assaracus and Dardanus, founder of Troy” (6.648–650); and Romulus' mother Ilia is “of the blood of Assaracus” (6.777–778).
