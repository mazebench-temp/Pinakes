## In Homer

The poem gives him a craft and an age: μάντις, “seer” (13.663), and γέρων ἀγαθός, “the good old man” (13.666; see [[divination]] and [[prophecy]]). His prophecy was for his own son, and he gave it “often”: death by a grievous sickness at home, or at the Trojans' hands among the ships (13.666–668). His son lived at Corinth (13.664; see [[corinth]]); Homer gives Polyidus no father and no other deed. The name joins πολύς, “much”, to the stem of εἰδέναι, “know”: “knowing much”, a name for a seer, and also for a Trojan whom Diomedes kills (5.148).

He is one of three fathers in the Iliad who foresee, or fail to foresee, a son's death at Troy. Merops of Percote forbade his sons to go, and they did not obey him (2.831–834 = 11.329–332; see [[merops]] and [[the-sons-of-merops]]); Eurydamas read no dreams for his sons when they set out (5.148–151; see [[eurydamas]]); Polyidus told his son both fates, and his son went knowing (see [[euchenor]]).

## Outside Homer

In Pindar's thirteenth *Olympian*, for Xenophon of Corinth, Athena gives Bellerophon a golden bridle in a dream. He shows “the local seer”, the son of Coeranus, the whole matter, and how he had slept on the goddess's altar at the seer's bidding, and the seer tells him to obey the dream and set up an altar to Athena Hippia (*Olympian* 13.63–82; see [[bellerophon]]). Apollodorus names the seer who found Minos' son Glaucus, drowned in a jar of honey, “Polyidus, son of Coeranus”: he found the body by divination, was shut up with it, and brought it back to life with a herb that he saw a serpent lay on its dead mate, and Minos would not let him go home to Argos until he had taught Glaucus his art (3.3.1–2; see [[minos]]). Euripides wrote a *Polyidus*, now in fragments; in one, a sea-eagle that has flown from the waves to the shore is read as a sign that a lost boy did not die at sea. At Megara, Pausanias saw the grave of the daughters of Polyidus, “son of Coeranus, son of Abas, son of Melampus”, who came there to purify Alcathous, son of Pelops (not the Trojan of 13.427), after he had killed his own son (1.43.5).

Homer's Polyidus has a son at Corinth, and Pindar's seer is a Corinthian; the Polyidus of Apollodorus goes home to Argos, and the one of Pausanias comes of the line of Melampus, who in the Odyssey left Pylos to rule over the Argives (Od. 15.225–242). Whether the poet of the Iliad knew any of these stories is not known.
