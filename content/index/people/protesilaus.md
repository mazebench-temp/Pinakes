## Etymology

Πρωτεσίλαος: πρῶτος ("first") + λαός ("people, army"), "first of the people". The name suits the one thing everyone knew about him, that he was first ashore and first to fall. Whether the story grew from the name or the name was chosen for the story cannot be known.

## Descriptions

ἀρήϊος, "warlike" (2.698, 2.708; see [[warlike]]); μεγάθυμος, "great-hearted" (2.706; see [[great-hearted]]); ἥρως, "hero" (2.708; see [[heros]]). He was "both older and braver" than his brother (πρότερος καὶ ἀρείων, 2.707), and his men "longed for the good man they had lost" (2.709).

## In the Iliad

{{quote:18c19717-694e-5eba-ac7d-260fa39432fa}}

The Catalogue lists the fleet that sailed to Troy, but here it stops to say that one of its leaders is already dead. The tense changes with him: he led them "while he lived; but by now the black earth held him fast". See [[the-death-of-protesilaus]].

His ship is still on the beach. When the Trojans reach the ships, Hector seizes the stern of the fair, swift ship "that had brought Protesilaus to Troy, and did not take him home again" (15.704–706). It is the ship the Trojans set on fire, and Patroclus kills his first man beside it and drives them off (16.284–294).

## Outside Homer

In Proclus' summary of the *Cypria*, the Trojans oppose the landing and Hector kills Protesilaus. Later writers add an oracle: the first man to set foot on Trojan soil would die (Hyginus, *Fabulae* 103).

The Iliad does not name his wife. The *Cypria* called her Polydora, a daughter of Meleager (Pausanias 4.2.7); most later writers call her Laodameia, daughter of Acastus. Her grief became a famous story. The gods let Protesilaus come back from the dead for a few hours, and when he went down again she killed herself, or clung to a likeness of him that she had made (Apollodorus, *Epitome* 3.30; Hyginus, *Fabulae* 104). Euripides wrote a *Protesilaus*, now lost. Catullus compares his own love to Laodameia's (68.73–130), Ovid gives her a letter to her husband (*Heroides* 13), and Wordsworth a poem, *Laodamia* (1815).

His tomb and shrine were shown at Elaeus, at the tip of the Thracian Chersonese across the strait from Troy. Herodotus tells how the Persian governor Artayctes plundered it, and was crucified by the Athenians in 479 BC (9.116–120). In Philostratus' *Heroicus*, a vinedresser at Elaeus talks with the hero's ghost.
