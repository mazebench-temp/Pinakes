## Etymology

Φοῖνιξ (Phoinix) is also a common word. It is the red-purple dye and its color (see [[purple]]), the date palm (*Odyssey* 6.163), and, in the plural, the Phoenicians (Φοίνικες, 23.744). Whether the name was heard as “the red one” or as “the Phoenician” is not known. Homer has a second Phoenix, the father of the girl who bore Minos and Rhadamanthys to Zeus (14.321–322); later writers call her Europa (Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, fr. 19 Evelyn-White).

## Family

{{quote:991cec53-e0c0-5094-b22a-cf769925fbd0}}

He is the son of [[Amyntor]], son of Ormenus (Ἀμύντορος Ὀρμενίδαο, 9.448; see [[ormenus-father-of-amyntor]]). His mother is not named. She begged him to lie with his father's concubine, and his father's curse left him without a son: “the gods fulfilled his curses, Zeus of the underworld and dread Persephone” (9.456–457; see [[the-flight-of-phoenix]], [[concubines]] and [[curses]]). His “kinsmen and cousins”, ἔται καὶ ἀνεψιοί, tried to keep him at home (9.464–465). In Phthia [[Peleus]] took him in and “loved me as a father loves his son, his only son, his darling, heir to great possessions” (9.481–482; see [[as-a-father-loves-his-son]]), made him rich, and gave him a people to rule (9.483–484).

The son he raised was Achilles, the son of the house that received him:

{{quote:ab8c2061-6e2c-5e76-b6bc-af74c9bf9470}}

τοσοῦτον ἔθηκα, “I made you what you are” (9.485), is a father's claim. The gods would give him no child of his own, “but it was you, Achilles, like the gods, that I made my son, so that one day you might ward off shameful ruin from me” (9.494–495; see [[fathers-and-sons]] and [[shameful-ruin]]). The curse that denied Amyntor a grandson on his knees (9.455) is answered by the child Phoenix set on his own (9.488).

## Descriptions

He is an old man, γέρων (9.432, 9.662, 9.690), and he would not leave Achilles “not even if a god himself should promise me to scrape away my old age and make me young and in my prime” (9.445–446). Nestor calls him Διῒ φίλος, “dear to Zeus” (9.168; see [[dear-to-zeus]]). The narrator calls him γέρων ἱππηλάτα, “the old horse-driving” Phoenix (9.432; 16.196, 19.311; see [[horse-driving]]): ἱππηλάτα = ἵππος (“horse”) + ἐλαύνω (“drive”). It is the title of old men who once drove chariots, of his lord Peleus (7.125, 9.438), of Oeneus (9.581), and in the Odyssey of Nestor (Od. 3.436, 3.444).

Achilles addresses him as Φοῖνιξ ἄττα γεραιὲ διοτρεφές, “Phoenix, old father, nurtured by Zeus” (9.607; see [[zeus-nurtured]]), and Menelaus, speaking to Athena in his shape, as ἄττα γεραιὲ παλαιγενές, old father, born long ago (17.561). ἄττα is a younger man's affectionate word for an elder; in the Odyssey Telemachus uses it to the swineherd Eumaeus (Od. 16.31, 16.57, 17.599). In Book 23 he is ἀντίθεος, “godlike”, and ὀπάων πατρὸς ἑοῖο, the attendant of Achilles' father (23.360; see [[antitheos]]). ὀπάων, “squire, attendant”, is the word for Meriones beside Idomeneus (7.165, 8.263).

## Elsewhere in Homer

When Achilles sends the Myrmidons out with Patroclus, he divides them into five companies, and “the old horse-driving Phoenix” leads the fourth (16.168–197). In the fight over the body of Patroclus, Athena takes Phoenix's form and voice to put heart into Menelaus (17.553–573). When Achilles will not eat after the reconciliation, he sends the other kings away, but the sons of Atreus, Odysseus, Nestor, Idomeneus and Phoenix stay to comfort him (19.303–313). At the chariot race Achilles sets him at the turning post as watcher, to remember the laps and report the truth (23.358–361). The Odyssey does not name him.

## Outside Homer

**His people and his home.** Pindar made Phoenix lead to Troy “a bold throng of Dolopians” who fought with slings, in lines that Strabo quotes. Strabo notes that Homer never brings a Dolopian force into the fighting, and supposes that it is understood (Strabo 9.5.5; see [[dolopians]]). Where Phoenix came from was disputed. Demetrius of Scepsis made him a native of Ormenium in Thessaly and read Ὀρμένιον πολύμηλον, “Ormenium rich in flocks”, for “Hellas of the lovely women” at 9.447; Crates made him a Phocian, from an Eleon on Parnassus (Strabo 9.5.18; see [[hellas-or-ormenium]] and [[amyntor]]).

**The blinding.** Euripides' lost *Phoenix*, which is older than Aristophanes' *Acharnians* (425 BC), showed him blinded. In the *Acharnians* Euripides offers Dicaeopolis the rags of “blind Phoenix” among the costumes of his ragged heroes (*Acharnians* 421). The fragments include a speaker who used to mock the blind as cowards for clinging to life and has now fallen into their state (fr. 816 Nauck), a farewell to a native land (fr. 817), and complaints about an old man who takes a young wife (frr. 804, 807). Apollodorus gives the version that became standard. Amyntor blinded his son when his concubine Phthia falsely accused him of seducing her; Peleus brought him to Chiron, who restored his sight, and made him king of the Dolopians (*Library* 3.13.8; see [[chiron]] and [[apollodorus]]). In Homer Phoenix did lie with the girl, at his mother's urging. The later story makes him a young man falsely accused, like Bellerophon (see [[bellerophon]]) or Euripides' Hippolytus.

**After Achilles.** When Achilles was dead, the Achaeans sent Odysseus and Phoenix to Lycomedes on Scyros to bring his son Neoptolemus to Troy (Apollodorus, *Epitome* 5.11; see [[scyros]]). In Sophocles' *Philoctetes* Neoptolemus says that Odysseus came for him with ὁ τροφεὺς τοὐμοῦ πατρός, the man who reared his father (*Philoctetes* 343–344; see [[sophocles]]). Proclus' summary of the epic *Returns* says that Neoptolemus, going home by land, buried Phoenix, who died on the way; Apollodorus puts his death on the road to the Molossians (*Epitome* 6.12; see [[proclus]]).

**Plato.** Socrates would not have the young hear Phoenix, Achilles' παιδαγωγός (“tutor”), praised for advising him to take the gifts and help the Achaeans, but not to give up his wrath without them (*Republic* 3.390e; see [[plato-phoenix-and-the-gifts]]). Cicero and Quintilian held him up as the teacher of both speaking and doing (see [[phoenix-the-model-teacher]]).
