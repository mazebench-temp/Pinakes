Iphis is named once in Homer. When the envoys have gone, Achilles lies in the inner part of the hut with Diomede, and “Patroclus lay down on the other side; and beside him too / lay fair-belted Iphis, whom brilliant Achilles gave him / when he took steep Scyros, the citadel of Enyeus” (9.666–668; see [[diomede]], [[scyros]] and [[enyeus]]). She is a share of Achilles' spoil passed on to his companion, as Agamemnon gives prizes to the chiefs and kings (9.334; see [[captive-women]] and [[geras]]). ἐΰζωνος, “fair-belted”, is said of Briseis (1.429), of Meleager's wife Cleopatra (9.590), and of the women Achilles will carry home from Troy (9.366; see [[fair-belted]]).

The name belongs with ἶφι, “by might”, the first part of other names in Homer, Iphianassa (9.145), Iphidamas (11.221) and Iphinous (7.14). The poem does not mention her again.

## Outside Homer

In Polygnotus' painting of the fall of Troy at Delphi, as Pausanias describes it, Iphis stands before Briseis and Diomede, looking at Helen's beauty (10.25.4).
