## The name

Φήμιος belongs with φήμη, “speech, report”, and φημί, “speak”: a speaking name for a singer. His father's name, in the patronymic Τερπιάδης, “son of Terpes” (*Odyssey* 22.330), belongs with τέρπω, “delight”, the verb of the song's pleasure: Telemachus asks why his mother begrudges the singer “giving delight” (τέρπειν, *Odyssey* 1.347). Odysseus calls him πολύφημος ἀοιδός, the singer “of many songs” (*Odyssey* 22.376), with the adjective that is also the name of the Cyclops (see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]).

## Descriptions

- ἀοιδὸς … περικλυτός, “the renowned singer” (*Odyssey* 1.325). The same words close Demodocus' song of the quarrel of Odysseus and Achilles, at which Odysseus weeps (*Odyssey* 8.83; see [[demodocus]]).
- θεῖος ἀοιδός, “the divine singer” (*Odyssey* 1.336), the standing title of a singer: of the singer at Menelaus' wedding feast (*Odyssey* 4.17), of Demodocus (*Odyssey* 8.43, 8.87), and of the singer at the dance on the Shield of Achilles (*Iliad* 18.604; see [[the-singer-on-the-shield]]). See [[divine]].
- ἐρίηρος ἀοιδός, “the trusty singer” (*Odyssey* 1.346), again of Demodocus (*Odyssey* 8.62, 8.471). Elsewhere ἐρίηρος is the word of faithful companions (see [[trusty]]).
- θεοῖς ἐναλίγκιος αὐδήν, “like the gods in his voice” (*Odyssey* 1.371), the praise that Odysseus repeats of Demodocus (*Odyssey* 9.3–4; see [[like-a-god-in-his-voice]]).
- ὅς ῥʼ ἤειδε παρὰ μνηστῆρσιν ἀνάγκῃ, “who sang among the suitors under compulsion” (*Odyssey* 1.154). The verse returns, with μετά for παρά, when he is still escaping death in the killing of the suitors (*Odyssey* 22.330–331).

## Later in the Odyssey

The singer goes with the suitors' feasts. Telemachus counts “Medon the herald and the divine singer” in their company (*Odyssey* 16.252). When Odysseus, disguised as a beggar, comes to his own door, the sound of the lyre reaches him, for Phemius was striking up a song; Odysseus knows that many men are feasting inside by the savor of the meat and by the lyre, “which the gods have made the companion of the feast” (*Odyssey* 17.260–271; see [[lyre]]).

In the killing of the suitors he stands by the side door with his lyre, wondering whether to slip out to the altar of Zeus of the courtyard or to supplicate Odysseus. He lays the lyre down between the mixing bowl and a silver-studded chair, clasps Odysseus' knees, and pleads that he is self-taught, that a god has planted all kinds of songs in his mind, and that he came to sing for the suitors not willingly but under force (*Odyssey* 22.330–353; see [[i-am-self-taught]] and [[supplication]]). Telemachus bids his father spare him, and the herald Medon too, and Odysseus sends the two of them out to sit by the altar in the courtyard (*Odyssey* 22.354–380). Afterwards “the divine singer”, who can only be he, takes up his lyre at Odysseus' command and leads a dance in the hall, so that passers-by will think a wedding is being held and the news of the killing will not go through the town before Odysseus has gone out to his farm (*Odyssey* 23.130–152); and he comes with Medon to the assembly of the suitors' kin (*Odyssey* 24.439–440).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates asks Ion whether, in playing the pipe or the lyre, in singing to the lyre or in rhapsody, he has ever seen a man clever at explaining Olympus or Thamyras or Orpheus or “Phemius the rhapsode of Ithaca” but at a loss over Ion of Ephesus (*Ion* 533b–c; see [[plato]] and [[thamyris]]). Aristotle quotes Phemius' plea, “I am self-taught”, for the topic that what is natural is greater than what is acquired, “for it is harder” (*Rhetoric* 1365a29–30; see [[i-am-self-taught]] and [[aristotle]]). The Suda (s.v. ἀοιδός) says that the race of singers was temperate of old and had the disposition of philosophers: Agamemnon left one with Clytemnestra to keep her chaste, and Aegisthus did not seduce her until he had killed him (the singer of *Odyssey* 3.267–271); Demodocus sings of Ares and Aphrodite to turn the luxurious Phaeacians from lawless desires; and Homer makes Phemius sing πρὸς τὴν βουλὴν αὐτῶν, “to their purpose”.
