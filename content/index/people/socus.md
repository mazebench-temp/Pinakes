**The challenge.** Socus is εὐηφενής, “wealthy” (11.427), a word used elsewhere of the Trojans themselves (23.81), and ἰσόθεος φώς, “a man equal to the gods” (11.428; see [[equal-to-the-gods]]). When Odysseus wounds his brother Charops, he comes up close and challenges him:

{{quote:b1cb2e9b-64bd-5860-b2b7-9d2f9a1f5c7d}}

πολύαινε, “much-praised”, is the word with which Agamemnon and Nestor greet Odysseus when he comes back from the embassy and from the night raid (9.673, 10.544), here in an enemy's mouth (see [[much-praised]]); ἆτε, “insatiable”, is the word of Ares, “insatiable of war” (5.388; see the translation's note on 11.430). Odysseus is insatiable of tricks.

**The wound.** His spear goes through the shining shield, forces its way through the corselet, and tears all the skin from Odysseus' ribs, “but it was not allowed / by Pallas Athena to mingle with the man's entrails” (11.434–438; see [[athena]] and [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). Odysseus knows that the wound is not deadly, draws back, and threatens him in the words of Sarpedon to Tlepolemus: “you will give me triumph, and your soul to Hades of the famous horses” (11.443–445 ≈ 5.652–654; see [[of-the-famous-horses]]).

**The death.** Socus turns to flee, and Odysseus plants his spear between his shoulders and drives it through his chest (11.446–448), in the verses of Diomedes' killing of Agelaus (8.258–259). “He fell with a thud” (11.449; see [[fell-with-a-thud]]), and Odysseus boasts over him:

{{quote:1a818b9c-d039-5af2-807c-d9aa1699534c}}

The unburied body torn by birds is a threat the Iliad repeats, and Odysseus sets it against the rites the Achaeans will give him (see [[birds]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). Then he pulls Socus' spear out of his own flesh, and the blood that spurts brings the Trojans down on him (11.456–460; see [[odysseus]]).

The name is the word σῶκος, an epithet of Hermes (20.72) of uncertain meaning, perhaps “strong”. The poem names him nowhere else.
