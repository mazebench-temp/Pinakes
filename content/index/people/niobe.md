Homer names Niobe only once, in the example that Achilles gives Priam in the last book of the Iliad (24.602–617; see [[the-children-of-niobe]]). She is ἠΰκομος, “of the lovely hair”, the epithet of the goddess she likened herself to, Leto (24.602; 1.36, 19.413; see [[leto]]); she had twelve children, six daughters and six sons; Apollo and Artemis killed them all, and the gods buried them on the tenth day; she ate when she was worn out with weeping; and now, turned to stone among the rocks of Sipylus, she broods over the sorrows the gods gave her (24.602–617; see [[apollo]], [[artemis]] and [[sipylus]]). Homer gives her no father, husband or city.

## Outside Homer

Walter Leaf notes that her father Tantalus, whom Odysseus sees punished in the underworld (*Odyssey* 11.582–592), is nowhere linked with her in Homer, and takes the relationship for a later notion; he thinks the setting of the story on Sipylus older than the Theban form of it (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.602). In Apollodorus she is the daughter of Tantalus and the wife of Amphion of Thebes; she says that she is more blessed in children than Leto, Artemis shoots her daughters in the house and Apollo her sons as they hunt on Cithaeron, and Niobe herself leaves Thebes for her father's home at Sipylus, where at her prayer Zeus turns her to stone, and tears flow from the stone night and day (*Library* 3.5.6). The number of her children varied: Apollodorus gives seven sons and seven daughters, and reports ten of each from Hesiod and two sons and three daughters from Herodorus, besides Homer's six and six (3.5.6).

Aeschylus wrote a *Niobe* (see [[niobe-play]]). A papyrus preserves part of a speech from it, which tells how she sits on the tomb brooding over her dead children, and names mighty Tantalus as the father who begot her and gave her in marriage (PSI 1208). Plato's Socrates quotes from a play on “the sufferings of Niobe” the iambics of Aeschylus, that “god plants a cause in mortals when he wishes to ruin a house utterly”, among the things that the young must not hear of the gods (*Republic* 2.380a; see [[plato-broken-oaths]]). In Aristophanes' *Frogs* Euripides mocks Aeschylus for seating a veiled figure, “an Achilles or a Niobe”, without showing the face and without a word (*Frogs* 911–913). Leaf adds that Sophocles too wrote a *Niobe*, and alluded to her in the *Electra* (150–152) and the *Antigone* (823–832; on 24.602). For the weeping rock on Sipylus in Sophocles, Ovid and Pausanias see [[the-weeping-rock-of-sipylus]], and for Ovid's telling of her story [[ovid-niobe]].
