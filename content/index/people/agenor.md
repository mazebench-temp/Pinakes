## Etymology

The name is the adjective ἀγήνωρ, from ἄγαν (“very much”) and ἀνήρ (“man”): “very manly”, and so “bold”, or in a bad sense “overbearing”. Homer uses the adjective of a man's proud spirit (θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ, 2.276) and, in the Odyssey, of the overbearing suitors (μνηστῆρας ἀγήνορας, Od. 1.106).

## Family

He is one of the “three sons of Antenor” who stand with Hector, Polydamas and Aeneas in *Iliad* 11, with Polybus and the young Acamas (11.59–60), and Book 21 calls him “son of Antenor” twice (21.546, 21.579). Several of his brothers fight and die in the Iliad (see [[antenor]]). Achilles kills an Echeclus, “son of Agenor” (20.474); the poem does not say whether his father is this Agenor, and the index keeps the two apart (see [[echeclus-son-of-agenor]] and [[agenor-father-of-echeclus]]).

## Descriptions

- δῖος (“brilliant”; [[brilliant]]) is his usual epithet (11.59, 13.490, 14.425, 15.340, 16.535, 21.545).
- μεγάθυμος (“great-hearted”; [[great-hearted]]) when he acts (4.467, 13.598).
- In *Iliad* 21 he is ἀμύμων τε κρατερός τε, “noble and strong” (21.546), and ἀντίθεος, “godlike” (21.595).

## Elsewhere in Homer

He is one of the Trojan leaders gathered around Hector on the plain (11.56–60). In the attack on the Achaean wall he commands the second of five companies with Paris and Alcathous (12.93), and he is one of the men Aeneas calls to his side (13.489–490). He draws the spear out of the hand of the wounded Helenus and binds it with a sling of twisted wool (13.598–600). When Ajax fells Hector with a stone, he is one of the best men who stand over Hector, with Polydamas, Aeneas, Sarpedon and Glaucus (14.424–426). He kills Clonius (15.340).

His great scene is in *Iliad* 21, when Apollo sets him against Achilles before the gates, and then snatches him away and takes his shape to lead Achilles off while the Trojans crowd into the city (21.544–611; see [[apollo-deceives-achilles]]). His debate with his own heart there is one of four in which a warrior alone weighs flight against standing his ground, with those of Odysseus (11.404–410), Menelaus (17.91–105) and Hector (22.99–130); see [[pondering-two-ways]] and [[decision-scene]].

## Outside Homer

In later myth the name belongs above all to Agenor the Phoenician, father of Cadmus and Europa (Herodotus 4.147.4). He is a different man.
