**At the wall.** When the Trojans leave their chariots at the ditch and form five companies, Alcathous is the second of the three leaders of the second company, between Paris and Agenor (12.93; see [[paris]], [[agenor]] and [[the-fight-at-the-wall]]). Book 12 names him only here.

**His family.** Book 13 tells who he was as it tells his death. He is “the dear son of Aesyetes, whom Zeus nurtured”, and Anchises' son-in-law: he married Hippodameia, the eldest of Anchises' daughters, whom her father and mother loved with all their hearts, because she surpassed every girl of her age in beauty, handiwork and wits, “and so / the best man in wide Troy had married her” (13.427–433). Whether his father is the old Aesyetes whose tomb was a lookout in Book 2 (2.793) the poem does not say (see [[aesyetes]]). His wife is not the Hippodameia of the Lapiths ([[hippodameia]], 2.742). Deiphobus reminds Aeneas that Alcathous, “being your brother-in-law, reared you in his house when you were small” (13.465–466; see [[aeneas]] and [[anchises]]).

**His death.** Poseidon beats him down under Idomeneus' spear, “bewitching his bright eyes and fettering his shining limbs” (13.435). He can neither run nor dodge, and Idomeneus stabs him in the chest as he stands still “like a pillar or a tree with high foliage” (13.437). The bronze tunic that had kept death from him “cried out dry” as the spear tore it, and his heart, still beating, shook the butt of the spear (13.434–444; see [[idomeneus]] and [[poseidon]]). Idomeneus boasts that three have died for one (13.446–447), and Aeneas, with Deiphobus, Paris and Agenor, leads the fight over the body (13.455–505).

The name joins ἀλκή, “strength, defense”, and θοός, “swift”. Homer has no other man of the name. Later poets knew an Alcathous of Megara, a different hero, whose land Euripides calls “the farthest borders of Alcathous” (*Children of Heracles* 278).
