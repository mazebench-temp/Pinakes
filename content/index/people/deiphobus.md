## Etymology

Δηΐφοβος (Dēiphobos) is usually explained as δήϊος, “hostile, the enemy” (δηΐων ἀνδρῶν, “enemy men”, 12.57), and φόβος, “rout”: “rout of the enemy”, a name for a fighter.

## Family

He is a son of Priam: with Helenus one of “two sons of Priam” (12.95), and Πριαμίδης, “son of Priam”, when he goes against Meriones (13.157; see [[son-of-priam]] and [[sons-of-priam]]). Hector calls him the dearest of the brothers “whom Hecuba and Priam bore” (22.233–234), so his mother is Hecuba (see [[hecuba]] and [[kasignetos]]). Polites, αὐτοκασίγνητος, “his own brother”, leads him out of the battle when he is wounded (13.533–535; see [[polites]]). In the last book he is one of the nine sons whom Priam abuses as “liars and dancers”, while the best are dead (24.249–262).

## Descriptions

θεοειδής, “godlike” (12.94; see [[godlike]]), and in the Odyssey θεοείκελος, “godlike” (Od. 4.276). He goes into battle μέγα φρονέων, “in high spirits” (13.156), and Meriones calls him ὑπερηνορέων, “overweening” (13.258), the word Agamemnon uses of the Trojans (4.176). Hector calls him λευκάσπις, “of the white shield” (22.294), a word found only there in Homer.

## Elsewhere in Homer

**At the ships.** In Book 13 he comes forward stepping lightly behind his round shield; Meriones' spear strikes it and breaks off at the socket, and Meriones goes back to his hut for another (13.156–168, 13.246–258; see [[meriones]]). When Idomeneus has killed Asius, Deiphobus, “grieving for Asius”, throws at him. The spear flies over Idomeneus' shield and kills Hypsenor, son of Hippasus, and Deiphobus boasts that Asius, on his way to the house of Hades, “the strong gatekeeper”, will be glad, since he has given him an escort (13.402–416; see [[asius]], [[idomeneus]] and [[gatekeeper]]). Idomeneus kills Alcathous and taunts him: is three for one a fair exchange? (13.445–454; see [[alcathous]]). Deiphobus weighs whether to fight him alone and goes instead to Aeneas, who stands at the back of the throng, angry with Priam, and calls on him to defend Alcathous, the husband of Aeneas' sister (13.455–467; see [[aeneas]]). Aeneas calls Deiphobus, Paris and Agenor to his side (13.489–490). When Idomeneus draws back, Deiphobus throws at him again, “for he always held a grudge against him”, misses, and kills Ascalaphus, son of Ares (13.516–520; see [[ascalaphus]]). As he tears the helmet from the body, Meriones stabs him in the arm, and Polites leads him to his chariot, which carries him groaning to the city (13.526–539). Hector looks for him among the foremost, and Paris tells him that Deiphobus and Helenus have gone, both wounded in the hand (13.758–783; see [[helenus]] and [[paris]]).

**Athena's disguise.** When Zeus' scales have sunk for Hector, Athena comes to him “in the likeness of Deiphobus in body and untiring voice” and offers to stand with him against Achilles (22.208–231; see [[athena]] and [[the-scales-of-zeus]]). Hector answers that Deiphobus was always the dearest of his brothers, and that he will honor him more now, since he alone dared to come out of the wall for his sake while the others stay inside (22.233–237). The false brother says that their father, their mother and their companions begged him to stay, but grief wore him down, “and so with cunning Athena led him on” (22.238–247). When Hector's spear glances off Achilles' shield, he shouts for Deiphobus of the white shield to bring him another, and no one is there. He understands: “I thought that the hero Deiphobus was beside me, but he is inside the wall, and Athena has deceived me” (22.294–299). The gods, he says, have called him to his death (22.297–305; see [[hector]]).

**The fall of Troy.** The Odyssey puts him twice at the end of the city. When Helen walked around the hollow horse and called the Achaean leaders by name in the voices of their wives, “godlike Deiphobus followed you”, Menelaus tells her (Od. 4.274–279). Demodocus sings how the Achaeans poured out of the horse and sacked the city, and how Odysseus went “like Ares” to the house of Deiphobus with Menelaus, and there dared his most terrible fight and won it with Athena's help (Od. 8.514–520). The Odyssey does not say why the two went to that house.

## Outside Homer

Later poets made Deiphobus Helen's husband after Paris' death. In Euripides' *Trojan Women* Helen pleads that when Paris died she tried to escape to the Achaean ships, letting herself down from the walls on ropes, and that “this new husband”, Deiphobus, took her by force and kept her as his wife against the Trojans' will (951–962). Murray's text brackets the two verses that name him (959–960) as suspect, and verse 962 still speaks of the man who married her by force (see [[euripides-trojan-women]]). The epic cycle and the mythographers on the same marriage are cited under [[paris]] and [[helenus]].
