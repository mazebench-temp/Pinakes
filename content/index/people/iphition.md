**The first man.** “And Achilles leapt among the Trojans, clothed in courage in his heart, / shouting terribly, and first he killed Iphition” (20.381–382; see [[achilles]] and [[the-aristeia]]). Achilles has killed no one in the poem before him: Aeneas, whom he met first, was carried out of his reach by Poseidon (20.318–340; see [[achilles-and-aeneas]]). Walter Leaf thought that the first of these verses may well have opened Achilles' career in the original poem of the Wrath (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.381).

**His people.** The narrator gives him the history of a victim (see [[androktasia]]): ἐσθλὸν Ὀτρυντεΐδην πολέων ἡγήτορα λαῶν, “the brave son of Otrynteus, leader of many people” (20.383; see [[brave]] and [[otrynteus]]), the son of a Naiad nymph. Three Naiads in the Iliad bear sons to mortal men, and all their sons die in battle: Abarbarea bore Aesepus and Pedasus to Bucolion, and Euryalus killed them (6.21–28; see [[abarbarea]] and [[aesepus-son-of-bucolion]]); another bore Satnius to Enops by the Satnioeis, and the son of Oileus killed him (14.443–447; see [[satnius]]); and Iphition's mother bore him “beneath snowy Tmolus, in the rich land of Hyde” (20.385; see [[nymphs]], [[tmolus]], [[hyde]], [[niphoeis]] and [[rich-land]]). The places are those of the Maeonians of the Trojan Catalogue, the people of the land later called Lydia, whose leaders Mesthles and Antiphus were sons “whom the Gygaean lake bore”, and who “were born beneath Tmolus” (2.864–866; see [[maeonians]], [[mesthles]] and [[antiphus-of-maeonia]]). The Catalogue does not name Iphition, and he is named nowhere else.

**The blow and the boast.** As he came on, eager, Achilles struck him “full on the head; and it was all split in two, / and he fell with a thud” (20.387–388; see [[fell-with-a-thud]]). Then Achilles spoke over him, the first of his boasts over the dead:

{{quote:dc1e51a0-bb23-5959-b654-bfbc414983a7}}

κεῖσαι, “you lie there”, sets the place of death against the place of birth: ἐνθάδε τοι θάνατος, “here is your death”, but the γενεή, the “birthplace”, is far away by the lake (see [[genee]] and [[gygaean-lake]]). The father's τέμενος, his “domain”, lies there, by the Hyllus and the Hermus (see [[hyllus]], [[hermus]], [[fish-filled]] and [[eddying]]); Earlier in the book Achilles asked Aeneas whether the Trojans had marked out a domain for him if he killed Achilles (20.184–186; see [[aeneas]]). “Most terrible of all men”, πάντων ἐκπαγλότατʼ ἀνδρῶν, is what Agamemnon and Iris called Achilles himself (1.146, 18.170; see [[most-terrible-of-men]]). “So he spoke, boasting, and darkness covered his eyes” (20.393; see [[euchomai]] and [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). The body is left to the chariots: “the horses of the Achaeans tore him apart with the tires of their wheels / in the front line of battle” (20.394–395), as Achilles' own horses will trample the dead at the end of the book (20.498–502; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]). Leaf calls δατέοντο, “tore apart”, a strange phrase (on 20.394).

**A later episode?** Leaf thought the verses on Iphition (20.383–395) betray a later origin than the line that introduces him: the first syllable of Ὀτρυντεΐδης and Ὀτρυντῆϊ is short three times, against Homeric practice and never so in the common verb ὀτρύνω; the allusion to the Catalogue is plain, and some even read 20.385 after 2.866; the poet's familiarity with the places of Asia Minor is itself suspicious; perhaps also the short ending of the dative ἐπισσώτροις (20.394); and “most terrible of all men” means nothing when said of an unknown warrior. He thought it quite possible that the lines were inserted to glorify a local family of Otrynteus' descendants by giving one of them a part in the Trojan war (on 20.381; see the translation's note on 20.381–395). Strabo reports the added verse in the Catalogue (*Geography* 13.4.6; see [[hyde]]).

**The name.** Ἰφιτίων seems to be formed, like Ἴφιτος and Ἰφιδάμας, on ἶφι, “with might” (see [[iphitus]] and [[iphidamas]]).

## Outside Homer

Hyde, his birthplace, is known only from this line: Strabo found no Hyde among the Lydians, and reports that some called Sardis or its acropolis by that name (*Geography* 13.4.6; see [[hyde]] and [[hyle-or-hyde]]).
