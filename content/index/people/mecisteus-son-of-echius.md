**The bearer.** When Hector's stone has felled Teucer and Ajax stands over him with his shield, two companions carry him off: “Mecisteus, son of Echius, and brilliant Alastor” (8.332–333; see [[teucer]], [[ajax]], [[echius]] and [[alastor-who-carries-teucer]]). In Book 13 the same four verses carry another man from the field (8.331–334 = 13.420–423). Deiphobus' spear has struck down Hypsenor, son of Hippasus, and Deiphobus boasts over him; Antilochus, whose companion he was, covers him with his shield, and Mecisteus and Alastor carry him to the ships (13.411–423; see [[antilochus]] and [[hypsenor-son-of-hippasus]]). There the last verse still says that the man they carry is “groaning heavily”, though Hypsenor's wound was told in the verse of a killing, “and at once loosened his knees” (13.412). Either he is dying rather than dead, or the verse has come over from Book 8 with its groaning. Aristarchus read the dual, στενάχοντε, so that it is Mecisteus and Alastor who groan as they carry him (see the translation's note on 13.412–423). The poem has a stock pair of bearers, and a block of verses for the rescue.

**His death.** In Book 15 “Polydamas killed Mecisteus, and Polites Echius, in the front of the fighting” (15.339–340). No other Achaean Mecisteus appears in the Iliad, and this is presumably the bearer. The poem does not say whether the Echius killed in the same verse is his father (see [[echius]]).

**The name.** Μηκιστεύς is formed from μήκιστος, “tallest”, the superlative of μακρός; Nestor calls Ereuthalion “the tallest and the strongest man I ever killed” (7.155). The older Mecisteus, son of Talaus and father of Euryalus, belongs to the generation of the Seven against Thebes (2.566, 23.677–680; see [[mecisteus]] and [[euryalus]]).
