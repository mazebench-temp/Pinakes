**The mace-man.** Nestor tells his story in passing, to explain the armor worn by Ereuthalion, the champion he killed in his youth (7.136–150; see [[ereuthalion]] and [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]). Areithous was a lord (ἄναξ, 7.137), whom “both men and lovely-girdled women” called κορυνήτης, “the mace-man” (7.138–139), because he did not fight with bows or the long spear but broke the battalions with an iron mace (7.140–141; see [[mace]] and [[iron]]). κορύνη and κορυνήτης occur nowhere else in Homer. His armor was a gift of Ares (7.146; see [[bronze-ares]]).

**His death.** Lycurgus killed him “by guile, not by strength at all” (7.142). The guile lay in the place and the moment: in a narrow road, where the mace could not save him, Lycurgus struck first and ran him through the middle with his spear, and Areithous fell on his back (7.143–145; see [[lycurgus-of-arcadia]]). He did not fight with the long spear, and a spear killed him. Lycurgus stripped the armor, wore it in battle, and in old age gave it to Ereuthalion (7.146–149).

**His son.** The same title marks him as the father of Menesthius of Arne, whom Paris kills at the start of the book, and whose mother was Phylomedusa (7.8–10; see [[menesthius]] and [[phylomedusa]]).

The name is the adjective ἀρηΐθοος, “swift in war”, said of young fighters (ἀρηϊθόων αἰζηῶν, 8.298, 15.315, 20.167), from Ἄρης and θοός (“swift”). The charioteer of Rhigmus whom Achilles kills bears it too (20.487).

## Outside Homer

Pausanias names his killer as Lycurgus son of Aleus, king of the Arcadians, who is remembered for killing Areithous by treachery and not in fair fight (8.4.10). He saw the tomb of Areithous in the territory of Mantinea, at a place called Phoezon, where the road grows very narrow (8.11.4); compare the “narrow road” of the Iliad (7.143).
