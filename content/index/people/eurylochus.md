## The name

Εὐρύλοχος is formed from εὐρύς, “wide”, and λόχος, “ambush, armed band”, the second part of the names Antilochus and Orsilochus in the Iliad.

## Family

The poem gives him no father, but makes him Odysseus' πηός, his kinsman by marriage: when Eurylochus calls him reckless, Odysseus thinks of striking off his head, “kinsman by marriage though he was, and very near” (*Odyssey* 10.441). W. W. Merry takes πηός as a connection by marriage, like the Latin *affinis*, with μάλα σχεδόν, “very near”, adding to it (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 10.441; see [[in-laws]]); Alcinous uses the word when he asks the weeping stranger whether some kinsman of his by marriage perished at Troy, “a son-in-law or a father-in-law, who are / the dearest after one's own blood and kin” (*Odyssey* 8.581–583). A later story, Merry notes, made Eurylochus the husband of Ctimene, Odysseus' sister, of whom Eumaeus says that he was brought up with her and that she was given in marriage to Same (Merry on *Odyssey* 10.441; *Odyssey* 15.363–367, a plain rendering).

## Descriptions

- θεοειδής, “godlike” (*Odyssey* 10.205; see [[godlike]]).
- μεγαλήτωρ, “great-hearted” (*Odyssey* 10.207; see [[great-hearted]]). Merry, writing on the word as it is said of the Cyclops a few lines before (*Odyssey* 10.200) and comparing this verse, allows that it may be only a standing epithet, or mean “stout-hearted”, or else “haughty”, as Nestor calls Agamemnon's headstrong temper his μεγαλήτωρ θυμός (σῷ μεγαλήτορι θυμῷ, *Iliad* 9.109, “your proud heart”) and the king of the Giants is “great-hearted Eurymedon” (*Odyssey* 7.58) (Merry on *Odyssey* 10.200).

## Outside Homer

In Ovid, Macareus tells how the lot sent him, “faithful Polites”, Eurylochus, Elpenor, “too fond of wine”, and twice nine companions to Circe's walls; when the others had drunk her cups and been shut in the sty, “we saw that Eurylochus alone lacked the form of swine: he alone fled the cups she offered”, and from him Ulysses learned of the disaster and came to Circe to avenge it (*Metamorphoses* 14.250–253, 14.286–290; see [[ovid]], [[polites-the-companion]] and [[elpenor]]).
