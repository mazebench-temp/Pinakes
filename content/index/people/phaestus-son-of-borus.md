He is the second of six men on the Trojan side killed by Achaean leaders when the Trojans give way at the start of the book (5.37–83). The poem gives him a father, a people and a home: Μῄονος υἱὸν Βώρου, “son of the Maeonian Borus, who had come from deep-soiled Tarne” (5.43–44; see [[maeonians]] and [[deep-soiled]]). His own name is that of a Cretan city, [[phaestus|Phaestus]] (2.648), and the man who kills him is the king of Crete.

Idomeneus stabs him “on the right shoulder as he was mounting his chariot” (5.46). He falls from the chariot, and “hateful darkness seized him” (5.47; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]]). The king does not strip the body himself: “the attendants of Idomeneus stripped him of his armor” (5.48). In Book 16 Idomeneus' squire Meriones kills Acamas with the same verse and the same fall (16.342–344). See [[idomeneus]] and [[androktasia]].
