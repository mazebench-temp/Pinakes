## The name

Εὐρύπυλος joins εὐρύς, “wide”, and πύλη, “gate”. Homer gives the name to two other men: the Thessalian leader Eurypylus, son of Euaemon, whom Paris wounds in the Iliad (see [[eurypylus]]), and the king of Cos, whose island the Catalogue calls “the city of Eurypylus” (see [[eurypylus-of-cos]]).

## Outside Homer

W. W. Merry gives the later story: he was the son of Telephus, king of the Mysians, by Astyoche, a sister or a daughter of Priam, and the last of the allies who came to the help of the Trojans, his mother having been persuaded to send him by the gift of a golden vine from Priam (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.520). Strabo, telling how Teuthras and Telephus ruled the country around Teuthrania and the Caicus, says that Homer mentions only so much of the story, “but what a man he slew with the bronze, the son of Telephus, / the hero Eurypylus”, and so sets a riddle rather than saying anything clear: “for we know neither who the Ceteians are to be taken to be, nor what ‘for the sake of gifts to a woman’ means”, and the grammarians, bringing in little myths, invent rather than solve the questions (*Geography* 13.1.69; see [[ceteians]] and [[strabo]]). Aristotle names a *Eurypylus* among the tragedies drawn from the *Little Iliad* (*Poetics* 1459b6; see [[eurypylus-play]] and [[neoptolemus-kills-eurypylus]]).
