κρατερός τʼ Ἐφιάλτης, “mighty Ephialtes” (5.385), is named with his brother at the head of Dione's examples of gods who suffered at the hands of men (see [[dione]]). The Iliad tells nothing of him apart from Otus; their story is told under [[otus]], [[the-aloadae-bind-ares]] and [[the-aloadae-threaten-olympus]]. The Odyssey calls him τηλεκλειτός, “far-famed” (*Odyssey* 11.308; see [[famous]]).

The name has been explained from ἐφάλλομαι, “leap upon”. In later Greek ἐφιάλτης is also the word for a nightmare, and it still is in Greek today. W. W. Merry reports two explanations that tie the name to the work of the farm, as he ties the names of Aloeus and Otus to the threshing floor: from ἐπί and ἰάλλω, “send”, as Eustathius read it, Ἐπιάλτης, so that Ephialtes tosses the threshed grain up for the wind to winnow; or from ἐφάλλομαι, of the leaping on the grapes in the wine-press (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.307).
