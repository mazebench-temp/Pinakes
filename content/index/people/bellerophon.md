## Etymology

Βελλεροφόντης (Bellerophontēs), in English usually shortened to Bellerophon. The ending looks like -φόντης, “slayer”, as in Hermes' title ἀργεϊφόντης (see [[argeiphontes]]), and later Greeks explained the name as “slayer of Belleros”, a Corinthian whose death drove the hero into exile. The first part has no convincing explanation, and the story looks invented to fit the name.

## Family

{{quote:08be46f7-65bf-59a7-b947-53f78d61e986}}

Son of [[glaucus-son-of-sisyphus|Glaucus]] and grandson of [[Sisyphus]], at [[ephyra-in-argos|Ephyra]] “in a corner of horse-pasturing Argos”. The king of Lycia gave him his daughter (6.192), and she bore him three children:

{{quote:2b9a4840-5398-5cbf-95d0-901be00285a3}}

[[Hippolochus]] is the father of the Lycian Glaucus, who tells the story, and [[Laodameia]] bore [[Sarpedon]] to Zeus, so the two Lycian leaders at Troy are his grandsons (see also [[isander]]). The Lycian king recognized him as “the noble offspring of a god” (θεοῦ γόνον ἠΰν, 6.191). Homer names Glaucus as his father, and the phrase may mean only descent from the gods; it may also point to the story, told by Hesiod and Pindar, that his father was Poseidon.

## Descriptions

ἀμύμων, “noble” (6.155, 6.190, 6.216; see [[blameless]]), and δαΐφρων, “wise-hearted” (6.162, 6.196; see [[wise-hearted]]). The gods gave him κάλλος (“beauty”) and ἠνορέη ἐρατεινή, “lovely manhood” (6.156–157), and Anteia could not persuade him, ἀγαθὰ φρονέοντα, “for his thoughts were good” (6.162). In the first half of his life the gods escort him and send him portents (6.171, 6.183); in the second he is “hated by all the gods” (6.200).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod names the Chimaera's killers as “Pegasus and noble Bellerophon” (*Theogony* 325). His winged Pegasus sprang from Medusa's neck when Perseus beheaded her, and carries the thunder and lightning of Zeus (280–286; see [[gorgon]]). A papyrus of the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* makes Bellerophon's mother Eurynome, the wife of Glaucus, and his father Poseidon, who gave him Pegasus; its broken lines go on to the fire-breathing Chimaera and a marriage to a king's daughter (fr. 43a Merkelbach–West, fr. 7 Evelyn-White). [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] calls his mother Eurymede (*Library* 1.9.3).

In [[pindar|Pindar]] he longs to bridle Pegasus by the spring at Corinth until Athena gives him a golden bridle in a dream and bids him sacrifice to Poseidon the Tamer, “your father”. From the horse's back he strikes down the Amazons, the Chimaera and the Solymi. Pindar keeps silent about his death, and says only that Pegasus is stabled in the mangers of Zeus on Olympus (*Olympian* 13.63–92). Elsewhere Pegasus throws his master when he tries to reach heaven and the company of Zeus (*Isthmian* 7.44–47). Strabo was shown the spring Peirene on Acrocorinth, where Bellerophon caught Pegasus as he drank (8.6.21), and Corinth stamped Pegasus on its coins.

[[euripides|Euripides]] wrote two tragedies about him, both lost. In the *Stheneboea*, named for Proetus' wife, the queen calls out to “the Corinthian guest” whenever something falls from her hand (fr. 664 Nauck), and an ancient summary tells how he punished her with a fall from Pegasus. In the *Bellerophon* the hero, bitter that tyrants and oath-breakers prosper while the pious suffer, declares that there are no gods in heaven (fr. 286 Nauck) and flies up on Pegasus (frr. 306–308); a fragment says that the horse went “under the chariot of Zeus” to carry his lightning (fr. 312). Aristophanes parodied the flight in *Peace* (421 BC), where Trygaeus rides a dung beetle to heaven.

Apollodorus names the Lycian king Iobates and Proetus' wife Stheneboea (*Library* 2.2.1); when Bellerophon has survived every task, Iobates shows him the letters and gives him his daughter Philonoe and, at his death, the kingdom (2.3.1–2). [Aristotle] cites 6.200–202 to show that Bellerophon was a melancholic who “sought out the wilderness” (*Problems* 30.1, 953a), and Cicero translated the lines (*Tusculan Disputations* 3.63).

The two motifs of the story have been compared with Near Eastern tales: the woman who fails to seduce a young man and then accuses him, as Potiphar's wife accuses Joseph (Genesis 39.7–20), and the letter that orders the death of its bearer, as David's letter about Uriah does (2 Samuel 11.14–15). Whether the Greek story borrowed from such tales or shares a common stock of motifs with them is debated (see [[bellerophons-letter]] and [[writing]]).
