summary: Athenian general (c. 415–353 BCE) of humble birth, who destroyed a Spartan regiment with light-armed troops in 390; his boast of his rise, “from what to what”, became a byword.

Iphicrates made his name in the Corinthian War, when his peltasts cut down a Spartan regiment near Lechaeum (390 BCE; Xenophon, *Hellenica* 4.5.11–17). Ancient writers credit him with lightening the arms of the infantry (Diodorus 15.44). He made his low birth a point of pride. When a descendant of the tyrant-slayer Harmodius reproached him with it, he answered that his own nobility began with him, and Harmodius' ended with him (Plutarch, *Sayings of Kings and Commanders*, *Moralia* 187B).

The *Rhetoric* quotes him more than any other speaker of the fourth century: in the speech against Harmodius (2.23, 1397b30–32, 1398a17–22; see [[iphicrates-against-harmodius]]), against Aristophon, who was asked whether he would betray the fleet for money (2.23, 1398a4–8; see [[iphicrates-and-aristophon]]), in his answer to those who made his son perform a public service (2.23, 1399a35–39), and in several turns of phrase in Book III (3.2, 1405a19–21; 3.10, 1411a11–13, 1411b2–3; 3.15, 1416a10–12).
