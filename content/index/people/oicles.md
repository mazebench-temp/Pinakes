Ἀντιφάτης μὲν ἔτικτεν Ὀϊκλῆα μεγάθυμον, / αὐτὰρ Ὀϊκλείης λαοσσόον Ἀμφιάραον, “Antiphates begot great-hearted Oicles, / and Oicles Amphiaraus, who rouses armies” (*Odyssey* 15.243–244; see [[antiphates-son-of-melampus]], [[amphiaraus]] and [[great-hearted]]). The poem names him in two verses of the pedigree of the seer Theoclymenus, and tells nothing else of him (see [[theoclymenus]] and [[melampus]]). The nominative is Ὀϊκλείης; tragedy has the genitive Οἰκλέους (Aeschylus, *Seven against Thebes* 609; Euripides, *Suppliant Women* 925). W. W. Merry, who spells it Oecles, sets him rightly in his table of the house, between Antiphates and Amphiaraus, but his note makes him a son of Melampus, “among other children”, which the poem does not (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.281).

## Outside Homer

He is known as the father of Amphiaraus. In the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* the two suitors of Helen from Argos are “the two sons of lord Amphiaraus, son of Oicles”, Ὀικλείδαο ἄνακτος (fr. 68 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]). In Aeschylus' *Seven against Thebes* Tydeus reviles “the wise seer, the son of Oicles”, μάντιν Οἰκλείδην σοφόν, and Eteocles speaks of “the seer, the son of Oicles I mean” (382, 609; see [[aeschylus-seven]]); in Euripides' *Suppliant Women* Theseus says that the gods snatched “the noble son of Oicles” alive into the depths of the earth with his four-horse chariot (925–927; see [[euripides]]).
