## Family

Antiope, the daughter of Asopus, bore Zeus “two sons, Amphion and Zethus, / who first founded the seat of seven-gated Thebes / and fenced it with towers” (*Odyssey* 11.262–264; see [[antiope]], [[amphion-of-thebes]], [[zeus]] and [[thebes]]). In *Odyssey* 19 Penelope likens her wavering heart to the nightingale, the daughter of Pandareus, who mourns her dear son Itylus, “whom once with the bronze / she killed in her folly, the son of lord Zethus” (*Odyssey* 19.522–523, a plain rendering). The poem does not say whether this Zethus is Antiope's son.

## Outside Homer

Euripides gave the brothers a famous debate in his lost *Antiope*: Zethus rebuked his brother for neglecting what he ought to care for and wasting a noble nature on music, and Plato's Callicles takes his part against Socrates, “what Zethus felt towards Amphion in Euripides”, while Socrates swears “by Zethus” in reply and would have paid Callicles back “with Amphion's speech for Zethus'” (Plato, *Gorgias* 485e–486c, 489e, 506b; see [[plato]] and [[euripides]]). Palaephatus reports that “Hesiod and others” told how Zethus and Amphion walled Thebes with the lyre (Palaephatus 42, printed as fr. 96 Evelyn-White among the fragments of the *Catalogue of Women*; see [[hesiod]]). In Euripides' *Phoenician Women* Antigone sees a warrior pass “around the tomb of Zethus” (145), and his Amphitryon calls the brothers “the two of the white horses, the offspring of Zeus” (*Heracles* 29–30). W. W. Merry notes that later mythology made Zethus a son of Epopeus, a mortal and the lesser of the two, as Castor beside Polydeuces and Iphicles beside Heracles (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.260).
