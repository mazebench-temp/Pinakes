The Catalogue introduces him by comparison. Homer has two heroes called Ajax, and this is the smaller: "not so great as Telamonian Ajax ([[telamonian]]), but much smaller" (2.528–529).

## Descriptions

- ταχύς "swift" ([[swift]]) is his mark. When the Trojans are routed he kills the most, "for there was none like him to pursue men in flight" (14.520–522).
- μείων "lesser", the comparative of μικρός, tells the two Ajaxes apart.
- λινοθώρηξ "with the linen corselet" = λίνον (linen) + θώρηξ (corselet) ([[with-linen-corselet]]). Homer uses it only of him and of the Trojan ally Amphius (2.830).
- ἐγχείῃ δ' ἐκέκαστο "with the spear he surpassed" (from κέκασμαι, "excel") all the [[Panhellenes]] and [[Achaeans]].

## The two Ajaxes

The dual Αἴαντε, "the two Ajaxes", pairs him with the son of Telamon. They are among the chief kings Agamemnon invites to his sacrifice (2.406), and in battle they stand together "like two wine-dark oxen" dragging a plough through fallow land (13.701–708). Some scholars think the dual once meant Ajax and his half-brother Teucer and was later understood as the two Ajaxes; this is a theory, not a fact. The Locrian has a half-brother of his own, the bastard [[Medon]], son of [[Oileus]] and Rhene, who takes command of Philoctetes' men (2.726–728; 13.694–697).

## Elsewhere in Homer

At Patroclus' funeral games he quarrels with Idomeneus about who is leading the chariot race, and Achilles stops them (23.473–498). In the footrace he is ahead when Athena makes him slip in the dung of the sacrificed cattle, and Odysseus wins; spitting out dung, he complains that the goddess always stands by Odysseus "like a mother" (23.754–783). The Odyssey tells his end. Poseidon saved him from shipwreck at the Gyraean rocks, but when he boasted that he had escaped the sea in spite of the gods, Poseidon split the rock with his trident and he drowned (Od. 4.499–511).

## Outside Homer

The *Iliupersis*, as summarized by [[proclus]], told how he dragged Cassandra away from the image of Athena during the sack of Troy, pulling the image down with her. The Achaeans wanted to stone him, and he escaped by taking refuge at Athena's altar; the goddess's anger fell on the whole fleet on its way home (see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]). Alcaeus told the story in a poem preserved on papyrus (fr. 298 Voigt). For the Locrians' atonement, the "Locrian Maidens", see [[Locrians]].
