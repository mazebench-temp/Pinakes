**A bastard son.** υἱὸν Πριάμοιο νόθον, “a bastard son of Priam” (4.499). A νόθος is a son born to a concubine rather than to the wedded wife (see [[nothos]]). Priam tells Achilles that he had fifty sons, nineteen of them from one womb, and the rest born to other women in his halls (24.495–497). The Iliad names several of his bastard sons, and all of them die in battle: Democoon here, Isus (11.101–109), Doryclus (11.489–490) and Hector's charioteer Cebriones (16.738). Democoon is the first son of Priam to die in the poem, and he is named only here. See [[sons-of-priam]].

**From Abydos.** ὅς οἱ Ἀβυδόθεν ἦλθε παρʼ ἵππων ὠκειάων, “who had come to him from Abydos, from his swift mares” (4.500). οἱ, “to him”, is Priam, and the adjective ὠκειάων is feminine: the horses are mares. The line is usually taken to mean that Democoon kept a stud of Priam's mares at [[Abydos]] on the Hellespont and came from there to fight for his father. The Trojans are “horse-taming” (see [[tamer-of-horses]]), and Abydos lay in the realm of Asius, who came to Troy behind “great and tawny horses” (2.835–839).

**The death.** Odysseus, enraged by the death of his companion [[Leucus]], comes up close, glances around him and throws, and the Trojans shrink back (4.494–498). The spear goes in at one temple (κόρση) and out at the other. Then comes the full formula of a warrior's fall: “darkness covered his eyes, and he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him” (4.503–504; see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]] and [[fell-with-a-thud]]). The Trojan champions and Hector give ground, and the Argives drag away the dead (4.505–506).

The name joins δῆμος (“people”) with a second element that is usually connected with κοέω (“perceive, heed”), as in Δηϊκόων (5.534) and Ἱπποκόων (10.518).
