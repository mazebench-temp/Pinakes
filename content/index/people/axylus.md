**The host.** The narrator gives Axylus three lines of life before his death. He “lived in well-built Arisbe, wealthy in substance” (6.13–14), in the words that described Diocles, the father of the twins whom Aeneas killed in Book 5: he “lived in well-built Pherae”, ἀφνειὸς βιότοιο (5.543–544; see [[diocles]] and [[wealthy]]). He was φίλος … ἀνθρώποισι, “a friend to all men” (6.14), and the next line says why: he lived beside the road and φιλέεσκεν, “used to welcome”, every passer-by. φιλέω is the verb of hospitality, as when Antenor “welcomed” Menelaus and Odysseus in his halls (3.207; see [[philos]] and [[xenia]]). The iterative form (-εσκ-) makes it his habit.

**No one to help.** “But not one of them was there then to keep grim destruction from him” (6.16). The men he had welcomed were not there to stand in front of him, and Diomedes kills him and his attendant together. The dual ends the scene: “the two of them went down beneath the earth” (6.19). The death is told in words used only here: ὑπαντιάσας (“meeting the enemy”, 6.17), ὑφηνίοχος (“charioteer”, 6.19) and γαῖαν ἐδύτην (“went down beneath the earth”). See [[death-in-battle]] and [[androktasia]].

**Before Glaucus.** The killer of the man who welcomed all comers finds a guest-friend a hundred lines later. Diomedes spares Glaucus because their grandfathers once exchanged gifts, and he proposes that they exchange armor so that “these men too may know that we claim to be guest-friends from our fathers' time” (6.215–231; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]). Hospitality saves one man in Book 6 and not the other.

His home, Arisbe, was one of the towns near the Hellespont led by Asius (2.835–839; see [[asius]]). His name is the adjective ἄξυλος, used once in Homer of a forest “uncut”, thick with timber (11.155). Whether the poet meant anything by it is unknown.
