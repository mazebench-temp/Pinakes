Peisander and Hippolochus ride in one chariot, and when the reins slip from their hands they beg Agamemnon to take them alive, offering the ransom their father would pay (11.126–135; see [[sons-of-antimachus]] and [[supplication]]). Agamemnon answers that they will pay for their father's counsel against the Achaean envoys (11.138–142; see [[antimachus]]). Then he “thrust Peisander from the chariot to the ground, / striking him in the chest with his spear, and he was flung on his back on the ground” (11.143–144). The last half-verse is the fall of Areithous (7.145) and returns for Antiphates (12.192).

He is named before his brother both times (11.122, 11.143), and he dies first. The name, from πείθω, “persuade”, and ἀνήρ, “man”, is borne by three more men in Homer.
