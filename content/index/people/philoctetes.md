## Etymology

Φιλοκτήτης combines φίλος ("dear, fond of") with the root of κτάομαι ("acquire"), as φιλοκτέανος ("fond of possessions, greedy") does in Achilles' insult to Agamemnon (1.122). What the name meant to those who gave it is not clear.

## Descriptions

τόξων ἐῢ εἰδώς, "well skilled with the bow" (2.718; see [[skilled-with-the-bow]]). His men are archers too: fifty rowers in each ship, "well skilled with the bow, to fight with might" (2.720). His father is [[Poias]]: Ποιάντιον ἀγλαὸν υἱόν, “the splendid son of Poias” (*Odyssey* 3.190).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the Odyssey Odysseus tells the Phaeacians that at Troy only Philoctetes surpassed him with the bow (*Odyssey* 8.219–220).

## Outside Homer

The *Cypria*, in Proclus' summary, placed the snakebite on Tenedos, during a feast, and said that the Achaeans left him on Lemnos because of the stench of the wound. In the *Little Iliad*, after the captured seer Helenus prophesied how Troy would be taken, Diomedes brought Philoctetes back from Lemnos; Machaon healed him, and he killed Paris in single combat.

His bow was the bow of [[Heracles]], given to him, or in some accounts to his father [[Poias]], for lighting Heracles' pyre on Mount Oeta (Sophocles, *Philoctetes*; Apollodorus 2.7.7). Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides all wrote a *Philoctetes*, and Dio Chrysostom compared the three (*Oration* 52), but only Sophocles' play (409 BC) survives. In it Lemnos is a deserted island (1–2), and Odysseus and Achilles' son Neoptolemus come to take the bow by trickery. Later legend sent Philoctetes to southern Italy, where he founded Petelia and other towns (Virgil, *Aeneid* 3.401–402). Seamus Heaney adapted Sophocles' play as *The Cure at Troy* (1990).
