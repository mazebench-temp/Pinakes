**Two brothers against two.** “And of the sons of Nestor, one stabbed Atymnius with his sharp spear, / Antilochus” (16.317–318): the narrator names the pair of killers first and then divides it, so that the sons of Nestor meet the sons of Amisodarus (see [[antilochus]], [[thrasymedes]] and [[nestor]]). Antilochus drives the bronze spear through Atymnius' flank, and he falls forward. His brother Maris springs at Antilochus, “angered for his brother, / standing in front of the corpse”, but Thrasymedes reaches him first (16.318–325; see [[maris]] and [[fighting-over-the-body]]). “So these two, brought down by two brothers, / went down to Erebus” (16.326–327; see [[erebus]] and [[kasignetos]]). They are the fourth and fifth of the nine men whom the Achaean leaders kill one by one when the Trojans are driven from the ships (16.306–351; see [[areilycus-killed-by-patroclus]]).

**Sarpedon's men.** They were “brave companions of Sarpedon, / the spearmen sons of Amisodarus, who reared the Chimaera” (16.327–328; see [[sarpedon]], [[amisodarus]] and [[chimaera]]). Through their father the brothers belong to the story of Bellerophon, Sarpedon's grandfather, who killed the Chimaera in Lycia (6.179–183, 6.196–199; see [[bellerophon-in-lycia]]). A little later Sarpedon sees “his companions, who wear their tunics without the band, / brought down at the hands of Patroclus”, and goes out to meet him (16.419–420; see [[lycians]]).

**The name.** Elsewhere in Homer Atymnius is only a father's name: Mydon, the charioteer of Pylaemenes, is “the brave son of Atymnius” (5.581; see [[atymnius]] and [[mydon]]). Antilochus kills both men, Mydon in *Iliad* 5 and this Atymnius here; the poem does not connect them.

## Outside Homer

In Apollodorus another Atymnius, son of Zeus and Cassiepea, is the boy whom, some said, Minos and Sarpedon, the sons of Europa, both loved and quarreled over; that Sarpedon went on to become king of Lycia, and Zeus granted him to live for three generations (*Library* 3.1.2; see [[apollodorus]]).
