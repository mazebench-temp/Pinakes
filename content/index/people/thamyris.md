Homer tells his story in seven lines of the Catalogue, attached to the town of [[Dorium]] in Nestor's kingdom (see [[thamyris-and-the-muses]]). He is "Thamyris the Thracian" (τὸν Θρήϊκα; see [[thracians]]), a traveling singer, coming "from Oechalia, from Eurytus of Oechalia", and he vowed that he would win even if the [[Muses]] themselves sang against him.

## Descriptions

- στεῦτο γὰρ εὐχόμενος: "he boasted and vowed". στεῦμαι is to declare, to promise stoutly; εὔχομαι here is to boast, not to pray.
- πηρὸν θέσαν: "they maimed him". πηρός means "disabled, crippled", and later "blind". Homer does not say what the Muses did to his body; later writers understood that they blinded him.
- ἀοιδὴν θεσπεσίην ἀφέλοντο: "they took away his wondrous song".
- ἐκλέλαθον κιθαριστύν: "they made him forget his lyre-playing". ἐκλέλαθον is a reduplicated aorist of ἐκλανθάνω, "make forget".

The Muses give song and can take it back. The Odyssey's blind singer Demodocus is the reverse case: the Muse "gave him both good and evil: she took away his eyes, but gave him sweet song" (Od. 8.63–64).

## Outside Homer

Later writers made him the son of Philammon and the nymph Argiope. Apollodorus gives the terms of the contest: if he won, he would lie with each of the Muses; if he lost, they could take from him whatever they wished; they won and took his eyes and his singing (1.3.3). Pausanias says that the Messenian river Balyra was named because Thamyris threw away his lyre there (4.33.3), and he describes Polygnotus' painting of the underworld at Delphi, where Thamyris sits blind, with a broken lyre at his feet (10.30.8). Sophocles wrote a *Thamyras*, now lost, in which he is said to have played the lyre himself. In Plato's myth of Er, the soul of Thamyras chooses to be reborn as a nightingale (*Republic* 10, 620a).
