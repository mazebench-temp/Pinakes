## Etymology

Ἀντίνοος is compounded of ἀντί (“against, facing”) and νόος (“mind”), like the name of the Phaeacian king Ἀλκίνοος, from ἀλκή (“strength”) and νόος (see [[alcinous]]).

## Family

He is introduced as his father's son in a verse first heard when he answers Telemachus, τὸν δʼ αὖτʼ Ἀντίνοος προσέφη, Εὐπείθεος υἱός, “Then Antinous, son of Eupeithes, spoke to him in turn” (*Odyssey* 1.383), and the patronymic returns in nine more verses of the poem. The family is of Ithaca. Penelope reminds him that his father once came to Odysseus' house as a fugitive, and that Odysseus saved him from the people (*Odyssey* 16.424–430; see [[eupeithes]]).

## Descriptions

The poem gives him the common epithet θεοειδής, “godlike” (*Odyssey* 21.277; see [[godlike]]), and, with Eurymachus, the rank of the suitors' leaders: ἀρχοὶ μνηστήρων, ἀρετῇ δʼ ἔσαν ἔξοχʼ ἄριστοι, “leaders of the suitors, and in excellence they were by far the best” (*Odyssey* 4.629 = 21.187; see [[eurymachus]] and [[the-suitors]]). Telemachus, as host, calls the two “kings”, “both wise” (βασιλῆες … πεπνυμένω ἄμφω, *Odyssey* 18.64–65; see [[wise]]). Others speak of his insolence. Telemachus says that he is always used to provoking with harsh words, and urges the others on (*Odyssey* 17.394–395); Penelope calls him ὕβριν ἔχων, κακομήχανε, “arrogant, evil-scheming” (*Odyssey* 16.418; see [[evil-scheming]] and [[hybris]]), and tells her housekeeper Eurynome that of all the suitors “Antinous most of all is like black death” (*Odyssey* 17.500). The renderings of later books here are plain renderings, not the translation.

## Elsewhere in Homer

When the ambush he laid for Telemachus between Ithaca and Samos fails (see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]), he proposes to kill Telemachus in the fields or on the road before he can call the Achaeans to an assembly, and to divide his possessions among the suitors (*Odyssey* 16.363–386). Penelope comes down and rebukes him in the hall (*Odyssey* 16.409–433).

When Odysseus comes to his own hall as a beggar, Antinous strikes him with a footstool (*Odyssey* 17.462–465; see [[footstool]]), sets him to fight the beggar Irus for a goat's paunch (*Odyssey* 18.34–50), and threatens him when he asks to try the bow (*Odyssey* 21.285–310). At the contest he orders the suitors to rise in turn (*Odyssey* 21.140–143), and when Eurymachus fails he puts off the trial, since it is a holy feast of the god (*Odyssey* 21.256–268). He is the first man Odysseus shoots, through the throat, as he lifts a golden cup to drink (*Odyssey* 22.8–21). Eurymachus then lays everything on him: he did not want the marriage so much as to be king of Ithaca himself and to kill Telemachus from ambush (*Odyssey* 22.48–53). In the underworld the shade of Amphimedon tells how Odysseus “struck lord Antinous” (βάλε δʼ Ἀντίνοον βασιλῆα, *Odyssey* 24.179). His father's grief for “Antinous, the first whom brilliant Odysseus killed”, leads the suitors' kin against Odysseus (*Odyssey* 24.422–437, 24.465–471; see [[eupeithes]]).
