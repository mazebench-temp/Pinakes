**From Thrace.** Rhigmus and his attendant are the last two men Achilles kills by name in the book, after Deucalion (20.478–489; see [[deucalion-the-trojan]], [[areithous-the-attendant]] and [[achilles]]). ὃς ἐκ Θρῄκης ἐριβώλακος εἰληλούθει, “who had come from deep-soiled Thrace” (20.485; see [[thrace]], [[thracians]] and [[deep-soiled]]): the verse-end introduces men from far away, Phaestus, son of the Maeonian Borus, “who had come from deep-soiled Tarne” (5.44), and the Paeonian Apisaon, “who had come from deep-soiled Paeonia” (17.350; see [[phaestus-son-of-borus]], [[tarne]] and [[apisaon-son-of-hippasus]]). The Thracian leader Peirous, whom the index takes for his father, “had come from Aenus” (4.520).

**His father.** μετʼ ἀμύμονα Πείρεω υἱὸν, “after the noble son of Peirous” (20.484; see [[blameless]]). Walter Leaf notes that Πείρεω is not a Homeric form, and refers it to a nominative Πείρεως, comparing Πείρως, the form the Catalogue's Πείροος takes at 4.520; he thinks Brandreth's Πειρόου doubtless right, which makes the father the Thracian leader of the Catalogue (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.484; see the translation's note on 20.484). That Peirous led the Thracians with Acamas, struck the Epeian leader Diores on the shin with a stone in the first battle and speared him, and was killed by the Aetolian Thoas (2.844, 4.517–531; see [[peirous]], [[diores]] and [[thoas]]). The poem never says that he had a son.

**The death.** “He hit him in the middle with a javelin, and the bronze stuck in his belly, / and he fell from the chariot” (20.486–487; see [[chariot]]). Many manuscripts read πνεύμονι, “in his lung”, for νηδύϊ, “in his belly”. Leaf takes it for a reminiscence of the verse in which Thoas' spear struck Peirous himself, “and the bronze stuck in his lung” (4.528), since μέσσον, “in the middle”, means the belly, not the chest (on 20.486; see the translation's note on 20.486). Then Achilles stabs Areithous in the back as he turns the horses, and the horses are thrown into confusion (20.487–489). The similes of the fire and the threshing floor follow (20.490–503; see [[forest-fire]] and [[bulls-on-the-threshing-floor]]).
