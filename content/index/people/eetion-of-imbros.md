**The ransom.** The narrator calls him only ξεῖνος, “a guest-friend” (21.42; see [[xenia]]), and says nothing more of the tie. He appears nowhere else. Achilles had taken Lycaon by night in Priam's orchard and sold him into Lemnos, “and the son of Jason paid the price” (21.35–41; see [[euneus]], [[lemnos]] and [[trade]]). Eetion bought him back, “and paid much” (21.42). Lycaon himself names both prices when he begs Achilles for his life: when he was sold, “I fetched you the worth of a hundred oxen”, and “now I have been ransomed, paying three times as much” (21.79–80; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). Walter Leaf takes the much that Eetion gave for this ransom, the worth of three hundred oxen (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.43 and 21.80; see [[apoina]]). The ransom is wasted: “For eleven days he delighted his heart with his friends, / after he came from Lemnos”, and on the twelfth a god threw him into Achilles' hands again (21.45–47; see [[the-death-of-lycaon]]).

**Imbros and Arisbe.** Ἴμβριος, “of Imbros”, distinguishes him from the father of Andromache, king of the Cilicians at Thebe under Placus (Leaf on 21.43; see [[eetion]] and [[imbros]]); a third Eetion is the father of Podes (17.575; see [[eetion-father-of-podes]]). Imbros lies off the Thracian Chersonese, northwest of the mouth of the Hellespont, and Lemnos beyond it to the southwest. Eetion sent Lycaon on to “shining Arisbe”, a town on the Hellespont, the home of Asius (21.43; 2.836–838; see [[arisbe]]), “and from there he escaped and came to his father's house” (21.44). Leaf reads in ὑπεκπροφυγών, “escaped”, that Lycaon was meant to be kept in custody there for his own safety (on 21.44). The islands of the north Aegean were markets for the captives of the war: Hecuba says that Achilles sold her sons across the sea, to Samos, Imbros and Lemnos (24.751–753; see [[slavery-and-freedom]]).
