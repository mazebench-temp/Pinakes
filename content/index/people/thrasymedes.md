**The name.** Θρασυμήδης joins θρασύς (“bold”) and μήδεα (“counsels”), as in Diomedes: “bold in counsel”. The poem gives him a range of standing titles: “shepherd of the people” (9.81; see [[shepherd-of-the-people]]), “steadfast in war” (μενεπτόλεμος, 10.255; Od. 3.442), “tamer of horses” (14.10), “godlike” (ἀντίθεος, 16.321; Od. 3.414) and “high-hearted” (Od. 3.448).

**The watch.** His father proposed the watch in the assembly (9.66–68), and Thrasymedes is named first of the seven captains, each with a hundred young men, who sit between the ditch and the wall that night (9.80–88; see [[night-watch]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). Νεστορίδην, “son of Nestor”, names him by his father, as the patronymic Νεστορίδης names Antilochus (6.33) and, in the Odyssey, Peisistratus (see [[nestor]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The watch goes on in Book 10. Agamemnon tells Menelaus that the guards are led by Nestor's son and by Meriones, for “to them above all we entrusted it” (10.56–59; see [[meriones]]). The kings call the son of Nestor and Meriones to their council across the ditch (10.196–197), the son of Nestor is eager to go with Diomedes on the raid (10.229), and when Diomedes and Odysseus arm, Thrasymedes gives Diomedes a two-edged sword, a shield and a leather cap (10.255–259). In Book 14 Nestor takes up the shield of his son, “Thrasymedes, tamer of horses”, which lay in the hut, while the son carried his father's (14.9–11). In Book 16 the two sons of Nestor kill two sons of Amisodarus, Sarpedon's companions: Antilochus kills Atymnius, and when Maris attacks his brother, Thrasymedes strikes first, at the shoulder (16.317–329). In Book 17 he and Antilochus, fighting apart as Nestor had ordered, have not yet heard that Patroclus is dead (17.377–383), and when Antilochus runs with the news, Menelaus sends Thrasymedes to the leaderless Pylians (17.702–706).

The Odyssey finds him at home. When Telemachus lands at Pylos, Peisistratus seats him at the feast on the shore beside “his brother Thrasymedes” and their father (Od. 3.36–39). He is one of Nestor's six sons (Od. 3.412–415), and at the sacrifice to Athena it is Thrasymedes who stands by with the axe and fells the heifer (Od. 3.442–450). Antilochus, his brother at Troy, did not come home; the son of the Dawn killed him (Od. 3.111–112, 4.187–188).
