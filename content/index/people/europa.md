## In Homer

Homer does not name her. In the list of his loves Zeus calls her Φοίνικος κούρη, “the daughter of far-famed Phoenix”, and of all the sons in the list hers are the only ones he calls his own: ἣ τέκε μοι, “who bore me” (14.321–322; see [[phoenix-father-of-europa]], [[minos]], [[rhadamanthus]] and [[the-loves-of-zeus]]). The Iliad gives Sarpedon, whom later writers make her third son, another mother, Laodameia, Bellerophon's daughter (6.198–199; see [[sarpedon]] and [[laodameia]]).

## Outside Homer

**The bull.** The scholia on the Iliad tell her story from Hesiod and Bacchylides. Zeus saw Europa, the daughter of Phoenix, gathering flowers in a meadow with the nymphs, and fell in love with her. He came down, changed himself into a bull, and breathed saffron from his mouth; so he deceived her, carried her across the sea to Crete and lay with her there, and afterward he gave her as wife to Asterion, king of the Cretans. She bore three sons, Minos, Sarpedon and Rhadamanthys (fr. 19 Evelyn-White, from the scholia on 12.292). The papyrus fragments of the *Catalogue of Women* call her “slender-ankled Europa”, who crossed the salt water “overcome by the wiles of Zeus”, and name “just Rhadamanthys” among her sons (fr. 19a Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]).

**The daughter of Agenor.** In [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] she is the daughter of Agenor, who had settled in Phoenicia, and Telephassa, though some made her the daughter of Phoenix. Zeus loved her, became a tame bull, and carried her on his back through the sea to Crete, where she bore Minos, Sarpedon and Rhadamanthys; “but according to Homer”, he adds, Sarpedon was the son of Laodameia. Asterius, the ruler of the Cretans, married her and raised her sons, and her brothers, sent by Agenor to find her, never came home, but settled where their search had taken them (*Library* 3.1.1–2; see [[cadmus]]). Herodotus too has Cadmus, son of Agenor, “searching for Europa” when he put in at Thera (Herodotus 4.147.4).

**Aeschylus.** In the *Carians* or *Europa*, Europa herself tells the story: the bull, the theft that Zeus contrived from her aged father, her three sons, Minos first, then Rhadamanthys, “who of my children is immortal”, and now her fear for the third, Sarpedon, gone to the war at Troy (fr. 99 Nauck; see [[aeschylus]]).

**Herodotus.** The Persians' version of the quarrel between Europe and Asia makes her one of the stolen women: after the Phoenicians had carried off Io, some Greeks, Herodotus guesses Cretans, put in at Tyre and carried off the king's daughter Europa, and so the account was even (1.2.1; see [[herodotus]] and [[who-is-to-blame]]). He doubts that the continent was named after her, since Europa was from Asia and never came to the land the Greeks call Europe, but only from Phoenicia to Crete and from Crete to Lycia (4.45.4–5). Her sons Minos and Sarpedon quarreled over the kingship of Crete, and Minos drove Sarpedon out to the land that is now Lycia (1.173.2).

Ovid's Arachne weaves her on the bull at sea, looking back at the land she has left and calling to her companions (*Metamorphoses* 6.103–107). Hesiod names another Europa among the daughters of Oceanus and Tethys (*Theogony* 357).
