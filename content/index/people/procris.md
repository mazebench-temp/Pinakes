## The name

W. W. Merry explains Πρόκρις as ἡ προκεκριμένη, “the chosen one”, Latin *eximia*, from προκρίνω, “prefer” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.321).

## In Homer

Homer names her once, between Phaedra and Ariadne: “And I saw Phaedra and Procris and beautiful Ariadne” (*Odyssey* 11.321; see [[phaedra]] and [[ariadne]]). The three names, Merry notes, belong to Attic legend, and he thinks the verses a later interpolation (on *Odyssey* 11.321). He gives her the usual family: she was the daughter of Erechtheus, king of Athens, and the wife of Cephalus (see [[erechtheus]]).

## Outside Homer

**Merry's account.** The story of her fate, Merry says, takes many different forms, but Cephalus generally appears as a young hunter carried off by Dawn for his beauty, as in Euripides' *Hippolytus*, where the nurse argues that the gods themselves yield to love: those who know the writings of the old poets know that Zeus once loved Semele, and that “the fair-shining Dawn once snatched Cephalus up to the gods / for love” (*Hippolytus* 451–456; see [[eos]] and [[euripides]]); other forms made Cephalus a son of Hermes by Herse, daughter of Cecrops. In the story as Merry tells it, Dawn tempted Cephalus to test his wife's faithfulness; when it failed, Procris fled to Crete and joined the huntress Artemis, and coming back to her husband she brought him the famous javelin that never missed; at last, suspecting that he visited a lover, she followed him to the woods and hid in a thicket, and he, seeing something move, threw the javelin, and Procris fell by her own weapon. If a nature myth is sought in the legend, Merry suggests, Cephalus may be the morning star and Procris, “the chosen one”, the moon, dying pale before the darts of the rising day; and since the mother of Cephalus is called Herse, “Dew”, Dawn's carrying him off may have to do with the morning rays drying up the dew (on *Odyssey* 11.321).

**The javelin of Procris.** Eustathius, as Merry reports him, says that she was famed for her courage and that a proverb came from her, “the javelin of Procris”, of a dart that never misses its mark (on *Odyssey* 11.321). The Suda gives the proverb, Πρόκριδος ἄκοντα, “said of those who hit every mark; for Procris, having such a javelin, hunted everything” (Suda π 2484).

**Ovid.** In Ovid's *Metamorphoses* Cephalus tells the story to Phocus, who has admired his javelin; one of the young Athenians with him explains that it overtakes whatever it is aimed at and flies back bloodied with no one to bring it (*Metamorphoses* 7.681–684). Procris, Cephalus says, was the sister of the ravished Orithyia, and her father Erechtheus joined her to him (*Metamorphoses* 7.694–697); Aurora carried him off against his will from the top of Hymettus as he set nets for deer (*Metamorphoses* 7.700–704). Overcome by shame after he had tested her faithfulness, she fled the house and, hating the whole race of men, wandered the mountains devoted to Diana's pursuits; when he begged her pardon she came back, and gave him a hound that Diana had given her, and the javelin (*Metamorphoses* 7.743–756). Hunting at dawn, he called on the breeze, *aura*, to come and cool him, and when he heard a rustle in the leaves he thought it a beast and threw: “it was Procris”, struck in the breast, drawing from her wound the gift she had given him (*Metamorphoses* 7.835–846; see [[ovid]]). In Virgil she is among the women of the Fields of Mourning, with Phaedra and Eriphyle (*Aeneid* 6.440–447; see [[virgil]], [[phaedra]] and [[eriphyle]]).
