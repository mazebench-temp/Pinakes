## Descriptions

μεγαλήτωρ "great-hearted" ([[great-hearted]]) = μέγας (great) + ἦτορ (heart).

τέκε δὲ ζείδωρος ἄρουρα: "the grain-giving earth bore him" ([[grain-giving]]; ζείδωρος = ζειά, "spelt, grain", + δῶρον, "gift"). He had no human mother. Later Athenians made this birth from the soil the charter of their claim to be autochthonous, sprung from their own land.

θρέψε "raised, reared": [[Athena]] brought him up and "set him down in Athens, in her own rich temple" (2.549).

## The cult

"And there with bulls and rams they seek his favor, the young men of the Athenians, as the years come round" (2.550–551). The pronoun μιν, "him", may refer to Erechtheus or to Athena, and the Greek does not settle it; the translation takes it as Erechtheus. Either way, hero and goddess share a house. In the Odyssey, when Athena goes to Athens, she enters "the strong house of Erechtheus" (Od. 7.80–81). See [[athena-and-erechtheus]].

## Outside Homer

Herodotus saw on the Acropolis "a temple of Erechtheus, who is said to be earth-born", with the olive tree and the salt spring left by Athena's contest with Poseidon (8.55). He says that the Athenians, earlier called Cecropidae, took the name Athenians when Erechtheus became king (8.44). In Euripides' *Erechtheus* (about 422 BC; fragments), the king sacrifices a daughter to save the city in its war with Eumolpus of Eleusis and is struck down by Poseidon; the great speech of his wife Praxithea is quoted by Lycurgus (*Against Leocrates* 100). Later mythographers split the figure in two: Erichthonius, born from the earth after Hephaestus' seed fell on it, raised by Athena and entrusted in a chest to the daughters of Cecrops (Euripides, *Ion* 20–26, 267–274; Apollodorus 3.14.6), and a later king Erechtheus (Apollodorus 3.15.1–5). The Erechtheion, built on the Acropolis in the late fifth century BC, housed the cults of Athena Polias, of Poseidon and of Erechtheus.
