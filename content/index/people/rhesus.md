## Etymology

Ῥῆσος has no clear Greek etymology. A river of the Troad bears the same name, one of the rivers flowing from Ida that Poseidon and Apollo turn against the Achaean wall (12.17–24; see [[the-achaean-wall]]).

## Family

Dolon names him as “Rhesus, their king, the son of Eioneus” (10.435; see [[eioneus-father-of-rhesus]]), and Homer says no more of his parents. His cousin (ἀνεψιός) Hippocoon is a counselor of the Thracians (10.518–519; see [[hippocoon]]).

## Descriptions

{{quote:da76b026-1430-5e60-876b-d87c8e405607}}

He is βασιλεύς, “king” (10.435, 10.494), and ἄναξ, “lord” (10.559; see [[kings]] and [[anax]]), of Thracians who are νεήλυδες, “newly come” (10.434, 10.558; see [[newly-come]]). His horses are the finest Dolon has seen (see [[the-horses-of-rhesus]] and [[whiter-than-snow]]), “and his chariot is finely worked with gold and silver; / and armor of gold, huge, a wonder to see, / he brought with him; it is not fit at all for mortal / men to wear, but for the immortal gods” (10.438–441; see [[gold-and-silver]]). Glaucus too wore golden armor, and gave it to Diomedes for bronze (6.234–236; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]). Rhesus' chariot and armor are left behind: Diomedes thinks of carrying off the chariot “where the inlaid armor lay”, and Athena sends him away (10.503–511).

## In Homer

**Newly come.** The Thracians lie apart from the rest, “the farthest out of all” (10.434; see [[thracians]]). The spies find them asleep, their armor laid in three rows and a yoke of horses beside each man, and Rhesus in the middle, his horses tied to the rail of his chariot (10.470–475). Odysseus sees him first: “Here is the man, Diomedes, and here are the horses / that Dolon, whom we killed, told us about” (10.477–478; see [[dolon]]).

**The thirteenth.** Athena breathes might into Diomedes, and he kills twelve men like a lion among flocks that have no herdsman, while Odysseus drags the bodies aside so that the horses will not shy (10.482–493; see [[lion-among-the-flocks]] and [[diomedes]]). Then the king:

{{quote:bf31520c-0daa-5b09-9018-98f86bbca9a0}}

The “evil dream” is Diomedes himself, standing over the sleeper's head as dreams stand over the heads of sleepers (2.20, 2.59; 23.68), by Athena's design; the verses can also be read of a dream in which Rhesus saw him, and ancient editors are reported to have rejected the second of them (see the translation's note on 10.494–497, [[the-evil-dream]], [[onar]] and [[athena]]). Odysseus drives off the horses, and Apollo wakes Rhesus' cousin Hippocoon, who finds the place where they stood empty (10.498–522; see [[apollo]]). At the ships Odysseus reports that “their lord was killed by brave Diomedes, / and beside him twelve companions, all of the best” (10.559–560).

Rhesus never fights and never speaks. Dolon describes him, Diomedes kills him asleep, Odysseus reports his death, and the poem does not name him again (see [[the-doloneia]]).

## Outside Homer

The tragedy *Rhesus*, handed down among the plays of Euripides, is named after him (see [[euripides-rhesus]]). There he is the son of the river Strymon and a Muse, who conceived him on her way to the Muses' contest with Thamyris (279, 919–931; see [[thamyris]] and [[muses]]); he comes late, boasting that one day will be enough for him to end the war (443–453), and Athena sends Odysseus and Diomedes to kill him, since if he lives till morning no one could save the ships (600–604). At the end his mother carries in his body and foretells that he will not go down to Hades but live on, hidden in a cave of the silver land, as a man-god (ἀνθρωποδαίμων, 962–973). Apollodorus names the Muse Euterpe, or, as some said, Calliope (1.3.4). In the *Epitome* he had arrived the day before and had not yet fought when Odysseus and Diomedes killed him and the twelve men sleeping around him (*Epitome* 4.4). In Carthage Aeneas weeps to see “the tents of Rhesus with their snow-white canvas”, betrayed in their first sleep (Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.469–473; see [[virgil-rhesus-and-dolon]]), and Ovid's Ulysses boasts of killing him in his own camp and riding back in his chariot like a man in a triumph (*Metamorphoses* 13.249–252; see [[ovid-dolon-and-rhesus]]).
