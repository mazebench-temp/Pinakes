## Etymology

Δόλων looks like a name made from δόλος, “trick, snare”: a trickster's name for a spy. The book that tells his story was called the Δολώνεια after him (see [[the-doloneia]] and [[title-of-book-10]]), and the *Rhesus* plays on the name: Hector calls him “well named indeed” (ἐπώνυμος μὲν κάρτα, 158), and Dolon calls his disguise a δόλος (215). In Homer the tricks belong to the other side: Odysseus is δόλων ἆτʼ, “insatiable of tricks” (11.430; *Odyssey* 13.293). Whether the name was made for the story cannot be known.

## Family

{{quote:d76cc4d0-d04d-5abd-af0c-ae39d974e77b}}

His father is a herald, and θεῖος, “divine”, is a herald's epithet (4.192; see [[eumedes]] and [[heralds]]). “Rich in gold, rich in bronze” is said once more in the Iliad, of Priam's city before the war (18.288–289; see [[rich-in-gold]] and [[brazen]]), and the wealth stored at home is the ransom Dolon will offer (10.378–381). As the only son among five sisters (see [[kasignetos]]) he is the heir. The poem names neither his mother nor his sisters.

## Descriptions

εἶδος μὲν ἔην κακός, ἀλλὰ ποδώκης, “ill-favored to look at, but swift-footed” (10.316; see [[eidos]]). Aristotle took it of an ugly face, not a misshapen body (*Poetics* 25, 1461a; see [[aristotle-dolons-face]]). ποδώκης is elsewhere the epithet of Achilles (2.860, 8.474; see [[swift-footed]]), whose horses Dolon wants. Odysseus fears that he may outrun them (10.346), but the two cut him off (10.358–364).

## In Homer

**The volunteer.** Hector offers a chariot and the two best horses at the Achaean ships to a man who will spy on the ships (10.303–312; see [[hector]]). The council is silent, in the verse that met Nestor's call in the Achaean council (10.313 = 10.218), and Dolon answers in Diomedes' words there: “Hector, my heart and my proud spirit urge me” (10.319; 10.220). His price is “the horses and the chariot inlaid with bronze / that carry the noble son of Peleus” (10.322–323; see [[the-horses-of-achilles]]), and he asks Hector to swear by his scepter:

{{quote:eb8ca461-10ef-5e52-b297-7e7e43eaa2a4}}

The oath proves false because Dolon never comes back; the narrator does not say that Hector meant to deceive (see the translation's note on 10.330–332 and [[oath]]). Its words come true another way: no Trojan ever rides behind those horses, and Zeus says that Hector himself will not (17.448–449).

**The capture.** He arms with a curved bow, “the skin of a gray wolf” and “a cap of ferret skin”, and a javelin, and the narrator looks ahead: “but he was not to / come back from the ships and bring word to Hector” (10.333–337; see [[wolf-skin]], [[ferret]] and [[arming-scene]]). He runs past the two spies lying among the dead “in his thoughtlessness” (10.350; see [[ambush]]), stops at the sound of their steps, hoping that Hector has sent companions to call him back (10.354–356), and is run down like a fawn or a hare by two hounds (10.360–364; see [[two-hounds]]). When Diomedes' spear flies wide on purpose, he stands “stammering” and “pale with fear”, and he offers a ransom in Adrastus' verses (10.372–381; 10.379–381 ≈ 6.48–50; see [[pale-fear]] and [[apoina]]).

**The questioning.** Odysseus' “Take heart” promises nothing (10.383). Dolon blames Hector, “With many blind follies Hector led me astray from my good sense” (10.391; see [[ate]]), and repeats Hector's errand in Hector's words, down to “beaten down already by our hands” (10.396–399 = 10.309–312). He tells of Hector's council “by the mound of divine Ilus”, of the Trojans awake at their fires and the allies asleep, and of where each people lies (10.413–431; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]], [[night-watch]] and [[trojan-allies]]). Of Hector's armor and horses, which Odysseus asked about (10.407), he says nothing. Then, unasked, he points the two to the newly come Thracians and their king (10.432–441; see [[rhesus]]).

**The death.** Diomedes, looking at him darkly, answers that a spy set free comes back (10.446–453; see [[looking-darkly]]).

{{quote:6875253e-0325-5834-9dd7-dc780f9ab8cf}}

The sword falls as he reaches for Diomedes' chin, the suppliant's gesture (see [[supplication]]). No one who begs for his life on the battlefield of the Iliad is spared (6.45–65, 11.130–147, 20.463–472, 21.64–119; see [[adrastus-the-trojan]] and [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]). The look, the blow to the middle of the neck and the head in the dust return together in the Odyssey, when Odysseus kills the suppliant Leodes (*Odyssey* 22.320, 22.328–329; see [[aristotle-the-speaking-head]]). Whether the poem shows the killing as a necessity of war or as a cruelty is disputed (see [[the-doloneia]]).

**The spoils.** The cap, the wolfskin, the bow and the spear, the javelin now become “the long spear”, are held up to Athena, “goddess of the spoil”, and later laid in a ship's stern for an offering (10.458–468, 10.526–529, 10.570–571; see [[stripping-the-dead]], [[of-the-spoil]] and [[dedication-of-spoils]]). In Odysseus' report Dolon is “a thirteenth, a spy” (10.561; see [[spies-and-scouts]] and the translation's note on 10.559–561).

## Outside Homer

In the *Rhesus*, handed down among the plays of Euripides, Dolon turns down Hector's offers of a royal marriage and gold and asks for Achilles' horses, and Hector, who loves them himself, promises them without an oath (*Rhesus* 154–194). He means to go out in a wolf's skin with its jaws over his head, on all fours like a wolf, and boasts that he will bring back the head of Odysseus or Diomedes (208–223). He is killed off stage (591–593; see [[euripides-rhesus]]).

In the *Aeneid* his son Eumedes, “bearing his grandfather's name”, is killed by Turnus; his father had asked for Achilles' chariot as his price, and Diomedes paid him “another price” (*Aeneid* 12.346–361; see [[virgil-rhesus-and-dolon]]). Ovid's Ajax scorns Ulysses' night work against Rhesus and “unwarlike Dolon” (*Metamorphoses* 13.98–100), and Ulysses boasts that he made Dolon betray everything before he killed him (13.243–246; see [[ovid-dolon-and-rhesus]]). For Servius, Rhesus was killed “betrayed by the Trojan Dolon” (*Dolone prodente*, on *Aeneid* 1.469).
