## The name

Αἴολος has the letters of the adjective αἰόλος, “flashing” of a shield or of a horse's feet, “nimble” of wasps, “writhing” of a snake (*Iliad* 7.222, 19.404, 12.167, 12.208). W. W. Merry takes both his names, Αἴολος and Ἱπποτάδης, as describing the rapid movement of the wind, the second from ἵππος, “horse”, and compares Sophocles' Boreas, ἅμιππος, “swift as horses” (*Antigone* 985). He counts three Aeoluses in myth: a son of Poseidon; the son of Hellen, whose son Cretheus is “son of Aeolus” in the Odyssey (Κρηθῆος … Αἰολίδαο, *Odyssey* 11.237; see [[aeolus-father-of-sisyphus]]); and this son of Hippotes, whose mother he names as Melanippe (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 10.1). In the genitive Αἰόλου (*Odyssey* 10.36) the ο may be taken as lengthened before the liquid λ, unless one writes, with Ahrens, the old genitive Αἰόλοο (Merry on *Odyssey* 10.36).

## Family

He is Ἱπποτάδης, “son of Hippotes” (*Odyssey* 10.2, 10.36; see [[hippotes]]). He lives with his wife, the “devoted mother” of his children (*Odyssey* 10.8, 10.61), and their twelve children, whom he has married to one another:

The marriages, Merry thinks, point to no custom of any age or country, but show the loneliness of the island and how little its people had to do with the rest of mankind: the brothers married their sisters because there were no other women to marry (Merry on *Odyssey* 10.5; see [[marriage]]).

## Descriptions

- φίλος ἀθανάτοισι θεοῖσιν, “dear to the immortal gods” (*Odyssey* 10.2). He is not a god himself in Homer: not until the Alexandrians, Merry notes, is he spoken of as one, and here he is only the gods' friend and the keeper of the winds by the order of Zeus (Merry on *Odyssey* 10.1).
- ταμίης ἀνέμων, “dispenser of the winds”, the office that the son of Cronus gave him, “both to still and to rouse whichever he wishes” (*Odyssey* 10.21–22; see [[dispenser-of-the-winds]] and [[zeus]]).
- μεγαλήτωρ, “great-hearted”, in the companions' grudging talk of his gifts: “gifts from Aeolus, great-hearted son of Hippotes” (*Odyssey* 10.36; see [[great-hearted]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

When Odysseus tells Penelope the story of his wanderings, Aeolus comes after the Lotus-eaters and the Cyclops: “and how he came to Aeolus, who received him with a ready heart / and sent him on his way; but it was not yet his lot to reach his dear native land, / but the storm wind snatched him up again / and carried him, groaning heavily, over the fish-filled sea” (*Odyssey* 23.314–317, a plain rendering; see [[odysseus-and-aeolus]]).

## Outside Homer

**The islands of Aeolus.** Strabo held that Homer took the starting points of the wanderings from history: Aeolus, it is said, ruled the islands around Lipara, as the Cyclopes and the Laestrygonians held the country about Aetna and Leontini (*Geography* 1.2.9; see [[strabo]]). He reports with approval the view of Polybius, that Aeolus, who told sailors when to put out in the region of the strait, where the reversing currents make departure difficult, was called the dispenser of the winds and held to be a king, and Polybius' rejection of Eratosthenes' jest that one will find where Odysseus wandered when one finds the cobbler who stitched the skin of the winds (*Geography* 1.2.15; see [[skin-of-the-winds]]). The seven Liparaean islands, “which some call the islands of Aeolus”, have hot springs and vents of fire; from the roaring of the craters on the island sacred to Hephaestus, and from the place where the blasts, the flames and the smoke begin, Polybius says, the wind that will blow on the third day can be foretold, and when the weather kept men from sailing at Lipara some foretold it without error; so the poet's most fabulous saying, that Aeolus is dispenser of the winds, was not idly said but hints at the truth (*Geography* 6.2.10). On Strongyle, “they say that Aeolus lived” (*Geography* 6.2.11). Merry notes the same transfer of the legend to the Liparaean or Aeolian islands, to Lipara or to Strongyle, citing Strabo and Pliny's “Strongyle, in which Aeolus reigned” (*Natural History* 3.9, as Merry quotes him), but finds little advantage in seeking an exact position for a place in the region of fable (Merry on *Odyssey* 10.1).

**The king of the winds.** Virgil made him a king. His Aeolus, *rex Aeolus*, keeps the struggling winds in a vast cave in Aeolia, the country of the storm clouds, and rules them with his command, with chains and a prison (*Aeneid* 1.51–54); at Juno's request he strikes the hollow mountain with his spear, and the winds rush out where a door is given them (*Aeneid* 1.81–83; see [[virgil]] and [[dispenser-of-the-winds]]). In Ovid, Odysseus' companion Macareus tells of “Aeolus, son of Hippotes”, who reigns over the Tuscan sea and holds the winds in prison, and gave the Dulichian leader the winds shut in an ox's hide, “a memorable gift”; after nine days with a following wind the land they sought was in sight, and on the next morning the companions, overcome by envy and the desire for plunder and thinking it was gold, untied the bonds of the winds (*Metamorphoses* 14.223–230; see [[ovid]]). Ovid also gives the keeper of the winds a daughter, Alcyone, who warns her husband Ceyx not to trust that his father-in-law, the son of Hippotes, “holds the strong winds in prison” and calms the seas when he wishes (*Metamorphoses* 11.431–432).

**The scholia.** The scholia, as Merry reports them, give Aeolus a wife, Telepatra, daughter of Laestrygon; the allegorists made him the year, his six sons the six colder months and his six daughters the six warmer. A scholiast defends the marriages of the brothers and sisters as an ancient custom, for Zeus too lives with his sister Hera, and praises such unions at length, which Merry takes for flattery of the Ptolemies, who married their sisters (Merry on *Odyssey* 10.5).
