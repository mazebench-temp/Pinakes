**The bastard and the true-born.** νόθον καὶ γνήσιον, “one a bastard and one born in wedlock” (11.102). γνήσιος occurs only here in the Iliad; in the Odyssey Odysseus, telling a false tale, sets himself, the son of a bought mother, beside his father's sons “born in wedlock” (Od. 14.202–203). Isus is the νόθος (see [[nothos]]), and he drives, while “renowned Antiphus” stands beside him to fight (11.103–104). Another bastard son of Priam, Cebriones, drives for Hector (8.318–319, 16.738; see [[cebriones]]), and two more die in the war, Democoon (4.499) and Doryclus (11.489–490; see [[democoon]], [[doryclus]] and [[sons-of-priam]]).

**Taken once before.** Achilles had caught the two brothers on the slopes of Ida as they herded their flocks, bound them with willow shoots, and let them go for a ransom (11.104–106; see [[achilles]], [[ida]] and [[apoina]]). Agamemnon knows them, for he had seen them at the ships when Achilles brought them in (11.111–112). Achilles would later say that before Patroclus died he spared many Trojans and sold them (21.100–102).

**The second time.** Agamemnon spears Isus in the chest above the nipple, strikes Antiphus beside the ear with his sword, and in haste strips them both (11.107–112; see [[stripping-the-dead]]). The narrator likens him to a lion that crushes the young of a hind in their lair while the mother, close by, can do nothing (11.113–121; see [[the-lion-and-the-fawns]]). No Trojan could help them, “but they themselves were fleeing before the Argives” (11.121).

The name looks like the adjective ἶσος, “equal”. The poem names him nowhere else.
