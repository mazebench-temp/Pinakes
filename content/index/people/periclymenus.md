## Family

Chloris bore Neleus “splendid children, / Nestor and Chromius and proud Periclymenus”, and then Pero (*Odyssey* 11.285–287; see [[chloris]], [[neleus]], [[nestor]], [[chromius-son-of-neleus]] and [[pero]]). The Iliad does not name him, but Nestor's tale of the twelve sons of Neleus, of whom he alone survived Heracles' attack on Pylos, includes him (*Iliad* 11.690–693; see [[heracles-sacks-pylos]]).

## Descriptions

ἀγέρωχος, “proud” (*Odyssey* 11.286; see [[proud]]). The word, W. W. Merry notes, “has thoroughly exercised the ingenuity of etymologists”: the older commentators took it for γεραόχος, “holding a prize of honor”, Hesychius and the *Etymologicum Magnum* derived it from gathering food, and Buttmann and Lobeck despaired of a derivation (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.286).

## Outside Homer

The Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* gives him the same three words, Περικλύμενόν τʼ ἀγέρωχον, and tells his gift: “fortunate man, to whom the earth-shaker Poseidon gave gifts / of every kind: sometimes he would appear among the birds / as an eagle, and sometimes he would become, a marvel to see, / an ant, and again the shining tribes of bees, / and again a dread, relentless snake”; but these gifts ensnared him at last “through the counsel of Athena” (fr. 10 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]] and [[poseidon]]). The scholion on Apollonius that quotes the verses tells the end: changed into one of his forms, he settled on the boss of the yoke of Heracles' horses to fight him, and when Athena pointed him out, Heracles shot him (fr. 10 Evelyn-White; see [[heracles]] and [[athena]]).
