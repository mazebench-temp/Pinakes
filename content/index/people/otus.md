The first of Dione's examples of gods who suffered at the hands of men (see [[dione]] and [[the-aloadae-bind-ares]]). The Iliad names Otus only here, with his brother, and calls the pair “the sons of Aloeus” (παῖδες Ἀλωῆος, 5.386; see [[aloeus]]); later writers called them the Aloadae. It does not say why they bound Ares. Another Otus, of Cyllene, a companion of Meges and a leader of the Epeians, is killed by Polydamas at 15.518–519 (see [[otus-of-cyllene]]).

## The name

W. W. Merry takes the names of the family from the threshing floor: Aloeus is the man of the ἀλωή, Otus the one who tramples the corn from the husk, and Ephialtes the one who tosses it up for the wind to winnow (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.307; see [[ephialtes]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Odyssey makes the brothers the sons of Iphimedeia, the wife of Aloeus, who said that she had lain with Poseidon; they were the tallest and handsomest men the earth ever reared, threatened the gods with war and meant to pile mountains on Olympus to climb the sky, but Apollo killed them before their beards grew (*Odyssey* 11.305–320; see [[iphimedeia]] and [[the-aloadae-threaten-olympus]]).

## Outside Homer

In the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, as a scholiast on Apollonius reports it, the two were the sons of Aloeus and Iphimedeia in name, but in truth of Poseidon and Iphimedeia (fr. 6 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]). In [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] they also woo goddesses, Ephialtes Hera and Otus Artemis, and they bind Ares, whom Hermes steals away. Artemis kills them on Naxos: she leaps between them as a deer, and aiming at the deer they spear each other (*Library* 1.7.4). In Virgil the Sibyl tells Aeneas that she saw the twin sons of Aloeus in Tartarus (*Aeneid* 6.582–584).
