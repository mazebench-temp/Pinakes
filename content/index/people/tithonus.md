## Etymology

Τιθωνός (Tithōnos). The name has no agreed etymology.

## Family

He is the husband of Dawn, who rises from his bed (11.1; see [[eos]]). In Aeneas' account of his line he is the first-named son of Laomedon: “Laomedon fathered Tithonus and Priam, Lampus and Clytius and Hicetaon, offshoot of Ares” (20.237–238; see [[laomedon]], [[priam]], [[lampus]], [[clytius]] and [[hicetaon]]). Three of these brothers sit with Priam among the elders on the wall (3.146–147; see [[trojan-elders]]), and Ganymede, whom the gods carried off for his beauty, is his great-uncle (20.231–235; see [[ganymede]]).

Homer names no children of his. In the Odyssey Memnon, who killed Nestor's son Antilochus, is “the splendid son of shining Dawn” (*Odyssey* 4.187–188; see [[antilochus]]), but his father is not named.

## Descriptions

ἀγαυός, “noble” (11.1), an epithet of kings and heroes (see [[noble]]).

## In Homer

Tithonus is a name in a dawn verse. He neither acts nor speaks, and Homer says nothing of his age. The two verses of 11.1–2 also open the fifth book of the Odyssey (*Odyssey* 5.1–2); in the Iliad they bring the dawn of the third day of battle, the longest day of the poem (see [[dawn-line]] and [[the-third-day-of-battle]]).

## Outside Homer

The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* tells his story. Golden-throned Dawn carried him off and asked Zeus to make him immortal, and Zeus granted it, but she did not think to ask for youth. When he grew gray she left his bed, and when he could no longer move his limbs she shut him in a chamber, where his voice flows on without end, “and there is no strength / such as there was before in his supple limbs” (218–238; see [[aphrodite-and-anchises]] and [[immortal-and-ageless]]). The verse is the one with which Nestor laments his own lost strength in Book 11 (11.669; see [[the-tithonus-verse]]).

Hesiod names their sons, “Memnon, armed in bronze, king of the Ethiopians, and lord Emathion” (*Theogony* 984–985). In [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] Tithonus is the first of Laomedon's five sons, and Dawn carries him off for love to Ethiopia, where she bears him Emathion and Memnon (*Library* 3.12.3–4). [[mimnermus|Mimnermus]] calls the gift a curse: Zeus gave Tithonus an evil that does not perish, old age, more chilling than death (fr. 4 West). In [[sappho|Sappho]] rosy-armed Dawn carries Tithonus to the ends of the earth, and old age seizes him all the same, though his wife is immortal (fr. 58). The story that he shrank into a cicada is later than the Hymn (see [[cicadas]]).

[[virgil|Virgil]]'s dawn, *Tithoni croceum linquens Aurora cubile*, “Aurora, leaving the saffron bed of Tithonus” (*Georgics* 1.447; *Aeneid* 4.585 = 9.460), joins the bed of 11.1 to the saffron of Homer's “saffron-robed Dawn” (8.1; see [[saffron-robed]]). [[tennyson|Tennyson]] gave Tithonus a monologue, “Tithonus” (1860), in which the old man asks Dawn to take back her gift.
