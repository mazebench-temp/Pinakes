Hector's charge has broken the Achaeans, and Odysseus has called Diomedes to stand with him (11.310–315). Diomedes answers that he will stay, though Zeus wishes to give power to the Trojans, and his spear takes Thymbraeus at once (11.317–321; see [[diomedes]], [[odysseus]] and [[odysseus-and-diomedes]]). Odysseus kills his attendant, “the godlike attendant of that lord” (τοῖο ἄνακτος, 11.322). “These they left, since they had stopped them from fighting” (11.323), and the two go on through the throng like two boars falling on the hunting dogs (11.324–326; see [[two-boars]]).

Θυμβραῖος is “of Thymbre”, the place toward which, Dolon says, the Lycians, Mysians and other allies were camped (10.430–431; see [[thymbre]]). Whether the man came from there, the poem does not say, and it names him nowhere else.
