## In Homer

Zeus names her twice in three verses. οὐδʼ ὅτε περ Σεμέλης οὐδʼ Ἀλκμήνης ἐνὶ Θήβῃ, “nor when I loved Semele, nor Alcmene in Thebes” (14.323): the two Theban women share a verse, and their sons follow in reverse order, Alcmene's Heracles first, then Semele's son, ἣ δὲ Διώνυσον Σεμέλη τέκε χάρμα βροτοῖσιν, “and Semele bore Dionysus, a joy to mortals” (14.325; see [[alcmene]], [[dionysus]] and [[the-loves-of-zeus]]). “In Thebes” stands with Alcmene, but Semele was Theban too, a daughter of Cadmus in Hesiod (see [[thebes]] and [[cadmus]]). This is all that Homer says of her, and the only line in Homer that tells the birth of Dionysus.

## Outside Homer

**Hesiod and the Hymns.** Hesiod lists her among the daughters of Cadmus and Harmonia, born in “well-crowned Thebes”, and among the women who bore children to Zeus: “Cadmeian Semele bore him a splendid son, / joined with him in love, Dionysus of many joys, / an immortal, she a mortal; and now both are gods” (*Theogony* 940–942, 975–978; see [[hesiod]]). The *Homeric Hymns* call Dionysus “the son of glorious Semele” (*Hymn* 7.1, 26.2), and in one of them he names his mother himself, “Cadmeian Semele”, who lay with Zeus (*Hymn* 7.56–57). One hymn rejects Thebes as his birthplace: some say Dracanum, Icarus, Naxos or the Alpheus, others Thebes, “but they lie”, for the father bore him far from men, hidden from Hera; the same hymn says that men call her Thyone (*Hymn* 1.1–7, 1.21).

**The thunderbolt.** The later story makes her death Hera's doing. In [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]], Zeus loved her without Hera's knowledge and promised to do whatever she asked; deceived by Hera, she asked him to come to her as he came when he was wooing Hera. He came in his chariot with lightning and thunder and hurled a thunderbolt, and she died of fright. Zeus snatched the child, born in the sixth month, from the fire and sewed it into his thigh, and Cadmus' other daughters said that she had lain with a mortal and lied about Zeus, and had been struck by the thunderbolt for it (*Library* 3.4.3). Euripides' *Bacchae* opens with Dionysus at the tomb of his mother, “delivered by the lightning-bearing fire”, beside the ruins of her house, still smoldering with the fire of Zeus, the undying outrage of Hera against her (*Bacchae* 1–9; see [[euripides]]). Herodotus reports that the Greeks say Zeus sewed the newborn child into his thigh and carried him to Nysa, beyond Egypt in Ethiopia, and he puts Dionysus son of Semele about sixteen hundred years before his own time (2.145.4, 2.146.2; see [[nysa]] and [[herodotus]]).

**A goddess.** [[pindar|Pindar]] says that long-haired Semele, who died in the roar of the thunderbolt, lives among the Olympians; Pallas loves her always, and father Zeus, and her ivy-bearing son loves her (*Olympian* 2.25–27).
