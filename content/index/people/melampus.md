## The name

Μελάμπους is “Black-foot”, from μέλας, “black”, and πούς, “foot”. Homer names him once, in the genitive, when the seer Theoclymenus is brought in as one “of the race of Melampus” (*Odyssey* 15.225, a plain rendering). In *Odyssey* 11 he is μάντις ἀμύμων, “the noble seer” (*Odyssey* 11.291; see [[divination]] and [[blameless]]), the words used of Teiresias earlier in the same book (*Odyssey* 11.99; see [[teiresias]]).

## Family

Homer gives him no father. Herodotus calls him “Melampus son of Amytheon” (Herodotus 2.49.1; see [[herodotus]]), the Amythaon whom Tyro bore to Cretheus (*Odyssey* 11.259; see [[amythaon]]), and W. W. Merry makes Bias, to whom he gave Pero, his brother, both sons of Amythaon by Idomene (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.259, 11.281; see [[pero]]). In *Odyssey* 15 he begets Antiphates and Mantius in Argos; Antiphates is the father of Oicles and the grandfather of Amphiaraus, and Mantius' grandson is Theoclymenus (*Odyssey* 15.241–256, a plain rendering; see [[amphiaraus]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

*Odyssey* 15 tells his story at more length, when Theoclymenus, his descendant, comes to Telemachus' ship as a fugitive. Melampus once lived in Pylos, rich, in a house that stood out among the Pylians; he went to another people, fleeing his country and great-hearted Neleus, who held his many possessions by force for a full year, while he lay in grievous bonds in the halls of Phylacus, suffering hard pains, for the sake of Neleus' daughter and the heavy blind folly that the goddess, the dread Erinys, had put in his mind. But he escaped death, drove the lowing cattle from Phylace to Pylos, avenged himself on godlike Neleus for his shameful deed, and brought the woman home for his brother; then he went to horse-pasturing Argos, where it was his lot to dwell, ruling over many Argives, married a wife and built a high-roofed house (*Odyssey* 15.225–242, a plain rendering; see [[neleus]], [[phylace]], [[erinyes]], [[argos]] and [[melampus-and-the-cattle-of-iphiclus]]).

## Outside Homer

**The seer.** In the Hesiodic *Great Eoiae* Melampus was dearest of men to Apollo: when the servants of a king killed a snake at a sacrifice, he buried it and reared its young, and they licked his ears and breathed prophecy into him; so, when he was caught stealing the cattle of Iphiclus and bound, he told an old serving woman of Iphiclus that the house he was in would fall, and for that he was set free (fr. 12 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]] and [[iphiclus]]). Merry reports the two ancient readings of θέσφατα πάντʼ εἰπόντα, “when he had told all the oracles” (*Odyssey* 11.297): Eustathius took it of a prophecy that the childless Iphiclus would have children, a scholion of his foreknowledge that he would be caught and held a year; he adds that the later logographers made Melampus understand the speech of all animals, and told of his questioning the birds and overhearing the worms that bored through the beam of his chamber (on *Odyssey* 11.297).

**Argos and Dionysus.** Herodotus makes him the man who taught the Greeks the name of Dionysus, his sacrifice and the procession of the phallus, which he learned from Cadmus and the Phoenicians who came with him to Boeotia (Herodotus 2.49.1–3; see [[dionysus]]). When the women of Argos went mad and the Argives sent to Pylos for him, Melampus asked half the kingship as his fee; refused, and then sought again as the madness spread, he demanded a third for his brother Bias as well, and the Argives agreed (Herodotus 9.34.1–2). Strabo reports that some said he used the waters of the Anigrus in Triphylia to purify the daughters of Proetus (*Geography* 8.3.19; see [[strabo]]), and Herodotus traces the seer Megistias, who stayed with Leonidas at Thermopylae, to Melampus (Herodotus 7.221.1).
