## The name

Μελάμπους is “Black-foot”, from μέλας, “black”, and πούς, “foot”. Homer names him once, in the genitive, when the seer [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] is brought in as “a descendant of Melampus” (*Odyssey* 15.225). In *Odyssey* 11 he is μάντις ἀμύμων, “the noble seer” (*Odyssey* 11.291; see [[divination]] and [[blameless]]), the words used of Teiresias earlier in the same book (*Odyssey* 11.99; see [[teiresias]]).

## Family

Homer gives him no father. Herodotus calls him “Melampus son of Amytheon” (Herodotus 2.49.1; see [[herodotus]]), the Amythaon whom Tyro bore to Cretheus (*Odyssey* 11.259; see [[amythaon]]), and W. W. Merry makes Bias, to whom he gave Pero, his brother, both sons of Amythaon by Idomene (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.259, 11.281; see [[pero]]). In Argos he married and begot Antiphates and Mantius: Antiphates is the father of Oicles and the grandfather of the seer Amphiaraus, and Mantius' son is the seer Polypheides and his grandson the seer Theoclymenus (*Odyssey* 15.241–256; see [[antiphates-son-of-melampus]], [[mantius]], [[oicles]], [[amphiaraus]], [[polypheides]] and [[theoclymenus]]). Plato calls Theoclymenus “the seer of the Melampodidae”, of the house of Melampus (*Ion* 538e; see [[plato-ion-the-seers-passages]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

*Odyssey* 15 tells his venture again, this time with his name, when his descendant [[theoclymenus|Theoclymenus]] comes to Telemachus' ship as a fugitive: Melampus, once a rich man in Pylos, left his country because Neleus held his goods by force for a year while he lay bound in the halls of Phylacus for the sake of Neleus' daughter; he escaped his doom, drove the cattle to Pylos, avenged himself on Neleus and brought the woman home for his brother, and then went to Argos, where he ruled over many Argives, married and built a house (*Odyssey* 15.225–242; see [[neleus]], [[phylacus]], [[pero]], [[argos]] and [[melampus-and-the-cattle-of-iphiclus]]).

## Outside Homer

**The seer.** In the Hesiodic *Great Eoiae* Melampus was dearest of men to Apollo: when the servants of a king killed a snake at a sacrifice, he buried it and reared its young, and they licked his ears and breathed prophecy into him; so, when he was caught stealing the cattle of Iphiclus and bound, he told an old serving woman of Iphiclus that the house he was in would fall, and for that he was set free (fr. 12 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]] and [[iphiclus]]). Merry reports the two ancient readings of θέσφατα πάντʼ εἰπόντα, “when he had told all the oracles” (*Odyssey* 11.297): Eustathius took it of a prophecy that the childless Iphiclus would have children, a scholion of his foreknowledge that he would be caught and held a year; he adds that the later logographers made Melampus understand the speech of all animals, and told of his questioning the birds and overhearing the worms that bored through the beam of his chamber (on *Odyssey* 11.297).

**Argos and Dionysus.** Herodotus makes him the man who taught the Greeks the name of Dionysus, his sacrifice and the procession of the phallus, which he learned from Cadmus and the Phoenicians who came with him to Boeotia (Herodotus 2.49.1–3; see [[dionysus]]). When the women of Argos went mad and the Argives sent to Pylos for him, Melampus asked half the kingship as his fee; refused, and then sought again as the madness spread, he demanded a third for his brother Bias as well, and the Argives agreed (Herodotus 9.34.1–2). Strabo reports that some said he used the waters of the Anigrus in Triphylia to purify the daughters of Proetus (*Geography* 8.3.19; see [[strabo]]), and Herodotus traces the seer Megistias, who stayed with Leonidas at Thermopylae, to Melampus (Herodotus 7.221.1).

**The seer's name.** His name stood for the seer's art. A poem ascribed to Hesiod was called the *Melampodia*, after him; it told of other seers, Teiresias, Calchas and Mopsus, and in one of its fragments “the seer”, Μάντης, takes the ox by its tether while Iphiclus feels its back and Phylacus walks behind with a cup and a scepter (*Melampodia* frr. 1–3, 6 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]] and [[phylacus]]). Cicero names him with Polyidus, Mopsus, Amphiaraus, Calchas and Helenus among the augurs whose names would never have been so great had not long experience shown their predictions to be true (*On the Laws* 2.33; see [[cicero]]). Virgil makes him a healer of beasts: in the cattle plague of Noricum “the masters gave up, / Chiron, son of Philyra, and Melampus, son of Amythaon” (*Georgics* 3.549–550, *cessere magistri, / Phillyrides Chiron Amythaoniusque Melampus*; see [[virgil]] and [[chiron]]); and in Ovid the spring of Clitor in Arcadia makes those who drink from it hate wine because, the people say, “the son of Amythaon”, when he had freed the maddened daughters of Proetus from their frenzy “by spells and herbs”, threw the cleansings of their minds into its water (*Metamorphoses* 15.322–328; see [[ovid]]).
