## Name

Ἰασίων (Iasiōn), dative Ἰασίωνι (*Odyssey* 5.125). Evelyn-White's text of Hesiod's *Theogony* has the same name, Ἰασίωνʼ ἥρωι, “the hero Iasion” (970), where W. W. Merry cites the verse with the form Ἰάσιος, Iasius (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 5.125). Merry thinks his story points to an early legend that identified Iasion with the fertile power of the soil (on *Odyssey* 5.125).

## In Homer

He has one moment in Homer. Calypso, protesting that the gods begrudge goddesses their mortal husbands, gives him as her second example after Orion: Demeter lay with him “in a thrice-plowed fallow field”, and Zeus, who soon heard of it, killed him with a gleaming thunderbolt (*Odyssey* 5.125–128). The story is told in [[demeter-and-iasion]]; see also [[the-jealousy-of-the-gods]] and [[thunderbolt]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod's list of the goddesses who lay with mortal men begins with Demeter, who bore Plutus, “a kindly god”, to the hero Iasion “in a thrice-plowed fallow, in the rich land of Crete” (*Theogony* 969–974, a plain rendering; see [[hesiod]] and [[crete]]). Merry reports that later mythology made Iasion the first to grow grain, like Triptolemus, that Theocritus couples him with Endymion (*Idylls* 3.50), and that he appears in different genealogies and with different mystic meanings in the legends of Arcadia, Crete and Samothrace (on *Odyssey* 5.125).
