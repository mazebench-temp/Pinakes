ναυσικλειτοῖο Δύμαντος, “Dymas, famed for ships” (*Odyssey* 6.22). ναυσικλειτός joins ναῦς, “ship”, and κλειτός, “famous”, and is found only here in Homer (see [[famed-for-ships]] and the Odyssey translation's key words), where the Phaeacians as a people are ναυσίκλυτοι, “famed for ships” (*Odyssey* 7.39; 8.191, 8.369; 13.166; 16.227; see [[phaeacians]]); the Homeric *Hymn to Apollo* gives ναυσικλειτός to Euboea (*Hymn to Apollo* 31, 219). He is named once, for his daughter's sake, and she is not named at all.

Homer's other Dymas is the Phrygian [[dymas|father of Asius]], Hecuba's brother, whose likeness Apollo takes to rouse Hector (*Iliad* 16.715–721). In both poems the name comes as a god takes the likeness of one of his children, and the god speaks in the same verse: τῷ μιν ἐεισάμενος προσέφη Διὸς υἱὸς Ἀπόλλων, “in his likeness Apollo, son of Zeus, spoke to him” (*Iliad* 16.720), and τῇ μιν ἐεισαμένη προσέφη γλαυκῶπις Ἀθήνη, “In her likeness gray-eyed Athena spoke to her” (*Odyssey* 6.24).
