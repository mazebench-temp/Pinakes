## Family

Πανθοΐδης Εὔφορβος, “Euphorbus, son of Panthous” (16.808): his father is one of the Trojan elders who sit with Priam on the wall by the Scaean gates (3.146; see [[panthous]] and [[trojan-elders]]). The same patronymic names his brother Polydamas (13.756, 16.535; see [[polydamas]]). In Book 17, when Menelaus reminds him of Hyperenor, whom he killed at the end of Book 14 (17.24–28; 14.516–519), Euphorbus calls the dead man his brother (γνωτὸν ἐμόν, 17.35; see [[hyperenor]]), and he hopes to bring Menelaus' head and armor home and put them in the hands of his father and of his mother, Phrontis (17.38–40).

## Descriptions

He “surpassed all of his age / with his spear and his horsemanship and his swift feet” (16.808–809). In Book 17 he is ἐϋμμελίης, “with good ash spear” (17.9, 17.59), as the sons of Panthous are ἐϋμμελίαι (17.23; see [[with-good-ash-spear]]), and Apollo calls him the best of the Trojans (Τρώων τὸν ἄριστον, 17.80). His hair, like that of the Graces (Χαρίτεσσιν ὁμοῖαι), was braided with gold and silver (17.51–52).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In Book 17 Menelaus stands over Patroclus' body, and Euphorbus claims the spoils, since no Trojan or ally hit Patroclus before him (17.9–17). Menelaus warns him off, reminding him that Hyperenor did not go home on his own feet (17.18–32). Euphorbus answers that Menelaus will pay for his brother, whose wife he widowed in a new bridal chamber, and his spear bends back on Menelaus' shield (17.33–45). Menelaus stabs him at the base of the throat, the point goes through his neck, and he falls like a young olive tree, flourishing and white with blossom, that a sudden storm wind tears from its trench (17.46–60). Menelaus would have carried off the armor of the son of Panthous (Πανθοΐδαο, 17.70) if Apollo had not roused Hector against him (17.70–73), and he gives way before Hector and the Trojans (17.106–113; see [[menelaus]] and [[hector]]).

## Outside Homer

[[Pythagoras]] claimed to have been Euphorbus. According to Heraclides of Pontus, he said that his soul had passed from Aethalides, son of Hermes, to Euphorbus, who was wounded by Menelaus, and then to Hermotimus, who proved it by pointing out in the temple of Apollo at Branchidae the shield that Menelaus had dedicated there (Diogenes Laertius 8.4–5). In Ovid's *Metamorphoses* Pythagoras says that in the time of the Trojan war he was “Panthoides Euphorbus”, in whose breast the spear of the younger son of Atreus stuck, and that he recently recognized the shield he had carried on his left arm in the temple of Juno at Argos (*Metamorphoses* 15.160–164). Horace makes him the son of Panthous whom Tartarus holds, sent down to Orcus a second time (*Odes* 1.28.9–13). Pausanias lists among the offerings in the fore-temple of the Heraeum, Hera's great temple in the Argolid, the shield that Menelaus took from Euphorbus at Troy (Pausanias 2.17.3). In the Iliad Menelaus does not take the armor (17.70–73).
