## Family

υἱὸς Πανοπῆος Ἐπειός, “the son of Panopeus, Epeius” (23.665): Homer names his father and nothing more (see [[panopeus-father-of-epeius]]). Panopeus is also the name of a town of the Phocians (2.520, 17.307; see [[panopeus]]), and later writers made the father its eponym (see Outside Homer).

## Descriptions

- δῖος, “brilliant” (23.689, 23.838, 23.839; see [[brilliant]]), and μεγάθυμος, “great-hearted” (23.694; see [[great-hearted]]).
- ἠΰς τε μέγας τε, “tall and brave” (23.664; see [[brave]]), and εἰδὼς πυγμαχίης, “skilled in boxing” (23.665).

The Iliad names him only in its last games, where he wins the boxing and throws first with the lump of iron, and all the Achaeans laugh (23.653–699, 23.836–840). He is the only competitor in those games whom the poem has never shown in battle, and he says so himself: “Is it not enough that I fall short in battle?” (23.670).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In the Odyssey he is the builder of the wooden horse. Odysseus asks Demodocus to sing of the horse “which Epeius made with Athena”, and which Odysseus brought into the citadel as a trick, filled with the men who sacked Ilios (*Odyssey* 8.492–495); he tells the shade of Achilles of the day when the best of the Argives went down into “the horse that Epeius made” (*Odyssey* 11.523–524; see [[sinon-and-the-wooden-horse]] and [[athena]]). The Odyssey gives the craftsman no father; later writers call the maker of the horse “son of Panopeus”, as the Iliad calls the boxer.

## Outside Homer

**Family.** Pausanias reports that the epic poet Asius made Panopeus and Crisus sons of Phocus, the son of Aeacus for whose murder Peleus and Telamon were exiled, and Epeius the son of Panopeus, “who made, according to Homer, the wooden horse” (*Description of Greece* 2.29.2–4; see [[pausanias]]). The people of Panopeus said that their town was named after the father of Epeius (10.4.1), and Strabo calls Panopeus “the native land of Epeius” (*Geography* 9.3.14; see [[strabo]]). Strabo also makes Lagaria, a stronghold in southern Italy, a foundation of Epeius and the Phocians (6.1.14).

**The craftsman.** Plato's Socrates names him as a sculptor beside “Daedalus son of Metion” and “Theodorus of Samos” (*Ion* 533a–b; see [[plato]] and [[daedalus]]), and at Argos Pausanias was shown a wooden image of Hermes said to be his work (2.19.6). In the myth of Er the soul of “Epeius son of Panopeus” passes into the nature of a craftswoman (*Republic* 10.620c).

**The boxer and the coward.** In the *Laws* the tricks of boxing that Epeius or Amycus devised, like the wrestling of Antaeus or Cercyon, served a useless rivalry and are of no use in war (7.796a). The Suda explains the saying “more cowardly than Epeius” (Ἐπειοῦ δειλότερος), said of the comic poet Cratinus: “for Epeius was a coward” (Suda ε 2131).

**Another Epeius.** In the Elean legend that Pausanias tells, Endymion set his sons to race at Olympia for the throne; Epeius won, and his subjects were then first called Epeians (5.1.4; see [[epeians]]). That Epeius, a king of Elis, is not the son of Panopeus.
