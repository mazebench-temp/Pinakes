## The name

Ἀρήτη δʼ ὄνομʼ ἐστὶν ἐπώνυμον, “and Arete is her name, a name with a meaning” (*Odyssey* 7.54, a plain rendering). ἐπώνυμον is properly a name added with a special significance, as Cleopatra's parents called her Alcyone because her mother wept like the halcyon (*Iliad* 9.561–564); the scholia explain that Arete was so called because she was born in answer to prayers, ἀρητῶς καὶ εὐκταίως, from ἀράομαι, “pray” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 7.54). The same root gives Ἄρητος, the name of a son of Nestor and of a Trojan, and in later times the name Theaetetus corresponds to it (Merry on *Odyssey* 7.54; *Odyssey* 3.414; *Iliad* 17.494; see [[aretus-son-of-nestor]] and [[aretus]]). The name is not the word ἀρετή, “excellence” (see [[arete]]).

## Family

Her father was Rhexenor, a son of Nausithous, whom Apollo struck down in his hall, newly married and without a son, so that she was his only child; his brother Alcinous made her his wife (*Odyssey* 7.62–66; see [[nausithous]] and [[alcinous]]). Merry renders ἄκουρον … νυμφίον, “without male issue … before his bridal days were over” (on *Odyssey* 7.64). So she is her husband's niece, and τοκήων, “parents”, at *Odyssey* 7.54 means her forebears: she comes of the same ancestors as the king (Merry on *Odyssey* 7.54). Odysseus greets her as “daughter of godlike Rhexenor” when he clasps her knees (*Odyssey* 7.146). She is the mother of Nausicaa (*Odyssey* 6.25, 6.51–52, 6.305–311; see [[nausicaa]]), and the poem speaks of her children (*Odyssey* 7.70, 13.62).

## Descriptions

- λευκώλενος, “white-armed” (*Odyssey* 7.233, 7.335; 11.335; see [[white-armed]]), the epithet of her daughter (*Odyssey* 6.101).
- δέσποινα, “mistress”, “lady”, the mistress of the house (*Odyssey* 7.53, 7.347), a word not found in the Iliad, Merry explains, because the relation it denotes is a domestic one (on *Odyssey* 7.53).
- βασίλεια, “queen”, in Odysseus' address (*Odyssey* 7.241, 13.59), and βασίλεια περίφρων, “the wise queen” (*Odyssey* 11.345; see [[basileus]]).

## Later in the Odyssey

The renderings of the later books here are plain renderings, not the translation. Athena, in the likeness of a young girl, tells Odysseus to find the queen first in the hall. Alcinous honors her as no other woman on earth is honored of all who keep house under their husbands, and so do her children and the people, who look on her as a god and greet her when she walks through the city; she lacks nothing of good sense, and settles quarrels even among men; if she thinks of him in friendship, there is hope that he will reach home (*Odyssey* 7.53–77). Wrapped in a mist, Odysseus goes through the hall and throws his arms around her knees, and the mist is shed; he calls on her, her husband and their guests, prays the gods to give the feasters prosperity, and asks for an escort home (*Odyssey* 7.139–152).

After the meal it is she who speaks first, for she knows the mantle and the tunic he wears, which she made with her women, and asks who he is and who gave him those clothes (*Odyssey* 7.233–239); she has a bed made for him under the portico (*Odyssey* 7.335–339). She brings out a chest for the Phaeacians' gifts and puts a mantle and a tunic in it herself (*Odyssey* 8.433–445). In a pause in his tale she asks the Phaeacians what they think of him, in looks and stature and in mind, calls him her own guest, and bids them not to send him off in haste nor stint their gifts; old Echeneus answers that the wise queen speaks to the point, but that deed and word depend on Alcinous (*Odyssey* 11.335–346). At parting, Odysseus puts the cup in her hand and bids her farewell until old age and death come to her, and she sends three slave women to the ship, with a mantle and a tunic, a chest, and bread and red wine (*Odyssey* 13.56–69).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod made Arete the sister of Alcinous, as a scholium on *Odyssey* 7.54 reports (*Catalogue of Women* fr. 49 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]).
