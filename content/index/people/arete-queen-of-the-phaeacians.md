## The name

Ἀρήτη δʼ ὄνομʼ ἐστὶν ἐπώνυμον, “Arete is her name, a name with a meaning” (*Odyssey* 7.54). ἐπώνυμον is properly a name added with a special significance, as Cleopatra's parents called her Alcyone because her mother wept like the halcyon (*Iliad* 9.561–564); the scholia explain that Arete was so called because she was born in answer to prayers, ἀρητῶς καὶ εὐκταίως, from ἀράομαι, “pray” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 7.54). The same root gives Ἄρητος, the name of a son of Nestor and of a Trojan, and in later times the name Theaetetus corresponds to it (Merry on *Odyssey* 7.54; *Odyssey* 3.414; *Iliad* 17.494; see [[aretus-son-of-nestor]] and [[aretus]]). The name is not the word ἀρετή, “excellence” (see [[arete]]).

## Family

Her father was Rhexenor, a son of Nausithous, whom Apollo struck down in his hall, newly married and without a son, so that she was his only child; his brother Alcinous made her his wife (*Odyssey* 7.62–66; see [[rhexenor]], [[nausithous]] and [[alcinous]]). So she is her husband's niece, and τοκήων, “parents”, at *Odyssey* 7.54 means her forebears: she comes of the same ancestors as the king (Merry on *Odyssey* 7.54), a line that goes back to Poseidon and Periboea, the daughter of Eurymedon, king of the Giants (*Odyssey* 7.56–61; see [[poseidon]] and [[periboea-mother-of-nausithous]]). Odysseus greets her as “daughter of godlike Rhexenor” when he clasps her knees (*Odyssey* 7.146). She is the mother of Nausicaa (*Odyssey* 6.25, 6.51–52, 6.305–311; see [[nausicaa]]), and the poem speaks of her children (*Odyssey* 7.70, 13.62).

## Descriptions

- λευκώλενος, “white-armed” (*Odyssey* 7.233, 7.335; 11.335; see [[white-armed]]), the epithet of her daughter (*Odyssey* 6.101).
- δέσποινα, “mistress”, “lady”, the mistress of the house (*Odyssey* 7.53, 7.347), a word not found in the Iliad, Merry explains, because the relation it denotes is a domestic one (on *Odyssey* 7.53).
- βασίλεια, “queen”, in Odysseus' address (*Odyssey* 7.241, 13.59), and βασίλεια περίφρων, “the wise queen” (*Odyssey* 11.345; see [[basileus]]).

## Later in the Odyssey

The renderings here are plain renderings, not the translation. On the day of the games Alcinous bids her bring out a chest for the Phaeacians' gifts; she has a cauldron set on the fire for the stranger's bath, brings the chest from her chamber, lays in it the clothing and the gold that the Phaeacians gave, puts in a mantle and a tunic herself, and bids him tie the lid fast, so that no one may rob him on the way while he sleeps in the ship (*Odyssey* 8.423–445). In a pause in his tale she asks the Phaeacians what they think of him, in looks and stature and in mind: “he is my guest”, mine especially, Merry explains, because he made his first appeal to me, though each of them has a share in the honor; let them not send him off in haste nor stint their gifts. The old Echeneus answers that the wise queen speaks to the point, but that deed and word depend on Alcinous (*Odyssey* 11.335–346; Merry on *Odyssey* 11.338; see [[echeneus]]). At parting, after the last libation, Odysseus puts the cup in her hand and bids her farewell until old age and death come to her, and she sends three slave women to the ship, with a mantle and a tunic, a chest, and bread and red wine (*Odyssey* 13.56–69; see [[pontonous]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod made Arete the sister of Alcinous, as a scholium on *Odyssey* 7.54 reports: Ἡσίοδος δὲ ἀδελφὴν Ἀλκινόου τὴν Ἀρήτην ὑπέλαβεν (*Catalogue of Women* fr. 49 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]).
