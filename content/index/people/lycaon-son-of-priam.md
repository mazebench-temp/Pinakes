Λυκάων recalls λύκος, "wolf". In *Iliad* 3 he is present only through his corselet (θώρηξ). Paris came out as an archer in a leopard's skin (3.17), and for the duel he arms as a spearman, in a corselet borrowed from his brother, and "fitted it to himself" (ἥρμοσε δʼ αὐτῷ). Menelaus' spear goes through it and cuts the tunic beneath, and Paris escapes death by swerving (3.357–360). See [[corselet]] and [[arming-scene]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

His own story is told in *Iliad* 21 (see [[the-death-of-lycaon]]). Achilles had once caught him by night in Priam's orchard, cutting young fig shoots for the rails of a chariot, and sold him into Lemnos. A guest-friend, Eetion of Imbros, ransomed him, and he escaped home; on the twelfth day after his return he met Achilles at the river, clasped his knees and begged for his life, and Achilles killed him (21.34–135; see [[eetion-of-imbros]]). His mother was Laothoe, daughter of Altes, king of the Leleges, and Polydorus, whom Achilles kills in Book 20, was his full brother (21.84–91, 22.46–48; see [[laothoe]], [[altes]] and [[polydorus-son-of-priam]]). Apollo takes his likeness to rouse Aeneas against Achilles (20.81–82). In Book 22 Priam, on the wall, cannot see Lycaon and Polydorus among the Trojans who have fled into the city, and would ransom them if they were alive (22.46–53). The silver mixing bowl that Achilles sets out as a prize at Patroclus' games had been given by Euneus of Lemnos to Patroclus as the price of Lycaon (23.740–747; see [[euneus]]).

## Outside Homer

Apollodorus lists Lycaon among the sons whom Priam had by women other than Hecuba (*Library* 3.12.5), and tells that in the first raids of the war Achilles went to the city by night and took him (*Epitome* 3.32; see [[apollodorus]] and [[the-death-of-lycaon]]). Strabo names him with Polydorus as the sons whom Priam had by the daughter of Altes, king of the Leleges (*Geography* 13.1.7; see [[altes]]).
