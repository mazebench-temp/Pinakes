## The name

W. W. Merry derives Τυνδάρεος from the same stem as Τυδεύς, Tydeus, comparing Latin *tutudi*, “I struck” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.298; see [[tydeus]]).

## Family

Homer names him three times, always through his family. Leda is “the wife of Tyndareus” and bore to Tyndareus two fierce-hearted sons, Castor and Polydeuces (*Odyssey* 11.298–300; see [[leda]], [[castor]] and [[polydeuces]]), and in the last book Agamemnon's shade contrasts the faithful Penelope with “the daughter of Tyndareus”, who devised evil deeds and killed her wedded husband (*Odyssey* 24.199–200, a plain rendering; see [[clytemnestra]]). Helen, whose two brothers had the same mother as she (*Iliad* 3.238), is called in both poems the daughter of Zeus, never of Tyndareus (*Iliad* 3.199; *Odyssey* 4.184; see [[helen]]).

Merry gives the later story: Tyndareus was a prince of Sparta who, driven out by his brothers, found a home with Thestius in Aetolia and married his daughter Leda (on *Odyssey* 11.298).

## Outside Homer

**The exile.** Strabo tells it of Tyndareus and his brother Icarius, Penelope's father: driven from home by Hippocoon, they went to Thestius, ruler of the Pleuronians, and helped him win much of the land beyond the Achelous; Tyndareus went home again, having married Leda, the daughter of Thestius, while Icarius stayed on with a part of Acarnania (*Geography* 10.2.24; see [[strabo]] and [[icarius]]).

**The house of Tyndareus.** In the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* the suitors of Helen come with their gifts “to the house of wise Tyndareus, son of Oebalus”, Τυνδαρέου ποτὶ δῶμα δαΐφρονος Οἰβαλίδαο (fr. 68 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]] and [[bride-price]]). A scholiast on Euripides' *Orestes* reports from Stesichorus that Tyndareus, sacrificing to the gods, forgot Aphrodite, and that the angry goddess made his daughters marry twice and three times and desert their husbands; he quotes Hesiod for the same: Timandra left Echemus for Phyleus, Clytemnestra left noble Agamemnon to lie with Aegisthus and chose a worse husband, and Helen shamed the bed of fair-haired Menelaus (fr. 67 Evelyn-White). In Euripides' *Iphigenia at Aulis* Agamemnon tells how Tyndareus, at a loss when the suitors threatened one another, made them swear to defend the husband of the girl against anyone who carried her off, and then let her choose; she chose Menelaus, and when Paris took her, Menelaus called on “the old oaths of Tyndareus” (49–79; see [[iphigenia-at-aulis]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]).

**The old man in mourning.** In the *Orestes* Tyndareus comes to Argos from Sparta dressed in black, his hair shorn in mourning for his daughter Clytemnestra (457–458); Menelaus greets him as the old man “who shared a marriage bed with Zeus” (476), and he turns on Orestes as a matricide (479–481; see [[orestes-play]], [[orestes]] and [[euripides]]). Electra says that Tyndareus fathered a race of daughters notorious for blame throughout Greece (*Orestes* 249–250).

**The sons of Tyndareus.** The Homeric Hymns call Castor and Polydeuces Τυνδαρίδαι and sons of Zeus at once (*Hymn* 17.2, 33.2; see [[leda]]), and Aristotle uses them in an argument from the more and the less: “if not even Theseus did wrong, neither did Alexander; and if not the sons of Tyndareus, neither did Alexander” (*Rhetoric* 1397b22–24; see [[theseus]] and [[paris]]).
