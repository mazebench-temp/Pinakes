## Name

Μυκήνη is also Homer's name for the city of Mycenae in the singular, beside the plural Μυκῆναι: “wide-streeted Mycenae” (εὐρυάγυια Μυκήνη, *Iliad* 4.52) and “Mycenae rich in gold” (πολυχρύσοιο Μυκήνης, *Iliad* 7.180, 11.46; *Odyssey* 3.305; see [[mycenae]]). The heroine bears the city's name, and later tradition made her its eponym (see below).

## Descriptions

- ἐϋστέφανος, “well-crowned” (ἐυστέφανός τε Μυκήνη, *Odyssey* 2.120; see [[well-crowned]]). The Iliad gives the epithet to one city, “well-crowned Thebes” (*Iliad* 19.99; see [[thebes]]), and to one goddess, Artemis (*Iliad* 21.511; see [[artemis]]); the Odyssey gives it to Aphrodite (*Odyssey* 8.267, 8.288, 18.193; see [[aphrodite]]). Here it is said of a woman who bears the name of a city.

## Outside Homer

Pausanias, describing Mycenae, gives the stories of its name. Perseus founded the city where the cap (μύκης) of his scabbard fell off, which he took for a sign, or named the place from a mushroom (μύκης) that he picked up when he was thirsty, and from which water flowed (Pausanias 2.16.3; see [[pausanias]] and [[perseus]]). Then he quotes the verse of the Odyssey, and adds that the poem the Greeks call the Great Eoiae made Mycene the daughter of Inachus and the wife of Arestor, and that the city was said to take its name from her. The account attributed to Acusilaus, that Myceneus was the son of Sparton, son of Phoroneus, he will not accept, since the Lacedaemonians themselves do not (Pausanias 2.16.4; Hesiod, *Great Eoiae* fr. 9 Evelyn-White; see [[hesiod]]).
