## Etymology

Πουλυδάμας joins πουλύς, the epic form of πολύς, “much”, to the stem of δάμνημι, “tame, subdue”: “subduer of many”. Two other Trojans of Book 11 have names in -δάμας, [[Iphidamas]] and [[Chersidamas]].

## Family

He is Πανθοΐδης, “son of Panthous” (13.756, 14.450, 16.535, 18.250), one of the elders who sit with Priam at the Scaean gates (3.146; see [[panthous]] and [[trojan-elders]]). His brothers Euphorbus and Hyperenor are killed by Menelaus (14.516–519, 17.9–60). Book 18 joins him to Hector: “He was Hector's companion, and they were born in one night; but the one excelled far in words, the other with the spear” (18.251–252).

## In Homer

**First named.** He enters the poem in Book 11, in the list of the Trojan leaders on the rise of the plain, where he stands next to Hector (11.57). His epithet there, ἀμύμων, “noble”, stays with him (12.88, 13.790, 14.469; see [[blameless]]). Book 11 gives him nothing more to do.

**Four counsels.** Three times a verse brings him to Hector's side: “Then Polydamas came and stood beside bold Hector and said” (12.60, 12.210, 13.725), and once more he speaks in assembly (18.249). At the ditch he advises leaving the chariots and going against the wall on foot, and the plan pleases Hector, who leaps down at once (12.60–81). All but Asius obey “the counsel of blameless Polydamas” (12.108–115; see [[asius]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). When an eagle flying on the left drops a blood-red serpent among the Trojans, he reads it as a sign that they will not come back from the ships in good order, and complains that Hector always rebukes him in assembly when he speaks well (12.200–229; see [[divination]] and [[birds]]). Hector, looking darkly at him, answers in the words with which Paris rejected Antenor's advice (12.231–234 = 7.357–360), refuses to obey birds against the promises of Zeus, and threatens to kill him (12.230–250): “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (12.243). At the ships Polydamas tells Hector that a god gives one man the works of war and another a good mind (13.726–734), and urges him to call the best men to council, since “a man insatiable of war”, Achilles, still waits by the ships (13.735–747). This time Hector agrees (13.748–753).

**In battle.** He kills Prothoenor and boasts over him; Ajax's answering spear misses him and kills Antenor's son Archelochus (14.449–474). He kills Mecisteus and Otus of Cyllene, and Apollo will not let “the son of Panthous” fall among the champions (15.339, 15.518–522). He wounds Peneleos (17.597–600).

**The last counsel.** On the night after Achilles shows himself at the trench, the Trojans hold their assembly standing. Polydamas, who “alone saw before and after” (18.250; see [[before-and-after]]), urges them to go back into the city before dawn: Achilles will not stay on the plain but will carry the fight to the city and its women, and dogs and vultures will eat many Trojans (18.254–283). Hector calls him a fool and refuses, the Trojans shout their applause, and the narrator calls them fools: they praised Hector, who devised evil, “but no one praised Polydamas, who gave good counsel” (18.284–313; see [[boule]]). Alone before the gates, Hector remembers: “Polydamas will be the first to heap reproach on me”, for he did not take his advice (22.99–107).

The poem sets the counselor beside the fighter. Each time Hector refuses him, events bear Polydamas out, and Hector pays for the last refusal with his life.

## Outside Homer

[[Aristotle]] quotes Hector's “Polydamas will be the first to heap reproach on me” (22.100) as the voice of the courage of citizens, who face danger for fear of reproach (*Nicomachean Ethics* 3.8, 1116a21–26; again *Eudemian Ethics* 3.1, 1230a19–21). He cites both of Hector's answers to Polydamas, “One omen is best: to fight in defense of our native land” (12.243) and “Enyalius is common to all” (18.309), as maxims so well worn that everyone accepts them (*Rhetoric* 2.21.11, 1395a). [[Strabo]], following Demetrius of Scepsis, cites his words “we are far from the wall” (18.256) as evidence that the Achaean ships lay far from the city (13.1.36).
