**Alexander's gold.** The narrator names the father before the sons die, and gives his reason first: Antimachus, “having taken Alexander's gold, splendid gifts” (11.124), kept refusing to give Helen back. οὐκ εἴασκε is iterative: he “would not let” her go, again and again (11.125; see [[paris]] and [[helen]]). When Antenor proposed in the Trojan assembly to return Helen and her possessions, Paris refused for himself (7.347–364; see [[antenor]]). Antimachus, bought with his gold, would not let the city give her up.

**The envoys.** Agamemnon gives a second reason. Antimachus “once in the Trojans' assembly urged that Menelaus, / when he came on an embassy with godlike Odysseus, / be killed on the spot, and not be let go back to the Achaeans” (11.139–141). Only here is this told. Antenor remembers the same embassy from the other side: he received the two men as guests and welcomed them in his halls (3.205–224; see [[the-embassy-to-troy]] and [[embassy]]). The host of the envoys and the man who wanted one of them killed both lose sons to Agamemnon in this book, Antenor his Iphidamas and Coon (11.221–263).

**His house.** δαΐφρων, “wise-hearted”, is his epithet both times he is named in full (11.123, 11.138; see [[wise-hearted]]). His sons promise “many treasures lying in the house of Antimachus, / bronze and gold and iron worked with much toil” as their ransom (11.132–133), in nearly the words of the suppliant Adrastus (6.47–48; see [[adrastus-the-trojan]] and [[apoina]]). Agamemnon answers that the sons will pay for “your father's shameful insult” (λώβη, 11.142; see [[sons-of-antimachus]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]).

In Book 12 Leonteus kills “the son of Antimachus”, Hippomachus (12.188–189; see [[leonteus]]). The poem does not say whether his father is this Antimachus.
