## Etymology

Γανυμήδης is usually explained from γάνυμαι (“be glad”) and μήδεα (“counsels”). Xenophon's Socrates uses the derivation to argue that Zeus loved Ganymede for his mind, not his body (*Symposium* 8.30). The Latin form of the name, Catamitus, gave English the word “catamite”.

## In Homer

{{quote:e88cd9d8-2f05-5531-ad15-b9a53f1f3459}}

The Iliad names him twice. In Book 5 Zeus gives the breed of Aeneas' horses to Tros “in recompense for his son Ganymede” (5.265–266; see [[tros]] and [[ganymede-and-the-horses-of-tros]]). In Book 20 Aeneas names him among the sons of Tros: “godlike Ganymede, who was born the most beautiful of mortal men; him the gods snatched up to pour wine for Zeus, for the sake of his beauty, so that he might live among the immortals” (20.232–235).

The gods take him, not Zeus alone, and there is no eagle and no word of love. The verb, ἀνηρείψαντο (“snatched up”), is the one the Odyssey uses of the storm winds and Harpies that carry people off without trace (*Odyssey* 1.241, 4.727, 20.77), and the last half-verse is used again of Cleitus, whom the Dawn carried off (*Odyssey* 15.250–251). Among the gods the cupbearer is otherwise Hebe (4.2–3) or, once, Hephaestus (1.597–598); see [[cupbearing]] and [[hebe]].

## Outside Homer

The *Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* makes Zeus himself take him for his beauty, and tells of the grief of Tros and the horses given in payment (202–217; see [[tros]]). Later poets made it a story of love. Theognis cites it as a precedent for loving boys (1345–1348), Pindar says that Ganymede came to Zeus “for the same service” as Pelops to Poseidon (*Olympian* 1.43–45), and Plato's Athenian says the Cretans invented the tale to excuse their own custom (*Laws* 1.636c–d). [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] says that Zeus carried him off by means of an eagle (*Library* 3.12.2), and in Ovid Jupiter becomes the eagle himself (*Metamorphoses* 10.155–161). Virgil counts “the honors of the stolen Ganymede” among the causes of Juno's anger at Troy (*Aeneid* 1.28). Some made Laomedon his father: the *Little Iliad*, in lines quoted by a scholiast on Euripides, has Zeus give Laomedon a golden vine made by Hephaestus in exchange for him, and Euripides' chorus calls Ganymede “son of Laomedon” (*Trojan Women* 821–822).
