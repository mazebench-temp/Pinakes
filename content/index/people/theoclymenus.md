## The name

Θεοκλύμενος joins θεός, “god”, and κλύμενος, a participle of κλύω, “hear”, which is a name by itself, that of Clymenus, the father of Nestor's wife (*Odyssey* 3.452; see [[clymenus]]), and the second half of the name of Nestor's brother Periclymenus (*Odyssey* 11.286; see [[periclymenus]]). The poem does not explain it. Euripides gives the name to the son of Proteus, the king of Egypt who wants Helen for his wife, with an explanation that editors bracket as a later addition, “Theoclymenus, a son, [because he passed his life revering the gods]”; the king's sister Eido is called Theonoe when she comes of age, “for she knew all the things of the gods, those that are and those to come” (Euripides, *Helen* 9–15; see [[euripides-helen]]).

## Family

ἀτὰρ γενεήν γε Μελάμποδος ἔκγονος ἦεν, “and by birth he was a descendant of Melampus” (*Odyssey* 15.225; see [[melampus]]). The poet traces the line before he names him: Melampus, settled in Argos, begot Antiphates and Mantius; Antiphates' son was Oicles, and Oicles' son the seer Amphiaraus, the father of Alcmaeon and Amphilochus; Mantius begot Polypheides and Cleitus, whom Dawn carried off, and Apollo made Polypheides the best of seers after Amphiaraus died; Polypheides moved to Hyperesia, angry with his father, “His son it was who came up, and Theoclymenus was his name” (*Odyssey* 15.241–256; see [[antiphates-son-of-melampus]], [[mantius]], [[oicles]], [[amphiaraus]], [[amphilochus]], [[cleitus-son-of-mantius]], [[polypheides]] and [[hyperesia]]). ἔκγονος is “great grandson”, and τοῦ, “his”, is Polypheides (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 15.225, 15.256), as W. W. Merry's table of the house shows: Melampus, Mantius, Polypheides, Theoclymenus (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.281). His mother is not named.

## Descriptions

- θεοειδής, “godlike”, in the verse that brings in his answers, τὸν δʼ αὖτε προσέειπε Θεοκλύμενος θεοειδής, “Then godlike Theoclymenus spoke to him in turn” (*Odyssey* 15.271 = 15.508; again 17.151, 20.350, 20.363; see [[godlike]]).
- τηλεδαπός, “from a far land”, and μάντις, “a seer” (*Odyssey* 15.224–225; see [[divination]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

On Ithaca Peiraeus keeps him in his house until Telemachus brings him to the palace (*Odyssey* 17.52–56, 17.71–84; see [[peiraeus-son-of-clytius]]). There, at Penelope's table, he swears by Zeus, the table of hospitality and the hearth of Odysseus that Odysseus is already in his native land, “sitting or moving about, learning of these evil deeds”, and planting evil for all the suitors: “Such a bird of omen I made out, as I sat on the well-benched ship, / and I declared it aloud to Telemachus” (*Odyssey* 17.151–161, a plain rendering; see [[the-omen-of-the-falcon]]). At the suitors' last feast, when Athena has set them laughing “with jaws that were not their own”, he sees their heads and faces wrapped in night, the walls spattered with blood, the doorway and the court full of phantoms going down to Erebus, “and the sun / has perished out of the sky, and an evil mist has spread over all” (*Odyssey* 20.345–357, a plain rendering). They laugh at him, Eurymachus calls him mad, and he goes out to Peiraeus, “who received him gladly” (*Odyssey* 20.358–372, a plain rendering; see [[eurymachus]]). Monro: “Theoclymenus by his gift of divination or ‘second sight’ has the future scene before him as if it were already present”; the mist that covers the suitors to the head is a sign of death very near, as in Celtic belief, and he sets the blood on the walls beside an oracle that Herodotus reports, in which black blood runs down from the topmost roofs of the temples (Monro on *Odyssey* 20.351 ff.; Herodotus 7.140.3).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates, asked which passages of Homer belong to the seer's art, begins with “what the seer of the Melampodidae, Theoclymenus, says to the suitors”, and recites his vision (*Ion* 538e–539a, quoting *Odyssey* 20.351–357 without 20.354; see [[plato-ion-the-seers-passages]] and [[plato]]): the family name, the Melampodidae, is Plato's.
