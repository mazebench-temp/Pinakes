## The name

Δημόδοκος joins δῆμος, “people”, with a stem of δέχομαι, “receive”. W. W. Merry renders it *populo acceptus*, “welcome to the people”, and finds the name explained in the king's words that the god has given him song above all, “to give delight in whatever way his heart urges him to sing” (*Odyssey* 8.44–45; *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 8.44); the formula Δημόδοκον λαοῖσι τετιμένον, “Demodocus, honored by the people” (*Odyssey* 8.472; 13.28), probably stands as an interpretation of the name (Merry on *Odyssey* 8.472).

## Descriptions

The singer “whom the Muse loved above all, and she gave him both good and evil: / she robbed him of his eyes, but gave him sweet song” (*Odyssey* 8.63–64; see [[muses]] and [[zeus-gives-good-and-evil]]). He is blind, and the poem gives him the standing titles that it gives Phemius, the singer of Odysseus' house (see [[phemius]] and [[singers]]):

- θεῖος ἀοιδός, “the divine singer” (*Odyssey* 8.43, 8.47, 8.87, 8.539; 13.27), as Phemius (*Odyssey* 1.336; see [[divine]]).
- ἐρίηρος ἀοιδός, “the trusty singer” (*Odyssey* 8.62, 8.471), as Phemius (*Odyssey* 1.346; see [[trusty]]).
- ἀοιδὸς … περικλυτός, “the renowned singer” (*Odyssey* 8.83, 8.367, 8.521), as Phemius (*Odyssey* 1.325; see [[famous]]).
- λαοῖσι τετιμένον, “honored by the people” (*Odyssey* 8.472; 13.28).
- ἥρως, “the hero” (*Odyssey* 8.483; see [[heros]]).

## Later in the Odyssey

The renderings here are plain renderings, not the translation. Odysseus begins the tale of his wanderings with praise of him: it is a fine thing to listen to such a singer as this one, like the gods in his voice, and nothing is more delightful than a feast at which the banqueters sit in rows listening to a singer (*Odyssey* 9.2–11; see [[like-a-god-in-his-voice]] and [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]). On the day of the escort, at the last feast in the king's hall, “the divine singer sang among them, Demodocus, honored by the people”, while Odysseus kept turning his head toward the sun, eager for it to set (*Odyssey* 13.27–30). It is his last appearance.

## Outside Homer

**The lyre player.** Aristotle names him by his instrument. In the third kind of recognition, through memory, his example is “the one in the tale told to Alcinous, where, hearing / the lyre player, he remembered and shed tears, and so they were recognized” (*Poetics* 1455a2–3; see [[aristotle]], [[the-tale-to-alcinous]] and [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]]). κιθαριστής is not a Homeric word (Homer has κίθαρις, “the lyre”, *Odyssey* 8.248, and κιθαριστύς, “lyre playing”, *Iliad* 2.600). Hesiod uses it when he says that singers and lyre players come from the Muses and far-shooting Apollo, the two whom Odysseus names as the singer's teachers (*Theogony* 94–95; *Odyssey* 8.488; see [[hesiod]]).

**The blind Homer.** Demodocus was read from antiquity as Homer's portrait of himself. The singer of the Homeric *Hymn to Apollo* calls himself a blind man who lives in rocky Chios (*Hymn to Apollo* 172–173), and that verse, Merry suggests, is the foundation of the tradition of Homer's blindness, and secondarily of the tradition that the poet meant his description of Demodocus for himself (Merry on *Odyssey* 8.64). Proclus says that the likeness was much talked of, and makes Demodocus, who began “stirred by the god” (*Odyssey* 8.499), the inspired one among Homer's singers, beside the knowing Phemius and the imitative singer of Clytemnestra (*On the Republic* I 174, 193–194 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]], [[phemius]] and [[the-singer-of-clytemnestra]]). See [[demodocus-and-the-blind-homer]].

**A moral singer.** The Suda, under ἀοιδός, makes his song of Ares and Aphrodite a lesson for its hearers, sung not in approval of that passion but to turn the luxurious Phaeacians from lawless desires (Suda α 4402; see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite-in-later-writers]]); its entry on his name sends the reader there (Suda δ 440).
