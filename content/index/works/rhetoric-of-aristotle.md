The *Rhetoric* survives, like the *Poetics*, as the text of Aristotle's teaching; its examples come from the poets and from the speeches and politics of the fifth and fourth centuries BCE. It defines rhetoric as a capacity to see, in each case, the possible means of persuasion (1.2, 1355b25–26), by the character of the speaker, by the emotion of the hearer and by argument (see [[rhetorike]] and [[three-kinds-of-proof]]).

**Book I** sets out the art as the counterpart of dialectic, its proofs, enthymeme and example, and the three kinds of speech, deliberative, judicial and epideictic, and then gives the materials of each: for deliberation, the subjects of public debate, happiness, the good, the greater good and the constitutions; for praise, virtue and the noble; for the courts, wrongdoing and its causes, pleasure, the wrongdoer and his victim, law and equity, greater and lesser wrongs; and last the non-artistic proofs, laws, witnesses, contracts, tortures and oaths (see [[three-kinds-of-rhetoric]], [[subjects-of-deliberation]], [[the-parts-of-virtue]], [[seven-causes-of-action]], [[epieikes]] and [[non-artistic-proofs]]). **Book II** analyzes the emotions, among them anger, pity, fear and indignation (see [[aristotle-anger]], [[pity]] and [[aristotle-nemesis]]), and the characters of the young, the old and men in their prime, and lists the topics common to all speeches. **Book III** treats style and arrangement: the excellence of style, clarity and appropriateness (see [[excellence-of-diction]] and [[prepon]]); its words, metaphor and the simile, and the frigidity of words too poetic for prose (see [[metaphora]], [[eikon]] and [[psychron]]); speaking good Greek, bulk, rhythm and the period (see [[hellenizein]], [[onkos]], [[rhythmos]] and [[periodos]]); urbane sayings and setting things before the eyes (see [[asteion]] and [[before-the-eyes]]); the written style and the style of debate (see [[styles-of-writing-and-debate]]); and the parts of a speech, proem, narration, proof and epilogue, with slander, interrogation and jest (see [[taxis]], [[prooimion]], [[diegesis]], [[diabole]], [[erotesis]] and [[epilogos]]). Its examples come from Homer and the tragedians, and from Isocrates, Gorgias and the orators of Aristotle's own century.

In the *Poetics*, “what concerns thought may be left to my discussions of rhetoric, for it belongs more properly to that inquiry” (1456a34–36; see [[dianoia]]); the *Rhetoric* in turn refers to the *Poetics* for the laughable (1.11, 1372a1–2; 3.18, 1419b5–7; see [[the-lost-book-on-comedy]]) and for the style of poetry, the kinds of word and metaphor (3.1, 1404a39; 3.2, 1404b7, 1404b28, 1405a3–6; see [[poetics]]).
