## The poem

Livius Andronicus put the Odyssey into Latin verse. Cicero says that he was the first to produce a play at Rome, and dates it to the consulship of Gaius Claudius, son of Caecus, and Marcus Tuditanus, “in the very year before Ennius was born”, the five hundred and fourteenth year of the city, that is, 240 BCE; he rejects as a great error the dating of the poet Accius, who made Livius a captive taken at Tarentum by Quintus Maximus thirty years later (*Brutus* 72–73). Aulus Gellius saw a copy of the translation under its Greek title, Ὀδύσσεια (*Attic Nights* 18.9.5), and Cicero calls it the *Odyssia Latina*, “the Latin Odyssey” (*Brutus* 71).

The poem is lost. Its surviving lines, a verse or a phrase at a time, are collected on The Latin Library under the title *Odusia* and arranged by the lines of Homer they render, from Book 1 to Book 24. Eight of them render lines of Book 1, the first line of the poem among them.

## The first line

Gellius quotes the first line in a dispute over spelling. Was Cato's word *insecenda* or *insequenda*, and did Ennius write *insece* or *inseque* in his verse “Insece, Musa, …”, on what each Roman did in the war with King Philip? Gellius held for the forms without *u*, and gives his proof: “For in the library at Patrae I came upon a book of Livius Andronicus of real antiquity, entitled Ὀδύσσεια, in which the first line had this word without the *u*: *Virum mihi, Camena, insece versutum*, made from the line of Homer, ἄνδρα μοι ἔννεπε, μοῦσα, πολύτροπον. I trust that book, of great age and authority” (*Attic Nights* 18.9.3–5). He adds that the interpreters of Greek words take ἔννεπε in the Odyssey's first line, and ἔσπετε in “Tell me now, Muses”, to be what the Latin calls *inseque*, “tell on” (*Attic Nights* 18.9.9). Merry, who quotes the Latin line on *Odyssey* 1.1, explains ἔννεπε as an assimilated form of ἔνσεπε and compares *insece* with it (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 1.1; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 1.1).

The Latin keeps the man first and the epithet last, as the Greek does, and between them the same three words, “me”, “Muse” and “tell”. For the Muse it has *Camena*, the name by which Latin poets called her, as Horace speaks of “the tragic Camena” (*Ars Poetica* 275); and for πολύτροπον, “of many turns”, it has *versutum*, “turning, versatile”, the word with which Merry glosses the Greek (see [[of-many-turns]] and [[proem]]).

## Ancient readers

Cicero's verdict comes in his history of Roman oratory, where he argues that nothing is invented and perfected at the same time. There were poets before Homer, as the songs sung in his poem “at the feasts of the Phaeacians and of the suitors” show; and when Ennius boasted of his own priority among the Romans, Cicero says, he did not lie, “for the Latin Odyssey is like some work of Daedalus, and the plays of Livius are not worth reading a second time” (*nam et Odyssia Latina est sic tamquam opus aliquod Daedali et Livianae fabulae non satis dignae quae iterum legantur*, *Brutus* 71; see [[cicero]]). In his argument the works of Daedalus stand at the beginning of an art, not at its perfection.

Horace describes Rome learning by heart, and crowding the theater to watch, the poets whom it counts as its own “from the age of the writer Livius to our own time” (*Epistles* 2.1.60–62). He does not ask that the poems of Livius be destroyed, “which I remember flogging Orbilius dictating to me when I was small”, but wonders that anyone should think them correct and beautiful and little short of perfect (*Epistles* 2.1.69–72; see [[horace]]). He does not say which poems of Livius the schoolmaster dictated.
