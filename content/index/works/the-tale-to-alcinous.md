summary: The ancient name for Books 9–12 of the Odyssey, the story of his wanderings that Odysseus tells at the court of the Phaeacian king Alcinous, from the sack of Ismarus to the drift to Calypso's island; Aristotle cites the scene in which he weeps at the bard's song and so is recognized.

The name is as old as Plato. Socrates, about to tell the myth of Er, says that it will not be a tale to Alcinous, Ἀλκίνου γε ἀπόλογον, but the tale of a valiant man, ἀλκίμου μὲν ἀνδρός, playing on the king's name (*Republic* 614b; see [[plato]]). Aristotle names parts of the Odyssey in the same way, “the Bath” (*Poetics* 1454b30, 1460a26) and “the tale to Alcinous”: he says that the tale is retold to Penelope in sixty lines (*Rhetoric* 1417a14–15), and makes the moment before it begins his example of recognition through memory, when Odysseus, hearing Demodocus, weeps, Alcinous notices, and asks who he is (*Poetics* 1455a2–3; *Odyssey* 8.521–586, 9.1–20; see [[odysseus-weeps-at-the-feast]], [[alcinous]] and [[demodocus]]). Later the name was a proverb, “for those who talk nonsense and draw out a long story” (Suda α 3402; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-9]]).

The tale has a prelude in the seventh book, where Odysseus answers Arete's question with the end of his wanderings only, from the wreck of his last ship to the shore of Scheria, and keeps back his name (*Odyssey* 7.241–297; see [[odysseus-at-the-house-of-alcinous]]). A verse of that account stands again in the opening of the great tale (*Odyssey* 9.15 = 7.242), and the tale ends where the account began, with the nine days' drift to Ogygia, breaking off because he told the rest to the king and his wife the day before (*Odyssey* 12.447–453).

The tale keeps the order of the voyage. It begins with the raid on the Cicones, the storm off Malea and the Lotus-eaters, and the Cyclops (*Odyssey* 9; see [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]], [[the-lotus-eaters]] and [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]); then come Aeolus and the winds, the Laestrygonians and Circe (*Odyssey* 10; see [[odysseus-and-aeolus]], [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]], [[odysseus-and-circe]] and [[the-death-of-elpenor]]), the journey to the dead (*Odyssey* 11), and the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, the cattle of the Sun and the last shipwreck (*Odyssey* 12; see [[odysseus-and-the-sirens]], [[odysseus-passes-scylla]], [[the-cattle-of-helios]] and [[the-shipwreck-of-odysseus]]). Odysseus gives Penelope the same order when he tells her his wanderings in the twenty-third book (*Odyssey* 23.310–343).

D. B. Monro saw in the tale the poet's way of bringing the longest of the returns within the compass of one poem. Its immediate aim was to shorten the time of the action, but the first person gave the hero a dramatic interest, allowed some freedom in the order, so that Odysseus could tell the last stage of his wanderings first, to Arete, as a prologue, and softened the dissonance of fairy-tale wonders in a heroic poem: “We do not of course put the wonders of these four books in the same category with the deliberately false stories afterwards told in Ithaca. Yet the interposition of a narrator, and that narrator the master of fair-seeming falsehood, gives a certain sense of remoteness which is in harmony with the substance of the tale” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix I §5; see [[the-first-cretan-tale]]). The tale's spell on its hearers became a measure: an unnamed writer quoted in the Suda says of someone that “he enchanted his hearers no less, if not more, than Homer's Odysseus the Phaeacians” (Suda ο 787).
