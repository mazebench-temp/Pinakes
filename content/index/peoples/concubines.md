## Meaning

A παλλακίς shares a man's bed without being his wedded wife (κουριδίη ἄλοχος; see [[marriage]]). She might be bought: the Cretan whose part Odysseus plays in the Odyssey was the son of “a bought mother, a concubine” (ὠνητὴ … παλλακίς), honored by his father like the legitimate sons (Od. 14.199–206). Or she might be a captive. The Iliad does not call its captive women παλλακίδες, but their place in a man's house is the same: Agamemnon says that Chryseis will grow old in his house “as she walks to and fro at the loom and comes to share my bed” (1.29–31), and promises Teucer a woman “who would go up into your bed with you” (8.291; see [[captive-women]] and [[bed]]). Their sons are νόθοι, acknowledged but ranking below the sons of the wife (see [[nothos]]).

## The concubine and the wife

The word occurs twice in the Iliad, both times in Phoenix's account of why he left his father's house:

{{quote:991cec53-e0c0-5094-b22a-cf769925fbd0}}

Amyntor “dishonored his wife” (ἀτιμάζεσκε δʼ ἄκοιτιν, 9.450) for the woman he loved, and the wife struck back through her son, so that the concubine “would hate the old man”. The father answered with a curse of childlessness (9.453–457; see [[curses]], [[amyntor]] and [[the-flight-of-phoenix]]). Like the quarrel of Book 1, it is a quarrel of two men over a woman.

Other houses keep the peace in other ways. Laertes bought the young Eurycleia for the price of twenty oxen and honored her like his wife, “but he never lay with her, for he avoided his wife's anger” (Od. 1.429–433). Theano, Antenor's wife, reared his bastard son Pedaeus “like her own dear children, to please her husband” (5.69–71; see [[theano]] and [[pedaeus]]). Agamemnon prefers Chryseis to Clytemnestra, his wedded wife (1.113–115), and in the underworld he tells how Clytemnestra killed Cassandra, the daughter of Priam he had brought home, beside him (Od. 11.421–423; see [[clytemnestra]]). Priam had many women. Laothoe, a king's daughter, bore him Lycaon and Polydorus (21.84–88, 22.46–48; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]), and of his fifty sons nineteen were Hecuba's: “the others the women in my halls bore me” (24.495–497; see [[priam]] and [[hecuba]]). Menelaus' son Megapenthes was born to him by a slave woman, after Helen had borne Hermione and no more (Od. 4.10–14).
