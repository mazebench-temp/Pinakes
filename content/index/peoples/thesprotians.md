## In Homer

The Iliad does not name the Thesprotians. In the Odyssey their land is where the disguised Odysseus says that he was cast up after a shipwreck and heard news of Odysseus, and where Odysseus himself is now, alive, “near at hand, in the rich land of the Thesprotian men”, as Eumaeus reports the stranger's words to Penelope and the stranger then tells her himself (*Odyssey* 17.525–527, 19.270–272, plain renderings; see [[rich-land]]). Their king is Pheidon, who takes in the castaway (*Odyssey* 14.316, 19.287; see [[pheidon]]). Their ships sail to the islands: it is on a ship “of Thesprotian men” bound for Dulichium that the stranger says he came to Ithaca (*Odyssey* 14.334–335 = 19.291–292; see [[dulichium]]), and Eumaeus tells Telemachus that the stranger has “escaped from a ship of Thesprotian men” (*Odyssey* 16.65, a plain rendering). Pheidon says that Odysseus had gone from his house to Dodona, to hear the will of Zeus from the god's oak (*Odyssey* 14.327–330 = 19.296–299; see [[dodona]]); the poem puts the oracle within reach of the Thesprotian king, but does not say in whose land it lay.

Once the Thesprotians appear outside the tales. Penelope reminds Antinous that his father Eupeithes had come to Odysseus' house as a fugitive, because he had joined Taphian pirates and “grieved the Thesprotians; and they were at peace with us” (*Odyssey* 16.424–427, a plain rendering; see [[eupeithes]], [[taphians]] and [[piracy]]). The people of Ithaca were angry on the Thesprotians' behalf, and Odysseus kept them from killing him.

## Outside Homer

Later writers place the Thesprotians in Epirus, by the oracle. [[strabo|Strabo]] counts them among the peoples of Epirus (*Geography* 7.7.1), and says that the Chaonians, the Thesprotians and the Cassopaeans, who are also Thesprotians, hold the coast from the Ceraunian mountains to the Ambracian Gulf, a prosperous country; above the gulf into which the Acheron flows lies Cichyrus, formerly Ephyra, “a city of the Thesprotians” (*Geography* 7.7.5; see [[acheron]] and [[ephyra]]). Dodona, he says, was in ancient times under the Thesprotians, with Mount Tomarus, below which the sanctuary lies, and the tragic poets and Pindar called it Thesprotian; later it came under the Molossians (*Geography* 7.7.11; see [[dodona]]). He also says that Homer names the whole Greek coast “as far as the Thesprotians” (*Geography* 1.1.10, 1.2.20).

The tragic poets do call it so. Aeschylus' Prometheus tells Io how she came to Dodona, the seat of “Thesprotian Zeus” (Θεσπρωτοῦ Διός), where the oaks speak (*Prometheus Bound* 829–832), and in Euripides Creon sends his son past Delphi to the Aetolian land and on to “the Thesprotian soil”, which the boy takes to be “the holy seat of Dodona” (*Phoenician Women* 980–983; see [[aeschylus]] and [[euripides]]). The Suda calls Dodona “a city in Thesprotian Pelasgia”, where women prophets spoke for an oak, and explains Zeus' title Δωδωναῖος by his worship “at Dodona in Thesprotia” (Suda δ 1446–1447).

[[herodotus|Herodotus]] tells how the oracle began among them. Of the two priestesses of Theban Zeus whom Phoenicians carried off from Egypt, the one sold into Greece was sold, he thinks, among the Thesprotians, and there, a slave, she founded a sanctuary of Zeus under an oak and, when she had learned Greek, established the oracle (*Histories* 2.56.1–3). He also has the Thessalians come out of Thesprotia to settle the land that they held in his day (7.176.4), and tells how Periander sent to the Thesprotians, to the oracle of the dead on the river Acheron (5.92η.2; see [[acheron]]).

The *Telegony*, the sequel to the Odyssey ascribed to Eugammon of Cyrene, brought Odysseus back to them. In Proclus' summary of the poem, as D. B. Monro gives it, Odysseus went after the killing of the suitors to the country of the Thesprotians, married their queen Callidice and led them in a war against the Brygi, and when she died his son Polypoetes became king; Monro took the Thesprotian episode to have been made to find a place for families that claimed descent from Odysseus (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, Appendix III §9; see [[telegonus-kills-odysseus]]). Monro also counts the name of Thesprotia among the signs that the geography of the Odyssey goes beyond the Iliad's, since the country lay on the westward route from Greece (Appendix II §6).
