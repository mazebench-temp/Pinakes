## The name

Λωτοφάγοι, “lotus-eaters”, from λωτός, “lotus”, and φαγεῖν, “to eat”: the people are named for their food (see [[lotus]]). Odysseus calls them men “who eat a flowery food”, οἵ τʼ ἄνθινον εἶδαρ ἔδουσιν (*Odyssey* 9.84), which Merry takes as “vegetable” food; Bothe, he reports, conjectured ἀκάνθινον, “thorny”, from Polybius' description of the lotus as a small tree, rough and thorny (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 9.84; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 9.84).

## In Homer

Rounding Malea, Odysseus is driven off course past Cythera, and for nine days deadly winds carry him over the sea; on the tenth he sets foot “on the land of the Lotus-eaters” (*Odyssey* 9.80–84; see [[malea]] and [[cythera]]). He sends two men and a herald to find out “what men they might be who eat bread upon the earth” (*Odyssey* 9.88–90; see [[spies-and-scouts]]). The Lotus-eaters “devised no destruction for our companions, / but gave them some of the lotus to taste” (*Odyssey* 9.92–93). Whoever eats “the honey-sweet fruit of the lotus” no longer wishes to bring back word or to return, but to stay among them, “munching the lotus, and to forget their homecoming”; Odysseus drags the scouts weeping to the ships, binds them under the thwarts, and sails at once, so that no one else may eat of it (*Odyssey* 9.94–104; see [[nostos]] and [[the-lotus-eaters]]). The poem says nothing more of them: neither where their land lies nor how they live, beyond their food.

Odysseus names them once more, in the summary of his wanderings that he gives Penelope: he told her how he first overcame the Cicones, and then came to the rich plowland of the Lotus-eater men (*Odyssey* 23.310–311, a plain rendering; see [[cicones]]).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus places the Lotophagi on the coast of Libya: on a headland running out into the sea from the land of the Gindanes live the Lotophagi, who live on the fruit of the lotus alone; the fruit is as big as a mastic berry and as sweet as the date, and they make wine from it as well; their neighbors along the sea, the Machlyes, use the lotus too, but less (*Histories* 4.177, 4.178; see [[herodotus]]). Later writers looked for the Homeric land near the Lesser Syrtis. Polybius, as Strabo reports him, held that what is found on the island of Meninx agrees with what Homer says of the Lotus-eaters, and that the nine days of “deadly winds” from Malea (*Odyssey* 9.82) are better taken of a short distance, since such winds do not blow on a straight course, than of a voyage out into the Ocean (*Geography* 1.2.17; see [[polybius]] and [[strabo]]). Strabo himself says that the Lesser Syrtis is also called the Syrtis of the Lotus-eaters, and that Meninx is held to be the land of the Lotus-eaters of Homer: certain tokens are shown there, an altar of Odysseus and the fruit itself, for the tree called lotus grows in plenty on the island, with a most pleasant fruit (*Geography* 17.3.17). He reports too, after Artemidorus, that the people beyond Maurusia, near the western Ethiopians, were called Lotus-eaters, living on lotus, a kind of herb and root, and needing no drink, and that others of the name lived on Meninx (*Geography* 3.4.3). Pliny puts a coast of the Lotophagi, “whom some have called Machroae”, in the innermost part of the Greater Syrtis, by the Altars of the Philaeni, and says that Eratosthenes called Meninx Lotophagitis (*Natural History* 5.28, 5.41). Merry, reviewing these accounts, finds that their general result is to place the land of the Lotus-eaters somewhere near the Lesser Syrtis (on *Odyssey* 9.80).
