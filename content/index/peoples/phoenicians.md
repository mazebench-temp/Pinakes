## The name

Φοίνικες, the Phoenicians, have a name that sounds like the word φοῖνιξ, which in Homer is the purple-red of dye (4.141), the bay coat of a horse (23.454), and in the Odyssey the date palm (*Odyssey* 6.163); it is also the name of Achilles' tutor (see [[phoenix]] and [[purple]]). Whether the people's name and the word for the color are connected is uncertain. Their land is Φοινίκη, Phoenicia (*Odyssey* 4.83), and its city in Homer is [[Sidon]]; the poems never name Tyre.

## In Homer

{{quote:8a94f5a4-7b52-530d-aefc-309fd91a94e7}}

The Iliad names them once, in the history of a prize. The silver mixing bowl that Achilles sets out for the footrace was made by Sidonian craftsmen, and “Phoenician men brought it over the misty sea / and set it out in harbors, and gave it as a gift to Thoas” (23.744–745; see [[sidonians]], [[mixing-bowl]] and [[thoas-of-lemnos]]). Walter Leaf notes that Homer always keeps the Sidonians as craftsmen apart from the Phoenicians as traders, and that the Phoenicians, familiar in the Odyssey, are not mentioned again in the Iliad (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 23.743). στῆσαν may mean that they landed, or that they set the bowl out for show; Leaf suggests that they gave the king a present to buy leave to trade in his country (on 23.745). The bowl then passed to Euneus, who gave it to Patroclus as the price of Lycaon (23.746–747; see [[euneus]] and [[trade]]).

The Odyssey knows them well. Menelaus wandered to Cyprus, Phoenicia and Egypt on his way home (*Odyssey* 4.83; see [[cyprus]] and [[egypt]]). In the false tales Odysseus tells on Ithaca they are sailors for hire and dealers in men. He begged “the lordly Phoenicians” to carry him to Pylos or Elis, and the wind drove them off course against their will (*Odyssey* 13.271–277); and a Phoenician “skilled in deceits” took him home to Phoenicia, kept him a year, and then shipped him to Libya meaning to sell him there for a vast price (*Odyssey* 14.287–297; see [[libya]]). In Eumaeus' story Phoenician traders, “famed for ships”, came to his island with countless trinkets and stayed a whole year trading; a Phoenician woman of his father's house, herself a Sidonian carried off by pirates, went away with them and took the child, who would fetch a vast price wherever they sold him, and they sold him to Laertes (*Odyssey* 15.415–484; see [[eumaeus]] and [[slavery-and-freedom]]). The word for the price is ὦνος, the word of the bowl that bought Lycaon (23.746).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus begins his history with them. The learned among the Persians, he says, made the Phoenicians the cause of the quarrel between Greeks and barbarians: coming from the sea called Red, they settled the land they still hold and at once took to long voyages, carrying Egyptian and Assyrian wares; at Argos, when they had sold nearly all their cargo, they seized the king's daughter Io and other women who had come down to the shore, and sailed off to Egypt (Herodotus 1.1). He also tells that the Phoenicians who came to Greece with Cadmus brought the Greeks their letters, which the Ionians learned from them and called Phoenician letters, Φοινικήια (5.58; see [[herodotus]], [[cadmus]] and [[writing]]).
