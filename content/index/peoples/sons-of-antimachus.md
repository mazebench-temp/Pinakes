**In one chariot.** Agamemnon catches the two “in one chariot, as they were driving the swift horses together; / for the glossy reins had slipped from their hands, / and the two were thrown into confusion” (11.127–129), and he comes at them like a lion (11.129). From the chariot they beg him, speaking as one, in the dual:

{{quote:639575e0-8141-501c-ac17-b6be36960d51}}

Their plea is the plea of Adrastus, whom Agamemnon killed in Book 6, verse for verse (11.131–135 ≈ 6.46–50), with “we two” for “I” and “the house of Antimachus” for “the house of my wealthy father”. Dolon offered the same bronze, gold and worked iron (10.379–381; see [[supplication]], [[apoina]], [[adrastus-the-trojan]] and [[dolon]]).

**The answer.** They speak “with soothing words; but the voice they heard would not be soothed” (11.137): μειλιχίοις … ἀμείλικτον, the same word turned to its opposite. The half-verse returns when Achilles answers Lycaon (21.98; see [[lycaon-son-of-priam]]).

{{quote:63d8e970-c67a-5707-a767-485305567509}}

They named their father as the source of their ransom, and Agamemnon makes the name the reason for their death. In Book 6 he would not let Menelaus spare Adrastus, because no Trojan was to escape (6.55–60); here he punishes the sons for the father's counsel against the envoys (see [[antimachus]], [[the-embassy-to-troy]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). He thrusts Peisander from the chariot with a spear in the chest, cuts off Hippolochus' arms and head, and sends the trunk “rolling like a log through the throng” (11.143–147). No suppliant on the battlefield of the Iliad is spared (see [[supplication]]).
