## The pair

Αἴαντε, the dual of Αἴας, means “the two Ajaxes”, and usually comes with δύω, “two” (2.406, 5.519, 6.436, 10.228, 12.335). The plural serves the same pair: Αἴαντες (7.164, 8.79, 8.262) and the dative Αἰάντεσσι (4.273, 4.280, 12.353). The Iliad has two heroes of the name, the great Ajax, son of Telamon, and the lesser, son of Oileus, who leads the Locrians (2.527–529; see [[ajax]], [[ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[locrians]]). The dual names them together without telling them apart; when one of them is meant, the poem adds “Telamonian” or “son of Oileus” (12.364–365). In Book 17 Telamonian Ajax calls the two of them ὁμώνυμοι, “of one name”, who have long faced sharp Ares side by side (17.720–721). Some scholars have argued that the dual once meant Ajax and his half-brother Teucer; in the Iliad as we have it the pair is Telamonian Ajax and the son of Oileus, and the theory remains unproven (see [[teucer]]).

## Descriptions

- θεράποντες Ἄρηος, “attendants of Ares”, a title of the whole army (2.110, 6.67), is given to the pair when they run before Zeus' thunder and when they volunteer for the night raid (8.79, 10.228; see [[attendants-of-ares]]).
- θοῦριν ἐπιειμένοι ἀλκήν, “clothed in furious courage”, brings them to their feet for Hector's challenge and out behind Diomedes, and returns when they drive Hector from the body of Patroclus (7.164 = 8.262, 18.157; see [[furious]]).
- πολέμου ἀκορήτω, “insatiable of war” (12.335; see [[insatiable]]).
- Αἴαντʼ Ἀργείων ἡγήτορε χαλκοχιτώνων, “Ajaxes, leaders of the bronze-armored Argives”, is Agamemnon's address to them in the review and the herald Thootes' at the wall (4.285 = 12.354; see [[bronze-armored]]); Book 17 uses its first half again (17.508, 17.669).

## Elsewhere in Homer

**Before the wall.** Agamemnon calls them to his sacrifice among the elders, after Nestor and Idomeneus (2.404–406). In the review he finds them arming, with a cloud of foot soldiers behind them dark as a storm cloud over the sea, and gives them no orders, “for you yourselves bid the people fight with all their might” (4.273–291; see [[storm-cloud]] and [[the-review-of-the-army]]). They urge on the Danaans with Odysseus and Diomedes (5.519–520), and Andromache names them first among “the best of them”, who have three times made trial of the wall by the fig tree (6.435–437; see [[the-fig-tree]]). They rise third of the nine who would meet Hector (7.161–164), run with the rest when Zeus thunders and come out again behind Diomedes (8.78–79, 8.262), and both are willing to go with Diomedes into the Trojan camp (10.227–228).

**The ships and Patroclus.** In Book 13 Poseidon comes to them first, in the likeness of Calchas, and strikes them both with his staff to fill them with might; the son of Oileus is the first to know the god (13.43–82; see [[poseidon]] and [[calchas]]). Like two lions holding a goat high in their jaws, they lift the body of Imbrius and strip it, and the son of Oileus cuts off the head and sends it spinning through the throng to fall at Hector's feet (13.197–205). Idomeneus counts on “the two Ajaxes and Teucer” to hold the middle of the ships (13.312–316). Side by side they fight like two wine-dark oxen straining at one plow, though the Locrians, bowmen and slingers, do not follow the lesser Ajax into close fighting (13.701–722). When Sarpedon falls, Patroclus calls on them first (16.555–561). Over Patroclus' own body they hold back the Trojans while Menelaus and Meriones carry it away, as a wooded ridge holds back floodwater, and three times they drive Hector from it (17.707–761, 18.157–164).
