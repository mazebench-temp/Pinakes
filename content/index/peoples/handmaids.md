## Words

ἀμφίπολος, from ἀμφί (“around”) and πέλομαι (“move, be busy”), is the woman who waits on her mistress and goes about the house with her. δμῳή is the feminine of δμώς, “slave”: a woman owned, often won in war. The ταμίη, “housekeeper”, keeps the stores and gives out the food (Od. 1.139); the word is related to τάμνω, “cut”, and Zeus is ταμίης πολέμοιο, the “dispenser of war” (4.84; see [[dispenser-of-war]]). τιθήνη, “nurse”, is from the root of θῆσθαι, “suck the breast” (θήσατο μαζόν, 24.58). The words overlap: the woman who pours water for Priam's hands is his “handmaid housekeeper” (ἀμφίπολον ταμίην, 24.302).

## In Homer

A lady does not go out alone. “Not alone; two handmaids went along with her” is said of Helen going to the wall (3.143) and twice of Penelope coming down among the suitors (Od. 1.331, 18.207; see [[aethra]] and [[clymene]]). Handmaids work wool and weave beside their mistress (3.422, 6.323–324, 6.490–492; see [[weaving]]), heat water for a bath (22.442–444), and pour water over a man's hands before he prays or eats (24.302–304; Od. 1.136–138; see [[hand-washing]]). In a house of mourning they lament with its women (6.498–500, 18.28–31).

Many were captives. Achilles and Patroclus took the slave women of their hut in raids (18.28; see [[captive-women]] and [[raiding]]), and every Trojan wife knows that she may join them: Hector foresees Andromache weaving at another woman's bidding and carrying water in Argos (6.454–458; see [[doulios]]). Hephaestus is served by handmaids of gold, made like living girls, with mind and voice and strength (18.417–420; see [[hephaestus]]).

The Odyssey is full of them: Eurycleia, Odysseus' old nurse (Od. 2.361); Eurynome the housekeeper (Od. 17.495); and the fifty slave women of Odysseus' house, twelve of whom took up with the suitors and were hanged for it (Od. 22.421–425, 22.465–473).
