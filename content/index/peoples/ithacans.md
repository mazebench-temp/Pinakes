## The name

Ἰθακήσιοι, “men of Ithaca”, is the people's name, formed from Ἰθάκη. The Iliad has only the singular, Ἰθακήσιος, “of Ithaca”, said of Odysseus' herald Eurybates (2.184; see [[eurybates-of-ithaca]]), and the Odyssey says it of Odysseus himself (*Odyssey* 2.246, 22.45; see [[ithaca]]). The plural is found only in the Odyssey, in eight lines, six of them in address. The poems give the same people other names. They are Achaeans: Telemachus has the heralds summon “the long-haired Achaeans” to the assembly (*Odyssey* 2.7; see [[achaeans]]). With the other islands and the mainland coast that Odysseus rules they are the Cephallenians, whom he leads to Troy (2.631–635) and over whom Laertes was lord before him (*Odyssey* 24.378; see [[cephallenians]]).

## In the Odyssey

The Ithacans meet in assembly twice. Telemachus calls the first, the first since Odysseus sailed (*Odyssey* 2.26–27). The last meets when the news of the suitors' death has gone through the city and the families have buried their dead: they go to the assembly together, grieving at heart, and the next verse is the verse of the first gathering (*Odyssey* 24.413–421; 24.421 = 2.9). As in the first, a father who has lost his son speaks first, in tears: Eupeithes calls for vengeance for Antinous (*Odyssey* 24.422–437; 24.425 = 2.24, of Aegyptius; see [[eupeithes]]). The herald Medon tells them that Odysseus did not do these deeds against the will of the gods, for he saw a god standing beside him in the likeness of Mentor (*Odyssey* 24.443–449). Then the old Halitherses, son of Mastor, speaks to them with the verse he used in the first assembly, “Hear me now, men of Ithaca, in what I say” (*Odyssey* 24.454 = 2.161; see [[hear-me-now-men-of-ithaca]]): what has happened came of their own baseness, for they would not listen to him or to Mentor, shepherd of the people, and stop their sons' folly (*Odyssey* 24.455–462). The assembly splits. More than half leap up with a great shout, the rest stay together, and those whom the speech does not please follow Eupeithes to arms (*Odyssey* 24.463–469). Laertes kills Eupeithes, and Odysseus and his son fall on the front ranks, until Athena calls out, “Hold back from grievous war, men of Ithaca”, so that they may part without bloodshed (*Odyssey* 24.520–532), and in the likeness of Mentor sets oaths between the two sides (*Odyssey* 24.546–548; see [[athena]]).

Between the two assemblies the poem shows the people through others' eyes. Telemachus tells Theoclymenus that the Ithacans now look on Eurymachus as a god, the best of the suitors and the most eager to marry Penelope and have Odysseus' honor (*Odyssey* 15.519–522; see [[eurymachus]]). When the suitors lie dead, Laertes fears that all the Ithacans will come at once and send messages through the cities of the Cephallenians (*Odyssey* 24.353–355; see [[laertes]]). The renderings of Books 15 and 24 of the Odyssey, but for Halitherses' verse, are plain renderings, not the translation.
