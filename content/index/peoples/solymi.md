κυδάλιμοι, “glorious” (see [[kydalimos]]), is most often said of heroes, above all Menelaus, and here of a whole people; Σολύμοισι … κυδαλίμοισι frames the end of both lines that name them (6.184, 6.204). φάτο, “he said”: the verdict on the battle is Bellerophon's own, handed down in the family that Glaucus speaks for. The Solymi are enemies of two generations of Lycian kings: Bellerophon fought them, and his son [[Isander]] was killed by Ares “as he fought the glorious Solymi” (6.203–204). Homer does not say where they lived. In the Odyssey, Poseidon, coming back from the Ethiopians, sees Odysseus' raft “from far off, from the mountains of the Solymi” (Od. 5.282–283). See [[bellerophon]] and [[lycia]].

## Outside Homer

Herodotus says that the land the Lycians held was once the land of the Milyans, who in those days were called Solymi (1.173.2). Strabo notes that Homer keeps the Solymi apart from the Lycians, and prefers the view that Homer's Solymi were the Milyans of his own day (14.3.10). He places a mountain called Solyma above Phaselis, near Termessus in Pisidia, whose people were also called Solymi, and says that near Termessus were the palisade of Bellerophon and the tomb of his son (14.3.9, 13.4.16). Pindar names the Solymi among Bellerophon's victims (*Olympian* 13.90). Tacitus reports a view that the Jews descended from the Solymi, “a people celebrated in the poems of Homer”, who gave their own name to Jerusalem, Hierosolyma (*Histories* 5.2).
