## The word

δῆμος is first a district and its land, as in “the land of great-hearted Erechtheus” (δῆμον Ἐρεχθῆος μεγαλήτορος, 2.547), and then the people who live there. A δήμου ἀνήρ (“a man of the people”) is a man of the district, set against the βασιλεύς and the ἔξοχος ἀνήρ (“the man of note”, 2.188). See [[demos]] and [[laos]]. πληθύς (“multitude, crowd”) is the mass of the army as opposed to its leaders.

## Odysseus and the commoners

{{quote:a6e5efd3-f944-5871-af60-228c9844d8cf}}

To the kings [[Odysseus]] gives reasons; to the commoner he gives the scepter across his back and an order. The commoner is to listen to “others who are better than you”, and he is “never counted for anything in war or in council”. The Catalogue itself obeys this rule: it names the leaders and counts the ships, and the poet says he could not name the multitude even with ten tongues (2.488–490; see [[ten-tongues]]).

## The crowd in Book 2

It is the crowd, “every man in the crowd who had not heard the council” (2.143), that Agamemnon's test sends running for the ships. It is also the crowd that laughs at [[Thersites]] and speaks, through an anonymous “someone”, its approval of Odysseus (2.270–278; see [[tis-speech]]). The commoners have a voice in the assembly only as a noise: they shout approval (2.333–335, 2.394–397), or they are a din that nine heralds must quiet (2.95–100).

## Outside Homer

Socrates' accuser, Xenophon reports, charged that he used to quote these lines of Odysseus, to the kings and to the man of the people, as if the poet approved of beating the poor. Xenophon answers that Socrates meant only that men who were useless in word and deed should be kept in their place, even if they were rich (*Memorabilia* 1.2.58–59).
