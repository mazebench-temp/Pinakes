## The name

Κήτειοι is named once in Homer, of the men who fell around Eurypylus (*Odyssey* 11.521). The ancient readers did not know what to make of it. As W. W. Merry reports the scholia, Aristarchus understood “the great ones”, from κῆτος, “a sea monster”; others took them for a people of Mysia; some wrote κήδειοι, “kinsmen”, and others χήτειοι γυναικῶν, “bereft of their women” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on *Odyssey* 11.521; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 11.521). The name of a Mysian stock, Merry says, was said to come from the river Ceteius, which flows into the Caicus in the district of Teuthrania (on *Odyssey* 11.521; see [[mysians]]). He also reports that some saw in them the children of Heth of Genesis, the Kheta of the Egyptian monuments and the Khatti of the Assyrian inscriptions, a powerful people of northern Syria, citing Gladstone's *Homeric Synchronism*.

## In Homer

Odysseus tells the soul of Achilles of his son's deeds before Troy, and of the greatest of them: Neoptolemus killed “the son of Telephus, / the hero Eurypylus, and many of his companions around him, / the Ceteians, were killed, for the sake of gifts to a woman” (*Odyssey* 11.519–521; see [[neoptolemus]], [[eurypylus-son-of-telephus]] and [[neoptolemus-kills-eurypylus]]). The Iliad names neither them nor this Eurypylus, who in Merry's account of the legend was the last of the allies to come to the help of the Trojans (on *Odyssey* 11.520).

## Outside Homer

Strabo, describing Teuthrania between the Caicus and Pergamum, where Teuthras and after him Telephus ruled, says that the poet records only this much of the story, quoting the three verses, “setting us a riddle rather than saying anything clear: for we do not know whom we should take the Ceteians to be, nor what ‘for the sake of gifts to a woman’ means; and the grammarians too, bringing in little myths, argue ingeniously rather than solve the questions” (*Geography* 13.1.69). He leaves it at this: Eurypylus appears, by Homer, to have ruled in the country around the Caicus, and in its favor is a torrent shown in the territory of Elaea and called the Ceteius, which falls into another stream and then into the Caicus (13.1.70); and he names “the people called Ceteians, those under Eurypylus” between the Cilicians and the Pelasgians of the Aeolian coast (13.3.2; see [[strabo]] and [[telephus]]).
