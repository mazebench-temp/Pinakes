## The name

Homer has the name once, in the accusative: “and I came to the Ethiopians and the Sidonians and the Erembi” (*Odyssey* 4.84). Ἐρεμβούς is Aristarchus' reading; Crates read Ἐρεμνούς, “the dark ones”, the word the Iliad gives the aegis and a black squall (4.167, 12.375), and others, Zeno among them, wrote Ἄραβάς τε, “and the Arabs” (Merry's critical note on *Odyssey* 4.84; Strabo, *Geography* 1.2.34; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 4.84). Merry thinks the connection of the name with ἔρεβος, “darkness”, ἐρεβεννός and ἐρεμνός, “dark”, certain, and takes the Erembi for a branch of the Ethiopians living on the coast of the Mediterranean opposite Cyprus; he reports that the scholia and Eustathius make Aristarchus identify them with the Arabs, which Lehrs denied, and among modern guesses the islanders of Arad off the Phoenician coast, and Ameis' Hebrews, Aramaeans and Arabians (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 4.84; see [[ethiopians]] and [[cyprus]]).

## Outside Homer

Strabo, taking up the questions that Menelaus' wanderings raised, asks which Ethiopians he could have reached sailing from Egypt, who the Sidonians are, and “who the Erembi are, for the name is new”; the grammarian Aristonicus, he says, had gathered many answers to each in his book on the wanderings of Menelaus (*Geography* 1.2.31; see [[strabo]] and [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). His own answer is that the likeliest view takes them for the Arabs. Zeno, “our Zeno” as Strabo calls him, even wrote the verse “and I came to the Ethiopians and the Sidonians and the Arabs”; Strabo would not change an old reading, and blames instead the changes that names undergo among all peoples. He prefers Posidonius, who saw the kinship of the Armenians, the Syrians and the Arabs in their speech, their life and their looks, and in their names too: the Syrians call themselves Arammaei, and the names of the Armenians, the Arabs and the Erembi are like it, so that the old Greeks may have called the Arabs Erembi. Most derived the name from εἰς τὴν ἔραν ἐμβαίνειν, “going into the earth”, and later writers, to be plainer, called the same people Troglodytes, “cave-dwellers”: Arabs living on the side of the Arabian Gulf toward Egypt and Ethiopia, near the Thebaid. Homer, Strabo concludes, named them as he named the Ethiopians, to show the length and the fame of the journey. He adds that Hesiod, in the *Catalogue*, already names “the daughter of Arabus, whom guileless Hermaon begot”, so that the land may have been called Arabia in his day, though perhaps not yet in the age of the heroes (1.2.34; see [[hesiod]]). In his account of Arabia he gives Posidonius' reading of the verse, “and the Sidonians and the Arambi”, after the three neighboring peoples, Armenians, Aramaeans and Arambi, and rejects “the dark ones” as more proper to the Ethiopians (16.4.27). When he first lists what Homer knew of the far south, he counts the Erembi with Libya, Ethiopia and the Sidonians, and calls them, “it is likely”, Troglodyte Arabs (*Geography* 1.1.3).
