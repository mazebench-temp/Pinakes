## Etymology

Ἰάονες is the older, uncontracted form of the name that later Greek writes Ἴωνες. The Greeks' eastern neighbors knew it in the same older shape: the Hebrew Bible names Javan among the sons of Japheth (Genesis 10.2), and in Aeschylus' *Persians* the Persians call the Greeks who fought at Salamis Ionians (Ἰαόνων, *Persians* 178, 563; Ἰάνων, 950–951).

## Descriptions

ἑλκεχίτωνες, “with trailing tunics”, from ἕλκω, “drag, trail”, and χιτών, “tunic”: men whose tunics reach the ground (see [[with-trailing-tunics]]). The word occurs only here in Homer. Elsewhere in the Iliad trailing garments belong to women: the Trojan women are ἑλκεσίπεπλοι, “with trailing robes” (6.442, 7.297, 22.105).

## In Homer

Hector holds on where he first broke in, by the ships of Ajax and Protesilaus, where the wall was built lowest (13.679–684; see [[the-battle-at-the-ships]] and [[the-achaean-wall]]). There five peoples stand against him:

The poet then names the leaders, but not of the same peoples. “These were the picked men of the Athenians” (13.689), under Menestheus, Pheidas, Stichius and Bias; then the Epeians under Meges, Amphion and Dracius, and the Phthians under Medon and Podarces, who fight beside the Boeotians (13.689–700; see [[athenians]], [[epeians]], [[phthians]] and [[boeotians]]). The Locrians come in last, with the two Ajaxes (13.712–722; see [[locrians]]). The first list has no Athenians and the second no Ionians, so the picked men of the Athenians seem to be the Ionians under their city's name (see the translation's note on 13.685–689). The Catalogue of Ships has no Ionians. Athens sends fifty ships under Menestheus (2.546–556; see [[menestheus]]).

## Outside Homer

**The Athenians.** Strabo, explaining why the Catalogue names no towns of the Megarid, says that Ionians once held both the Megarid and Attica, that Attica was anciently called Ionia and Ias, and that “the poet, when he says ‘There the Boeotians and Ionians’, means the Athenians” (*Geography* 9.1.5; see [[strabo]]). Elsewhere he says that the Ionians who held Achaea in the Peloponnese were of Athenian stock, and that the land was called Ionia after them, “as Attica too was, from Ion son of Xuthus” (*Geography* 8.7.1), and that the Ionic dialect is the old Attic, “for the Attic people of that time were called Ionians” (*Geography* 8.1.2). Herodotus gives the Athenians a series of names: Pelasgians called Cranai at first, Cecropidae under Cecrops, Athenians under Erechtheus, and Ionians when Ion son of Xuthus became their war leader (Herodotus 8.44.2); in his own day, he says, the Athenians and most other Ionians were ashamed of the name (Herodotus 1.143.3; see [[herodotus]]).

**The hymn.** The Homeric Hymn to Apollo gives the same epithet to the Ionians who gather at Delos, “where the Ionians with trailing tunics gather for you, with their children and their modest wives”, and delight the god with boxing, dancing and song (*Hymn to Apollo* 146–150). In his account of the Athenians' purification of Delos, Thucydides quotes these verses, in a text with some differences of wording, as Homer's, “from the prooemium of Apollo”, to prove that there was once a great gathering of the Ionians and the neighboring islanders at Delos, with contests of athletics and music (Thucydides 3.104.3–6; see [[thucydides]]).

**The dress.** Strabo reports the view of some that the Curetes of Aetolia took their name from dressing like girls (κόραι), since the Greeks once had such a fashion: the Ionians were called “with trailing tunics”, and Leonidas' men combed their hair as they went out to battle (*Geography* 10.3.8; see [[curetes]]).
