The Ephyri appear only in the simile that sends Idomeneus and Meriones into battle:

{{quote:9a76f33d-c7a5-5802-aa31-48ece0fe2785}}

The poem says nothing more of them. They are paired with the Phlegyes as the two sides of a war to which the gods go out from Thrace, giving glory to one of them (see [[ares-and-rout]] and [[phlegyes]]). The name looks like that of men from an Ephyra, and Homer knows more than one town of the name: the Ephyra on the river Selleis from which Heracles carried off Astyocheia (2.659; see [[ephyra]]), the Ephyra “in a corner of horse-pasturing Argos” where Sisyphus lived, later identified with Corinth (6.152; see [[ephyra-in-argos]]), and in the Odyssey an Ephyra where Odysseus went for poison for his arrows (*Odyssey* 1.259–262). Which Ephyra, if any, the simile has in mind is not said (see the translation's note on 13.298–303).

## Outside Homer

[[Strabo]] says that the people of Crannon in Thessaly were formerly called Ephyri, as the people of Gyrton were called Phlegyae, so that it is hard to tell whom the poet means in this simile (Strabo 9.5.21). The fragments of his seventh book repeat the identification and place Crannon about a hundred stades from Gyrton, which lies near the mouth of the Peneius (Strabo 7, fragments 14 and 16; see [[gyrtone]]).
