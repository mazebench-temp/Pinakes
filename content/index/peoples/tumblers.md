## The word

κυβιστητήρ is “one who tumbles”, from κυβιστάω, “tumble, turn head over heels”. W. W. Merry derives the verb from κύβη, which the *Etymologicum Magnum* gives as another spelling of the Cretan κυφή, “head”, as κεφαλή had a by-form κεβαλή; κυβιστάω was explained as “leap onto the head”, εἰς κεφαλὴν πηδάω (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.18). The verb is also used of the fish that leap this way and that in the burning river (*Iliad* 21.354).

The same verses, with the singer leading, stand in the *Odyssey* at the wedding feast in Menelaus' house (*Odyssey* 4.15–19; see [[the-wedding-at-sparta]]). Whether the singer belongs in both places, or was moved from one to the other by Aristarchus as Athenaeus claimed, is the question of [[the-singer-on-the-shield]]. The scholia called the Shield's tumblers and its dance Cretan (see the translation's note on *Iliad* 18.590–606).

On the battlefield the tumbler is a joke. Patroclus' stone kills Cebriones, Hector's charioteer, and he falls from the chariot “like a diver” (*Iliad* 16.742–743; see [[like-a-diver]] and [[cebriones]]):
