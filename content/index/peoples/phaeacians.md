## Etymology

Merry reports two derivations of Φαίηκες: from φαιός, “dark”, for a people whose home is in the wonderland of the West, or from the root φα-, “bright” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 5.34). Mure set the name beside “Phoenician” (see below).

## Descriptions

They are ἀγχίθεοι, “near kin to the gods” (*Odyssey* 5.35 = 19.279; see [[near-kin-to-the-gods]]). Their king Nausithous, who led them from Hypereia to Scheria, was a son of Poseidon by Periboea, daughter of [[eurymedon-king-of-the-giants|Eurymedon]], king of the [[giants|Giants]], and the father of Alcinous (*Odyssey* 6.4–10, 7.56–63; see [[nausithous]], [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]], [[hypereia-of-the-phaeacians]], [[alcinous]] and [[scheria]]); Poseidon himself calls them “of my own line” (*Odyssey* 13.130, a plain rendering), and Alcinous says that the gods come among them openly at their hecatombs and sit beside them at the feast (*Odyssey* 7.201–206). Their epithets are a seafaring people's: φιλήρετμοι, “lovers of the oar” (*Odyssey* 5.386 and five times more; see [[lovers-of-the-oar]]), and δολιχήρετμοι, ναυσίκλυτοι ἄνδρες, “of the long oars, men famed for ships” (*Odyssey* 8.191, 8.369, 13.166; see [[long-oared]]). By their king's own account they are runners and seamen rather than boxers or wrestlers, and they love the feast, the lyre and the dance (*Odyssey* 8.246–253).

Their ships are “swift as a wing or a thought”, and they cross “the great gulf” because “the earth-shaker granted it to them” (*Odyssey* 7.35–36). Their city stands between two harbors, with the ships drawn up along the road and a place of assembly around a precinct of Poseidon (*Odyssey* 6.262–267; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]]). Their ships need neither helmsmen nor steering oars: they know the minds of men and the cities of all, and cross the sea wrapped in mist and cloud (*Odyssey* 8.557–563; see [[steering-oar]]). The Phaeacians are “escorts of all men” who “come to no harm”, πομποὶ ἀπήμονες ἁπάντων (see [[pompe]]), and for that, Nausithous used to say, Poseidon begrudged them (*Odyssey* 8.564–566, 13.172–174): after they carry Odysseus home, the god turns their ship to stone in sight of their city (*Odyssey* 13.159–187; see [[poseidon]]).

## Outside Homer

For the later identification of their land with Corcyra, see [[scheria]]. Merry reports two modern readings of the people (on *Odyssey* 5.34). Welcker compared them with forms of northern legend, laying stress on their name of “safe escorts” and on their carrying Odysseus across the sea in a deep sleep, and found in them the Ferrymen of the Dead; he also supposed that Homer drew their manners from those of his own Ionian countrymen. Mure, struck by the likeness of the names Phaeacian and Phoenician, noted that Homer gives both peoples the same epithets, ναυσίκλυτοι, “famed for ships” (*Odyssey* 7.39; 15.415), and ἀγαυοί, “noble” (*Odyssey* 13.120; 13.272), and took the Phaeacians for Homer's playful satire on some colony of these eastern adventurers in the western Mediterranean. Merry finds the idea fanciful.

Alcinous' account of what his people love, the feast, the lyre and the dance, changes of clothing, warm baths and beds (*Odyssey* 8.248–249), made them a byword for ease. A scholium, as Merry reports it, quotes Heracleides of Pontus for a severe judgment: conscious of their love of pleasure and afraid that someone might come and drive them from their land, the Phaeacians put on hospitality to those present and a quick sending-off of those who came. Horace counts himself among the idle, “sponsi Penelopae nebulones Alcinoique / in cute curanda plus aequo operata iuventus”, the good-for-nothing suitors of Penelope and the young men of Alcinous, too busy about the care of their skins, for whom it was fine to sleep till midday and to lull their cares to rest to the sound of the lyre (*Epistles* 1.2.27–31), and hopes to come home from a stay by the sea “fat, and a Phaeacian” (*Epistles* 1.15.24; Merry on *Odyssey* 8.244; see [[horace]]).
