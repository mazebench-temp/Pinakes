## Etymology

Merry reports two derivations of Φαίηκες: from φαιός, “dark”, for a people whose home is in the wonderland of the West, or from the root φα-, “bright” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 5.34). Mure set the name beside “Phoenician” (see below).

## Descriptions

They are ἀγχίθεοι, “near kin to the gods” (*Odyssey* 5.35 = 19.279; see [[near-kin-to-the-gods]]). Their king Nausithous, who led them from Hypereia to Scheria, was a son of Poseidon by Periboea, daughter of Eurymedon, king of the Giants, and the father of Alcinous (*Odyssey* 6.4–10, 7.56–63; see [[alcinous]] and [[scheria]]); Poseidon himself calls them “of my own line” (*Odyssey* 13.130, a plain rendering), and Alcinous says that the gods come among them openly at their hecatombs and sit beside them at the feast (*Odyssey* 7.201–206). Their epithets are a seafaring people's: φιλήρετμοι, “lovers of the oar” (*Odyssey* 5.386 and five times more; see [[lovers-of-the-oar]]), and δολιχήρετμοι, ναυσίκλυτοι ἄνδρες, “long-oared men, famed for ships” (*Odyssey* 8.191, 8.369, 13.166; see [[long-oared]]). Alcinous owns that they are no great boxers or wrestlers, but swift runners and the best in ships, and that the feast, the lyre and the dance, changes of clothing, warm baths and beds are always dear to them (*Odyssey* 8.246–249, a plain rendering).

Their ships are “swift as a wing or a thought”, and the earth-shaker gave them the power to cross the great gulf of the sea (*Odyssey* 7.34–36, a plain rendering). They need no steersmen and no steering oars, for they know the thoughts of men and all their cities, and cross the sea hidden in mist and cloud, with no fear of harm (*Odyssey* 8.557–563). The Phaeacians are “safe escorts of all men”, πομποὶ ἀπήμονες ἁπάντων, and for that, Nausithous used to say, Poseidon begrudged them (*Odyssey* 8.564–566, 13.172–174): after they carry Odysseus home, the god turns their ship to stone in sight of their city (*Odyssey* 13.159–187; see [[poseidon]]).

## Outside Homer

For the later identification of their land with Corcyra, see [[scheria]]. Merry reports two modern readings of the people (on *Odyssey* 5.34). Welcker compared them with forms of northern legend, laying stress on their name of “safe escorts” and on their carrying Odysseus across the sea in a deep sleep, and found in them the Ferrymen of the Dead; he also supposed that Homer drew their manners from those of his own Ionian countrymen. Mure, struck by the likeness of the names Phaeacian and Phoenician, noted that Homer gives both peoples the same epithets, ναυσίκλυτοι, “famed for ships” (*Odyssey* 7.39; 15.415), and ἀγαυοί, “noble” (*Odyssey* 13.120; 13.272), and took the Phaeacians for Homer's playful satire on some colony of these eastern adventurers in the western Mediterranean. Merry finds the idea fanciful.
