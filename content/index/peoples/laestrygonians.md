## The name

Homer has the people, Λαιστρυγόνες (*Odyssey* 10.119), the man, Λαιστρυγόνος, “the Laestrygonian”, of their king (*Odyssey* 10.106, 10.199), and the adjective of their land, Τηλέπυλον Λαιστρυγονίην, “Telepylus of the Laestrygonians” (*Odyssey* 10.82, again in the summary of the wanderings at *Odyssey* 23.318). W. W. Merry suggests that the name is made of an intensive λαι- or λα- and τρύχειν or τρύγειν, “devour”, and compares λαμυρός, “greedy”, and λαιδρός, “bold”; with the name of Lamus, whose citadel their city is, he compares Λαμία, the child-devouring ogress (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 10.81; see [[lamus]]).

## In Homer

The Laestrygonians live at Telepylus, “the steep citadel of Lamus”, a land of herds where “the paths of night and of day are close together” (*Odyssey* 10.81–86; see [[telepylus]]). Their city has a famous harbor shut in by sheer cliffs with a narrow mouth, a smooth road down which the wagons bring wood from the mountains, a spring called Artacia from which they carry water to the city, a king's high-roofed house and a place of assembly (*Odyssey* 10.87–114; see [[harbor]] and [[artacia]]). Merry observes that, having a king and an assembly, they are more advanced in the outward forms of civilization than the Cyclopes, though like the Cyclopes they are only graziers, with no plowed land or vineyards to be seen (on *Odyssey* 10.97, 10.114; see [[cyclopes]]). They are giants and eaters of men: the king's daughter is “strong”, his wife “as huge as a mountain peak”, and the king seizes one of Odysseus' scouts for his meal; then the people come “countless, not like men, but like the Giants”, pelt the ships from the cliffs with stones each a load for a man, and spear the men like fish to carry them off to a joyless meal (*Odyssey* 10.105–124; see [[antiphates-the-laestrygonian]] and [[giants]]). The other ships, eleven of the twelve that left the island of goats (*Odyssey* 9.159), perish in the harbor, and Odysseus' alone, moored outside it, escapes (*Odyssey* 10.125–132; see [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]]). The poem names them once more, in the summary of the tale that Odysseus tells Penelope: how he came to Telepylus of the Laestrygonians, “who destroyed his ships and all his well-greaved companions; / Odysseus alone escaped in his black ship” (*Odyssey* 23.318–320, a plain rendering).

## Outside Homer

Strabo held that the poet took his starting points from history: Aeolus ruled the islands around Lipara, and inhospitable peoples, Cyclopes and Laestrygonians, held the country around Aetna and Leontini, so that in those days no one could approach the strait (*Geography* 1.2.9; see [[strabo]], [[aeolia]] and [[sicily]]); but to take “the sizes of the Cyclopes and Laestrygonians” and the poet's other marvels for history is, he says, the worse way of reading him (*Geography* 1.2.11). Pliny names “the Laestrygonian plains” inland in Sicily (*Natural History* 3.89).

The Romans placed them at Formiae, on the coast of Latium. Pliny calls the town Formiae, “called Hormiae, as people have thought, the ancient seat of the Laestrygonians” (*Natural History* 3.59). Cicero, writing to Atticus from his villa there of the people's anger against Pompey, calls it “this Telepylus of the Laestrygonians, Formiae I mean” (*Letters to Atticus* 2.13.2; see [[cicero]] and [[telepylus]]). Horace greets Aelius Lamia as a descendant of “ancient Lamus”, who is said to have been the first to hold the walls of Formiae (*Odes* 3.17.1–8; see [[horace]] and [[lamus]]), and Silius Italicus names Caieta, “ruled by Lamus”, and “the house of Antiphates” among the towns of that coast (*Punica* 8.529–530). In Ovid's Metamorphoses Macareus, a companion of Ulysses left behind on that coast, tells how they came to “the ancient city of Lamus the Laestrygonian”, where Antiphates ruled: one of the three envoys stained the Laestrygonian's mouth with his blood, Antiphates roused his people, and they hurled stones and beams and sank men and ships, all but the one that carried Ulysses (*Metamorphoses* 14.233–242; see [[ovid]]).
