## The word

βασιλεύς (“king”, plural βασιλῆες) in Homer names a class, not a single monarch. The Catalogue names “the leaders and lords of the Danaans” (2.487, 2.760), and most of them are kings in their own lands. On Ithaca there are many kings, young and old (Odyssey 1.394–395), and among the Phaeacians twelve kings rule under a thirteenth, Alcinous (Odyssey 8.390–391). In the Mycenaean tablets the word, written *qa-si-re-u*, names a local official well below the king, the *wa-na-ka* (ἄναξ). See [[basileus]] and [[anax]].

## Kings and their epithets

{{quote:e03b0d4b-6d2e-5582-99ff-f78cbb822d0a}}

σκηπτοῦχοι (“scepter-bearing”, from σκῆπτρον + ἔχω): the scepter is the sign of their office. See [[scepter-bearing]] and [[scepter]]. They are also διοτρεφέες (“nurtured by Zeus”, 2.98, 2.196, 2.445): their honor, as Odysseus says, comes from Zeus. See [[zeus-nurtured]].

## Kings and commoners

{{quote:34021398-91b1-54ae-9cf7-9fdeddb4aa7c}}

Book 2 shows how the kings are to be handled, and how they differ from the rest. When the army runs for the ships, [[Odysseus]] stops each king or “man of note” with gentle words and an argument: Agamemnon is only testing them, and a king's anger is to be feared. The man of the people he strikes with the scepter and orders to sit (2.198–206; see [[commoners]]). His conclusion is the Iliad's most famous line on kingship: “We Achaeans shall not all be kings here … let there be one ruler, one king” (2.203–205; see [[one-ruler]] and [[koiranos]]).

The kings are also the target of [[Thersites]], who is “quarreling with the kings” (ἐριζέμεναι βασιλεῦσιν, 2.214) until Odysseus beats him. The army's comment is that he will not soon “rail at the kings” again (2.277). See [[kingship-and-authority]].

## Outside Homer

Hesiod complains of “gift-devouring kings” who give crooked judgments (*Works and Days* 38–39, 263–264). Aristotle quotes Odysseus' “one ruler” to close his *Metaphysics* Λ (1076a4). See [[aristotle-one-ruler]].
