## The name

Ἀχαιϊάδες and Ἀχαιΐδες are feminines of the people's name, Ἀχαιοί (see [[achaeans]]); in the singular Ἀχαιΐς is the land, Ἀχαιΐδα γαῖαν, “the land of Achaea” (1.254; see [[achaea]]). Ἀχαιαί, the feminine of the adjective Ἀχαιός, is found only in the Odyssey's phrase ἐυπλοκαμῖδες Ἀχαιαί, “the Achaean women with lovely tresses” (*Odyssey* 2.119, 19.542; see [[with-lovely-tresses]]). Thersites and Menelaus turn the name into an insult for the army, “Achaean women, Achaean men no longer” (2.235, 7.96; see [[achaean-women-no-longer-men]]).

## In Homer

The Achaean women are the women at home, seen from the war or from the house. In the Iliad Athena mocks the wounded Aphrodite before Zeus (see [[aphrodite]]):

{{quote:aa77859a-d270-5011-98d6-856cb8b793c5}}

Achilles, refusing the daughter Agamemnon offers him, says that “There are many Achaean women throughout Hellas and Phthia, / daughters of chieftains who guard the citadels”, and that Peleus will find him a wife among them (9.393–397; see [[hellas]] and [[phthia]]). In the Odyssey the suitors say the same when the bow defeats them. Leodes tells the others to woo another of the well-robed Achaean women with gifts, and Eurymachus grieves less for the marriage than for their weakness, since there are many other Achaean women, in sea-girt Ithaca itself and in other cities (*Odyssey* 21.159–162, 21.249–255; see [[well-robed]] and [[the-suitors]]).

They are also the women whose judgment counts. Penelope sets up her web “lest one of the Achaean women in the land be indignant with me” if Laertes lies without a winding-sheet (*Odyssey* 2.101–102, again at 19.146–147 and 24.136–137; see [[nemesis]]), as Helen fears that the Trojan women will blame her (3.410–412; see [[trojan-women]]). Nestor tells Telemachus that, had Menelaus found Aegisthus alive, no mound would have been heaped over him: dogs and birds would have eaten him, and none of the Achaean women would have wept for him (*Odyssey* 3.258–261; see [[aegisthus]]). And the Achaean women with lovely tresses gather round Penelope in her dream as she weeps for the geese the eagle has killed (*Odyssey* 19.541–543). The renderings of Books 3, 19 and 21 of the Odyssey are plain renderings, not the translation.
