## The name

Homer names the Cimmerians once, in the genitive: Κιμμερίων ἀνδρῶν δῆμός τε πόλις τε, “the land and the city of the Cimmerian men” (*Odyssey* 11.14). The ancient critics did not agree on the word. As W. W. Merry reports the scholia, Aristarchus, by one of them, and Crates, by another, read a name formed from Cerberus, the hound of Hades, and others wrote χειμερίων, “wintry”; Merry adds that Aristophanes has the Cerberian form (*Frogs* 187), and that the *Etymologicum Magnum* gives yet another spelling (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, critical note on *Odyssey* 11.14; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 11.14).

## In Homer

At sunset on the day it leaves Aeaea, Odysseus' ship reaches “the limits of deep-streaming Oceanus”, and there are the land and the city of the Cimmerians, wrapped in mist and cloud, where the sun never looks down on them, neither when he climbs the sky nor when he turns back toward the earth, “but deadly night is spread over those wretched mortals” (*Odyssey* 11.12–19; see [[oceanus]] and [[helios]]). There the ship is run ashore, and the men go along the stream of Oceanus to the place that Circe had shown, where Odysseus calls up the dead (*Odyssey* 11.20–22; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]). The poem tells nothing more of them: no king, no deed, no meeting with the travelers. They are the last people of the living world, at its edge, and the darkness that covers them is the darkness of the dead.

The darkness is described in words that Hesiod uses of the home of Sleep and Death, the children of Night, at the edge of the world before the halls of Hades: “Never does shining Sun look upon them with his rays, whether ascending into heaven or descending from heaven” (*Theogony* 759–761). The end of *Odyssey* 11.15 and the whole of 11.16 recur in Hesiod; the manuscripts of both poems have ἐπιδέρκεται, “looks upon”, where Aristophanes and Aristarchus read καταδέρκεται, “looks down on”, in the Odyssey (Merry's critical note on *Odyssey* 11.16). Night herself, in Hesiod, is Νὺξ ὀλοή, νεφέλῃ κεκαλυμμένη ἠεροειδεῖ, “ruinous Night, covered in a misty cloud” (*Theogony* 757; see [[hesiod]] and [[deadly]]).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus knew the Cimmerians as a people of the north. The land of the Scythians, he says, once belonged to them; when the Scythians came, the Cimmerian kings fought and died among themselves rather than flee, and the people left the country, and in his day there were still Cimmerian walls and crossings in Scythia, a district called Cimmeria and the strait called the Cimmerian Bosporus (*Histories* 4.11–12). Driven out by the Scythians, they came into Asia and took Sardis, all but its acropolis, in the reign of Ardys (*Histories* 1.15), and their raid on Ionia, before the time of Croesus, was a plundering, not a conquest of its cities (1.6.3; see [[herodotus]]).

Strabo held that Homer knew them. They overran the country from the Bosporus to Ionia in the poet's time or a little before, and he hints at the gloom of their northern land in these verses (*Geography* 1.1.10); knowing that they lived by the northern and gloomy Cimmerian Bosporus, he moved them to a dark place beside Hades, useful for the story of the wanderings (1.2.9), perhaps also because the Ionians shared a hatred of that people (3.2.12; see [[strabo]]). Strabo also reports that earlier writers set the Homeric Nekyia at Lake Avernus near Cumae, a place that people took for an entrance to the world below and said was the Cimmerians' home, and he gives Ephorus' account of them: they lived in underground houses called *argillae*, went to one another through tunnels, and received strangers at an oracle deep under the earth; by an ancestral custom no one at the oracle saw the sun, but went out of the chasms at night, “and for this reason the poet said of them that never does the shining sun look upon them” (*Geography* 5.4.5).

W. W. Merry would not identify Homer's Cimmerians with the historical people of that name. They stand for the land of darkness, and the description of their sunless life may hint at the long dark winters of northern latitudes, which may have suggested the reading χειμερίους, “wintry”, of which Κιμμερίους is perhaps only a slightly altered form (Merry on *Odyssey* 11.14).
