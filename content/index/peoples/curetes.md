The name is formed from κοῦρος, “young man”, and the same word is a common noun in Book 19, where “young men, the best of all the Achaeans”, carry Agamemnon's gifts (κούρητας, 19.193; κούρητες, 19.248). The Monro–Allen text gives the people a capital and a different accent, Κουρῆτες beside κούρητες, and the index claims only the capitalized forms.

Homer tells only what the story needs. The war began over the head and hide of the boar that Artemis had sent (9.547–549; see [[the-calydonian-boar]]). As long as Meleager fought, “it went badly for the Curetes, and they could not / hold their ground outside the wall, many as they were” (9.551–552). When he held back, they battered the gates and towers, and at last “onto the towers / the Curetes were climbing and setting fire to the great city” (9.573–574, 9.588–589; see [[calydon]] and [[aetolians]]). Phoenix does not say where they came from or who led them. In his likeness they stand where the Trojans stand, at the wall and with fire for the ships (see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]).

## Outside Homer

Bacchylides calls them “steadfast in war” (μενεπτολέμοις) and has them flee to Pleuron (Bacchylides 5.124–126, 5.149–151). Strabo, who begins his account of them with Homer, argues that they were Aetolians of Pleuron, since Oeneus' family lived “in Pleuron and steep Calydon” (Il. 14.116). He makes Thestius, Althaea's father, their leader, and their war with Oeneus and Meleager a war fought, “as the poet says, over the boar's head and hide”, but in all likelihood over land (10.3.1, 10.3.6; see [[althaea]] and [[pleuron]]). He reports that the name came from κουρά, “cropping”, because they cut their hair short in front so that enemies could not seize it, or from Mount Curium above Pleuron (10.2.4, 10.3.6). [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] calls the war of the Homeric version a war of “the Curetes and the Calydonians” (*Library* 1.8.3).

The Curetes of Crete share only the name. In the Cretan story of Zeus' birth they were young men who danced in arms with drums and din around Rhea, so that Cronus would not notice the child being taken away (Strabo 10.3.11). Hesiod called them “sportive gods, dancers” (quoted by Strabo 10.3.19), and Strabo says that the historians confused the two peoples because of the name (10.3.7).
