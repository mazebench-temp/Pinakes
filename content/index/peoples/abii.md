## The name

Ἄβιοι occurs only here, and so do the words before it, ἱππημολγοί, “mare-milkers” (ἵππος, “horse”, and ἀμέλγω, “milk”), and γλακτοφάγοι, “milk-eaters” (γάλα, “milk”, and φαγεῖν, “eat”). The text prints Ἀβίων as a name and the two others as common words: ἀγαυῶν ἱππημολγῶν / γλακτοφάγων, “the proud mare-milkers / who live on milk” (13.5–6). Whether the three words describe one people or several, the poem does not say. The Suda glosses ἱππημολγός as “one who milks horses” (ι 540).

Ancient readers also took ἀβίων as an adjective. Posidonius explained it as men who live without women, since the poet, he said, thought a widower's life half-complete, as he calls the house Protesilaus left behind “half-finished” (2.701); Strabo preferred men without hearths, who live in wagons, cheaply and on little, and so are the most just (*Geography* 7.3.3–4).

## In Homer

Zeus has brought Hector to the ships and turns his shining eyes away from Troy, “looking out far off” (νόσφιν, 13.4). The peoples he looks on are named in a list that seems to run from near to far: the horse-herding Thracians across the sea, the Mysians, the mare-milkers, and last the Abii, δικαιοτάτων ἀνθρώπων, “the most just of men” (13.6; see [[thracians]], [[mysians]] and [[most-just]]). The superlative is said elsewhere in the Iliad only of Chiron, “the most just of the Centaurs” (11.832; see [[chiron]]). The poem says no more of them. Zeus' eyes rest on the most just of men, far from the war, as in the first book the gods were away feasting with “the blameless Ethiopians” by Oceanus (1.423; see [[ethiopians]]). Poseidon, who keeps no such “blind watch”, comes to the Achaeans at once (13.10; see [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]).

## Outside Homer

Strabo discusses the verses at length (*Geography* 7.3.2–10; see [[strabo-on-the-mare-milkers]]). He takes the mare-milkers, the milk-drinkers and the Abii for the wagon-dwelling Scythians and Sarmatians beyond the Thracians (7.3.2). Posidonius had applied the words to Thracian peoples: the Mysians, who out of piety eat no living thing and live on honey, milk and cheese, and Thracians who live without women (7.3.3). Apollodorus, following Eratosthenes, had held that Homer did not know the Scythians and invented these peoples; Strabo answers that the nomads there still live on their flocks, on milk and cheese, above all mares' milk, and that Hesiod too calls the Scythians mare-milkers (7.3.6–7). He cites Ephorus, who wrote that some of the nomad Scythians, who live on mares' milk, surpass all men in justice, and who quoted Homer's verse and Hesiod's, in which the Harpies carry Phineus “to the land of the milk-drinkers, who have wagons for their homes” (*Geography* 7.3.9). The two Hesiodic verses are fragments 39 and 40 of the *Catalogue of Women* in Evelyn-White's edition.

Aeschylus speaks of the Scythians, “eaters of mare's-milk cheese”, as law-abiding (fr. 198 Nauck), and Strabo quotes the line as agreeing with the poet (*Geography* 7.3.7). In a fragment of his *Prometheus Unbound* a traveler is told that he will come to “a people most just of all mortals and most hospitable, the Gabii”, whose self-sown fields give a plentiful living without plow or mattock (fr. 196 Nauck); Stephanus of Byzantium quotes the lines in his entry on the Abii. Herodotus describes how the Scythians milk their mares, and says that they blind their slaves to tend the milk, “for they are not plowmen but nomads” (Herodotus 4.2.1–2; see [[herodotus]]).
