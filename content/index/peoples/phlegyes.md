The Phlegyes are named in the simile of Ares and Rout, who “arm themselves to go out from Thrace to the Ephyri or to the great-hearted Phlegyes; and they do not listen to both sides, but give glory to one or the other” (13.301–303; see [[ares-and-rout]] and [[ephyri]]). μεγαλήτορες, “great-hearted”, is a standing epithet of peoples and heroes (see [[great-hearted]]). The poem tells nothing more about them. The simile needs only a war to which the gods may go, and two peoples for them to choose between (see the translation's note on 13.298–303).

## Outside Homer

In the Homeric Hymn to Apollo the god, looking for a place for his temple, comes “to the city of the Phlegyes, arrogant men, who, heedless of Zeus, lived on the earth in a fair glen near the Cephisian lake”, before he goes on to Crisa under Parnassus (*Hymn to Apollo* 278–282; see [[cephisian-lake]]). Another hymn makes Coronis, the mother of Asclepius, the daughter of king Phlegyas (*Hymn* 16.1–3).

[[Strabo]] reports that the people of Gyrton were formerly called Phlegyae, after Phlegyas, the brother of Ixion, and the people of Crannon Ephyri, so that it is hard to tell whom the poet means in this simile (Strabo 9.5.21). The fragments of his seventh book place Gyrton near the mouth of the Peneius, a Perrhaebian and Magnesian town in which Peirithous and Ixion were kings (Strabo 7, fragments 14 and 16; see [[gyrtone]] and [[peirithous]]).
