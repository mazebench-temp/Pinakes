Τρῳαί, "Trojan women", from the adjective Τρῳός, "Trojan". In Book 3 they are named three times, all in the scene of Helen and Aphrodite. The goddess finds Helen on the tower, "and around her were Trojan women in plenty" (3.384). Helen refuses to go to Paris' bed because "the Trojan women would all hereafter blame me" (μωμήσονται, 3.411–412). When she goes, she escapes the notice of them all (3.420). Their blame is what she fears. Hector too would feel shame before "the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes" (Τρῳάδας ἑλκεσιπέπλους) if he held back from the fighting (6.441–443, 22.105).

## Elsewhere in Homer

In Book 6 Hecuba and the old women of Troy carry a robe to Athena's temple and pray, and the goddess refuses them (6.286–311). Andromache gives the fullest picture of what the war holds for them, the loss of husband, city and freedom (6.407–465, 24.725–745). By the springs outside the walls the wives and daughters of the Trojans used to wash their clothes in the time of peace, before the Achaeans came (22.153–156). At the end of the poem the women of Troy lament Hector, and Helen remembers the reproaches she heard from her husband's kin (24.723–775).

## Outside Homer

Euripides' *Trojan Women* (415 BC) follows the captive women of Troy on the day after the sack (see [[euripides-trojan-women]]).
