## In Homer

{{quote:28b36646-3452-5c0b-bcd0-801e7ec51533}}

τό τε σθένος Ὠρίωνος, “the strength of Orion” (18.486), names the constellation by the hunter's might, as Homer says βίη Ἡρακλῆος, “mighty Heracles”, of the man himself (18.117; see [[heracles]]). The Bear “turns in its place and watches Orion” (18.488; see [[the-bear]]). Walter Leaf explains the picture: when Orion is rising in the east, the Bear is low on the horizon, which in northern Greece it just touches, and then it moves upward, as though the coming of the great hunter had scared it from its bath; he adds that in Homer's time the Great Bear lay much nearer the pole than now, and that there was then no obvious pole star (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.488). He reports Payne Knight's observation that the older form of the name is Ὠαρίων, the form of Pindar, Corinna and Callimachus, and holds that it is right, though the manuscripts of Homer do not have it (on 18.486; see the translation's note on 18.486–489). The text of Hesiod prints Ὠαρίων.

The hunter has a dog in the sky: the star of late summer, the brightest of all and a sign of evil, “the star that men call by the name of the dog of Orion” (22.29; see [[the-autumn-star]]), to which Priam likens Achilles running over the plain (22.25–31).

The Odyssey tells of the man. Calypso, complaining of the gods' jealousy, recalls that when rosy-fingered Dawn took Orion, the gods begrudged it until Artemis killed him in Ortygia with her gentle arrows (*Odyssey* 5.121–124; see [[dawn-and-orion]]). Otus and Ephialtes were the tallest men the earth ever reared, and the most beautiful after famous Orion (*Odyssey* 11.309–310). Among the dead Odysseus sees huge Orion driving together over the meadow of asphodel the beasts that he himself had killed in the lonely mountains, holding a club all of bronze, never broken (*Odyssey* 11.572–575). The stars by which Odysseus steers include the Bear that watches Orion, in the verses of the Shield (*Odyssey* 5.273–275).

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod Orion's stars keep the farmer's year: the slaves are to thresh the grain “when the strength of Orion first appears” (*Works and Days* 597–598); the grapes are picked when Orion and Sirius have come into mid-heaven and rosy-fingered Dawn sees Arcturus (609–611); the plowing begins when the Pleiades, the Hyades and the strength of Orion set (614–617); and when the Pleiades, fleeing the mighty strength of Orion, fall into the misty sea, the sailing season is over (619–621). The *Astronomy* ascribed to Hesiod, as a later writer summarizes it, made Orion the son of Poseidon and Euryale, the daughter of Minos, with the gift of walking on the waves as on land; blinded by Oenopion of Chios for violating his daughter Merope, he went to Lemnos, where Hephaestus pitied him and gave him his servant Cedalion as a guide, and the Sun healed him; he hunted in Crete with Artemis and Leto and boasted that he would kill every beast on earth, until Earth sent up a huge scorpion that stung him to death, and Zeus set him among the stars (*Astronomy* fr. 4 Evelyn-White).
