## The name

Θῆβαι, “Thebes”, is Homer's name for the great city of Upper Egypt, which the Egyptians called Waset and later Greeks also called Diospolis, “city of Zeus”, after its god Amun. It shares the name with Boeotian Thebes and with Thebe in the Troad (see [[thebes]] and [[thebe]]). Why the Greeks gave the Egyptian city the same name is not known.

## In Homer

Achilles names it at the height of his refusal. Not if Agamemnon gave him ten and twenty times as much as he has, “not all that comes in to Orchomenus, nor all that comes to Thebes in Egypt, where the most treasures lie stored in the houses, which has a hundred gates”, and through each of them two hundred men march out with horses and chariots, would he be persuaded, until Agamemnon has paid for the whole insult (9.379–387; see [[achilles]] and [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). The two cities, the Minyan city of Boeotia and the Egyptian one, stand for wealth beyond counting (see [[orchomenus]]). ἑκατόμπυλος, “hundred-gated”, occurs only here (see [[hundred-gated]]); a hundred gates with two hundred men at each make twenty thousand men with their chariots. It is the only place in the Iliad that names Egypt (see [[egypt]]).

The Odyssey repeats the half-verse. Helen's silver workbasket on wheels was a gift from Alcandre, wife of Polybus, who lived in Thebes in Egypt, “where the most treasures lie stored in the houses” (Od. 4.126–127), and Polybus gave Menelaus two silver bathtubs, two tripods and ten talents of gold (Od. 4.125–132). Ten talents of gold head Agamemnon's gifts too (9.122). Thebes is the only Egyptian city Homer names.

## Outside Homer

Herodotus went to Thebes to test what the priests of Memphis had told him (Herodotus 2.3), reckoned the voyage up the Nile to it (2.9), and reports that “Thebes was formerly called Egypt” (Herodotus 2.15). The priests of Zeus at Thebes, he says, showed the genealogist Hecataeus three hundred and forty-five wooden statues of high priests, each the son of the one before, when Hecataeus traced his own descent to a god in the sixteenth generation (Herodotus 2.143; see [[herodotus]]). The city was the seat of the great temples of Amun at Karnak and Luxor, and the Assyrians sacked it in 663 BCE. Walter Burkert connected Achilles' lines with the fame of its wealth in that century and used them in dating the Iliad (“Das hunderttorige Theben und die Datierung der Ilias”, *Wiener Studien* 89, 1976); how early its fame could have reached Greek ears is uncertain.
