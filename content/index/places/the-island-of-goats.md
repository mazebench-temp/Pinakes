## In Homer

Before he tells how he came to the island, Odysseus describes it, as he describes the land of the Cyclopes before he meets them (*Odyssey* 9.106–115; see [[cyclopes]]):

{{quote:ee6f879b-116a-5f34-a9b5-a6c0ae7b0346}}

The island has no name. λάχεια, “of good soil”, the scholia refer to λαχαίνω, “dig”, and explain as “with good soil, easy to dig”; Zenodotus read ἐλάχεια, “small”, and Merry infers from the special mention of his reading that λάχεια was Aristarchus' (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 9.116 and his critical note; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 9.116 and [[zenodotus]]). ἔπειτα is “now”, a mere transition (Merry on *Odyssey* 9.116), as Menelaus begins his account of Pharos, νῆσος ἔπειτά τις ἔστι, “Now there is an island”, and goes on in the same words as Odysseus, ἐν δὲ λιμὴν ἐύορμος, “and in it there is a harbor with good anchorage” (*Odyssey* 4.354–358, 9.136; see [[pharos]]).

It is wooded and full of wild goats; no hunters go there, no flocks graze it and no one plows it, and it feeds only goats (*Odyssey* 9.118–124; see [[goats]]). The reason is the Cyclopes: they have no ships with red-painted prows and no shipwrights, “who might build / well-benched ships” to visit the cities of men, and “such men would also have made the island well settled for them” (*Odyssey* 9.125–130; see [[ships]]). The island lies ἄσπαρτος καὶ ἀνήροτος, “unsown and unplowed” (*Odyssey* 9.123), the words for the land of the Cyclopes across the water, where everything grows unsown and unplowed (*Odyssey* 9.109); but there wheat, barley and vines grow of themselves, and here only goats (*Odyssey* 9.110–111, 9.124). What it might bear the poem says: meadows by the gray sea, well-watered and soft, vines that would never fail, smooth plowland and a deep field of grain, for the soil beneath is very rich (*Odyssey* 9.131–135; see [[vine]] and [[wheat]]); a harbor where no cable is needed, neither mooring-stones nor stern-cables, but men may run their ships ashore and wait for a fair wind; and at its head a spring of bright water from under a cave, with poplars around it (*Odyssey* 9.136–141; see [[harbor]], [[mooring-stones]] and [[stern-cables]]).

The Phaeacians, ship people, show what such men do with an island. They once lived in Hypereia near the Cyclopes, who plundered them, until Nausithous led them away to Scheria, drove a wall around the city, built houses and temples of the gods, and divided the fields (*Odyssey* 6.4–10; see [[hypereia-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]).

## Outside Homer

Those who placed the whole scene in Sicily, Merry reports, looked for the island among the Aegates islands off Cape Lilybaeum, and Cluver chose one of them, Aegusa, as satisfying the Homeric description (on *Odyssey* 9.116); but Merry himself holds that throughout these books “we are in a wonder-land, which we shall look in vain for on the map” (on *Odyssey* 9.106; see [[cyclopes]]).
