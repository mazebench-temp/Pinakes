# Πυριφλεγέθων (Od. 10.513), the only place in Homer; it catches no other word of Homer, the Poetics or the Rhetoric.
grc: [Πυριφλεγέθων]
---

## The name

Πυριφλεγέθων is made of πῦρ, “fire”, and φλεγέθω, “blaze”, the river “blazing with fire”. W. W. Merry calls it the “river of Flaming Fire”, “an evident reminiscence of the lava-stream” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 10.513). Later writers also call it Phlegethon.

## In Homer

Homer names it once, in Circe's directions to Odysseus: “There into Acheron flow Pyriphlegethon / and Cocytus, which is a branch of the water of Styx, / and there is a rock, and the meeting of the two loud-resounding rivers” (*Odyssey* 10.513–515; see [[acheron]], [[cocytus]] and [[styx]]). The poem says nothing of its fire. Merry thought that the Styx was at first the only river of the underworld, and that the passage that brings in the other three is probably a later invention (on *Odyssey* 10.513).

## Outside Homer

In the myth of the *Phaedo*, Pyriphlegethon is the third of the four great rivers. It issues between Oceanus and Acheron, falls into a great region burning with much fire and makes a lake larger than our sea, boiling with water and mud; from there it coils round the earth, turbid and muddy, comes to the edge of the Acherusian lake without mingling with its water, and falls into Tartarus; its streams of lava throw up fragments wherever they reach the surface of the earth (Plato, *Phaedo* 113a–b). Those who have done violence to a father or a mother in anger are carried out of Tartarus by way of Pyriphlegethon, and those who have killed by way of Cocytus (*Phaedo* 114a; see [[plato]]).

Strabo reports that the people of Cumae, Dicaearchia and the country around Vesuvius spoke of Pyriphlegethon as of the Acherusian lake and the oracle of the dead at Avernus, and that they took the hot waters near the Acherusian lake for the river (*Geography* 1.2.18, 5.4.5; see [[strabo]]). In Virgil, “Tartarean Phlegethon” surrounds the walls of Tartarus with its rushing flames and rolls the sounding rocks (*Aeneid* 6.550–551; see [[virgil]] and [[tartarus]]). Cicero's Cotta names it with Acheron and Cocytus among the rivers “said to flow among the dead”, which would have to be gods if Jupiter and Neptune are (*On the Nature of the Gods* 3.43; see [[cicero]]).
