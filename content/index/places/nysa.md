Νυσήϊον, “Nysean”, is an adjective used as a noun, “the Nysean (place)”, and it occurs only here in Homer. The Iliad does not say whether it is a mountain; the translation, like the Homeric *Hymn to Dionysus*, makes it one. ἠγάθεος, “very holy”, is said in Homer only of places: Pylos (1.252), Lemnos (2.722, 21.79) and, in the Odyssey, Pytho (Od. 8.80). Homer tells nothing of Nysa but this one scene, in which the god's nurses are driven down it and the child god flees into the sea (see [[lycurgus]] and [[dionysus]]).

## Outside Homer

The Homeric *Hymn to Dionysus* places Nysa “far from Phoenicia, near the streams of Egypt”, a high mountain blooming with forest (*Hymns* 1.8–9). Another hymn tells how the nymphs received the newborn god from Zeus, “in their bosoms”, and reared him in the glens of Nysa (*Hymns* 26.3–5; compare Thetis, who takes him “to her bosom”, 6.136). In the *Hymn to Demeter* Hades carries off Persephone from “the Nysian plain” (*Hymns* 2.17). Herodotus puts Nysa above Egypt, in Ethiopia (2.146.2, 3.97.2). Later writers found Nysas in many lands, as far as India, where Alexander's men were shown a Nysa founded by Dionysus (Arrian, *Anabasis* 5.1.1–2).
