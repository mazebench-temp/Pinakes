## In Homer

{{quote:f6d95e11-513c-5d4c-9583-230d3674ac42}}

Achilles has told Priam how Niobe, whose twelve children Apollo and Artemis killed, remembered food when she was worn out with weeping, and he ends with the place where she is now: on Sipylus, among the rocks and the lonely mountains, where the nymphs who dance around the Achelous have their beds, “there, though she is stone, she broods over the sorrows the gods gave her” (24.602–617; see [[niobe]], [[the-children-of-niobe]], [[nymphs]] and [[achelous-of-lydia]]). ὅθι φασί, “where they say”, is the frame of the verse that puts the bed of Typhoeus among the Arimi (2.783), and Walter Leaf takes the εὐναί of the goddesses for no more than their dwellings (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.615; see the translation's note on 24.615).

**The rejected verses.** The Alexandrians, and Aristophanes before them, athetized 24.614–617: if Niobe was turned to stone, how did she take food, and what consolation is it to say “eat, since Niobe ate and was turned to stone”? The verses were also called Hesiodic in character, above all ἀμφʼ Ἀχελώϊον ἐρρώσαντο, “dance around the Achelous”, and the threefold ἐν and a stone that broods over sorrows were found strange. Leaf answers each point. The repetition of ἐν has a parallel in 22.503–504; the legend told that the water trickling down the rock kept Niobe's grief alive in stone; the verb is used of dancing in Hesiod, where the Muses' feet move nimbly on Helicon (ἐπερρώσαντο δὲ ποσσίν, *Theogony* 8), but that is a natural narrowing of its Homeric sense of quick movement; and Niobe was turned to stone at her own prayer, as a reward, not a punishment, so that the verses are needed for the thought: eat now, and mourn your son hereafter (on 24.614, 24.617; see the translation's notes on 24.602 and 24.613). An ancient note on 24.613 marks the difference between Homer and the later poets, who turn Niobe herself to stone; Leaf notes that the story as Apollodorus and the scholia tell it reconciles the two, for there the children are killed at Thebes, and Niobe goes home to Sipylus and long afterward prays the gods to end her grief by turning her to stone (on 24.613).

## The stone of Niobe

Leaf holds that the setting of the story on Sipylus is older than the Theban form of it (on 24.602). All antiquity, he says, knew the stone figure of Niobe on the mountain, and it has generally been recognized in a rough figure carved in front of a recess in a cliff near Smyrna, which Sayce took for a figure of the great goddess of Carchemish, with cartouches in Hittite and Egyptian writing beside it, and which an inscription of Roman date names as Plastene, Mother of the Gods. Leaf doubts that this is the ancient Niobe: Pausanias, who seems to have come from the region, and Quintus of Smyrna describe a rock that looks like a weeping woman only from a distance, and the carved figure never “weeps” and looks more human from near at hand; he thinks the ancient Niobe was more probably some natural rock farther inland (on 24.614, referring to Ramsay and to Frazer's *Pausanias*). For Pausanias' own account of the rock, and the weeping stone of the later poets, see [[the-weeping-rock-of-sipylus]].

## Outside Homer

Later Greeks made Sipylus the home of Tantalus, Niobe's father, and of his house. Strabo, following Demetrius of Scepsis, reports Democles' account of great earthquakes in Lydia and Ionia, as far as the Troad, by which villages were swallowed up and “Sipylus was overthrown in the reign of Tantalus” (*Geography* 1.3.17), and he insists that the stories of Sipylus and its overthrow are no myth, for in his own day earthquakes threw down Magnesia, the city that lies below it (12.8.18; 13.3.5). The old writers, he says, called the country around Sipylus Phrygia, and Tantalus, Pelops and Niobe Phrygians (12.8.2), and among the stories Demetrius collected from Callisthenes and others was that the wealth of Tantalus and the Pelopids came from the mines around Phrygia and Sipylus, as Priam's came from the gold mines of Astyra near Abydus (14.5.28). He charges Aeschylus with confusing the places, since his Niobe speaks of the kin of Tantalus, whose altar of their father Zeus stands on the hill of Ida, and of “Sipylus in the Idaean land” (*Geography* 12.8.21, quoting the *Niobe*). The Mother of the Gods was worshipped among the Phrygians as Sipylene, named from the mountain (10.3.12).
