The Athenian entry is unlike its neighbors. It names no other town of Attica: Athens stands for the whole land. In place of a list it gives a myth and a cult. Athens is "the well-built citadel" (ἐϋκτίμενον πτολίεθρον; [[well-built]]) and "the land of great-hearted [[Erechtheus]]" (δῆμος here means "land, district"). Athena raised Erechtheus, whom the earth had borne, and set him down "in Athens, in her own rich temple", where the young men of the [[Athenians]] worship with bulls and rams as the years come round (see [[athena-and-erechtheus]]). Then comes the leader, [[Menestheus]], with fifty ships (2.552–556), and after him Ajax's twelve ships from [[Salamis]], stationed beside the Athenians (2.557–558).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Athens has a small place in Homer. In the Odyssey, Athena leaves Scheria for "Marathon and wide-streeted Athens" and enters "the strong house of Erechtheus" (Od. 7.80–81), and Sunium is "the cape of Athens" (Od. 3.278). Theseus, the city's great hero, is named once in the Iliad, in a line that may be a later addition (1.265; see [[theseus-line]]).

## Outside Homer

Because the entry is so favorable to Athens, it was suspected in antiquity of Athenian tampering, together with the line that places the ships of Salamis beside the Athenians (see [[solon-and-salamis]]). Athenians in turn cited it to claim a leading place among the Greeks (see [[Menestheus]]). In the Mycenaean period the Acropolis was a fortified citadel. Stretches of its thirteenth-century wall survive, with a hidden stairway down to a spring on the north slope.
