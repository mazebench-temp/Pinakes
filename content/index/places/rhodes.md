## Etymology

Ῥόδος (Rhodos). The Greeks connected the name with ῥόδον ("rose"), and a rose was the badge on Rhodian coins. The real origin of the name is unknown.

## Descriptions

{{quote:acf6a974-5183-50c1-ba9e-292c02b75dd3}}

διὰ τρίχα κοσμηθέντες: "arranged in three divisions", one for each of the three cities. The story of the settlement makes the same point: the settlers "lived there in three groups, by tribes" (τριχθὰ ... καταφυλαδόν), "and were loved by Zeus ... and the son of Cronus poured wondrous wealth upon them" (2.668–670). ## Outside Homer

The three cities of the Catalogue stayed separate until 408/7 BC, when they joined to found the new city of Rhodes at the northern tip of the island (Diodorus 13.75.1). With Cos, Cnidus and Halicarnassus they made up the Dorian Hexapolis, the six Dorian cities of Asia (Herodotus 1.144). Homer's threefold Rhodes matches this historical island, and some scholars take the passage as a late addition to the Catalogue. Others point to the Mycenaean cemeteries at Ialysus, which show Greeks on the island in the Late Bronze Age.

Pindar's seventh *Olympian* (464 BC), for the Rhodian boxer Diagoras, tells the island's own myths. Rhodes rose from the sea and was given to Helios, whose grandsons Cameirus, Ialysus and Lindus divided the land in three (7.54–76). Zeus rained gold on the island (7.34, 7.49–50), a gift that recalls Homer's "wondrous wealth", and Tlepolemus is honored as its founder (7.77–80). The Colossus of Rhodes, a bronze statue of Helios, stood at the harbor of the new city in the third century BC.
