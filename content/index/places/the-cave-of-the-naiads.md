## In Homer

**The cave.** The cave lies at the head of the harbor of Phorcys on Ithaca, beside the long-leaved olive (*Odyssey* 13.102–103; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]], [[olive]] and [[ithaca]]). The poem describes it as the Phaeacian ship comes in to land:

{{quote:20f4966f-b577-5ea7-ba3a-d458a2aa1729}}

It is ἐπήρατον, “lovely”, and ἠεροειδές, “misty”, the word of the misty sea and of Scylla's cave (*Odyssey* 13.150, 12.80; see [[misty]] and [[scylla]]), and it is ἱρὸν νυμφάων, “sacred to the nymphs”, the Naiads, νηϊάδες, nymphs of springs, whose water is in the cave itself, ὕδατʼ ἀενάοντα, “ever-flowing waters” (see [[nymphs]]). The furniture of the cave is of stone. The mixing bowls and two-handled jars are λάϊνοι, “of stone”, and in them the bees τιθαιβώσσουσι, “store up honey”, a verb found only here (*Odyssey* 13.105–106; see [[mixing-bowl]], [[two-handled-jar]] and [[bees]]). The looms too are of stone, ἱστοὶ λίθεοι περιμήκεες, “towering looms of stone”, and on them the nymphs weave φάρεʼ … ἁλιπόρφυρα, “sea-purple webs”, φάρεα being “webs”, like the φᾶρος that Penelope set up her great loom to weave (D. B. Monro, *Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 13.108, comparing *Odyssey* 19.138; see [[loom]], [[weaving]] and [[purple]]). ἁλιπόρφυρος, “sea-purple”, is otherwise the color of the yarn that Arete spins at her hearth (*Odyssey* 6.53, 6.306; see [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**The two doors.** The cave has two θύραι, “doors” (see [[door]]). The one toward Boreas, the North Wind, is καταιβαταὶ ἀνθρώποισιν, “by which men go down”; the other, toward Notus, the South Wind, is θεώτεραι, “for gods”, which Monro takes not as “more divine” but as “divine” in contrast to the human door, and men do not go in by it, “but it is the way of the immortals” (*Odyssey* 13.109–112; Monro on *Odyssey* 13.111; see [[boreas]], [[notus]] and [[immortals]]). Neither καταιβάτης nor θεώτερος is found anywhere else in Homer.

**Odysseus' cave.** The cave was a place of Odysseus' own worship. When Athena shows him his island, she points to it: “and this is the vaulted cave where you used to offer / many perfect hecatombs to the nymphs” (*Odyssey* 13.349–350; see [[hecatomb]]). κατηρεφής, “vaulted”, is said too of the Cyclops' cave, “overhung with laurels” (*Odyssey* 9.183; see [[roofed]] and [[the-cave-of-polyphemus]]). The Naiads, to whom he prays at once, are “daughters of Zeus”, and he promises them gifts again, “as before” (*Odyssey* 13.355–360; see [[prayer]]). Then Athena goes into the cave, searching through it for its κευθμῶνες, its “hiding places”, lays away in its inner part the gold, the bronze and the clothes that the Phaeacians gave him, and sets a stone against the doors (*Odyssey* 13.361–371; see [[possessions]] and [[door-stone]]). Odysseus will tell his son that the gifts “lie in caves by the will of the gods” (*Odyssey* 16.232, a plain rendering; see [[telemachus]]).

Other nymphs in the Odyssey have caves: Calypso lives in one, and on Thrinacia the companions drag their ship into a hollow cave where the nymphs have their dancing floors and seats (*Odyssey* 5.57–75, 12.317–318; see [[calypsos-cave]] and [[thrinacia]]).

## Outside Homer

**Strabo.** Strabo counts the two doors among the proofs of Homer's wide learning in geography: he shows his knowledge of latitudes and winds in the way he places things, as when he puts Ithaca “toward the darkness” and the others “toward the dawn and the sun”, and gives the cave two doors, “one toward the north, the other toward the south” (*Geography* 1.2.20, quoting *Odyssey* 9.25–26 and, freely, 13.109–111; see [[strabo]]). Elsewhere, among the changes that the face of the land has undergone, he writes that “in Ithaca there is no such cave, nor any shrine of the nymphs, as Homer describes”, and that it is better to blame change than ignorance, or a misrepresentation of the places for the sake of the myth; the matter being unclear, he leaves it open (*Geography* 1.3.18).

**Numenius.** Proclus reports that Numenius, explaining the two chasms in the sky of the myth of Er as the two tropic signs of the zodiac, Cancer the chasm by which souls go down into birth and Capricorn the chasm by which they go up, called Homer's verses as witness: the doors toward Boreas are the ways down for men, while those toward Notus, by which men may not enter, are the ways of the immortals only, because Capricorn, leading souls up, frees them from their life among men and receives only the immortal and divine (Proclus, *Commentary on the Republic* II 129 Kroll; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]] and [[plato]]). Proclus objects that the account does not agree with Plato's own words (II 130 Kroll). The Neoplatonist Porphyry wrote an essay on the cave, *On the Cave of the Nymphs* (see [[nymphs]]).

**Virgil.** The harbor in Libya where Aeneas lands after the storm has under its cliff a cave, “within it sweet waters and seats of living rock, / the home of the nymphs” (*intus aquae dulces vivoque sedilia saxo, / nympharum domus*, *Aeneid* 1.166–168; see [[virgils-storm]] and [[virgil]]).

**The cave on Ithaki.** Sir William Gell, who in 1807 identified the places of the poem on the modern island, found the grotto and the harbor of Phorcys in Dexia Bay, west of Port Vathi (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix on Ithaca, p. 559). Merry quotes Hercher's scorn for the successors who, instead of questioning Gell, substituted for his grotto of the nymphs, which had disappeared, a new one “aus dem Naturalienkabinet der Insel”, from the island's natural-history cabinet; Thiersch, in Merry's words, “was no less fortunate than Gell in discovering the actual grotto of the nymphs, though in a different spot”, and Schliemann described his own descent into the grotto in the cliffs of Dexia Bay (Appendix on Ithaca, pp. 560–561). Merry's own view is that Homer meant the real Ithaca but knew it only by report, and that his details are a poet's picture of scenery common to many Greek islands (Appendix on Ithaca, p. 561).
