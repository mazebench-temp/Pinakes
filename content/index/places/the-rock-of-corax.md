## The name

κόραξ, “raven”, is found in Homer only in this name (see [[birds]]). The text prints Κόρακος as a proper name, and so does D. B. Monro; W. W. Merry writes the word as a common noun, “the ‘Raven's rock’ (κόρακος πέτρη 13.408), described as being near the spring Arethusa” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix on Ithaca, p. 556). Eustathius takes Κόραξ in the phrase as a proper name, as one says “Megalopolis, the one in Arcadia”, and tells its story (see below).

The same name belonged to Corax of Syracuse, named with Tisias as the first to write an art of rhetoric, whose art, Aristotle says, was composed of the argument from what is probable not absolutely but in some respect (*Rhetoric* 1402a17–18; see [[corax]]). The Suda gives under one headword the bird, the rhetor, “the inventor of rhetoric”, and a siege engine (Suda κ 2066).

## In Homer

Athena, when she has told Odysseus how she will make him unknown, sends him first to his swineherd, whom he will find with the swine where they feed “by the Rock of Corax and at the spring Arethusa” (*Odyssey* 13.404–411; see [[eumaeus]], [[arethusa]] and [[boar]]). The rock is not named again.

## Outside Homer

Eustathius, explaining the verse, tells a local story: Corax was a man of the place, the son of a local woman named Arethusa; hunting hares on Ithaca, he was carried over a cliff and died, and his mother, coming to the spring in her grief, died there, so that the spring was named Arethusa after the mother and the rock the Rock of Corax after the son; a man of the place, he adds, was called Korakopetrites, as a man of Megalopolis is a Megalopolites (Eustathius' note is among the witnesses printed with Aristotle fr. 489 Gigon, on the proverb “to the ravens”; paraphrased from an English translation). Merry counts the rock, with Neriton and Neion, among the heights of the island (Appendix on Ithaca, p. 556; see [[neritum]] and [[neion]]).
