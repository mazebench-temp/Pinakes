Troy is a walled city, and its wall is its defense for the whole of the war. The Achaeans fight on the plain below it, the Trojans come out through its gates, and the war ends only when the city is taken (see [[troy]]).

## Descriptions

The city's epithets are epithets of its fortifications. Troy and Ilios are εὐτείχεος, “well-walled” (1.129, 2.113; see [[well-walled]]), εὔπυργος, “of the fine towers” (7.71; see [[of-the-fine-towers]]), and ὑψίπυλος, “of the high gates” (16.698, 21.544). The wall is “steep” (αἰπύ, 6.327, 11.181) and “long” (τείχεα μακρά, 4.34, 22.507), and Hector calls its towers θεόδμητοι, “god-built” (8.519). Its gates are the [[scaean-gates|Scaean gates]] toward the plain and the [[dardanian-gates|Dardanian gates]], which may be the same gate under another name, and beside the Scaean gates stands “the great tower of Ilios” (6.386; see [[the-tower-of-ilios]]). A wagon road runs along its foot, past a lookout and the wild fig tree to the springs of the Scamander (22.145–148; see [[the-fig-tree]]). By the fig tree, Andromache says, “the city can be scaled and the wall is open to assault” (6.433–434). ἐπίδρομον, “open to assault”, is found only there, and the attempts she speaks of are told nowhere else (see [[andromaches-advice]]).

## Mythology

Poseidon built the wall. In the battle of the gods he reminds Apollo of the year the two of them spent in the service of “the proud Laomedon” for an agreed wage (21.441–445; see [[laomedon]]). “I built a wall around the city for the Trojans,” he says, “wide and very beautiful, so that the city might be unbreakable; and you, Phoebus, herded his cattle in the glens of wooded Ida” (21.446–449). When the year was out, Laomedon robbed them of their wages and sent them away with threats: he would bind them hand and foot and sell them into distant islands, and he would cut off the ears of both (21.450–457). θητεύσαμεν, “we served for hire” (21.444), puts the gods among the θῆτες, hired laborers who owned no land, the lowest place a free man could hold (Od. 11.489–491). In Book 7 Poseidon claims the building for both of them (7.452–453), and the two accounts do not agree about Apollo's part (see [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]]).

The wall did not save Laomedon. When he cheated Heracles too, Heracles sacked Ilios with six ships and few men (5.640–651; see [[heracles-sacks-troy]]). On the plain stands another wall, “the high, heaped-up wall of godlike Heracles”, which the Trojans and Athena built for him so that he could escape the sea monster (20.144–148).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Trojans watch the war from the wall. The elders sit with Priam on the tower by the Scaean gates, and Helen names the Achaean leaders to them (3.145–244; see [[the-view-from-the-wall]]). Zeus asks Hera whether her anger would be healed only if she could “go in through the gates and the long walls / and eat Priam raw” (4.34–35; see [[hera]]). In Book 6 the people are dying “around the city and the steep wall” (6.327), and Andromache goes hurrying to the wall because she has heard that the Trojans are hard pressed (6.386–389).

The gods defend it. Patroclus three times sets foot on the angle of the high wall, and three times Apollo, standing on the tower, pushes him back: it is not fated that the city be sacked by his spear, nor by Achilles' (16.698–711; see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]). When Achilles routs the Trojans, Apollo goes into Ilios, “for he was concerned for the wall of the well-built city, lest the Danaans sack it beyond fate that day” (21.515–517), and Priam, on the tower, orders the gatekeepers to hold the gates open for the fleeing army and close them behind it (21.526–537). Only Hector stays outside, bound there by his fate before the Scaean gates (22.5–6). Achilles chases him three times around the city of Priam, along the wagon road under the wall, and whenever Hector makes for the Dardanian gates, hoping for help from the men on the towers, Achilles turns him back toward the plain (22.136–166, 22.194–198). Andromache, running to the wall, sees him dragged away (22.460–465), and says in her lament that he alone guarded “the gates and the long walls” (22.507; see [[astyanax]]).

## Outside Homer

Later writers added a mortal to the builders. [[pindar|Pindar]] tells how Apollo and Poseidon took Aeacus as their fellow worker on the wall, and how an omen of three serpents showed that Troy would be taken where the mortal had built (*Olympian* 8.31–46; see [[aeacus]]). [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] says that the two gods took human form to test Laomedon's arrogance, and undertook to fortify Pergamum for a wage (*Library* 2.5.9; see [[pergamus]]). For [[horace|Horace]] the cheated wage began Troy's doom: Juno says the city was condemned “from the time when Laomedon cheated the gods of their agreed pay” (*ex quo destituit deos / mercede pacta Laomedon*, *Odes* 3.3.21–22). In [[virgil|Virgil]]'s account of the last night, Aeneas sees Neptune shaking the walls and their foundations with his trident and tearing the city from its seat (*Neptunus muros magnoque emota tridenti / fundamenta quatit*, *Aeneid* 2.610–611), and “Neptune's Troy” overturned from its base (*ex imo verti Neptunia Troia*, 2.625). The god who built the wall pulls it down.

At Hisarlık, the site of Ilion (see [[troy]]), the citadel of the sixth settlement, Troy VI, was ringed by a wall of fitted limestone with a sloping face, towers and gates. Wilhelm Dörpfeld, who uncovered it in 1893–1894, took it for the wall of Priam's Troy. Whether the poet knew these walls, or walls like them, is disputed.
