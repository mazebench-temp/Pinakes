## In Homer

Artacia is a κρήνη καλλιρέεθρος, a “fair-flowing spring”, below the city of the Laestrygonians, “for from there they used to carry water to the city” (*Odyssey* 10.107–108; see [[telepylus]]). The king's daughter has come down to it to draw water, and the scouts whom Odysseus has sent to find out what men live in the land meet her there and ask who the king is; she shows them her father's high-roofed house, and so leads them to Antiphates (*Odyssey* 10.105–116; see [[antiphates-the-laestrygonian]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]). The meeting at the water is a scene the poem knows from kinder places: Athena, in the likeness of a young girl carrying a water jar, meets Odysseus as he is about to enter the city of the Phaeacians and leads him to the house of Alcinous (*Odyssey* 7.18–20; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]]). W. W. Merry compares the daughters of Celeus in the *Hymn to Demeter*, who come for water to carry to their father's house in bronze pitchers and find Demeter sitting by the well (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 10.105; *Hymn to Demeter* 98–110). At Artacia the king's daughter is “strong”, and the house she shows the strangers is a man-eater's.

## Outside Homer

Merry notes that there seems to have been a spring Artacia near Cyzicus, alluded to by Alcaeus and by the other poets who told of the Argonauts, and he refers to Apollonius of Rhodes; the story of Circe, he adds, has several remarkable likenesses to the Argonauts' legend of Medea (on *Odyssey* 10.108; see [[apollonius]], [[alcaeus]] and [[medea]]). Herodotus names the town of Artace, from which a man of Cyzicus came, and which the Phoenicians of the Persian fleet burned with Proconnesus (*Histories* 4.14.2, 6.33.2; see [[herodotus]]), and Strabo a wooded mountain, Artace, on the Cyzicenes' island, with an islet of the same name lying off it (*Geography* 12.8.11; see [[strabo]]).
