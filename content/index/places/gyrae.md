## The name

Γυραί, “the Gyrae”, are the rounded rocks: Merry derives the name from γυρός, “round”, the word for the stooping shoulders of Odysseus' herald Eurybates (*Odyssey* 19.246), and the adjective is Γυραίη, “Gyraean” (*Odyssey* 4.507; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 4.499). Homer calls them “the great rocks”, πέτρῃσιν μεγάλῃσι (*Odyssey* 4.501), and names them only in Proteus' account of the death of the Locrian Ajax (see [[the-death-of-ajax-son-of-oileus]] and [[ajax-son-of-oileus]]).

## Outside Homer

Where the rocks were was not agreed. Eustathius and Hesychius, as Merry reports them, place them near Myconos, one of the Cyclades; Quintus of Smyrna sets the Gyraean rock off Caphereus, the southeastern cape of Euboea, which Merry thinks more correct (Merry on *Odyssey* 4.499, citing Quintus 14.560; see [[euboea]]). Euripides does not name the rocks, but his Poseidon, granting Athena a bitter homecoming for the Achaeans, foretells that “the shores of Myconos and the reefs of Delos, / Scyros and Lemnos and the headlands of Caphereus” will hold the bodies of many dead (*Trojan Women* 88–91; see [[euripides]]).
