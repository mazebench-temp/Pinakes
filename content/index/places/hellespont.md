ἀγάρροος = ἀγα- ("very") + ῥόος ("stream"): "strong-flowing", for the current that runs through the strait from the Propontis to the Aegean. The word occurs twice in the Iliad, both times of the Hellespont (here and 12.30). The Thracians are those "whom the Hellespont encloses" (ἐντὸς ἐέργει): seen from Troy, the strait is their boundary.

{{notes}}

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Hellespont is the sea by the Achaean camp. Hector imagines a warrior's tomb heaped up "by the broad Hellespont", which sailors will point out in years to come (7.84–91). Achilles threatens to sail home over "the fish-filled Hellespont" (9.360). Achilles tells Priam that his kingdom once reached from Lesbos to Phrygia and "the boundless Hellespont" (24.544–545). In the Odyssey the tomb of Achilles and Patroclus stands on a headland "by the broad Hellespont", to be seen far out at sea (24.80–84). Epithets such as πλατύς, "broad", and ἀπείρων, "boundless", suggest that Homer's Hellespont took in the open sea off Troy, not only the narrow strait.

## Outside Homer

The name means "the sea of Helle". Helle, fleeing with her brother Phrixus on the golden ram, fell into the strait and drowned (Aeschylus, *Persians* 70; Apollodorus 1.9.1). Xerxes bridged it with boats at Abydos in 480 BC and, when a storm broke the first bridges, had the water whipped (Herodotus 7.33–36). Leander swam it every night to reach Hero at Sestus (see [[sestus]]), and in 1810 Lord Byron swam it from Sestos to Abydos to show that it could be done.
