κρείων Ἀχελώϊος, “lord Achelous” (21.194; see [[powerful]]). Homer names the river once, in Achilles' boast over Asteropaeus, the grandson of the river Axius, whom he has just killed (21.184–199; see [[asteropaeus]], [[axius]] and [[zeus-is-mightier-than-rivers]]). A river's son, Achilles says, cannot strive with the sons of Zeus, for there is no fighting with Zeus, “with whom not even lord Achelous can match himself, / nor the great strength of deep-flowing Oceanus” (21.194–195; see [[oceanus]] and [[rivers]]). Achelous is the one river Achilles names before he comes to Oceanus, “from whom all rivers and all the sea / and all springs and deep wells flow” (21.196–197). Walter Leaf explains the honor: the Achelous was the only large river of Greece and probably connected with Dodona, so that it was held in special veneration and widely worshipped, and he reports the scholia's remark that for this reason all water is called by its name (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 21.194; see [[dodona]]).

**Zenodotus' text.** Zenodotus left out the verse of Oceanus (21.195), so that Achelous became the source of all rivers, the sea and the springs. Aristarchus kept it, objecting that in Homer the parent of rivers is Oceanus and not Achelous, and Leaf, who thinks the objection enough to save “one of the most majestic lines ever written”, also reports that Aristarchus read οὔτε for οὐδέ (on 21.195; see the translation's note on 21.194–197).

**Another Achelous.** In Achilles' story of Niobe, the nymphs who dance about another Achelous have their beds on Sipylus (24.615–616; see [[niobe]] and [[nymphs]]). Leaf calls that river quite different from this one, a stream of Lydia otherwise unknown, and notes other rivers of the name in the Troad, Achaea, Thessaly and Arcadia (on 21.194, 24.616).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod names Achelous, “silver-eddying” (ἀργυροδίνην), among the rivers that Tethys bore to Oceanus (*Theogony* 340; see [[hesiod]], [[tethys]] and [[silver-eddying]]); in Homer the epithet belongs to the Peneius and to the river of Troy (2.753; 21.8, 21.130). Herodotus says that the Achelous, flowing through Acarnania into the sea, has already made half of the Echinades islands mainland (2.10.3), and that with the Nestus it bounds the only part of Europe where lions are found (7.126.1; see [[herodotus]]). Aristotle places the flood of Deucalion chiefly in ancient Hellas, “the country about Dodona and the Achelous”, a river that has often changed its course, where the Selli lived (*Meteorologica* 1.14, 352a32–b2; see [[aristotle]] and [[selli]]). [[Strabo]] makes it the boundary of Aetolia and Acarnania, flowing south from Pindus into the sea where, as some say, the Gulf of Corinth begins; it was once called Thoas, and other rivers of the name flow by Dyme and by Lamia (*Geography* 10.2.1). Its silt, he says, joins the Echinades to the land and confounded the boundaries of the Acarnanians and the Aetolians, and from those wars came the story that Heracles overcame the river and won Deianeira, the daughter of Oeneus, as his prize. He quotes Deianeira's words in Sophocles: the river came to woo her in three shapes, a bull, a coiling serpent, and a man with the face of an ox. Some add that the horn Heracles broke off was the horn of Amaltheia, and others explain the bull from the roaring of rivers and the bends of their streams, which are called horns (10.2.19). Proclus, defending Achilles' fight with the Xanthus against Plato, answers that Heracles too is said to have fought Achelous (*Commentary on the Republic*, Kroll I, p. 149; see [[proclus-the-neoplatonist]] and [[plato-achilles-and-the-river]]).

The worship was not confined to the river's own country. At Megara Pausanias was shown a place called Rhus, “Stream”, where water had once flowed down from the mountains above the city, until the tyrant Theagenes turned it another way and made an altar there to Achelous (1.41.2; see [[pausanias]]); Walter Leaf cites the passage for the spread of the cult (on 21.194). Plato's Socrates, by the Ilissus outside Athens, takes the figurines and statues there for the shrine of some Nymphs and of Achelous, and later calls the Nymphs the daughters of Achelous (*Phaedrus* 230b, 263d; see [[plato]]). In Euripides Dirce is the “daughter of Achelous” (*Bacchae* 519–520), and the name stands for any water: Pentheus, thinking his palace on fire, orders his slaves to bring “Achelous” (*Bacchae* 625–626), and Hermione bids Andromache sprinkle “the dew of Achelous” from golden vessels as she sweeps the house (*Andromache* 166–167; see [[euripides]]).
