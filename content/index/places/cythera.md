## In Homer

Amphidamas of Cythera (Κυθηρίῳ, an adjective of the island's name) is one of the owners of the helmet of boar's tusks: Autolycus gave it to him at Scandeia, and he gave it to Molus as a guest-gift (10.268–269; see [[the-boars-tusk-helmet]] and [[scandeia]]). The Iliad's other Cytherian is Lycophron, son of Mastor, Ajax's attendant, who had lived with Ajax since he killed a man in Cythera, and whom Hector's spear, thrown at Ajax, kills instead (15.429–440; see [[ajax]] and [[exile-and-refuge]]). The poem calls the island ζάθεος, holy (Κυθήροισι ζαθέοισι, 15.432; see [[zatheos]]), the words with which Hesiod brings the newborn Aphrodite there (*Theogony* 192). In the Odyssey it is the last known place Odysseus passes before his wanderings: rounding Cape Malea, he is driven by the current and the north wind “past Cythera”, and on the tenth day reaches the Lotus-eaters (*Odyssey* 9.80–84).

## Aphrodite of Cythera

The Odyssey twice calls Aphrodite Κυθέρεια, “Cytherea” (*Odyssey* 8.288, 18.193); the Iliad does not (see [[aphrodite]]). Hesiod explains the title from the island, where the goddess came first from the sea before she went on to Cyprus (*Theogony* 192–198; see [[hesiod]]). The vowels of the two words differ (Κυθέρεια, Κύθηρα), and the connection may be no more than the ancient one.

## Outside Homer

Herodotus says that the sanctuary of Heavenly Aphrodite on Cythera was founded by Phoenicians from Syria (Herodotus 1.105.3; see [[herodotus]]). Thucydides describes the island as he tells of its capture by the Athenians under Nicias in 424 BC: it lay off Laconia by Malea, its people were Lacedaemonians of the perioeci, a “judge of Cythera” (κυθηροδίκης) came out from Sparta every year, merchant ships from Egypt and Libya put in there, and it kept pirates off the coast of Laconia (Thucydides 4.53–54; see [[thucydides]]). Pausanias names Scandeia as its harbor town, with the city ten stades inland, and calls the sanctuary of Heavenly Aphrodite the holiest, and the oldest of all her sanctuaries among the Greeks; the goddess was an armed wooden image (Pausanias 3.23.1).
