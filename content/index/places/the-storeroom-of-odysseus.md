## The room

θάλαμος is an inner room of a house, a bedchamber or a storeroom (see [[house]]). This one is “high-roofed” and “wide” (ὑψόροφον … εὐρύν, *Odyssey* 2.337–338; see [[high-roofed]]), and Telemachus goes down into it, κατεβήσετο, as Hecuba and Priam go down into the fragrant chamber where the robes of their house are kept (*Iliad* 6.288, 24.191; see [[priams-palace]]) and Menelaus into his own at Sparta (*Odyssey* 15.99).

It holds the wealth of the house. The gold and bronze lie νητός, “heaped”, the verbal adjective of νηέω, “heap up”, the verb of the gold and bronze with which Achilles may load his ship (*Iliad* 9.137) and of the ransom heaped on Priam's wagon (*Iliad* 24.276); beside them lie clothing in chests and fragrant oil, and jars of old wine stand in a row against the wall (*Odyssey* 2.338–342; see [[gold-and-silver]], [[bronze]], [[chest]], [[olive]], [[storage-jar]] and [[wine]]). The wine is kept for the master, “in case Odysseus ever / came home, even after toiling through many sorrows” (*Odyssey* 2.342–343; see [[nostos]]). The doors are closely fitted double doors that can be locked (*Odyssey* 2.344–345; see [[door]] and [[closely-fitted]]), and a woman keeps the room as housekeeper night and day, Eurycleia, the old nurse (*Odyssey* 2.345–347; see [[eurycleia]] and [[handmaids]]).

## Later in the Odyssey

In the twenty-first book Penelope goes to a treasure chamber of the house to fetch the bow for the contest. She takes a bronze key with an ivory handle and goes with her handmaids to the innermost chamber (θάλαμόνδε … ἔσχατον), where the master's treasures lie, bronze and gold and iron wrought with much toil, and his bow and quiver; she unties the strap from its hook, puts in the key and knocks back the bars, and the doors roar like a bull at pasture; standing on a raised floor among chests of fragrant clothing, she takes the bow down from its peg, sits with it on her knees and weeps (*Odyssey* 21.5–12, 21.42–56; see [[door]] and [[chest]]). The poem does not say whether this is the room of the second book. The renderings of the twenty-first book are plain renderings, not the translation.
