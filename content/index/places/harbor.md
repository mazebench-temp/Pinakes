## In the Iliad

The Iliad's one narrated voyage reaches its goal in a harbor: Odysseus' ship comes “within the deep harbor” of Chryse, where the crew furl the sail, lower the mast and row her to her anchorage (1.432–435; see [[deep-harbor]] and [[sailing-type-scene]]). The other harbors are in similes and stories: snow poured over “the harbors and shores of the gray sea” (12.284), fish fleeing a dolphin that “fill the recesses of a harbor with good anchorage” (21.23), and the Phoenicians who set out the silver bowl of Sidon “in harbors” before they gave it to Thoas (23.741–745).

## In the Odyssey

The Odyssey describes its harbors as a sailor would. On the island off the land of the Cyclopes there is “a harbor with good anchorage”, where there is no need to cast out mooring-stones or tie stern-cables, and a spring flows at its head (*Odyssey* 9.136–141; see [[mooring-stones]] and [[stern-cables]]). The harbor of Phorcys on Ithaca lies between two sheer headlands that keep out the swell, so that ships lie inside without a cable, with an olive at its head and the cave of the nymphs close by (*Odyssey* 13.96–104). The Phaeacians' city has “a fine harbor on either side” and a narrow entrance (*Odyssey* 6.262–265). The harbor of the Laestrygonians, ringed by a sheer rock, with headlands facing each other at a narrow mouth and water always calm, is the place where Odysseus' other ships are destroyed; he alone keeps his ship outside, at the far edge, tied to a rock (*Odyssey* 10.87–132). Pharos has a harbor from which ships put to sea after drawing water (*Odyssey* 4.358–359), and the rocky islet of Asteris, between Ithaca and Samos, has harbors with two entrances, where the suitors lie in wait for Telemachus (*Odyssey* 4.844–847). On Ithaca the stranger's ship lies “in the harbor of Rheithron” (*Odyssey* 1.186; see [[rheithron]]), Athena moors Telemachus' ship at nightfall “at the far edge of the harbor” (*Odyssey* 2.391), and the ship that brings him back from Pylos comes in λιμένος πολυβενθέος ἐντός, “within the deep harbor”, as the ship to Chryse did, and is drawn up on the land (*Odyssey* 16.322–325). The renderings of the Odyssey's later books are plain renderings, not the translation.

## Outside Homer

Aristotle takes “to Achaean harbors”, said of a single harbor, as an example of making one thing many, as the poets do (*Rhetoric* 1407b33–34).
