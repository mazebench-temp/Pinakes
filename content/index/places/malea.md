## The name

Homer has the plural, Μαλειάων ὄρος αἰπύ, “the steep mountain of Malea” (*Odyssey* 3.287 = 4.514; see [[steep]]), Μαλειῶν (*Odyssey* 19.187), and once the singular, Μάλειαν (*Odyssey* 9.80). Merry collects the later forms, Μαλέη in Herodotus (Herodotus 4.179) and Μαλέαι (Herodotus 1.82), and gives the modern names of the cape, Malia or St. Angelo (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 3.287).

## In Homer

Malea is the turning point of the voyages home, where the wind takes a ship out of its course. When Menelaus reached it, Zeus poured on him the breath of shrill winds and split his fleet between Crete and Egypt (*Odyssey* 3.286–300; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). Agamemnon, as Proteus tells Menelaus, was about to reach it when a storm caught him up and carried him over the fish-filled sea, groaning heavily, to the edge of the land where Thyestes had lived and Aegisthus now lived (*Odyssey* 4.514–518; see [[the-murder-of-agamemnon]]). Odysseus was rounding it when the wave, the current and the North Wind drove him off past Cythera, to nine days of drifting and the land of the Lotus-eaters (*Odyssey* 9.80–84; see [[cythera]] and [[boreas]]). In the tale he tells Penelope as a Cretan, the force of the wind drove Odysseus off course at Malea as he made for Troy, and brought him to Crete (*Odyssey* 19.186–188). The renderings of Books 4, 9 and 19 are plain renderings, not the translation.

## Outside Homer

Strabo says that in the old days the sea beyond Malea was as hard to sail as the strait of Sicily, because of its contrary winds, and quotes the proverb “When you round Malea, forget the things at home”; the merchants of Italy and of Asia were glad to unload at Corinth and give up the voyage round the cape (*Geography* 8.6.20; see [[corinth]]). Eustathius, Merry reports, quotes the same saying (on *Odyssey* 3.287). Herodotus tells that Jason, sailing round the Peloponnese for Delphi with the Argo, was caught off Malea by the North Wind and carried to Libya (Herodotus 4.179), and that the Corcyraeans, who had promised sixty triremes against Xerxes, excused their absence from Salamis by saying that the Etesian winds kept them from rounding Malea (7.168.4). In the *Hymn to Apollo* the Cretan ship that the god steers to Crisa “first passed Malea”, then the Laconian land and Taenarum (*Hymn to Apollo* 409–413; see [[crisa]]). In Euripides' *Cyclops* Silenus and the satyrs, searching the sea for Dionysus, “had already sailed close to Malea” when an east wind blew them to the rock of Aetna (*Cyclops* 18–20), and Odysseus counts “the recesses at the cape of Malea” among the temples of Poseidon that the Greeks kept safe (*Cyclops* 290–295; see [[geraestus]] and [[sunium]]).
