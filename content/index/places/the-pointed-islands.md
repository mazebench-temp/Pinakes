## The words

“And from there he sent her on toward the pointed islands” (*Odyssey* 15.299; see [[the-journey-of-telemachus]]). θοός is “swift”, the epithet of ships, of night and of men in battle (see [[swift]] and [[swift-ships]]), and only here is it said of islands. Strabo explained it as ὀξύς, “sharp”, of the islands' shape (*Geography* 8.3.26), and the meaning “pointed” has been drawn from the verb θοόω, “sharpen”, which Homer uses once, of the stake that Odysseus sharpened to blind the Cyclops (*Odyssey* 9.327; see [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). D. B. Monro doubted both: “The meaning ‘swift’ cannot be seriously thought of”, and θοόω may first have meant “to make quick” or “active”, and only have come to mean “sharpen” when it was said of weapons and tools; “It seems best, then, to leave θοῇσιν unexplained” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 15.299–300). The translation renders the words “the pointed islands”, in lowercase, as a description and not a name (see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 15.295–300).

## The islands of Ithaca

Without the adjective, “the islands” in the Odyssey are the islands named with Ithaca: the suitors are “all the best men who hold command over the islands, / Dulichium and Same and wooded Zacynthus”, with those of Ithaca (*Odyssey* 1.245–247; see [[dulichium]], [[samos]] and [[zacynthus]]), and Telemachus tells his mother that no one is mightier than he to give the bow or refuse it, “neither all who rule over craggy Ithaca, / nor all in the islands toward horse-pasturing Elis” (*Odyssey* 21.344–347, a plain rendering; see [[elis]]). Athena bids Telemachus keep his ship “far from the islands” on the way home (*Odyssey* 15.33; see [[ithaca]]). Monro took the islands of *Odyssey* 15.299 for these three, which the ship must pass between Elis and Ithaca (Monro on *Odyssey* 15.33, 15.299–300).

## Outside Homer

Strabo makes θοαί the poet's word for the Oxeiai, ὀξεῖαι, “the Sharp Islands”. Describing the islands east of Ithaca and Cephallenia, he names the Echinades, “among which is Dulichium, which they now call Dolicha, and the islands called the Oxeiai, which the poet called Θοαί”, lying off the mouth of the Achelous, and he notes that Homer gives the rule of the Echinades and the Oxeiai in the time of the Trojan War to Meges son of Phyleus (*Geography* 10.2.19; see [[echinae]], [[meges]] and [[strabo]]). In his account of Telemachus' voyage the ship turns east toward them after Elis, to avoid the strait where the suitors lay in wait: “By θοαί he means the sharp ones; these are among the Echinades, near the beginning of the Corinthian Gulf and the mouths of the Achelous”; from there it passes Ithaca, so that the island lies behind it, and turns back between Acarnania and Ithaca to land on the far side of the island, not in the Cephallenian strait (*Geography* 8.3.26; see [[asteris]]). In the *Hymn to Apollo*, as Monro notes, the Cretan ship that sails the same coast sees Ithaca and the other islands come into view once Elis is passed, Ἰθάκης τʼ ὄρος αἰπὺ … Δουλίχιόν τε Σάμη τε καὶ ὑλήεσσα Ζάκυνθος, “the steep mountain of Ithaca … and Dulichium and Same and wooded Zacynthus” (*Hymn to Apollo* 426–429; Monro on *Odyssey* 15.299–300).
