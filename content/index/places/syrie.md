## In Homer

Eumaeus names the island in the first verse of his story, when the stranger asks whether his city was sacked or whether he was carried off alone with the flocks (*Odyssey* 15.381–388, 15.403; see [[eumaeus]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]). It lies “above Ortygia, where the turnings of the sun are” (see [[ortygia]] and [[the-turnings-of-the-sun]]); it is “not so very populous, but a good one, / good for cattle, good for sheep, full of wine, rich in wheat”, no famine or sickness comes upon its people, and when they grow old Apollo and Artemis kill them with their gentle arrows (*Odyssey* 15.405–411; see [[gentle-arrows]] and [[rich-in-wheat]]). It has two cities, and Eumaeus' father, Ctesius son of Ormenus, was king over both (*Odyssey* 15.412–414; see [[ctesius]] and [[ormenus-father-of-ctesius]]). Phoenician traders came there and stayed a year, and when they sailed, the child's Phoenician nurse took him on board with her (*Odyssey* 15.415–475; see [[phoenicians]] and [[the-phoenician-nurse]]).

## Which island

W. W. Merry takes “above Ortygia” to mark the place of Syros in the Cyclades, west of Rhenaea, if Ortygia is Delos or Rhenaea, and finds this confirmed by the care of Apollo and Artemis together for the island (*Odyssey* 15.410; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 5.123; see [[ortygia]]). He compares the change of name from Συρίη to Σύρα with that from Ψυρίη, the island off Chios, to Strabo's Ψύρα (Merry on *Odyssey* 3.169; see [[psyria]]). D. B. Monro thought both islands mythical: “The islands Συρίη and Ὀρτυγίη have a mythical character, as the ‘turning points’ of the sun”, as Circe's island has the house of the dawn and the risings of the sun (*Odyssey* 12.3–4; see [[aeaea]]), “and also in respect of the superhuman felicity of the inhabitants. We need not seek to identify them with real places, such as the islands Syra and Delos” (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 15.403). The translation follows him in its note on *Odyssey* 15.403–404.

## Outside Homer

Strabo, going through the Cyclades, comes to Syros, “from which came Pherecydes son of Babys”, and adds: “The poet seems to mention this island, calling it Syrie”, quoting “There is an island called Syrie … above Ortygia” without the words between (*Geography* 10.5.8; see [[strabo]]). The Suda's life of Pherecydes calls the island Syra, “one of the Cyclades, near Delos” (Suda φ 214).
