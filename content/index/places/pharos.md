## In Homer

Homer names the island once, in Menelaus' tale of his return, and describes it in a few verses: an island “in the sea of many waves / in front of Egypt”, as far off as a hollow ship runs in a whole day before a shrill fair wind, with a harbor of good anchorage where ships draw water before they put out to sea (*Odyssey* 4.354–359; see [[egypt]] and [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]]). There the gods kept him for twenty days without a wind, and there Eidothea met him and taught him how to seize her father, Proteus, the unerring Old Man of the Sea, when he came out of the sea at midday to sleep among his seals (*Odyssey* 4.360–461; see [[eidothea]], [[proteus]] and [[seals]]).

## Outside Homer

In later times Pharos lay close off Alexandria. Strabo describes it as an oblong islet very near the mainland, which makes with it a harbor with two mouths; at its tip a rock washed by the sea bore a tower of white stone of many stories, named after the island, which Sostratus of Cnidus, a friend of the kings, dedicated, as its inscription said, for the safety of sailors; a causeway of seven stades, the Heptastadion, ran from the mainland to the island (*Geography* 17.1.6; see [[strabo]]). Caesar describes the tower of great height, “which took its name from the island”, and the island lying off Alexandria that makes its harbor (*Civil War* 3.112).

Homer's distance was an old difficulty. Some blamed him for putting in the open sea an island that lay off the shore, as if he did not know the country; Strabo answers that Pharos had once stood out at sea, before the silt of the Nile built out the shore, and that the poet, knowing it, set it farther off still “for the sake of the fabulous” (*Geography* 1.2.23, 1.2.30). Elsewhere he counts it among places that have changed: “Pharos by Egypt was once in the open sea, but now it has become in a way a peninsula” (*Geography* 1.3.17). Lucan, telling how Caesar seized the island, says that it once stood in the middle of the sea “in the time of the seer Proteus”, but now lies next to the walls of Alexandria (*Pharsalia* 10.509–511). Merry finds Pharos less than a mile from the coast of the Delta, with rocky shelves that rule out any advance of the land, and takes Homer's island for a poet's use of a current story, not a piece of topography (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 4.355).

Euripides makes Proteus a king of Egypt who lived on Pharos: “Proteus, when he was alive, was ruler of this land, / dwelling on the island of Pharos, lord of Egypt” (*Helen* 4–5; see [[euripides]] and [[helen-in-egypt]]).
