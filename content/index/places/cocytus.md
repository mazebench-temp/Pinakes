# Κώκυτός (Od. 10.514; other editions print Κωκυτός), the only place in Homer. Capitalized only, since κωκυτός is the
# common word "wailing" (κωκυτῷ, κωκυτοῦ, Il. 22.409, 22.447).
grc: ["^Κώκυτός"]

## The name

κωκυτός is “wailing”, the cry of mourning, from κωκύω, “wail”: when Hecuba sees Hector dragged in the dust she “wailed very loudly”, and the people “were seized by wailing and groaning throughout the city” (*Iliad* 22.405–409), and Andromache in her house “heard the wailing and the groaning from the tower” (*Iliad* 22.447; see [[lament]]). W. W. Merry calls it the “river of Howling”, comparing Theognis' πολυκώκυτοι Ἀίδαο δόμοι, the house of Hades “of much wailing” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 10.513).

## In Homer

Homer names it once, in Circe's directions to Odysseus: “There into Acheron flow Pyriphlegethon / and Cocytus, which is a branch of the water of Styx, / and there is a rock, and the meeting of the two loud-resounding rivers” (*Odyssey* 10.513–515; see [[acheron]] and [[pyriphlegethon]]). Στυγὸς ὕδατός ἐστιν ἀπορρώξ ends the verse as it ends the Catalogue's verse on the Titaressus, which flows on top of the Peneius like olive oil and does not mingle with it, “for it is a branch of the water of Styx, the dread oath” (*Iliad* 2.751–755; see [[styx]] and [[like-olive-oil]]). How Cocytus is a branch of the Styx, Merry says, is not easy to understand (on *Odyssey* 10.515); he thought that the Styx was at first the only river of the underworld, and that this passage, which brings in the other three, is probably a later invention (on *Odyssey* 10.513).

## Outside Homer

In the myth of the *Phaedo*, the fourth great river falls first into a region terrible and wild, all of a dark blue color, which they call Stygian, and makes there the lake called Styx; it sinks beneath the earth, coils round opposite to Pyriphlegethon, meets it in the Acherusian lake without mingling its water, and falls into Tartarus: “and its name, as the poets say, is Cocytus” (Plato, *Phaedo* 113b–c). Those who have killed are carried out of Tartarus by way of Cocytus, and cry out to those they killed as they pass the Acherusian lake, begging to be let out (*Phaedo* 114a–b). In the *Republic* Socrates would throw out, with the poets' verses on Hades, all the dread and fearful names that belong to them, “Cocytuses and Styxes”, the dead below and the sapless shades, which make every hearer shudder (*Republic* 387b–c; see [[plato]] and [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]).

Pausanias, as Merry reports him, held that Homer took the scenery of the journey to the dead from [[thesprotians|Thesprotia]], where the rivers Acheron and Cocytus flow by the Acherusian lake (on *Odyssey* 10.513; see [[pausanias]]). In Virgil, Acheron belches all its sand into Cocytus, and the Sibyl shows Aeneas “the deep pools of Cocytus and the Stygian marsh” (*Aeneid* 6.295–297, 6.323; see [[virgil]]). In Cicero the rivers “said to flow among the dead”, Acheron, Cocytus and Pyriphlegethon, would have to be gods if Jupiter and Neptune are (*On the Nature of the Gods* 3.43), and “the roaring of Cocytus” is among the portents of poets and painters that the young man of the *Tusculan Disputations* does not believe (*Tusculan Disputations* 1.10; see [[cicero]]).
