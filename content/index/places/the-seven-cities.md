## The offer

The cities come last in Agamemnon's list, after the gifts at hand, the spoil of Troy and a daughter in marriage (9.121–148; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]]). What he offers is lordship. The people of the seven towns, “rich in flocks and rich in cattle” (see [[rich-in-flocks]] and [[rich-in-cattle]]), will honor Achilles “with gifts like a god”, and under his scepter they will bring his θέμιστες (“ordinances”) to fulfillment (9.154–156; see [[scepter]], [[themis]] and [[kingship-and-authority]]). Whether λιπαρὰς τελέουσι θέμιστας means paying rich dues or carrying out his ordinances in prosperity is disputed (see the translation's note on 9.156). Odysseus repeats the eight verses with only the persons changed (9.291–298; see [[message-repeated]]). Nestor, whose kingdom the towns border, is in the hut when the offer is made, and his only answer is that the gifts are “no longer to be scorned” (9.164; see [[nestor]]).

Other heroes are offered land in the same way. The Lycian king gave Bellerophon his daughter and half of all his royal honor, and the Lycians cut him a domain (6.192–195; see [[bellerophon]]); Peleus made Phoenix wealthy and gave him the Dolopians to rule (9.483–484; see [[phoenix]] and [[dolopians]]); the Aetolian elders promise Meleager fifty acres of the richest plain (9.576–580; see [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). In the Odyssey Menelaus says he would have emptied one of the cities around him, “which are ruled by me myself”, to settle Odysseus and his people in Argos (Od. 4.174–177).

## Where they lay

Homer places the towns only by the sea and by Pylos: “All lie near the sea, on the borders of sandy Pylos” (9.153), where νέαται, from νέατος (“lowest, outermost”), marks the edge of Nestor's land (see [[pylos]] and [[sandy]]). None of them is named in the Catalogue of Ships, and only Pherae appears again in Homer, as the home of Diocles (5.543; Od. 3.488 = 15.186). Later Greeks found them around the Messenian Gulf, but did not agree which town was which:

- [[cardamyle|Cardamyle]]: on a strong rock (Strabo 8.4.4); eight stades from the sea, then belonging to Sparta (Pausanias 3.26.7).
- [[enope|Enope]]: Pellana, a place near Cardamyle, or Gerenia (Strabo 8.4.5); Gerenia (Pausanias 3.26.8).
- [[hire|Hire]]: on the mountain toward Andania near Megalopolis, or the later Mesola (Strabo 8.4.5); Abia on the Messenian coast (Pausanias 4.30.1).
- [[pherae-in-messenia|Pherae]]: Pharae, beside Thuria and Gerena (Strabo 8.4.4–5; Pausanias 4.30.2–4.31.1).
- [[antheia|Antheia]]: Thuria, or Asine (Strabo 8.4.5); Thuria (Pausanias 4.31.1).
- [[aepeia|Aepeia]]: Thuria, or Methone (Strabo 8.4.5); Corone (Pausanias 4.34.5).
- [[pedasus-in-messenia|Pedasus]]: Methone (Strabo 8.4.3; Pausanias 4.35.1), or Corone (Strabo 8.4.5).

Strabo puts the seven on the Messenian Gulf and the gulf beside it, named after Messenian Asine (8.4.1), and checks the words “near the sea”: Cardamyle stands on it, Pharae five stades away with a summer anchorage, and the rest at uneven distances (8.4.5). The border between Messenia and Laconia still ran among them in Roman times: Augustus gave Cardamyle, Pharae and Thuria to Laconia (Pausanias 3.26.7, 4.30.2, 4.31.1). See [[strabo]].

## Why Agamemnon could give them

The towns lie beyond Agamemnon's kingdom as the Catalogue draws it (2.569–580; see [[mycenae]]), on the borders of Nestor's, and the poem does not say how they are his to give. Strabo's answer was that in the Trojan period Messenia was part of Menelaus' Laconia, and that Agamemnon would not have promised towns that belonged neither to himself nor to his brother (8.4.1). Elsewhere he reads νέαται Πύλου as setting the towns on the sea of Pylos, which the poet, he says, extends that far (8.3.22). Modern readers have not agreed on an answer, and the poem gives none.
