Φειᾶς πὰρ τείχεσσιν, “beside the walls of Pheia” (7.135), is the second of the three landmarks by which Nestor places the battle of his youth, between the Celadon and the Iardanus (see [[celadon]], [[iardanus]] and [[nestor-and-ereuthalion]]). The Odyssey has the plural, Φεαί: Telemachus' ship, sailing north from Pylos, “made for Pheae, driven on by the wind of Zeus”, and passed Elis (*Odyssey* 15.297–298; see [[cruni]] and [[elis]]). Most manuscripts there read Φεράς, “Pherae”, as the *Hymn to Apollo* does in the same verse; Aristarchus and Strabo read Φεάς, and D. B. Monro accepts it, since a ship going north from Pylos would steer for Pheia and there is no Pherae on that coast (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 15.295–298).

## Outside Homer

[[strabo|Strabo]] describes a cape Pheia on the coast of Elis, where there was once a small town, with a little river nearby, and he quotes Nestor's verse of it (8.3.12); he also follows Telemachus' course past Pheae, quoting the verse with ἀγαλλομένη, “exulting”, for ἐπειγομένη, “driven on”, and counts Pheae, Chalcis and Cruni among the obscure rivers of that coast (8.3.26–27; see [[cruni]] and [[chalcis-in-elis]]). Pausanias reports that a battle on the Chest of Cypselus at Olympia was explained by some as the Pylians and the Arcadians about to fight by the city of Pheia and the river Iardanus (5.18.6). In 431 BC an Athenian fleet put in at Pheia, ravaged the country, and took the town for a short time ([[thucydides|Thucydides]] 2.25.3–5).
