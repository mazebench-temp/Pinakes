## The name

The harbor takes its name from Phorcys, ἁλίοιο γέροντος, “the Old Man of the Sea”, in both verses that name it (*Odyssey* 13.96, 13.345; see [[phorcys-the-sea-god]] and [[old-man-of-the-sea]]), the sea god whom the first book of the Odyssey makes the father of Thoosa and so the grandfather of the Cyclops (*Odyssey* 1.71–72; see [[thoosa]]). Homer names it only in *Odyssey* 13 and gives no reason for the name. The Suda has the name from Homer: “Phorcys: a name. The harbor of Phorcys” (Suda φ 599, a plain rendering).

## In Homer

“There is a harbor of Phorcys, the Old Man of the Sea, / in the land of Ithaca” (*Odyssey* 13.96–97; see [[ithaca]]). Two jutting headlands, sheer, crouch down toward it and keep off the great wave of the winds outside, so that within, ships stay without a mooring rope once they have reached the measure of anchorage (*Odyssey* 13.97–101; see [[harbor]]). At its head stands a long-leaved olive, and near the olive is the cave of the Naiads (*Odyssey* 13.102–112; see [[olive]] and [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]). The Phaeacians, who know the place, row the ship in at dawn, run her up on the land, lay Odysseus asleep on the sand, and set his gifts by the trunk of the olive, away from the road (*Odyssey* 13.113–124; see [[odysseus-set-ashore-on-ithaca]] and [[phaeacians]]). When he wakes, the mist that Athena has poured around him makes the harbors strange to him, and the harbor is the first thing she names when she shows him his island: “This is the harbor of Phorcys, the Old Man of the Sea” (*Odyssey* 13.194–196, 13.345). From it, at the beginning of the next book, he goes up “by the rugged path” to the swineherd's farm (*Odyssey* 14.1; see [[eumaeus]]).

The harbor is one of the Odyssey's well-sheltered harbors. On the island off the land of the Cyclopes there is a harbor “where there is no need of a cable”, and the harbor of the Laestrygonians is closed by “jutting headlands facing one another” at a narrow mouth (*Odyssey* 9.136–137, 10.89–90; see [[the-island-of-goats]] and [[laestrygonians]]). Aeneas' harbor in Libya, with its cave of the nymphs, has its features (see [[virgils-storm]] and [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]).

## Outside Homer

W. W. Merry, summing up the poem's picture of Ithaca, describes the harbor as a land-locked bay “formed by two projecting spurs of cliff, and so sheltered that vessels could lie therein without moorings” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, Appendix on Ithaca, p. 556). Sir William Gell, who in 1807 found almost every place of the poem on the modern island, saw the grotto and the harbor of Phorcys in Dexia Bay, a small bay west of Port Vathi (Appendix on Ithaca, p. 559). His followers did not agree among themselves: “The harbour of Phorcys is sometimes distinguished from, sometimes identified with, the harbour Rheithron; it is placed by one writer in Dexia Bay, by another in Port Frikes” (Appendix on Ithaca, p. 561; see [[rheithron]]). Merry himself held that Homer meant the real island but knew it only by report, and that details such as these are a poet's picture of scenery common to many Greek islands (Appendix on Ithaca, p. 561).
