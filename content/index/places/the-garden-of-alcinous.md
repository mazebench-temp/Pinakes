## In Homer

The garden lies outside the court of the house of Alcinous, near its doors. The poem describes it at the moment when Odysseus, hidden in Athena's mist, stands before the bronze threshold and gazes at the house before he goes in (*Odyssey* 7.81–83, 7.133–135; see [[alcinous]], [[house]] and [[threshold]]):

{{quote:74cf45a3-3761-5f1f-ad45-d48cf7a126b3}}

**The orchard.** ἔκτοσθεν δʼ αὐλῆς μέγας ὄρχατος ἄγχι θυράων / τετράγυος, “And outside the court, near the doors, is a great orchard / of four acres” (*Odyssey* 7.112–113). The court, αὐλή, is the walled space whose walls of bronze, with their coping of dark blue enamel, the poem has just described (*Odyssey* 7.86–87; W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.86; see [[kyanos]]). ὄρχατος is properly the same word as ὄρχος, the “row” of the vineyard (*Odyssey* 7.127), as μέσσατος is of μέσσος: ὄρχος came to mean a single row of plants or trees, and ὄρχατος the rows taken together, the orchard (Bothe, as Merry quotes him, on 7.112). So the Iliad gives Tydeus in Argos “many rows of orchard trees” around his house (φυτῶν … ὄρχατοι, *Iliad* 14.123), and the Odyssey's other μέγας ὄρχατος is the great orchard of Laertes (μέγαν ὄρχατον, *Odyssey* 24.222). τετράγυος, “of four acres”, is said again only of the field that the beggar Odysseus challenges Eurymachus to plow (τετράγυον, *Odyssey* 18.374, a plain rendering); the domain the Calydonians offered Meleager was πεντηκοντόγυον, “of fifty acres” (*Iliad* 9.579). “And a fence is drawn around it on both sides” (*Odyssey* 7.113): ἕρκος ἐλήλαται has the verb of the bronze walls “drawn out” across the court (ἐληλέδατο, *Odyssey* 7.86; see the translation's note there), and of the fence of tin that Hephaestus drew around the golden vineyard of the Shield (περὶ δʼ ἕρκος ἔλασσε, *Iliad* 18.564; see [[the-vintage]]).

The trees are five: “There tall trees grow, flourishing, / pears and pomegranates and apple trees with splendid fruit, / and sweet figs and flourishing olives” (*Odyssey* 7.114–116; see [[trees]] and [[olive]]). The first verse is nearly the verse of the tall trees at the far edge of Calypso's island, ὅθι δένδρεα μακρὰ πεφύκει (*Odyssey* 5.238, 5.241). πεφύκασι is Herodian's reading, where the manuscripts have πεφύκει (see the translation's note on 7.114); Merry notes its short penultimate syllable, and counts it with λελόγχασιν (*Odyssey* 11.304) as the only two instances of that ending in Homer (on 7.114). Of the five only the olive is named in the Iliad, whose fig is the wild fig, ἐρινεός, of the plain of Troy (see [[the-fig-tree]]). ῥοιή, the pomegranate, is named only in these verses and in their return in the underworld (*Odyssey* 11.589); the pear, the apple and the fig come back in Laertes' orchard (*Odyssey* 24.246–247, 24.340–341).

What makes the orchard a wonder is the next three verses: “Of these the fruit never perishes or fails, / in winter or in summer, lasting all the year; but always / the West Wind, blowing, brings some to growth and ripens others” (*Odyssey* 7.117–119; see [[zephyrus]]). To χείματος οὐδὲ θέρευς, “in winter or in summer”, ἐπετήσιος, “lasting all the year”, is added in agreement with καρπός, “the fruit” (Merry on 7.118); the translation keeps the word apart from ἐπηετανός, “unfailing” (see its table of key words). Ζεφυρίη is a feminine adjective used as a noun, the West Wind (Merry on 7.119). The wind that blows always does at once the work of spring and of autumn, τὰ μὲν φύει, ἄλλα δὲ πέσσει. The Iliad's leaves have the same turn of words with the other outcome: the wind scatters some leaves on the ground, and the forest, flourishing, puts forth others (φύλλα τὰ μέν τʼ ἄνεμος χαμάδις χέει, ἄλλα δέ θʼ ὕλη / τηλεθόωσα φύει, *Iliad* 6.147–148; see [[the-generations-of-leaves]]). In Alcinous' orchard nothing falls: “Pear upon pear grows old, apple upon apple, / and cluster upon cluster, fig upon fig” (*Odyssey* 7.120–121). γηράσκει, the verb of men growing old (γηράσκοντα, *Iliad* 2.663), is said here of the ripening fruit. σῦκον, the fig itself, is found only in this verse; μῆλον is the apple, as in the blossoms of the fruit trees that the boar of Calydon throws down (ἄνθεσι μήλων, *Iliad* 9.542), though more often in Homer the word is a sheep. Merry finds σταφυλή, the cluster of grapes, unexpected in the orchard, since the vines belong to the vineyard that comes next (on 7.122; see [[vine]]).

**The vineyard.** Merry divides the whole into three scenes, each introduced by ἔνθα δέ, “and there”: the fruit garden (*Odyssey* 7.114–121), the vineyard (7.122–126) and the garden beds (7.127–128) (on 7.122). “And there his fruitful vineyard is rooted, / one part of which, a warm spot on level ground, / is dried by the sun, while other grapes they are gathering, / and others they are treading; and in front are unripe grapes / shedding their bloom, and others are faintly turning dark” (*Odyssey* 7.122–126; see [[helios]]). ἀλωή of vines is “vineyard”, as on the Shield (*Iliad* 18.561; see the translation's table of key words). ἐρρίζωται, “is rooted”, the scholia gloss as “is planted”; Merry gives it the full sense of ῥιζόω, “fix firmly”, as Poseidon will turn the Phaeacian ship to stone and root it fast beneath (*Odyssey* 13.163, a plain rendering), and thinks it may point to the deep foundations of the wine-press, or to a trench around the vineyard such as the Shield's (on 7.122; *Iliad* 18.564).

The vineyard too is three scenes, a background, a center and a foreground, though the poet spares the words that would mark them and leaves the foreground to πάροιθε, “in front” (Merry on 7.123). In the background the ripe grapes: some are left on the vines to dry in the sun for raisin wine, *vinum passum*, while others are gathered; Merry finds this more in keeping with the scene than bunches already cut and hung up to dry. θειλόπεδον, “a warm spot”, stands as the subject of τέρσεται, “is dried”, where the grapes are meant (on 7.123); the variant εἱλόπεδον is printed by Bekker and Nauck (Merry's critical note; see the translation's note on 7.123–126). In the center the treading: in ἄλλας δὲ τραπέουσι, “and others they are treading”, a verb akin to ἀτραπός, “path”, and to English “tramp”, the poet presents the whole work of the wine-press, the full baskets emptied into the vat, the treading with mirth and song, the drawing off of the purple juice (Merry on 7.125). In front are rows of vines whose grapes are still unripe, ὄμφακες, some having just shed their blossom, others ὑποπερκάζουσιν, “faintly turning dark” with the color of the ripening grape, from περκνός, “dusky” (Merry on 7.125, 7.126). “The marvel both in the garden and the vineyard,” Merry writes, “is that the various stages of growth and maturity are all going on together” (on 7.126). The vineyard of the Shield shows one moment of the year, the gathering, with the boy singing the Linus song (*Iliad* 18.561–572; see [[the-vintage]]); the vineyard of Alcinous shows the whole of it at once.

**The beds and the springs.** “And there, by the last row, are trim garden beds / of every kind, growing, shining unfailingly” (*Odyssey* 7.127–128). The beds border the vineyard along its outermost row of vines. πρασιαί are properly leek beds, from πράσον, “leek”, and the scholia explain κοσμηταὶ πρασιαί as laid-out vegetable beds; Merry sees no flower garden in them, and no trace anywhere in Homer of the growing of flowers, but a kitchen garden, whose fresh green is γανόωσαι, “shining” (on 7.127). ἐπηετανόν, “unfailingly”, is the word of the unfailing store at which the Phaeacian leaders feast in the hall (*Odyssey* 7.99). The πρασιή comes back once, in Laertes' garden (*Odyssey* 24.247).

“And within are two springs: one scatters its water through all the garden, / and the other, on the other side, runs under the threshold of the court / toward the high house; from this spring the citizens drew water” (*Odyssey* 7.129–131; see [[the-city-of-the-phaeacians]]). The κῆπος here is the enclosure of the beds (Merry on 7.129). σκίδναται is “is led in rills” (Merry on 7.130), the work that the Iliad pictures in the simile of the man who leads a channel from a spring among his plants and gardens (*Iliad* 21.257–262; see [[the-channel-and-the-gardener]]). ἑτέρωθεν sets the springs opposite each other, not far apart; the second was carried under the entrance of the court and came out, perhaps in a basin, in the middle of the court, but ὅθεν refers to the spring itself, and the townspeople drew their water at its head, or at least before it reached the threshold (Merry on 7.130). ὅθεν ὑδρεύοντο πολῖται, “from this spring the citizens drew water”, is said again of the built fountain near the town of Ithaca (*Odyssey* 17.206, a plain rendering; see [[the-grove-of-athena]]).

**The gifts of the gods.** τοῖʼ ἄρʼ ἐν Ἀλκινόοιο θεῶν ἔσαν ἀγλαὰ δῶρα, “Such were the splendid gifts of the gods at the house of Alcinous” (*Odyssey* 7.132; see [[splendid]] and [[doron]]). The verse closes the whole description, house and garden together, which began with the gleam “as of the sun or of the moon” (*Odyssey* 7.84–132; see [[helios]]). In the Iliad ἀγλαὰ δῶρα of the gods are the immortal horses and the armor that the gods gave to Peleus (*Iliad* 16.381, 18.84; 24.534).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The two verses of the trees return once, in the underworld, where Odysseus sees Tantalus standing in a lake that drains away whenever he stoops to drink: trees with high foliage pour down their fruit over his head, pears and pomegranates and apple trees with splendid fruit, and sweet figs and flourishing olives, and whenever the old man reaches for them, the wind tosses them away to the shadowy clouds (*Odyssey* 11.582–592, a plain rendering, with 11.589–590 = 7.115–116). The same fruit that never fails in the garden of Alcinous is never reached in Hades.

In the land of the Cyclopes wheat, barley and vines grow unsown and unplowed, and the rain of Zeus makes them grow (*Odyssey* 9.108–111, a plain rendering; see [[cyclopes]]); in the land of a noble king the black earth bears wheat and barley and the trees are heavy with fruit (*Odyssey* 19.109–114, a plain rendering). The Elysian plain has the garden's weather: there is no snow there, nor severe winter, nor ever rain, “but always the breezes of the West Wind, blowing shrill, / Oceanus sends up to refresh men” (ἀλλʼ αἰεὶ Ζεφύροιο λιγὺ πνείοντος ἀήτας, *Odyssey* 4.566–568; see [[elysium]]), as in the garden ἀλλὰ μάλʼ αἰεὶ / Ζεφυρίη πνείουσα (*Odyssey* 7.118–119).

The garden the Odyssey sets beside it is Laertes' orchard on Ithaca, where Odysseus finds his old father digging around a plant, in rags, and praises his care: there is not a plant, a fig tree, a vine, an olive, a pear tree or a garden bed in it without tending. He proves who he is by naming the trees his father gave him there as a boy, thirteen pear trees, ten apple trees and forty fig trees, and fifty rows of vines, each to be gathered in its turn, with clusters of every kind on them whenever the seasons of Zeus weigh them down (*Odyssey* 24.221–247, 24.336–344, plain renderings; see [[laertes]]). In the Iliad the orchard is a king's wealth: the Lycians gave Bellerophon a domain “of orchard and plowland”, Sarpedon and Glaucus hold one by the Xanthus, and the Calydonians offered Meleager one of fifty acres, half of it vineyard (*Iliad* 6.194–195, 12.313–314, 9.577–580). Nausicaa names her father's domain and “his flourishing orchard” beside the grove of Athena outside the city (*Odyssey* 6.293), another place than this garden by the house.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod bids the farmer pick all his grapes and bring them home when Orion and Sirius have come into mid-heaven, and expose them to the sun for ten days and ten nights and keep them shaded for five, before drawing the wine into jars (*Works and Days* 609–614); in Merry's reading the grapes of Alcinous dry on the vine (on 7.123).

Merry compares a fragment of Sophocles' *Thyestes* on a vine of Euboea that passes through every stage of growth in a single day: at dawn it puts out its shoots in fair blossom, at midday the unripe grape swells and the cluster bends and darkens, and in the evening the whole crop is cut and the wine mixed (fr. 239 Dindorf, as Merry quotes it, on 7.126; see [[euboea]] and [[sophocles]]).

For the garden in the Latin poets, who made it the type of a rich man's orchard, see [[the-garden-of-alcinous-in-later-writers]].
