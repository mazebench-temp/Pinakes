ἐρινεός is the wild fig, the caprifig, as against the cultivated fig tree, συκέη, of the orchard of Alcinous (*Odyssey* 7.116; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]]). The Iliad has a wild fig tree as a landmark on the plain under the wall of Troy (see [[the-fig-tree]]); the Odyssey's grows on a crag of the sea, over Charybdis.

## In Homer

Circe tells Odysseus of the two crags, the higher with Scylla's cave in it, and the other:

{{quote:dde177df-0390-5831-8d52-4d9e80f2fbb8}}

The tree marks the place of the whirlpool: τῷ δʼ ἐν … τῷ δʼ ὑπό, “On it … and beneath it” (*Odyssey* 12.103–104; see [[charybdis]]). It is the only tree of the strait: the higher crag is bare rock, sheer as if polished, its peak always in cloud (*Odyssey* 12.73–79; see [[scylla]]). When Odysseus comes back to Charybdis alone on the keel and the mast, he sees the tree from below: he springs up to it and clings to it “like a bat”, for “its roots were far below, and its branches hung high out of reach, / long and great, and they overshadowed Charybdis” (*Odyssey* 12.432–436; see [[like-a-bat]]), and he hangs from it all day, until the whirlpool gives back his timbers (*Odyssey* 12.437–441; see [[the-judge-rising-for-supper]]).

## Outside Homer

[[strabo|Strabo]] makes the fig tree a proof that Circe exaggerated: she said that not even the earth-shaker could save Odysseus if he were there when Charybdis sucked down the water, yet he was there, clung to the fig tree like a bat and was saved (*Geography* 1.2.36). [[aristotle|Aristotle]] explains what the wild fig was good for. Its fruit breeds the fig wasps, ψῆνες, which fly into the figs of the cultivated tree and keep them from dropping, and so farmers hang wild figs on their fig trees and plant wild fig trees beside them (*History of Animals* 5.32).
