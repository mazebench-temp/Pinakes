## In Homer

Homer names Taygetus once, with Erymanthus, as a haunt of Artemis the huntress: “And as Artemis of the showering arrows goes over the mountains, / along towering Taygetus or Erymanthus, / delighting in boars and swift deer” (*Odyssey* 6.102–104; see [[artemis]], [[erymanthus]] and [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]). περιμήκετος, “towering”, is said in Homer only of this mountain and of the tall fir on Ida in which Sleep perches (*Iliad* 14.287). Merry pictures the goddess coming down from some peak and ranging along the ridges, κατά (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 6.102).

## Outside Homer

Merry explains the name as the “huge” mountain, from an adjective meaning “great” that he finds in the glosses of Hesychius (on *Odyssey* 6.102 and 4.11). He describes it as a range in the western part of Lacedaemon, running from north to south for nearly seventy miles to the promontory of Taenarus, called Pentadactylus from its five peaks, its sides covered with pine forest; the region about its highest summit, Taletum, was called Theras, “the hunting-grounds” (Pausanias 3.20.4–5, as Merry cites him, on *Odyssey* 6.102; see [[pausanias]]).

Strabo describes it as a high and steep mountain a little above the sea, which joins the foothills of Arcadia on the north, leaving a valley between by which Messenia borders on Laconia; Sparta lies beneath it inland, with Amyclae, where the temple of Apollo is (*Geography* 8.5.1; see [[strabo]], [[sparta]] and [[lacedaemon]]). Laconia is subject to earthquakes, he adds, and some record that peaks of Taygetus broke away; and a great quarry of costly stone had lately been opened in it (*Geography* 8.5.7).

The Homeric *Hymns* to the Dioscuri say that Leda bore Castor and Polydeuces under the peaks of Taygetus (*Hymn* 17.3; *Hymn* 33.4, “under the peak of Taygetus, the great mountain”; see [[castor]] and [[polydeuces]]).
