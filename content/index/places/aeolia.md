## The name

Αἰολίη is the island “of Aeolus”, named for the son of Hippotes who lives on it (see [[aeolus]]). Homer names it twice, both times with νῆσος: Αἰολίην δʼ ἐς νῆσον ἀφικόμεθʼ, “And we came to the island of Aeolia” (*Odyssey* 10.1), and αὖτις ἐπʼ Αἰολίην νῆσον, “back to the Aeolian island” (*Odyssey* 10.55). The islands north of Sicily were later called the islands of Aeolus (see below).

## In Homer

Aeolia is the first land Odysseus reaches after the Cyclops, and the poem describes it before it tells of the welcome: it is πλωτή, “floating”, and all around it runs a wall of bronze, unbreakable, above a sheer rock (*Odyssey* 10.3–4). Aeolus lives on it with his wife and their twelve children, six sons and six daughters married to one another, who feast every day in a house that resounds with the sound of the feast; there is a city too (*Odyssey* 10.5–13). Odysseus stays a month and leaves with the winds bound in an oxhide skin and the West Wind to carry him (*Odyssey* 10.14–27; see [[zephyrus]]); when the companions open the skin within sight of Ithaca, the storm drives the ships back, and from the island he had left as a guest he is sent away as a man “hated by the blessed gods” (*Odyssey* 10.47–76; see [[odysseus-and-aeolus]]). It is one of the two islands of the tale that Odysseus reaches twice; the other is [[aeaea|Aeaea]]. In the summary of his wanderings that he gives Penelope he tells “how he came to Aeolus, who received him with a ready heart / and sent him on his way” (*Odyssey* 23.314–315, a plain rendering).

The poem gives no number of days for the voyage from the land of the Cyclopes, as it does for long voyages, and it makes the West Wind alone carry the ships toward Ithaca, nine days and nights over the open sea. From this W. W. Merry infers that the island lay not far from the Cyclopes and to the west of Ithaca, though he sees “little advantage in seeking an exact geographical position for a place that belongs to the region of fable” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 10.1).

## Outside Homer

In later times, Merry notes, the scene was moved to the Liparaean or Aeolian islands north of Sicily, and the island of Aeolus was identified by some with Lipara, by others with Strongyle; Völcker took it for one of the Aegates off the northwest coast of Sicily, and Merry himself, if it must be placed at all, would put it to the southwest of Sicily (on *Odyssey* 10.1).

Strabo held that Homer took his starting points from history: Aeolus was said to have ruled the islands around Lipara, as inhospitable Cyclopes and Laestrygonians held the country around Aetna and Leontini (*Geography* 1.2.9; see [[strabo]] and [[laestrygonians]]). He names the islands of the Liparaeans, “which some call the islands of Aeolus”, and says of Strongyle, named for its round shape and fiery like Lipara, “here they say Aeolus lived” (*Geography* 6.2.10–11). Polybius, as Strabo reports him, made Aeolus a man who told sailors when to put out in the strait, where the currents made departure hard, and so was called the steward of the winds and held to be a king (*Geography* 1.2.15; see [[polybius]]). Polybius also described the craters of Hiera, the island of Hephaestus between Lipara and Sicily, and said that the wind of the third day could be foretold from their roaring, their flames and their smoke, and that some of the people of Lipara had foretold it without error; so the verse that seems most fabulous, Strabo concludes, which makes Aeolus steward of the winds, was not idly spoken but hinted at the truth (*Geography* 6.2.10). Eratosthenes had answered such readings with a jest: one would find where Odysseus wandered when one found the leatherworker who stitched the skin of the winds (*Geography* 1.2.15).

Pliny says that the islands were called Aeolian “because Aeolus reigned there in the times of Troy”, and that Aeolus reigned in Strongyle, six miles from Lipara toward the sunrise (*Natural History* 3.92, 3.94). In Virgil Juno comes to Aeolia, “the homeland of storm clouds”, where King Aeolus holds the struggling winds in a vast cave beneath the mountains (*Aeneid* 1.50–63), and Vulcan's island rises beside the coast of Sicily and “Aeolian Lipare” (*Aeneid* 8.416–417; see [[virgil]]).
