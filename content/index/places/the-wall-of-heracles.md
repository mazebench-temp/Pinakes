## The wall

{{quote:caf38fb8-3aa5-5b8f-bc78-9b3c5a2e55ac}}

ἀμφίχυτον, “heaped-up”, is from ἀμφί, “around”, and χέω, “pour, heap”: a rampart of earth thrown up, as a grave mound is χυτὴ γαῖα, “heaped earth” (6.464; Walter Leaf, *Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.145; see [[heaped-up]]). Leaf takes μιν, “him”, as Heracles and the monster as the subject of σεύαιτο, “chased” (on 20.148): when the monster came out of the sea and drove Heracles inland, the wall was his shelter. τὸ κῆτος, “the” sea monster, is spoken of as something known, a use of the article that Leaf finds very rare in Homer except with a few nouns, and confined to late passages of the Iliad (on 20.147; see [[sea-beasts]]). θείοιο, “divine”, is said of Heracles here, as of other heroes (see [[divine]] and [[heracles]]).

## In the poem

The poem names the wall only here, when the gods sit apart from the battle. Poseidon leads the gods of the Achaean side to it, “and the other gods, / and they wrapped an unbreakable cloud about their shoulders”, while the Trojans' gods sit on Callicolone (20.149–152; see [[poseidon]], [[the-battle-of-the-gods]] and [[callicolone]]). Its builders were the Trojans themselves and Athena, the goddess who, as she complains in Book 8, often saved Heracles in his labors (8.362–363; see [[athena]]).

It is one of three walls on the plain. Poseidon and Apollo built the wall of Troy for Laomedon (7.452–453; in Book 21 Poseidon says that he built it while Apollo herded the king's cattle, 21.446–449; see [[the-walls-of-troy]]); the Achaeans built theirs to protect their ships, and Poseidon feared it would make men forget his (7.446–453; see [[the-achaean-wall]]); and the Trojans built this one for Heracles, who later sacked their city when Laomedon refused him the horses he had earned (5.638–642, 5.648–651; see [[heracles-sacks-troy]] and [[laomedon]]). The poem does not say what service Heracles had done, or whether it was the killing of the monster.

## Outside Homer

Leaf reports the legend from the A scholia and Apollodorus, after Hellanicus: Poseidon, cheated by Laomedon of his hire for building the walls of Troy, sent a sea monster to ravage the land; Laomedon, obeying an oracle, exposed his daughter Hesione to it and promised his immortal horses to whoever killed it; Heracles killed it under the protection of a wall built for him by Pallas, and Laomedon cheated him by giving him mortal horses. Leaf calls it a version of the legend of Perseus (on 20.145; see [[hesione]] and [[perseus]]). In Apollodorus as it has come down, Apollo sent a pestilence and Poseidon a monster, carried up by a flood, that snatched away the people of the plain; Laomedon fastened Hesione to the rocks by the sea, and Heracles killed the monster for the promise of the mares that Zeus had given for Ganymede, but there is no wall (*Library* 2.5.9; see [[apollodorus]] and [[ganymede-and-the-horses-of-tros]]).
