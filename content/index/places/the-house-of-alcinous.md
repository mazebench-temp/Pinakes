## In Homer

**Easy to know.** Nausicaa describes the house before Odysseus sees it. He is to wait in the grove of Athena until she has reached home, “then go to the city of the Phaeacians and ask / for the house of my father, great-hearted Alcinous” (*Odyssey* 6.298–299; see [[the-grove-of-athena]] and [[nausicaa]]):

{{quote:5f814f8d-209d-5f54-bd62-c749286974cd}}

Once the house and the court have hidden him, he is to go quickly through the hall to the queen, who sits at the hearth in the light of the fire, spinning, with her chair against a pillar and the king's chair leaning against hers (*Odyssey* 6.303–309; see [[hearth]], [[throne]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]).

**The bronze house.** Odysseus stands and ponders before the bronze threshold (*Odyssey* 7.82–83), and the poem tells what he sees:

{{quote:d4c34df1-b430-5d93-8b31-95b27043ac5e}}

The gleam is the gleam of Menelaus' house at Sparta, in the same verse (*Odyssey* 4.45–46), a house that Nitzsch thought rather the more magnificent of the two (as W. W. Merry reports him, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 7.84), and the metals are those of Telemachus' wonder there, “the flash of bronze throughout the echoing halls, / and of gold and amber and silver and ivory” (*Odyssey* 4.72–73; see [[house]], [[bronze]] and [[gold-and-silver]]). Merry takes the bronze walls for the walls of the court, since a coping finishes a wall that stands in the open air, as Odysseus' own court is fitted with a wall and copings (*Odyssey* 17.266–267, a plain rendering); “the inner part” is then the inner end of the court, and the house itself is described only from the chairs on (on 7.86, 7.95; see [[kyanos]], [[door]] and [[threshold]]).

{{quote:5ab6d01f-07b8-5513-99ea-7c351d201fc8}}

The dogs are the work of a god (see [[the-gold-and-silver-dogs]] and [[hephaestus]]). Within, the leaders of the Phaeacians feast, and golden youths light them:

{{quote:34c0c49a-1750-56e4-8daf-b642e86db44c}}

The youths stand on pedestals, βωμοί, and hold the torch with both hands, as μετὰ χερσίν shows (Merry on 7.100; see [[torches]]). Merry takes them, like the dogs, for images: works of imitative art had not yet received names of their own and borrowed the names of what they imitated, as the golden herdsmen on the shield of Achilles do (on 7.91; *Iliad* 18.577; see [[the-shield-of-achilles]]). The household is the last part of the picture:

{{quote:0d80de47-9227-5142-8589-510e0bdbe363}}

The same number of slave women serve in the house of Odysseus (*Odyssey* 22.421; Merry on 7.103; see [[handmaids]], [[mill]], [[loom]] and [[weaving]]).

**The garden.** Outside the court, near the doors, lie an orchard, a vineyard, garden beds and two springs, one of which runs under the threshold of the court toward the house, where the townsfolk draw water (*Odyssey* 7.112–131; see [[the-garden-of-alcinous]]). One verse closes the whole description: “Such were the splendid gifts of the gods at the house of Alcinous” (*Odyssey* 7.132).

**The life of the house.** The leaders of the Phaeacians drink and eat in it, “for they had unfailing store” (*Odyssey* 7.99), and pour their last libation to Hermes before they go home to bed (*Odyssey* 7.136–138; see [[feast]] and [[libation]]). The queen sits at the hearth, and there the suppliant sits in the ashes (*Odyssey* 6.305, 7.153; see [[supplication]]). Guests sleep in the colonnade, and the king and queen in the inner part of the high house (*Odyssey* 7.335–347; see [[bed]]). The next day its colonnades, courts and rooms fill with the Phaeacians gathering for the feast in honor of the stranger (*Odyssey* 8.56–58, a plain rendering), and in the evening the gifts of the Phaeacian leaders are brought to it and set beside the queen (*Odyssey* 8.417–420). There Odysseus tells the tale of his wanderings (*Odyssey* 9–12; see [[the-tale-to-alcinous]]), and Alcinous speaks of it as “my bronze-floored house, / high-roofed” (*Odyssey* 13.4–5, a plain rendering; see [[bronze-floored]]). Odysseus leaves it by stepping over its threshold to go down to the ship (*Odyssey* 13.63).

## Outside Homer

Lucretius, telling how little the body needs, says that nature does not miss it “if there are no golden images of youths through the house / holding fiery torches in their right hands, / so that light may be supplied for nightly feasts” (*De rerum natura* 2.24–26; see [[lucretius]]), the ornament of the hall of Alcinous. Virgil gives Dido's palace fifty serving women within, whose task is to set out the long store in order and to honor the household gods with fire (*Aeneid* 1.703–704; see [[virgil]]); Merry compares the verse with the fifty slave women of Alcinous (on 7.103).
