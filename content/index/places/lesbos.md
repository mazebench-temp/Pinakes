## In Homer

{{quote:8627679b-8fbc-5f47-a53a-fa7aaf0ee33e}}

Lesbos enters the Iliad through its women. When Achilles took the island, “well-built Lesbos” (Λέσβον ἐϋκτιμένην), Agamemnon chose seven women from the spoil for himself, women “skilled in flawless handiwork” who “surpassed the tribes of women in beauty” (9.128–130). He offers them to Achilles first among the women, with Briseis (9.131), and Odysseus repeats the offer, turning it to Achilles: “when you yourself took well-built Lesbos” (9.270–272; see [[the-gifts-of-agamemnon]] and [[captive-women]]). In Book 19 the seven women are led out of Agamemnon's hut with the other gifts, and Briseis is the eighth (19.243–246). Λεσβίδες, “women of Lesbos”, is the only name the poem gives them. On the night of the embassy Achilles himself sleeps beside a woman of the island, “whom he had brought from Lesbos”, Diomede, daughter of Phorbas (9.664–665; see [[diomede]]).

The sack of Lesbos is one of the raids by sea of which Achilles boasts, “Twelve cities of men I have sacked with my ships” (9.328; see [[achilles-raids]]). In the last book he tells Priam that he was once first in wealth and in sons in all the land bounded by Lesbos, “seat of Macar”, by Phrygia and by the boundless Hellespont (24.543–546). In the Odyssey Nestor tells how Menelaus caught up with him and Diomedes on Lesbos on the way home, as they debated whether to sail outside Chios or inside it (Od. 3.165–175). Menelaus remembers, and Telemachus repeats it to Penelope, how Odysseus once rose up in a contest “in well-built Lesbos” and threw Philomeleides in wrestling (Od. 4.341–344 = 17.132–135).

## Outside Homer

The *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* (3.37) names “holy Lesbos, seat of Macar son of Aeolus” among the places Leto passed in her wanderings. Strabo, explaining Achilles' raids around the Troad, quotes 9.328–329 and 9.129 for the sack of the island (13.1.7), and calls Lesbos almost the mother city of the Aeolian cities (13.2.1). Its largest city was Mytilene, home of Pittacus, one of the Seven Sages, and of the poets Alcaeus and Sappho; Arion, the singer to the lyre, came from Methymna, Terpander was said to be of the same island, and Theophrastus came from Eresus (13.2.2–4). Beauty contests among the women of Lesbos are known from a poem of Alcaeus, which speaks of Lesbian women going to and fro at a sanctuary, “being judged for their beauty” (fr. 130b Voigt).
