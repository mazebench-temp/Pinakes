Ithaca comes first among the places of the [[Cephallenians]], with its mountain, [[Neritum]]. Book 2 names it once before: Odysseus' herald is "Eurybates of Ithaca" (Ἰθακήσιος, 2.184; see [[eurybates-of-ithaca]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Odyssey describes the island more fully. Odysseus himself calls it "clear to see", with its mountain Neritum "with quivering leaves"; it lies "low, farthest out in the sea toward the dark", while the other islands lie toward the dawn; it is "rugged, but a good nurse of young men" (Od. 9.21–27). Athena says it is rough and no place for horses, but not poor: it has grain and wine, rain and dew, pasture for goats and cattle, and timber (Od. 13.242–247).

## Outside Homer

Since antiquity Homer's Ithaca has been identified with the island that still bears the name, Ithaki. The identification has been challenged, because the Odyssey's description of Ithaca lying "farthest toward the dark", that is, the west, fits Ithaki poorly. Wilhelm Dörpfeld argued for Leucas, and Robert Bittlestone, James Diggle and John Underhill for the Paliki peninsula of Cephallenia (*Odysseus Unbound*, 2005). Neither view has won general assent. In the cave at Polis Bay on Ithaki, excavators found bronze tripods of the ninth and eighth centuries BC and a Hellenistic dedication to Odysseus, evidence of a cult of the hero on the island.
