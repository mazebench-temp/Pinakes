Ithaca comes first among the places of the [[Cephallenians]], with its mountain, [[Neritum]]. Earlier in the same book of the Iliad, Odysseus' herald is “Eurybates of Ithaca” (Ἰθακήσιος, 2.184; see [[eurybates-of-ithaca]]). In the Odyssey its people are addressed as Ἰθακήσιοι, “men of Ithaca” (see [[ithacans]]).

## Descriptions

κραναή, “craggy”, is its epithet in both poems: Helen points out Odysseus as the man raised “in the land of Ithaca, craggy though it is” (3.201), and the suitors are the men “who rule over craggy Ithaca” (*Odyssey* 1.247; see [[craggy]]). The Odyssey also calls it ἀμφίαλος, “sea-girt” (*Odyssey* 1.386, 2.293, 21.252; see [[sea-girt]]), and εὐδείελος, “clear-seen” (*Odyssey* 2.167, 9.21, and four times more), which the scholia also explain from δείελος, “evening” (21.232), as “lying toward the west”, while Merry prefers “conspicuous”, “clear to see” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 2.167; see [[clear-seen]] and the translation's note on *Odyssey* 2.167).

The Odyssey describes the island more fully. Odysseus himself calls it “clear-seen”, with its mountain, “Neritum with its quivering leaves”; it “lies low in the sea, farthest out of all / toward the darkness, while the others lie apart toward the dawn and the sun”; it is “rugged, but a good nurse of young men” (*Odyssey* 9.21–27; see [[neritum]] and [[rugged]]). Telemachus, refusing Menelaus' gift of horses, says that it has no wide courses for running and no meadow: “it is a goat-pasturing land, and lovelier than a horse-pasturing one” (*Odyssey* 4.601–608; see [[goats]]). Athena says it is rough and no place for horses, but not poor: it has grain and wine, rain and dew, pasture for goats and cattle, and timber (*Odyssey* 13.242–247). Its places include the harbor of Rheithron under Mount Neion (*Odyssey* 1.186; see [[rheithron]] and [[neion]]), the harbor of Phorcys with the cave of the Naiads (*Odyssey* 13.96–112; see [[the-harbor-of-phorcys]] and [[the-cave-of-the-naiads]]), and, where the swineherd's herd feeds, the Rock of Corax and the spring Arethusa (*Odyssey* 13.408; see [[the-rock-of-corax]] and [[arethusa]]).

## Outside Homer

The *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* sees the island from the sea: as the Cretan ship that the god is driving sails up the coast of the Peloponnese, “the steep mountain of Ithaca” appears from under the clouds, with Dulichium, Same and wooded Zacynthus (*Hymn to Apollo* 428–429). Aristotle's lost *Constitution of the Ithacans* is known from a few notices: Cephalus, from whom the Cephallenians were named, begot Arceisius, the grandfather of Odysseus, on a she-bear at an oracle's bidding (fr. 509–510 Gigon), and Telemachus married Nausicaa (fr. 512 Gigon); Gigon also prints with the fragments Plutarch's account of the Ithacan families, in which Neoptolemus, called in to arbitrate after the killing of the suitors, sent Odysseus into exile from Cephallenia, Zacynthus and Ithaca, and the families of the Koliadai and the Boukoloi traced their descent to Eumaeus and Philoetius (fr. 511 Gigon; see [[aristotle]] and [[plutarch]]). Aristotle notes too that hares brought to Ithaca and let go cannot live there, but are found dead, turned toward the sea where they were landed (*History of Animals* 8.28). Pliny gives the island a circuit of twenty-five miles, with Mount Neritus in it (*Natural History* 4.55; see [[neritum]]). In Virgil, Aeneas' men sail past “the rocks of Ithaca, the realm of Laertes”, and curse “the land that nursed cruel Ulysses” (*effugimus scopulos Ithacae, Laertia regna, / et terram altricem saevi exsecramur Ulixi*, *Aeneid* 3.272–273); Ovid calls it “the realm of deceitful Ulysses” (*regnum fallacis Ulixis*, *Metamorphoses* 13.712; see [[virgil]] and [[ovid]]). Cicero, praising love of country, says that the wisest of men preferred to immortality “that Ithaca of his, fixed like a little nest on the roughest of rocks” (*Ithacam illam in asperrimis saxulis tamquam nidulum adfixam*, *On the Orator* 1.196; see [[cicero]]).

Since antiquity Homer's Ithaca has been identified with the island that still bears the name, Ithaki. The identification has been challenged, because the Odyssey's description of Ithaca lying “farthest out of all / toward the darkness”, that is, the west, fits Ithaki poorly. The difficulty is old: Strabo reports interpreters who took χθαμαλή, “low”, to mean “near the mainland”, and πανυπερτάτη πρὸς ζόφον to mean “farthest toward the north” (*Geography* 10.2.12; see [[strabo]]), and Merry, who rejects both, held that Homer meant the real Ithaca but knew it only by report, and so placed it wrongly among the islands of the group (Merry, Appendix on Ithaca). Wilhelm Dörpfeld argued for Leucas, and Robert Bittlestone, James Diggle and John Underhill for the Paliki peninsula of Cephallenia (*Odysseus Unbound*, 2005). Neither view has won general assent. In the cave at Polis Bay on Ithaki, excavators found bronze tripods of the ninth and eighth centuries BC and a Hellenistic dedication to Odysseus, evidence of a cult of the hero on the island.
