Ithaca comes first among the places of the [[Cephallenians]], with its mountain, [[Neritum]]. Earlier in the same book of the Iliad, Odysseus' herald is “Eurybates of Ithaca” (Ἰθακήσιος, 2.184; see [[eurybates-of-ithaca]]). In the Odyssey its people are addressed as Ἰθακήσιοι, “men of Ithaca” (see [[ithacans]]).

## Descriptions

κραναή, “craggy”, is its epithet in both poems: Helen points out Odysseus as the man raised “in the land of Ithaca, craggy though it is” (3.201), and the suitors are the men “who rule over craggy Ithaca” (*Odyssey* 1.247; see [[craggy]]). The Odyssey also calls it ἀμφίαλος, “sea-girt” (*Odyssey* 1.386, 2.293, 21.252; see [[sea-girt]]), and εὐδείελος, “clear-seen” (*Odyssey* 2.167, 9.21, and four times more), a rendering the translation holds as provisional, since δείελος, “evening” (21.232), suggests another sense, “lying toward the west” (see [[clear-seen]] and the translation's note on *Odyssey* 2.167).

The Odyssey describes the island more fully. Odysseus himself calls it “clear-seen”, with its mountain Neritum “with quivering leaves”; it lies “low, farthest out in the sea toward the dark”, while the other islands lie toward the dawn; it is “rugged, but a good nurse of young men” (*Odyssey* 9.21–27). Athena says it is rough and no place for horses, but not poor: it has grain and wine, rain and dew, pasture for goats and cattle, and timber (*Odyssey* 13.242–247). Its places include the harbor of Rheithron under Mount Neion (*Odyssey* 1.186; see [[rheithron]] and [[neion]]) and the harbor of Phorcys with the cave of the nymphs (*Odyssey* 13.96–112; see [[harbor]]).

## Outside Homer

Since antiquity Homer's Ithaca has been identified with the island that still bears the name, Ithaki. The identification has been challenged, because the Odyssey's description of Ithaca lying "farthest toward the dark", that is, the west, fits Ithaki poorly. Wilhelm Dörpfeld argued for Leucas, and Robert Bittlestone, James Diggle and John Underhill for the Paliki peninsula of Cephallenia (*Odysseus Unbound*, 2005). Neither view has won general assent. In the cave at Polis Bay on Ithaki, excavators found bronze tripods of the ninth and eighth centuries BC and a Hellenistic dedication to Odysseus, evidence of a cult of the hero on the island.
