## The name

Αἰαίη is both the name of the island, νῆσος Αἰαίη, and an adjective of the goddess who lives on it, “Aeaean”, “of Aeaea”. In the preface to his tale Odysseus calls Circe Αἰαίη δολόεσσα, “the wily one of Aeaea” (*Odyssey* 9.32; see [[circe]] and [[wily]]), and Merry notes that the island where Circe lived is called Αἰαίη in *Odyssey* 10 (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 9.32). The island has the name when the ships reach it, Αἰαίην δʼ ἐς νῆσον ἀφικόμεθʼ, “we came to the island of Aeaea” (*Odyssey* 10.135, a plain rendering), when Teiresias foretells the return to it, and when the ship comes back from the underworld (*Odyssey* 11.70, 12.3); Circe is Κίρκη Αἰαίη, “Aeaean Circe”, in the verses on the warnings of Teiresias and of Circe (*Odyssey* 12.268, 12.273).

## In Homer

Circe lives there, “a dread goddess who speaks with human voice”, sister of Aeetes and daughter of the Sun and of Perse, child of Oceanus (*Odyssey* 10.135–139, a plain rendering; see [[helios]]). From a rugged lookout Odysseus sees an island ringed by the boundless sea, which “lies low”, with smoke rising in its midst through thick brush and woods (*Odyssey* 10.194–197, a plain rendering): χθαμαλή, the word he uses of Ithaca (*Odyssey* 9.25), and Merry, who takes χθαμαλή of Ithaca in its plain sense, “low”, adduces this passage among its uses (Merry, Appendix on Ithaca; see [[ithaca]]). The companions stay a full year, feasting on meat and sweet wine (*Odyssey* 10.467–470, a plain rendering). When they return from the underworld the poem places the island “where are the house and dancing floors of early-born Dawn, and the risings of the Sun” (*Odyssey* 12.3–4, a plain rendering; see [[eos]]), and from there Circe sends them on with a fair wind (*Odyssey* 12.143–150).

## Outside Homer

Strabo held that Homer, who knew of the Colchians and of Jason's voyage to Aea, and the stories of Circe and Medea and their skill with drugs, made the two kinswomen though they lived far apart, one in the recess of the Black Sea and the other in Italy, and moved both out into the Ocean, so that Aeaea was suggested by Aea (*Geography* 1.2.10). Homer, he says, agrees with history when he names Aeetes, Jason and the Argo, forms Aeaea after Aea, and makes his sorceress Circe like Medea, “own sister of baleful Aeetes”, and adds the myth when he moves the wanderings out into the Ocean (*Geography* 1.2.40, quoting *Odyssey* 10.137; see [[strabo]] and [[medea]]). He also describes the Circaeum on the coast of Latium, a mountain that stands like an island between the sea and the marshes, rich in roots, people say, as if to suit the myth of Circe, with a small town, a temple of Circe and an altar of Athena, where a cup of Odysseus was shown (*Geography* 5.3.6). In Virgil, Helenus counts “the island of Aeaean Circe”, “Aeaeaeque insula Circae”, among the places Aeneas must pass before he can found his city (*Aeneid* 3.385–387; see [[virgil]]).
