## The name

Αἰαίη is both the name of the island, νῆσος Αἰαίη, and an adjective of the goddess who lives on it, “Aeaean”, “of Aeaea”. In the preface to his tale Odysseus calls Circe Αἰαίη δολόεσσα, “the wily one of Aeaea” (*Odyssey* 9.32; see [[circe]] and [[wily]]), and Merry notes that the island where Circe lived is called Αἰαίη in *Odyssey* 10 (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 9.32). The island has the name when the ships reach it, Αἰαίην δʼ ἐς νῆσον ἀφικόμεθʼ, “And we came to the island of Aeaea” (*Odyssey* 10.135), when the soul of Elpenor foretells the return to it, and when the ship comes back from the underworld (*Odyssey* 11.70, 12.3); and in the verses on the warnings of Teiresias and of Circe she is “Circe of Aeaea”, Κίρκης τʼ Αἰαίης (*Odyssey* 12.268, 12.273), after which the name is not heard again. Nitzsch, as Merry reports, connected the word with αἶα, “land”, as if it pointed to some vague and distant land (on *Odyssey* 10.135).

## In Homer

Aeaea is Circe's island, wooded and low, ringed by the sea, with a harbor where ships can lie, and her house of polished stone stands in its glens (*Odyssey* 10.135–141, 10.194–197, 10.210–211, 10.308; see [[circe]]). Odysseus comes there twice. He comes first with his one remaining ship after the Laestrygonians, and stays a full year (*Odyssey* 10.133–574; see [[odysseus-and-circe]]); he comes back from the house of Hades, as the soul of Elpenor foretells, to bury Elpenor and to hear Circe's warnings for the rest of the voyage, and from there she sends him on with a fair wind (*Odyssey* 11.69–72, 12.1–150; see the Book 12 note).

## Where it lies

The poem gives no number of days for the voyage from the Laestrygonians to Aeaea, and from this W. W. Merry infers that the two were not far apart, and that Circe's island belongs with the wonders of the West (on *Odyssey* 10.135); from it the North Wind carries the ship across Oceanus to the dead (*Odyssey* 10.507–508). But when the ship comes back from the dead, the poem places the island “where are the house of early-born Dawn / and her dancing floors, and the risings of the Sun” (*Odyssey* 12.3–4; see [[eos]] and [[helios]]). Merry calls this a real contradiction, which the ancient commentators evaded rather than explained; his own answer is that Homer regards the extreme West and the extreme East as almost one point, as in the short nights of the Laestrygonians (on *Odyssey* 12.3; see the Book 12 note and [[telepylus]]). On *Odyssey* 10.135 he reports a mythological explanation, that the Odyssey has two forms of the story of Circe, one that connects her with the East, through her brother Aeetes and the Argonauts' Colchis, whose ancient name was Aea, and one that connects her with the West (see [[aeetes]] and [[medea]]).

## Outside Homer

Strabo held that Homer, who knew of the Colchians and of Jason's voyage to Aea, and the stories of Circe and Medea and their skill with drugs, made the two kinswomen though they lived far apart, one in the recess of the Black Sea and the other in Italy, and moved both out into the Ocean, so that Aeaea was suggested by Aea (*Geography* 1.2.10). Homer, he says, agrees with history when he names Aeetes, Jason and the Argo, forms Aeaea after Aea, and makes his sorceress Circe like Medea, “own sister of baleful Aeetes”, and adds the myth when he moves the wanderings out into the Ocean (*Geography* 1.2.40, quoting *Odyssey* 10.137; see [[strabo]] and [[medea]]). Herodotus names Aea in Colchis as the place to which the Greeks sailed in a long ship before they carried off Medea, and to which Jason and the Argonauts were sailing for the fleece (*Histories* 1.2.2, 7.193.2; see [[herodotus]] and [[jason]]).

The West kept Circe too. In verses near the end of the *Theogony* she bears to Odysseus Agrius and Latinus, who rule “far away, in a recess of the holy islands”, over all the famous Tyrsenians (Hesiod, *Theogony* 1011–1016; see [[hesiod]]); Merry thinks the passage spurious, but useful as evidence that Circe was early transferred to Italy (on *Odyssey* 10.135). Strabo describes the Circaeum on the coast of Latium, a mountain that stands like an island between the sea and the marshes, rich in roots, people say, as if to suit the myth of Circe, with a small town, a temple of Circe and an altar of Athena, where a cup of Odysseus was shown (*Geography* 5.3.6). Pliny says that Circeii was once an island surrounded by the boundless sea, as Homer is believed to say, and is now surrounded by a plain; Theophrastus, in a book written in the archonship of Nicodorus, gave the island's measure as eighty stadia, and whatever land now joins it to Italy, Pliny concludes, has been added since (*Natural History* 3.57–58). In Virgil, Helenus counts “the island of Aeaean Circe”, “Aeaeaeque insula Circae”, among the places Aeneas must pass before he can found his city (*Aeneid* 3.385–387; see [[virgil]]).
