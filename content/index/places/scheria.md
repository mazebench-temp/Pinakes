## The name

Merry takes Scheria and Hypereia, the Phaeacians' earlier home (*Odyssey* 6.4), for merely topographical descriptions, the “Coast” and the “Highlands” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 5.34). He reports two other accounts. A scholium tells that the island was first called Drepane, “Sickle”, because the sickle with which Cronus was mutilated was kept there, and was named Scheria afterwards because Poseidon, at Demeter's request, consented to stop (σχεῖν) a flood; and Mure took the name for a Phoenician word for a “busy port”, a fancy in Merry's judgment.

## In Homer

Homer names Scheria four times, all in the Odyssey. Zeus names it as the goal of Odysseus' raft, “deep-soiled Scheria, / the land of the Phaeacians” (*Odyssey* 5.34–35; see [[deep-soiled]]). Nausithous, leading the Phaeacians away from Hypereia, where the Cyclopes harmed them, settled them in Scheria, ἑκὰς ἀνδρῶν ἀλφηστάων, “far from toiling men”, drove a wall round the city, built houses and temples of the gods, and divided the fields (*Odyssey* 6.4–10; see [[cyclopes]] and [[toiling-men]]). Athena leaves “lovely Scheria”, Σχερίην ἐρατεινήν, for Marathon and Athens (*Odyssey* 7.79–80; see [[lovely]], [[marathon]] and [[athens]]), and Poseidon goes “to Scheria, where the Phaeacians live” to turn their ship to stone in sight of the city (*Odyssey* 13.159–164). Otherwise the poem calls it the land of the Phaeacians (see [[phaeacians]]).

It is a place apart. Nausicaa says of her people, “we live far off in the sea of many waves, the farthest, and no other mortal has dealings with us” (*Odyssey* 6.204–205, a plain rendering). The poem fixes it only by distance: Odysseus sails seventeen days from Calypso's island, steering with the Bear on his left hand, before its mountains appear (*Odyssey* 5.270–280; see [[ogygia]] and [[the-bear]]), and the Phaeacians' ship carries him from it to Ithaca in one night, coming in to land as the morning star rises (*Odyssey* 13.78–95). Nausithous had foretold that Poseidon would one day wreck a Phaeacian ship on its way back from an escort and cover the city with a great mountain (*Odyssey* 8.564–569); when the ship is turned to stone, Alcinous recalls the prophecy and bids his people give up their escorts and sacrifice twelve bulls to Poseidon, in the hope that he may pity them and not hide the city under the mountain (*Odyssey* 13.171–187; see [[alcinous]] and [[poseidon]]).

## Outside Homer

Later Greeks took Scheria for Corcyra. Strabo, telling how Archias sailed from Corinth to found Syracuse, says that he left Chersicrates, of the line of the Heraclidae, with part of his force to settle “the island now called Corcyra, formerly Scheria”, and that Chersicrates drove out the Liburnians who held it (*Geography* 6.2.4; see [[strabo]]). Callimachus called Gaudos the island of Calypso and Corcyra Scheria. Apollodorus of Athens, following Eratosthenes, blamed him for it, as a scholar who should have kept to Homer's design of setting the wanderings in the Ocean; Strabo acquits him, since the wandering cannot plausibly be called a mere fiction and no other places have been shown with better claims (*Geography* 7.3.6, 1.2.37; see [[callimachus]] and [[ogygia]]). Thucydides, as Merry reports him, speaks of the Phaeacians' earlier settlement of Corcyra, a people famed for ships, and of a precinct of Alcinous at Corcyra (Thucydides 1.25, 3.70; see [[thucydides]]). Merry finds the hint for the identification in Odysseus' false tale to Penelope, where, after his stay with the Phaeacians, Odysseus is heard of among the Thesprotians, on the mainland opposite (*Odyssey* 19.270–282; Merry on *Odyssey* 5.34).

Merry himself thinks that the attempt to fix the place “melts into romance”. He notes the likeness of the Phaeacians' land to the Elysian plain: the West Wind blows always in both (*Odyssey* 4.567, 7.118–119), and Rhadamanthus, who lives in Elysium, was carried to Euboea by Phaeacian ships (*Odyssey* 4.564, 7.321–324); a scholiast on Euripides' *Hippolytus* (742), he reports, puts the Elysian plain and the land of the Phaeacians in one land of wonders (see [[elysium]] and [[rhadamanthus]]). Nitzsch, he reports, saw in Phaeacia a trace of Italian scenery idealized. Merry ends with the judgment of Eratosthenes, as Strabo reports it, that Homer neither knew such places nor wished to set the wandering in known places (*Geography* 1.2.14). Strabo himself, against Polybius, read Nausicaa's verses on her people, who live “far off in the sea of many waves, the farthest”, with those on Ogygia, as plain signs that the poet set these places in the Atlantic, as fictions (*Geography* 1.2.18).
