## Etymology

Κρήτη (Krētē) is not a Greek word, and its origin is unknown. Egyptian texts of the second millennium BC name a land Keftiu, which most scholars take to be Crete.

## Descriptions

{{quote:48248bc9-f36a-5050-a76f-1920c2fbb93a}}

ἑκατόμπολις = ἑκατόν ("hundred") + πόλις ("city"): "of the hundred cities". Homer uses the word only of Crete, and only here. The Catalogue names seven of the cities, [[Cnossus]], [[Gortyn]], [[Lyctus]], [[miletus-crete|Miletus]], [[Lycastus]], [[Phaestus]] and [[Rhytium]], and leaves the rest to the round number. Eighty ships put Idomeneus among the greatest leaders of the army: only Agamemnon, with a hundred, and Nestor, with ninety, bring more, and Diomedes also brings eighty.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Odyssey counts ninety cities, not a hundred, and many peoples and languages: Achaeans, Eteocretans ("true Cretans"), Cydonians, Dorians and Pelasgians. Chief among the cities is Cnossus, where Minos ruled, "the companion of great Zeus" (*Odyssey* 19.172–179). Idomeneus is Minos' grandson (*Iliad* 13.449–453). Odysseus makes himself a Cretan in the false stories he tells on Ithaca (*Odyssey* 13.256–286, 14.199–359, 19.165–202).

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod, Rhea bears Zeus in Crete and hides him in a cave there (*Theogony* 477–484). Crete was the center of the Bronze Age civilization that Arthur Evans named Minoan, after Minos. Its palaces at Knossos, Phaistos and elsewhere were first built early in the second millennium BC. The Linear B tablets found at Knossos are written in Greek, and show that Greek-speaking rulers held the palace in its last phase.
