## The name

Τηλέπυλον Λαιστρυγονίην, “Telepylus of the Laestrygonians” (*Odyssey* 10.82), is in apposition to Λάμου αἰπὺ πτολίεθρον, “the steep citadel of Lamus” (*Odyssey* 10.81; see [[lamus]]); the summary of the wanderings repeats the name and the adjective (*Odyssey* 23.318). W. W. Merry takes Τηλέπυλος for the town's own name and Λαιστρυγονίη for its geographical epithet. Its meaning depends, he says, on that of τηλύγετος, whose first part he takes to mean “big”, so that Τηλέπυλος is “big-gated”, a town with gates on the scale of a people “not like men, but like the Giants” and wide enough for the herds going in and out to pass abreast; he rejects the modern picture of a town with a straight street through it and gates “far apart” at either end (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 10.81; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 10.81–86). Some commentators, he reports, took Lamus for the name of the town, as the city of Ilios is Ἰλίου … πόλιν (*Iliad* 5.642).

## In Homer

Odysseus reaches it on the seventh day after Aeolus drives him away, and describes it before he tells what happened there (*Odyssey* 10.80–86): a land of herdsmen, where a herdsman driving his flock in hails another, who answers as he drives his herd out, and where a man who took no sleep could earn two wages, “for the paths of night and of day are close together”. It has a famous harbor shut in by sheer cliffs, a road down which wagons bring wood from the high mountains, a spring called Artacia outside the city, the king's high-roofed house and a place of assembly (*Odyssey* 10.87–114; see [[harbor]], [[artacia]] and [[antiphates-the-laestrygonian]]). There eleven of Odysseus' twelve ships are destroyed (*Odyssey* 10.118–132; see [[laestrygonians]] and [[odysseus-among-the-laestrygonians]]).

## Outside Homer

The Romans placed the city on the coast of Latium. Cicero, writing from his villa at Formiae, calls the town “this Telepylus of the Laestrygonians, Formiae I mean”, full of grumbling and of anger at Pompey (*Letters to Atticus* 2.13.2; see [[cicero]]), and Pliny calls Formiae “the ancient seat of the Laestrygonians” (*Natural History* 3.59). Horace makes Lamus the first to hold the walls of Formiae and the ancestor of the Aelii Lamiae (*Odes* 3.17.1–8; see [[horace]]), and Ovid calls the place “the ancient city of Lamus the Laestrygonian” (*Metamorphoses* 14.233; see [[ovid]]). Greek writers placed the Laestrygonians in Sicily (see [[laestrygonians]]).

The verses on the herdsmen were read as a marvel of the far north. Crates, as the scholia report him, gave the Laestrygonians short nights, placing them near the head of the constellation of the Dragon, where, in Aratus' verse, the settings and the risings mingle with one another; and Eustathius says that there the day was long and the night short, the reverse of the land of the Cimmerians (Merry on *Odyssey* 10.81). Tacitus says of the farthest part of Britain that the night is bright and short, so that the end and the beginning of daylight are told apart by a slight interval (*Agricola* 12), and Merry, who quotes him, held that the story of the herdsmen points to the short nights and the midnight sun of high latitudes, but that the poet “numbers them among the marvels of the West” (on *Odyssey* 10.81). He compares Hesiod's house of Night, before which Atlas holds up the wide sky, “a place notoriously in the west”: there Night and Day come near and greet each other as they cross the great bronze threshold, one going in as the other goes out, so that the house never holds both (*Theogony* 744–754; see [[hesiod]] and [[atlas]]), though, Merry adds, the rest of Hesiod's description does not agree.
