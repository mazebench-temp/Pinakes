## Etymology

φηγός is the same word as Latin *fāgus* and English *beech*, but in Greek it names a kind of oak. Homer does not describe the tree, beyond calling it “beautiful” (περικαλλής, 5.693) and “high” (ὑψηλή, 7.60).

## In Homer

The tree belongs to Zeus. It is “the beautiful oak of aegis-bearing Zeus” when Sarpedon's companions lay him under it (5.693), and “the high oak of father Zeus, the aegis-bearer” when Athena and Apollo, who have met “beside the oak”, sit in it like vultures to watch the armies (7.22, 7.58–61; see [[vultures]] and [[watching-the-war]]). Homer never tells of worship at it.

It stands where the plain meets the city. Hector reaches “the Scaean gates and the oak” on his way back from the battle (6.237), and the Trojans in flight halt there to wait for one another (11.170). Achilles measures his own absence by it: while he fought, Hector would not come out beyond “the Scaean gates and the oak” (9.352–355; see [[scaean-gates]]). In the last battle Apollo stands “leaning against the oak”, hidden in mist, to keep death from Agenor (21.549).

In Book 5 it is a place of refuge for an ally: there Pelagon draws the spear from Sarpedon's thigh, and Sarpedon faints and revives (5.692–698; see [[sarpedon]] and [[pelagon-of-lycia]]).

## Outside Homer

The oak was Zeus' tree elsewhere too. At Dodona, Odysseus is said to have gone to hear the will of Zeus “from the high-leafed oak” (δρῦς, Od. 14.327–328, 19.296–297; see [[dodona]]). In the story that the priestesses of Dodona told Herodotus, a black dove settled on a φηγός and declared in a human voice that an oracle of Zeus should be founded there (Herodotus 2.55).
