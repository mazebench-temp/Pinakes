## In Homer

Homer names Delos once. Odysseus, praising Nausicaa, remembers a sight he saw there: “Once in Delos, beside the altar of Apollo, such a thing / I saw, a young sapling of a palm tree springing up” (*Odyssey* 6.162–163; see [[apollo]], [[altar]], [[palm]] and [[the-palm-on-delos]]). He had come there with a great following, “on that journey on which I was to have evil sorrows” (*Odyssey* 6.164–165). The poems say nothing else of the island, its people or the birth of its god.

Merry places the visit on the voyage from Aulis to Troy, when the Greeks would have passed through the Cyclades to Delos, then by Icaria to Samos and by Lesbos to Lemnos and the Trojan coast, comparing the route by the islands that Nestor describes for the voyage home (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 6.162; *Odyssey* 3.169–175). He reports Nitzsch's quotation from Voss, that Delos was in Agamemnon's time the regular oracle for seafaring men (on *Odyssey* 6.162). He takes the Ortygia where Artemis killed Orion for Delos itself (on *Odyssey* 5.123; see [[ortygia]]).

## Outside Homer

The Homeric *Hymn to Apollo* tells how the island became holy. Leto bore Artemis in Ortygia and Apollo “in rocky Delos”, leaning against the long mountain and the hill of Cynthus, close by the palm, at the streams of the Inopus (*Hymn to Apollo* 14–18). No land would receive her until she came to Delos and promised the poor and rocky island a temple and hecatombs, and she swore that Apollo's fragrant altar and precinct would be there forever (*Hymn to Apollo* 47–88). When her pains came, she threw her arms around the palm and set her knees on the soft meadow, and the god leapt forth to the light (*Hymn to Apollo* 115–119). Delos is the place he loves best, where the long-robed Ionians gather with their children and wives and delight him with boxing, dance and song when they hold the contest (*Hymn to Apollo* 146–150; see [[leto]] and [[artemis]]). The palm and the laurel of Leto's labor return in Euripides (*Hecuba* 455–461; *Ion* 919–922; *Iphigenia in Tauris* 1097–1102, where the hill of Cynthus, the palm, the laurel and the sacred olive stand together; see [[euripides]]), and Cicero's brother Quintus names the palm that Homer's Ulysses said he saw at Delos among the things that last longer in remembrance than in nature (*On the Laws* 1.2; see [[palm]] and [[cicero-the-palm-of-delos]]).

Strabo describes the island: its city lies on a plain, with the temple of Apollo and the Letoon; above it rises Cynthus, a bare and rugged mountain, and the Inopus, a small river, runs through it, for the island is small. It was honored from the heroic age for the gods' sake, since the myth placed Leto's labor there, and the Cyclades around it made it famous, sending envoys, sacrifices and choruses of maidens to its festivals (*Geography* 10.5.1–2; see [[strabo]]). No one might be buried or burned on Delos; the Delians buried their dead on Rhenaea, an islet four stades away, which was once called Ortygia (*Geography* 10.5.5). Aristotle, in the *Constitution of the Delians*, described an altar of Apollo the Begetter on the island, called the bloodless altar, on which no victim was offered, only wheat, barley and cakes laid on it without fire (fr. 497 Gigon; see [[aristotle]]).
