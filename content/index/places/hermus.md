Ἕρμῳ δινήεντι, “the eddying Hermus” (20.392). Homer names the river once, in Achilles' boast over Iphition, whose father's domain lay by the Gygaean lake between the Hyllus and the Hermus (see [[iphition]], [[otrynteus]], [[gygaean-lake]] and [[hyllus]]). δινήεις, “eddying”, is said of the Xanthus, both the Lycian river and the Trojan (2.877, 14.434), and of rivers in general (8.490; see [[eddying]]). Walter Leaf calls the Hyllus a feeder of “the great Lydian Hermos” (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.392).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod names the Hermus among the rivers that Tethys bore to Oceanus (*Theogony* 343; see [[hesiod]]). Herodotus says that the rivers of the plain before Sardis, the Hyllus among them, rush together into the greatest of them, the Hermus, which flows from the sacred mountain of Mother Dindymene and reaches the sea by the city of Phocaea (1.80.1), and that the Pactolus, bringing gold dust down from Tmolus through the middle of the market place of Sardis, empties into the Hermus, and the Hermus into the sea (5.101.2; see [[herodotus]] and [[tmolus]]). The oracle that warned Croesus of a mule who would be king of the Medes told the Lydian to flee “along the pebbly Hermus” (Herodotus 1.55.2; see [[croesus]]). [[Strabo]] follows Herodotus: the Pactolus and the Hyllus, “now called the Phrygius”, flow into the Hermus, which rises in Mysia on the sacred mountain of Dindymene, runs through the country called the Burnt Land into the territory of Sardis, and reaches the sea near Phocaea (*Geography* 13.4.5). Pliny the Elder makes it rise near Dorylaeum in Phrygia and gather many rivers, among them the Hyllus (*Natural History* 5.119). Aristotle's example of a compound noun of many parts, Ἑρμοκαϊκόξανθος, joins the Hermus with the Caicus and the Xanthus (*Poetics* 21, 1457a34–b1; see [[hermocaicoxanthus]]). The river is the modern Gediz.
