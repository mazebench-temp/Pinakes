## The name

ἀσφοδελὸς λειμών is “the meadow of asphodel”, or “the asphodel meadow”: ἀσφοδελός, accented on the last syllable, is used as an adjective, as the noun ἀσφόδελος is the plant (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.539). The asphodel is a plant that Merry describes as “a kind of squill with a large stalk and long leaves and pale blossoms”. Hesiod names it with the mallow as the food of the poor: fools, he says, “do not know how much more the half is than the whole, nor how great a benefit there is in mallow and asphodel” (*Works and Days* 40–41; see [[hesiod]]). Merry adds that it is still planted on graves in the Greek islands, a custom that shows its ancient connection with the scenery of the underworld, and that the asphodel was planted to give the dead some simple food (on *Odyssey* 11.539). A scholium was unsure whether to read κατʼ ἀσφοδελόν or κατὰ σφοδελόν (Merry's critical note on *Odyssey* 11.539).

## In Homer

The meadow is named three times, always in the same half-verse, κατʼ ἀσφοδελὸν λειμῶνα, “across the meadow of asphodel”, at the end of a line. The soul of Achilles, glad at the news of his son, goes ranging across it with long strides (*Odyssey* 11.538–540; see [[achilles]]); the phantom of Orion herds the wild beasts he killed in life across it, club in hand (*Odyssey* 11.572–575; see [[orion]]); and Hermes leads the souls of the suitors past the streams of Oceanus and the White Rock, the gates of the Sun and the land of dreams, until “they quickly came to the meadow of asphodel, where the souls dwell, the phantoms of the dead” (*Odyssey* 24.11–14, a plain rendering; see [[hermes]], [[the-suitors]] and [[eidolon]]). It is the open ground where the souls go about, as Erebus is the darkness into which they withdraw (see [[erebus]] and [[hades]]).

## Outside Homer

The *Homeric Hymn to Hermes* uses the same words of a meadow on earth: the tracks of the cattle that the infant Hermes stole from Apollo point back “toward the asphodel meadow” where they had grazed (*Hymn to Hermes* 221, 344; see [[hermes]] and [[apollo]]). There the asphodel meadow is a pasture of the living; in the Odyssey it is the meadow of the dead.
