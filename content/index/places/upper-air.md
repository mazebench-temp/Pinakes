αἰθήρ is the clear, shining air of the heights, above the ἀήρ, the mist that lies low over the earth. The difference is plainest in two places. On Ida, Sleep climbs the tallest fir, which “reached up through the mist into the upper air” (διʼ ἠέρος αἰθέρʼ ἵκανεν, 14.288), and over the body of Patroclus the best men fight in a mist (ἠέρι, 17.368) while the rest of both armies fight at their ease “under the upper air”, in the sharp light of the sun (17.370–372; see [[like-blazing-fire]]). Above the upper air is the sky, the οὐρανός: the gleam of the army's bronze goes up “through the upper air to the sky” (2.458; see [[forest-fire]]), and the iron din of the fight over Patroclus goes up “through the barren upper air to the bronze sky” (17.425; see [[sky]] and [[barren-sea]]). When the clouds are lifted, “from the sky the boundless upper air breaks open”, and the peaks and headlands stand out (8.557–558 = 16.299–300; see [[stars-around-the-moon]] and [[the-cloud-lifted-from-the-peak]]); on a clear night the upper air is “windless” (8.556).

It is Zeus' region. He is αἰθέρι ναίων, “who dwells in the upper air” (2.412, 4.166; see [[dwelling-in-the-upper-air]]), and in the division by lot he got “the wide sky, in the upper air and the clouds” (15.192; see [[the-lots-of-the-three-brothers]]). There he hung Hera with anvils on her feet (15.18–21; see [[hera-hung-from-the-sky]]); from there he would have thrown Sleep into the sea (14.258), and from there he defends Hector (15.610). He sends down dew wet with blood from the upper air at the start of a day of slaughter (11.53–55), and a storm cloud comes into the sky “out of the shining upper air” when he spreads a tempest (16.364–365; see [[the-cloud-from-olympus]]).

The things of battle go up to it: the roar of both armies, which “reached the upper air and the rays of Zeus” (13.837), the voice of Ajax as he strides along the decks of the ships (15.686), the smoke of the city in the simile of the beacon fires and the blaze from Achilles' head (18.207, 18.214; see [[the-beacon-fires]]), and the blaze of his shield (19.379; see [[the-fire-seen-by-sailors]]). The gods pass through it: Athena leaps down from the sky through the upper air (19.351; see [[like-a-bird-of-prey]]).

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod Aether is a person, born with Day to Night and Erebus (*Theogony* 124–125), and in the war of the gods and the Titans the flame of Zeus' thunderbolts reaches “the divine upper air” (*Theogony* 697). Aristotle quotes Empedocles on the law of all that stretches without a break “through the wide-ruling air” (αἰθέρος, *Rhetoric* 1373b16; see [[the-law-of-all]]).
