## Descriptions

The wall stands against a grave. Nestor proposes to heap “a single tomb” over the pyre of the Achaean dead, “one for all alike”, and “close against it” (ποτὶ δʼ αὐτόν) to build “high towers, a safeguard for the ships and for ourselves” (7.336–338; see [[tomb]] and [[cremation]]). εἶλαρ, “safeguard”, is the word that returns when the wall has fallen: Nestor and Agamemnon had trusted it to be “an unbreakable safeguard for the ships and for ourselves” (ἄρρηκτον νηῶν τε καὶ αὐτῶν εἶλαρ, 14.56, 14.68). In the towers are “gates that fit well”, to make “a way for driving horses” (7.339–340, 7.438–439). Outside runs “a deep ditch … wide and great”, set with stakes (σκόλοπες, 7.440–441), which is to “hold back horses and men” (7.342).

Book 12, whose ancient title is the Τειχομαχία, “the battle at the wall”, fills in the rest. The wall rests on foundations “of logs and stones that the Achaeans had laid with toil” (12.28–29). It is “well-built” (ἐΰδμητον, 12.36, 12.137), the fighting rages “around the wall of stone” (12.177–178), and the timbers of its towers ring under the missiles (12.36–37). It has battlements (ἐπάλξεις), and props set in the earth to hold up the towers (στῆλαι, 12.258–260). There are several gates (12.175, 12.340). On the left of the ships, where the chariots came in from the plain, one stands open for fleeing comrades when Asius drives at it (12.118–123). The one that Hector breaks with a stone has high double doors, held inside by two crossbars and fastened with a bolt (12.453–462). The ditch has steep, overhanging banks on either side, with sharp stakes set close along the top, so that no horse can pull a chariot across it (12.52–59). The wall runs along the sterns of the first row of ships, drawn up in rows because the beach could not hold them all (14.30–36), and it is lowest where the ships of Ajax and Protesilaus lie (13.681–684).

Hector calls it ἀβλήχρʼ οὐδενόσωρα, “feeble things, worth nothing” (8.178); οὐδενόσωρος, “worth nothing”, is found only there.

## Elsewhere in Homer

For the next eight books the wall is the line between the Achaeans and defeat. Hector scorns it on the first day of fighting after it is built: “Fools, who contrived these walls, … They will not hold back my might, and the horses will easily leap over the ditch they have dug” (8.177–179). Twice that day the Achaeans are driven back inside the ditch (8.213–216, 8.336–345), and at nightfall the Trojans camp on the plain (8.489–565). Nestor posts sentries between the ditch and the wall (9.66–67, 9.80–88), and Odysseus tells Achilles that the Trojans have camped “near the ships and the wall” (9.232). Achilles answers with the words the narrator used for the building (9.350 ≈ 7.441): Agamemnon has done much without him, “he has built a wall, and driven a ditch along it, wide and great, and in it he planted stakes; but not even so can he hold the strength of man-slaying Hector in check” (9.348–352). While Achilles fought, Hector would not come out beyond the Scaean gates and the oak (9.352–355; see [[the-walls-of-troy]]). The wall is the measure of Achilles' absence. The leaders hold their night council across the ditch (10.194–200), and the charioteers wait at it the next morning (11.47–52).

Book 12 is the battle for the wall. Polydamas persuades the Trojans to leave their chariots at the ditch and attack on foot (12.60–87). Asius will not, and is held at the gate (12.108–174). Sarpedon tears away a battlement and lays the wall bare (12.397–399), and Hector smashes a gate with a stone and leaps in, and the Trojans pour over the wall and through the gates (12.445–471). In Book 14 the wall is down (14.14–15, 14.55–56, 14.66–68). When Hector leads the Trojans back to the ships in Book 15, Apollo kicks the banks of the ditch into the middle to make a causeway “long and wide, as far as a spear's cast”, and throws down the wall as easily as a boy by the sea scatters the sand he has heaped up in play (15.355–366). The besiegers are now besieged. Ajax tells the Achaeans that they have no stronger wall behind them, and no “city fitted with towers” near (15.733–738).

Patroclus drives the Trojans back across the ditch, where many chariots are wrecked while his immortal horses leap it (16.367–381), and he kills Sarpedon, “who first leapt the wall of the Achaeans” (16.558). Achilles, unarmed, stands at the ditch and shouts three times, and the Trojans panic (18.215–229). When the gods go into battle Athena shouts from the ditch outside the wall (20.48–50). The last time the poem names it, Hermes brings Priam past “the towers of the ships and the ditch”, puts the sentries to sleep, and opens the gates (24.443–447).

## Its end

Zeus' promise to Poseidon (7.459–463) is kept after the poem ends, and the narrator tells how at the start of Book 12, before the Trojans attack. The wall had been built “against the will of the gods, the immortals; and so it did not stand firm for long” (12.8–9). As long as Hector lived, Achilles kept his wrath and Priam's city stood, it stood (12.10–12). But when the best of the Trojans were dead and the city had been sacked in the tenth year and the Argives had sailed home, Poseidon and Apollo brought the rivers against it: the Rhesus, Heptaporus, Caresus, Rhodius, Granicus and Aesepus, the Scamander and the Simoeis, where many shields and helmets had fallen in the dust, “and the race of men half-divine” (ἡμιθέων γένος ἀνδρῶν, 12.17–23; see [[scamander]], [[simoeis]] and [[aesepus]]). Apollo turned their mouths together and drove their flood against the wall for nine days, while Zeus rained without ceasing. Poseidon, trident in hand, swept the foundations into the waves, leveled the shore of the Hellespont, covered the great beach again with sand, and sent the rivers back into their beds (12.24–33; see [[hellespont]]).

The passage takes up the words of Book 7. Poseidon complained that the Achaeans “have not given glorious hecatombs to the gods” (7.450), and the narrator repeats the verse (12.6). Zeus told him to “cover the great beach over again with sand” (7.462), and the narrator says that he did (12.31). ἡμίθεοι, “demigods”, is used of the heroes only here in Homer. It is Hesiod's name for the race of heroes who died at Thebes and at Troy (*Works and Days* 159–165; see [[hesiod]] and [[men-of-today]]). The rivers that wash the wall away flow past the place where that race fell, and the wall that was to be famous “as far as the dawn spreads” (7.451) is left only in the poem that tells of its end (see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]).

## Outside Homer

[[thucydides|Thucydides]], arguing that the Greeks at Troy were short of supplies, says that they must have won a battle when they first landed, “for otherwise they would not have built the fortification for their camp” (1.11.1). He puts the wall at the beginning of the war, not in its tenth year (see [[thucydides-and-the-wall]]).

[[aristotle|Aristotle]] doubted that there had ever been a wall. [[strabo|Strabo]], asking how near the Naval Station by Sigeum lay to the city, says that Homer makes the wall a recent one, “or it never existed, but the poet who invented it made it vanish, as Aristotle says” (13.1.36). He adds that the plain in front of the city is silt brought down by the rivers since the war. Elsewhere Strabo uses the same comparison without Aristotle's name. Posidonius, he says, was right to allow that Plato's Atlantis might be no fiction, rather than say that “the one who invented it made it vanish, as the poet did the wall of the Achaeans” (2.3.6; see [[aristotle-and-the-wall]]).

The gods' debate over the wall (7.443–464) was suspected by some ancient critics and by modern ones (see the translation's note on 7.443–464). Analytic critics took the wall itself, its building and its destruction for late additions to the poem: it is built only in the tenth year, and Thucydides seemed to know another story. Denys Page argued that the Iliad Thucydides read did not yet tell of the building (*History and the Homeric Iliad*, 1959). Others answer that the fighting at the ships in Books 12–15 needs a wall, and that the poem gives its reason, since the Achaeans build it when Achilles has withdrawn and their dead are many (7.327–330, 9.348–355; see [[the-wall-in-the-tenth-year]]). The destruction has been read as an echo of the myth of a flood that ends the age of heroes, with its rivers, its rain and its “race of demigods” (Ruth Scodel, “The Achaean Wall and the Myth of Destruction”, *Harvard Studies in Classical Philology* 86, 1982). James I. Porter has traced how critics from Aristotle on have made the vanished wall a test of how far Homer could invent (“Making and Unmaking: The Achaean Wall and the Limits of Fictionality in Homeric Criticism”, *Transactions of the American Philological Association* 141, 2011). No remains of the wall have been found on the plain of Troy.
