## The name

Merry takes Hypereia and Scheria for “merely topographical descriptions”, the “Highlands” and the “Coast” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 5.34; see [[scheria]]). Elsewhere he sets it beside Apeire, the land from which the old woman who nursed Nausicaa was brought, as a name that is “purely mythical” (Merry on *Odyssey* 7.8). Its one epithet, εὐρύχορος, “with wide dancing floors”, is a standing epithet of cities and lands (see [[with-wide-dancing-floors]]).

## In Homer

The poem names Hypereia once, when Athena comes to the Phaeacians' city at the opening of *Odyssey* 6: the Phaeacians “once lived in Hypereia with its wide dancing floors, / near the Cyclopes, overweening men, / who used to plunder them, and were the stronger in strength” (*Odyssey* 6.4–6; see [[cyclopes]] and [[overweening]]). Nausithous led them away from it and settled them in Scheria, “far from toiling men” (*Odyssey* 6.7–8; see [[nausithous]] and [[the-migration-of-the-phaeacians]]). Nothing else is told of the place: not where it lay, nor how far it was from their new home.

## Outside Homer

The verses served Aristotle in one of his Homeric problems. Why was Odysseus not afraid to tell the Phaeacians, who were themselves descended from Poseidon, that he had blinded Poseidon's son? Because, Aristotle answered, he knew that they were hostile to the Cyclopes, who had harassed them until they left their land and came to Scheria (*Homeric Problems* fr. 396 Gigon, citing *Odyssey* 6.4–8; see [[aristotle]]).

The Thessalian Hypereia, a spring in the realm of Eurypylus, is another place: the Catalogue names it, and Hector imagines Andromache carrying water from it as a slave (*Iliad* 2.734, 6.457; see [[hypereia]]).
