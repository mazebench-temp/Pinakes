## The name

Homer does not use the compound Σαμοθρᾴκη. He calls the island Samos and marks it with an epithet, Σάμου ὑληέσσης / Θρηϊκίης, “wooded Samos, / the Thracian” (13.12–13), or names it with its neighbors: Iris dives into the sea between Samos and Imbros, and Hecuba says that Achilles sold her sons to Samos, Imbros and Lemnos (24.78, 24.753; see [[imbros]] and [[lemnos]]). The other Samos of the Iliad is the island of Odysseus' Cephallenians (2.634; see [[samos]]). Apollodorus, as Strabo reports, gave “Thracian Samos” and “between Samos and Imbros” as examples of the way the poet keeps places of the same name apart, here from the Ionian Samos, which Homer never names (*Geography* 8.3.6). Strabo adds that in the time of the war both Cephallenia and Samothrace were called Samos, or else Hecuba could not have spoken of Samos and Imbros (10.2.17). The Suda explains Σάμου ὑληέσσης as Samothrace said not in the usual way but in separate words, just as ἐν πόλει ἄκρῃ, “on the height of the city” (6.88), is the acropolis (σ 81).

Strabo thought it more plausible that the island was named from σάμοι, “heights”, and cited the verse “for from there all of Ida could be seen” (13.13). Others derived the name from the Saii, Thracians who once lived there and whom Archilochus mentions (*Geography* 10.2.17).

## In Homer

{{quote:acadcc9f-8855-5637-b022-836c6c09ca56}}

ὑψοῦ ἐπʼ ἀκροτάτης κορυφῆς, “high up on the topmost peak” (13.12): in the next book Hera sees Zeus sitting ἐπʼ ἀκροτάτης κορυφῆς, “on the topmost peak” of Ida (14.157–158). The two brothers, “with their minds set on different sides” (13.345), watch the same battle from two summits facing each other across the sea (see [[ida]], [[poseidon]] and [[zeus]]). Seen from the plain of Troy, the mountain of Samothrace rises behind Imbros.

The island is “wooded” (ὑλήεις) and its mountain “rocky” (παιπαλόεις, 13.17), the epithet of Imbros (13.33). Poseidon had come up out of the sea to sit there (13.15). When he comes down, “the high mountains and the woods trembled / under the immortal feet of Poseidon as he went” (13.18–19), and in four strides he reaches Aegae (13.20–22; see [[aegae]] and [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]).

## Outside Homer

Later Greece knew Samothrace for its mysteries. Herodotus says that the Samothracians perform the rites of the Cabeiri, received from the Pelasgians who once lived on the island, and that a sacred story of Hermes is revealed in the Samothracian mysteries (Herodotus 2.51.2–4; see [[pelasgians]]). Strabo reports that many held the gods honored there to be the Cabeiri, though no one could say who the Cabeiri were (*Geography* 7, fr. 51; 10.3.19–21). He also reports that the brothers Iasion and Dardanus lived there, and that when Iasion was struck by lightning for his offense against Demeter, Dardanus sailed to the foot of Ida, founded Dardania and taught the Trojans the mysteries of Samothrace (7, fr. 50). In the Iliad Dardanus, son of Zeus, founds Dardania on the slopes of Ida, and the poem does not say where he came from (20.215–218; see [[dardanus]]). The Suda, quoting Antiphon's Samothracian speech, says that the first settlers were Samians driven from Samos by tyrants (σ 79); Strabo calls the tale of a colony from Samos an invention of the Samians for their own glory (10.2.17). The island lies off the coast of Thrace, with Imbros not far from it (*Geography* 7, fr. 48).
