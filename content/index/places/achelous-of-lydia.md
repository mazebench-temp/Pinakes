## In Homer

At the end of the story of Niobe, Achilles tells Priam that she broods over her sorrows in stone “on Sipylus, where they say the goddesses have their beds, / the nymphs who dance around the Achelous” (24.615–616; see [[sipylus]], [[nymphs]] and [[niobe]]). The stream is otherwise unknown. Walter Leaf finds nothing strange in an Achelous in Lydia: the scholia report a stream there called Ἀχέλης, and there were other rivers of the name in the Troad, Achaea, Thessaly and Arcadia (citing Pausanias 8.38.7 and Strabo); the name, he says, implies an intimate knowledge of the country, which need not surprise us in this book, and in our ignorance of the local names the variants Ἀχελήϊον and Ἀχελήσιον have as good a claim as the text (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 24.616; see the translation's note on 24.615). On the Greek river he calls this one quite different (on 21.194; see [[achelous]]).

The four verses that end the story were athetized by the Alexandrians, and among their reasons was that the verses were Hesiodic in character, ἀμφʼ Ἀχελώϊον ἐρρώσαντο, “dance around the Achelous”, most of all; Leaf defends them (on 24.614; see [[sipylus]]).

## Outside Homer

The name was shared by several rivers. Strabo, describing the Greek river that divides Aetolia from Acarnania, adds that there is another of the same name by Dyme in Achaea and another near Lamia (*Geography* 10.2.1): the Achaean one was also called the Peirus (8.3.11), and beside the one near Lamia lived the Paracheloitae (9.5.10).
