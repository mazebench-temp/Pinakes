## In Homer

{{quote:48a43bd2-af2c-526a-92e2-51b39e11e435}}

Homer names Lectum once. Hera and Sleep, clothed in mist, leave Lemnos and Imbros, cross the sea, and come to Ida “to Lectum, where they first left the sea” (14.283–284; see [[lemnos]], [[imbros]] and [[ida]]). The verse that brings them to “Ida of the many springs, mother of wild beasts” is the one that brought Zeus there in *Iliad* 8, but where Zeus came to Gargarus, the summit, they come to Lectum, the headland where the range meets the sea (8.47–48; see [[gargarus]]). From there they go over dry land, and the treetops shake beneath their feet (14.284–285), until Sleep stops in a tall fir and Hera goes on to the peak of Gargarus (14.286–293; see [[sleep]] and [[gargarus]]).

## Outside Homer

Strabo makes Lectum one of the two outermost spurs of Ida, with the headland by Zeleia: Zeleia ends inland, but Lectum runs down into the Aegean on the course of those who sail from Tenedos to Lesbos. Quoting 14.283–284, he says that the poet describes it rightly, for Lectum belongs to Ida, and it is the first landing from the sea for those who go up the mountain (*Geography* 13.1.5; see [[strabo]]). At Lectum an altar of the twelve gods was shown, said to have been founded by Agamemnon, and the place could be seen from Ilium (13.1.48). Rounding it, one came to the gulf of Adramyttium and the cities of the Aeolians, with Lesbos lying off the coast (13.1.49, 13.2.1). In 479 BCE the Greek fleet, sailing from Mycale to the Hellespont, was held at Lectum by winds before it reached Abydos (Herodotus 9.114.1; see [[herodotus]]). The headland is the modern Cape Baba (Babakale), the westernmost point of mainland Asia.
