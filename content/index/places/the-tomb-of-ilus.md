σῆμα, “marker”, is here a grave mound (see [[tomb]]); at 11.371 it is a τύμβος, “man-made”, with a στήλη, “pillar”, on it, and at 24.349 “the great mound”. It lies in mid-plain, on the way to the city: the routed Trojans rush past it “through the middle of the plain” (11.166–167), past the wild fig tree, to the Scaean gates (11.166–170; see [[the-fig-tree]] and [[scaean-gates]]); and it is near the river (24.349–351). Like the tomb of Aesyetes and the mound of Myrine (2.793, 2.811–814; see [[aesyetes]] and [[batieia]]), it is one of the old graves that mark the plain. Ilus was Priam's grandfather (see [[ilus]]).

## In Homer

{{quote:516517be-6733-5153-9b01-d402381278d3}}

The Trojan leaders meet at their kings' ancestral grave, away from the noise of the camp, while the Achaean chiefs sit beyond their ditch (10.194–202; see [[hector]] and [[dolon]]). The next day Agamemnon drives the Trojans past it (11.166–168), and Paris, leaning against the pillar, shoots Diomedes through the foot and leaps out “from his ambush” (ἐκ λόχου, 11.369–381; see [[ambush]]). In the last book Priam and his herald pass it at dusk and stop to water the mules and horses in the river, where Hermes meets them (24.349–353).

## Outside Homer

Strabo names the tomb of Ilus among the places of Homer's plain that were still pointed out, with the fig tree, the tomb of Aesyetes and Batieia (13.1.34; see [[strabo]]).
