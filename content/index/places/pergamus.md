## The name

Πέργαμος is feminine in Homer: Apollo's temple stands Περγάμῳ εἰν ἱερῇ, “in sacred Pergamus” (5.446). The origin of the name is unknown; a connection with πύργος (“tower”) has been suggested but not shown. Later poets, Greek and Latin, used the neuter plural, Πέργαμα, *Pergama*, for the citadel and for Troy itself. The same name belonged to Pergamon in Mysia, later the capital of the Attalid kings.

## In Homer

{{quote:c687b0f3-a0ae-5cfd-8a55-a1bda23e67e2}}

The Iliad names Pergamus first here. It is Apollo's place. From it he looks down on the battle (4.508, 7.21), in his temple there he has the wounded Aeneas healed (5.445–448), and there he sits to watch the fighting (5.460). It is also the high point of the city. Paris comes down from “the height of Pergamus” in his shining armor (6.512), and Cassandra, having climbed it, is the first to see Priam bringing Hector's body home (24.699–700). The poem also speaks of the “high city” (πόλις ἄκρη), where Athena's temple and the houses of Priam, Hector and Paris stood (6.88, 6.317); it is usually taken to be the same height.

## Outside Homer

On his way to Greece in 480 BC, Xerxes went up to “the Pergamon of Priam”, wishing to see it, and sacrificed a thousand oxen to Athena of Ilion (Herodotus 7.43). In the *Aeneid* the ghost of Hector tells Aeneas that if any hand could have defended Pergama, his own would have done it (*Aeneid* 2.291–292).
