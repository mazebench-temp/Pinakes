## The name

Ἀχέρων is commonly heard as the river of ἄχος, “grief”: W. W. Merry calls it the “river of Miseries”, quoting Pindar's line that Acheron ferries griefs to mortals (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 10.513; see [[pindar]]).

## In Homer

Homer names Acheron once, in Circe's directions for the journey to the dead. The North Wind is to carry the ship across Oceanus to a shore of good soil and the groves of Persephone; there Odysseus is to beach the ship and go himself “into the dank house of Hades” (*Odyssey* 10.507–512; see [[oceanus]], [[the-groves-of-persephone]] and [[hades]]):

{{quote:ea0a5a8b-c511-5295-9942-3f190bfa630e}}

Acheron is the river that receives the others: Pyriphlegethon and Cocytus, “a branch of the water of Styx”, flow into it, and they meet at a rock (see [[pyriphlegethon]], [[cocytus]] and [[styx]]). There, at the entrance to the house of Hades, as Merry takes ἔνθα (on *Odyssey* 10.513), Odysseus is to dig the pit for the offering to the dead (*Odyssey* 10.516–537). When the poem tells the journey in *Odyssey* 11, it names Oceanus and the place that Circe had shown, but not the rivers (*Odyssey* 11.13–22).

Merry pictures the scene from the Greek landscapes it recalls: Cocytus and Pyriphlegethon running at a higher level and plunging together over a ledge of rock into Acheron, with the noise of a cataract in ἐριδούπων, “loud-resounding”, and he wonders whether the white rock that the souls of the suitors pass on their way to the meadow of asphodel, Λευκὰς πέτρη, “the Leucadian rock” (*Odyssey* 24.11), is this rock, white in the foam (on *Odyssey* 10.515; see [[loud-resounding]]). He thought that the Styx was at first the only river of the underworld, and that this passage, which brings in Acheron, Cocytus and Pyriphlegethon, “is probably a later invention” (on *Odyssey* 10.513).

## Outside Homer

In the myth of the *Phaedo*, Acheron is the second of the four great rivers of the earth, flowing opposite to Oceanus through desert places and under the earth into the Acherusian lake, where the souls of most of the dead come and stay for appointed times before they are sent back to be born as living things; those judged to have lived neither well nor ill go to Acheron, embark on the boats there and come to the lake to be purified (Plato, *Phaedo* 112e–113a, 113d; see [[plato]]).

There was an Acheron on earth. Herodotus tells how Periander sent to the [[thesprotians|Thesprotians]], to the oracle of the dead on the river Acheron, and how the ghost of his wife Melissa appeared there (*Histories* 5.92η.2), and he makes the Thesprotians and the river Acheron the boundary of the Greeks who fought at Salamis (*Histories* 8.47.1; see [[herodotus]]). Strabo describes the river flowing out of the Acherusian lake into the Sweet Harbor on the Thesprotian coast, below Cichyrus, which had once been Ephyra (*Geography* 7.7.5; see [[strabo]] and [[ephyra]]). Pausanias, as Merry reports him, held that Homer took the scenery of the journey to the dead from the Acherusian lake of Thesprotia and its rivers Acheron and Cocytus; Merry thought that the truth might lie the other way, and that the rivers had taken their names from the poetry (on *Odyssey* 10.513; see [[pausanias]]). In Italy the people around Cumae spoke of an Acherusian lake, and placed the Homeric oracle of the dead at Lake Avernus (Strabo, *Geography* 1.2.18, 5.4.5); some took the Lucrine gulf for the Acherusian lake, and Artemidorus Avernus itself (*Geography* 5.4.6). In Virgil the road to the underworld leads “to the waves of Tartarean Acheron”, a muddy whirlpool that belches all its sand into Cocytus (*Aeneid* 6.295–297; see [[virgil]]). In Cicero, Cotta argues that if Jupiter and Neptune are gods, so must be the rivers “said to flow among the dead”, Acheron, Cocytus and Pyriphlegethon, and Charon and Cerberus too (*On the Nature of the Gods* 3.43), and “the crossing of Acheron” and “the roaring of Cocytus” are among the terrors of the world below that the young man of the *Tusculan Disputations* calls the portents of poets and painters, and does not believe (*Tusculan Disputations* 1.10; see [[cicero]]).
