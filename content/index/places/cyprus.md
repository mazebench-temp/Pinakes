## In Homer

The Iliad names the island only in Agamemnon's arming. Cinyras gave the king his corselet as a guest-gift: “For he had heard in Cyprus the great report that the Achaeans / were about to sail up to Troy in their ships” (11.21–22; see [[cinyras]] and [[corselet]]). The digitized Greek prints Κύπρον δέ, but the particle δέ, “and”, cannot follow γάρ, “for”, and the words are read as one, Κύπρονδε, “to Cyprus”, with the suffix of direction: the report reached as far as Cyprus (see the translation's note on 11.21). Strabo quotes the verse in that form (1.2.32). No Cypriots fight at Troy, and the Catalogue of Ships does not name the island. Aphrodite's name Κύπρις, “the Cyprian”, which only Book 5 uses, is formed from it (see [[cypris]] and [[aphrodite]]).

The Odyssey names Cyprus in three passages. Menelaus gathered his wealth wandering to Cyprus, Phoenicia and the Egyptians (Od. 4.83; see [[egypt]]). Freed from Hephaestus' net, Aphrodite goes to Cyprus, to Paphos, where she has a precinct and a fragrant altar (Od. 8.362–363). In the tale Odysseus tells the suitors, his Egyptian captors handed him over to Dmetor, son of Iasus, who ruled Cyprus by might, to take him there, and Antinous answers that he will soon come to a bitter Egypt and Cyprus (Od. 17.442–448).

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod, Aphrodite comes ashore first at Cythera and then at sea-girt Cyprus, and is called Κυπρογενής, “Cyprus-born”, because she was born there (*Theogony* 192–199; see [[hesiod]] and [[cythera]]). The Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite takes her to her fragrant temple at Paphos (5.58–59), and the sixth Hymn gives her all Cyprus as her portion (6.2). The Cypriots told Herodotus that their temple of Aphrodite was founded from the temple of the Heavenly Aphrodite at Ascalon in Syria (1.105.3), and that their people came from Salamis and Athens, from Arcadia, Cythnus, Phoenicia and Ethiopia (7.90; see [[herodotus]]). Strabo gives the island's legends of foundation: Salamis, founded by Teucer when his father Telamon drove him out; the ancient temple of the Paphian Aphrodite at Old Paphos; and Paphos itself, founded by Agapenor. He praises its wine, oil and grain, and the copper mines of Tamassus (14.6.3, 14.6.5; see [[teucer]], [[agapenor]], [[salamis]] and [[strabo]]). The Latin name of copper, *aes Cyprium*, “Cyprian bronze”, later *cuprum*, comes from the island. The *Cypria*, the epic of the war's beginnings, bears its name; Herodotus argued that it was not Homer's (2.117).
