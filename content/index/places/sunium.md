# Σούνιον (Od. 3.278), the only occurrence in Homer.
grc: [Σούνιον]

## Descriptions

Σούνιον ἱρόν, “holy Sunium” (see [[hieros]]), and ἄκρον Ἀθηνέων, “the cape of Athens” (*Odyssey* 3.278; see [[athens]]): Athens stands for the whole land, as it does in the Catalogue (2.546–556). Merry, who calls the cape by its modern name, Cape Colonna, from the columns of its ruined temple, offers three reasons for ἱρόν: the temple, which he took to be Athena's; the worship that sailors paid there to Poseidon, whom Aristophanes calls Σουνιάρατος, “prayed to at Sunium” (he cites *Knights* 559); and a cult of Zeus Sunieus, which was Damm's explanation (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 3.278).

## Outside Homer

Sunium was a deme of Attica as well as a cape: Strabo, sailing along the coast, lists the demes “as far as the cape of Sunium”, and “when one has rounded the cape at Sunium” comes to the notable deme of Sunium, then Thoricus (*Geography* 9.1.21–22). From Sunium to the southern cape of Euboea is a voyage of three hundred stadia (9.1.22), and Geraestus is the landfall for those who cross from Asia to Attica, “since it lies near Sunium” (10.1.7; see [[geraestus]]). Herodotus tells how the Aeginetans lay in wait for the sacred ship that the Athenians sent to their four-yearly festival at Sunium, and took it with the leading Athenians on board (Herodotus 6.87), and after Salamis the Greeks dedicated one of three captured Phoenician triremes there (8.121.1). In Euripides' *Cyclops* Odysseus counts “the rock of Sunium, sacred to divine Athena”, with its silver beneath it, among the temples of Poseidon that the Greeks kept safe (*Cyclops* 293–294). The marble temple of Poseidon on the headland was built in the fifth century BC, and Athena's sanctuary stood on a lower hill nearby.
