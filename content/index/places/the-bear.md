## In Homer

{{quote:28b36646-3452-5c0b-bcd0-801e7ec51533}}

Ἄρκτον θʼ, ἣν καὶ Ἄμαξαν ἐπίκλησιν καλέουσιν, “and the Bear, which they also call by the name of the Wagon” (18.487). ἄρκτος is the bear, and ἄμαξα a wagon, like those on which the dead are carried from the plain in Book 7 (7.426). The constellation has the two names that it has kept: Walter Leaf observes that it is still familiarly known by both, the Bear and the Wain, and that the name of the Bear is very old and widely spread, being found among the North American Indians (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.487). ἐπίκλησις is a second name that people use, as Areithous was called by the name of the mace-man (7.138). The two names are the people's, “they call”, and the poet gives both.

ἥ τʼ αὐτοῦ στρέφεται καί τʼ Ὠρίωνα δοκεύει, “which turns in its place and watches Orion” (18.488). αὐτοῦ is “in the same place”, never going down (Leaf on 18.488). The Bear wheels round the pole and keeps its eye on the hunter Orion across the sky; Leaf explains that when Orion rises in the east the Bear is low on the northern horizon, which in northern Greece it just touches, and then climbs, as though scared by the coming of the hunter from taking his bath (see [[orion]]).

οἴη δʼ ἄμμορός ἐστι λοετρῶν Ὠκεανοῖο, “and alone has no share in the baths of Oceanus” (18.489). The other stars set, and in setting they bathe in Oceanus, the river that runs around the earth and around the rim of the Shield (18.607–608; see [[oceanus]] and [[bathing]]); the star of late summer shines brightest “when it has bathed in Oceanus” (5.5–6; see [[the-autumn-star]]). The Bear never sets. Leaf explains οἴη, “alone”, by the fact that the other circumpolar constellations, none of them conspicuous, were later inventions of astronomy (on 18.489; see the translation's note on 18.486–489). The word was already a problem in antiquity, and Aristotle answers it in the *Poetics* (see [[alone-without-a-share]]).

The same three verses name the Bear in the Odyssey, where Odysseus, sailing from Calypso's island, watches the Pleiades, Boötes, which sets late, and the Bear, which Calypso told him to keep on his left hand as he crossed the sea (*Odyssey* 5.270–277; see [[pleiades]]). With the Bear on his left hand he is sailing east.

## Outside Homer

The *Astronomy* ascribed to Hesiod, as a later writer summarizes it, told how the Great Bear had been Callisto, daughter of Lycaon in Arcadia, who hunted in the mountains with Artemis; violated by Zeus, she was found out when Artemis saw her bathing, turned into a beast, and bore a son, Arcas; when her son and the Arcadians pursued her into the forbidden precinct of Zeus, Zeus saved her and set her among the stars, naming her the Bear for what had befallen her (*Astronomy* fr. 3 Evelyn-White). Leaf notes the story of Callisto in Arcadian legend (on 18.487).

Strabo reads the line as geography. By the Bear and the Wagon, he says, Homer means the arctic circle, and by Oceanus the horizon; it is unfair to charge him with ignorance for knowing one Bear and not two, since the second had probably not yet been made a constellation, and passed to the Greeks only after the Phoenicians had marked it and used it for sailing; and Crates was wrong to change the text to the masculine οἶος, avoiding, Strabo says, what needed no avoiding (*Geography* 1.1.6).
