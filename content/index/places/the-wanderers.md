## The name

Πλαγκταί is the name the gods use, “the Wanderers, as the blessed gods call them” (*Odyssey* 12.61), and Homer gives no human name beside it, as he gives none beside the gods' moly (*Odyssey* 10.305; see [[language-of-gods-and-men]] and [[moly]]). W. W. Merry holds that the regular meaning of πλαγκτός and its kin forces on us “the wandering rocks”: πλαγκτέ, the suitors' word for the swineherd as he carries the bow, is “vagabond” or perhaps “distraught” (*Odyssey* 21.363), and πλαγκτοσύνη is the roaming of a beggar (*Odyssey* 15.343), so that the Πλαγκταὶ πέτραι, like the floating island of Aeolus (πλωτῇ ἐνὶ νήσῳ, *Odyssey* 10.3; see [[aeolia]]), must be rocks that wander (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 12.61). D. B. Monro agrees: πλαγκτός is “sent adrift”, and the rocks were so called because they moved about (*Homer's Odyssey, Books XIII–XXIV*, 1901, on *Odyssey* 21.363). Most modern editors, Merry reports, preferred “striking”, since the root πλαγ- belongs both to πλάζω, “drive astray”, and to πλήσσω, “strike”, and took it of rocks dashing together or of the waves dashing against them. The ancient commentators were divided: one scholion explains the name by the waves that beat on the rocks, and says that “the younger” scholars went astray in understanding rocks that wander up and down; Crates, the wave that wanders around them; others, rocks that move and travel, like Delos; and Eustathius, rocks that wander and roll, their fire struck from their collision, though he allows that if the name came from πλήσσειν they too could be called Symplegades (Merry on *Odyssey* 12.61; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 12.61).

Merry also reads something in the gods' naming. The name may soon have lost its real meaning, or the story become half forgotten, “as is suggested by the words θεοὶ μάκαρες καλέουσι”, and that would account for the incongruities in the later part of Circe's description, which are accretions round the original legend (on *Odyssey* 12.61).

## In Homer

Homer tells of the Wanderers in Circe's directions and names them once more in the summary of Odysseus' tale for Penelope. They are overhanging rocks against which the great wave of Amphitrite roars; not even the winged things pass them, nor the timid doves that carry ambrosia to Zeus, for the sheer rock always takes one away; no ship of men that comes there has escaped, but the waves and the storms of deadly fire sweep away planks and bodies together; and only the Argo sailed past, coming home from Aeetes, because Hera loved Jason (*Odyssey* 12.59–72; see [[overhanging]], [[amphitrite]], [[doves]], [[the-argo]] and [[jason]]). Circe leaves Odysseus to choose between them and the strait of Scylla and Charybdis, though she plainly means to dissuade him from the Wanderers (Merry on *Odyssey* 12.55), and he takes the strait (see [[odysseus-passes-scylla]]). The summary in *Odyssey* 23 nevertheless says that he told Penelope “how he came to the Wandering Rocks and dread Charybdis / and Scylla, whom men have never yet escaped unharmed” (*Odyssey* 23.327–328, a plain rendering), in a verse shaped like the twelfth book's “when we had escaped the rocks and dread Charybdis / and Scylla” (*Odyssey* 12.260–261).

Homer does not show the rocks moving, nor closing on a ship. They destroy by surf and by fire (*Odyssey* 12.67–68), the doves are lost as they pass (παρέρχεται, *Odyssey* 12.62), and Hera saves the Argo from the waves that would have dashed her against them: “Not a word is said”, Merry notes, “of the rocks threatening to close in upon the ship” (on *Odyssey* 12.71; on the doves, 12.63).

## Outside Homer

**The Clashing Rocks.** The Wanderers were early identified with the Symplegades (Merry on *Odyssey* 12.61), the “dark Clashing Rocks” through which the hull of the Argo flew to the land of the Colchians (κυανέας Συμπληγάδας, Euripides, *Medea* 1–2; see [[medea-play]]), the rocks “that run together” (πέτρας τὰς συνδρομάδας, *Iphigenia among the Taurians* 421; see [[iphigenia-among-the-taurians]]). Herodotus tells that Darius, at the bridge over the Bosporus, sailed out to “the so-called Cyanean rocks, which the Greeks say were formerly wandering” (τὰς πρότερον πλαγκτὰς Ἕλληνες φασὶ εἶναι, *Histories* 4.85.1; see [[herodotus]]). Strabo explains Homer's Wanderers by the Cyanean rocks, which some call Symplegades, at the rough passage out of the mouth at Byzantium: as Aeaea came from Aea, so the Wanderers came from the Symplegades, and Jason's voyage through them seemed credible (*Geography* 1.2.10); the poet, always drawing his myths from some history, made the Wanderers like the Cyaneae, and the straits at the Pillars of Heracles and at Sicily suggested the story to him too (3.2.12), and some writers moved the Wanderers and the Symplegades to the Pillars (3.5.5; see [[strabo]]). Merry reports the same identification in Pindar (*Pythian* 4.208), in a periplus of the Euxine, and in Pliny's “islands in the Pontus, the Planctae, or Cyaneae, or Symplegades” (*Natural History* 6.12.13, as Merry quotes it), and adds that the danger of the Symplegades, that they would nip a ship between them, is not even alluded to in Homer (on *Odyssey* 12.61). Monro, who rejected Kirchhoff's argument that features of these books were taken from the Argonautic legend (Appendix, p. 318), still thought the Wanderers “evidently the same as the Symplegades”: since Circe's island is the place of the sunrise, the rocks beyond it are to be sought in the east (Monro, Appendix, p. 337; see [[aeaea]]). Apollonius, as Merry reports, gives the Argo both: a dove sent ahead through the Symplegades on the outward voyage, and the passage of the Planctae on the way home (*Argonautica* 2.561 ff., 4.924 ff., as Merry cites them; see [[apollonius]]).

**The strait of Sicily.** The ancients generally placed the Wanderers at the northern entrance of the strait of Sicily, and later writers identified them with the volcanic Lipari islands (Merry on *Odyssey* 12.61). The collection of marvels handed down under Aristotle's name reports the argument, with the poet as witness, that Jason did not sail out of the Pontus by the Symplegades but by the Ister and the Adriatic, since otherwise he would not have passed the Wanderers: no fire is said to rise at the Cyaneae, but the poet's “storms of deadly fire” (*Odyssey* 12.68) belong to the strait of Sicily, with its blasts of fire on either side, its island always burning, and the stream of Etna that has often overrun the land ([Aristotle], *On Marvellous Things Heard* 105; see [[aristotle]]).

**Merry's explanation.** Merry thought the name might preserve a sailor's story of floating icebergs, whose overhanging tops, with the water breaking against their sides, exactly meet Circe's first verses (*Odyssey* 12.59–60). He also set beside the surf, smoke and fire the sudden rise and disappearance of islets thrown up by submarine volcanoes, which would be seen first in one place and then in another: in July 1831 an island that rose off the coast of Sicily, opposite Sciacca, had sunk again below the sea within a few months (on *Odyssey* 12.61).
