**A river and a father.** The Spercheius is named first in the Iliad as a father. The Myrmidon who leads the first of Achilles' five companies is “son of Spercheius, the river fallen from Zeus” (16.174), whom Polydora, a daughter of Peleus, bore “to tireless Spercheius, a woman who lay with a god” (16.175–176; see [[menesthius-son-of-spercheius]], [[polydora]] and [[the-birth-of-menesthius]]). A river god founds a mortal line elsewhere in the poem too: the Alpheus begot Ortilochus, the grandfather of the twins Crethon and Orsilochus (5.544–549; see [[alpheus]] and [[diocles]]), and the Axius begot Pelegon, the father of Asteropaeus (21.141–143, 21.157–159; see [[axius]]).

**Its epithets.** διιπετής, “fallen from Zeus” (16.174), is said in Homer only of rivers: of the Spercheius, of a river in a simile (17.263), of the Scamander (21.268, 21.326) and, in the Odyssey, of the Nile (*Odyssey* 4.477, 4.581). Strabo explains that the poet calls all rivers so, not only torrents, because all are filled by rain (*Geography* 1.2.30). ἀκάμας, “tireless” (16.176), is otherwise the epithet of the sun (18.239, 18.484; see [[tireless]]): a river, like the sun, does not stop.

**Achilles' hair.** The river is named again at the funeral of Patroclus. Achilles stands apart from the pyre and cuts off the fair hair that he had let grow long for the Spercheius, and he speaks to the river: Peleus vowed that when his son came home he would cut his hair for it and offer a sacred hecatomb, with fifty rams sacrificed into its springs, where it has a precinct and a fragrant altar; but since Achilles will not return, he gives the hair to Patroclus to take with him, and puts it in his dead friend's hands (23.140–153; see [[hair]], [[fair-haired]], [[fragrant]] and [[hecatomb]]). A river with a precinct and an altar is a god with a cult, like the Scamander, which has a priest and receives bulls and horses (5.77–78, 21.130–132; see [[scamander]] and [[priesthood]]). The vow makes the Spercheius the god of Achilles' home country.

## Outside Homer

The Spercheius rises on Mount Tymphrestus and flows east through the valley that later belonged to the Aenianes, the Homeric Enienes, into the Malian Gulf, between Thermopylae and Lamia (Strabo, *Geography* 9.5.9; Herodotus 7.198.2; see [[enienes]]). Strabo takes the river as proof that Achilles' kingdom reached so far, since Homer names it “many times as a local river”: Achilles grows his hair for it, and Menesthius, one of his captains, is called the son of the Spercheius and of Achilles' sister (*Geography* 9.5.9). In Aeschylus' *Persians* the beaten army of Xerxes passes the Malian Gulf, “where the Spercheius waters the plain with kindly drink” (*Persians* 486–487), and the seer Megistias, who died at Thermopylae, was killed, his epitaph says, by Medes who had crossed the river Spercheius (Herodotus 7.228.3). A verse of Aeschylus' *Philoctetes* calls on the river: “River Spercheius, and bends where cattle graze” (fr. 249 Nauck; see [[aeschylus]]).
