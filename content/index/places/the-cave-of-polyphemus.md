## In Homer

The cave lies across the water from the island of goats, at the far edge of the land, close to the sea: “high, and overhung with laurels” (*Odyssey* 9.181–183; see [[the-island-of-goats]], [[roofed]] and [[odysseus-and-the-cyclops]]). Around it a high fold had been built “with stones set deep in the earth / and with tall pines and high-leafed oaks”, and there the sheep and the goats slept at night (*Odyssey* 9.184–186; see [[fold]]). Inside are racks laden with cheeses, pens crowded with lambs and kids, each age penned apart, and vessels swimming with whey (*Odyssey* 9.219–223), and the dung lies spread throughout it in great abundance (*Odyssey* 9.329–330). The ewes and she-goats are driven into “the wide cave” at night, while the rams and he-goats are left outside, in the fold (*Odyssey* 9.237–239, 9.337–338). The mouth is a high doorway, which the Cyclops shuts with a stone that twenty-two four-wheeled wagons could not have heaved from the ground, and sets back in place in the morning “as one sets the lid on a quiver” (*Odyssey* 9.240–243, 9.304–305, 9.313–314; see [[wagon]] and [[quiver]]). Within there is an inner part, μυχός, into which the companions shrink in terror (*Odyssey* 9.236), and the rock rings round about when the blinded Cyclops groans (*Odyssey* 9.395). His neighbors, who live “in caves among the windy heights”, come and stand around it, and he answers them from inside (*Odyssey* 9.399–408); for all the Cyclopes live “on the peaks of high mountains / in hollow caves” (*Odyssey* 9.113–114; see [[cyclopes]] and [[hollow-caves]]).

Merry pictures the cave running back into the cliff, with a yard around its mouth walled with stone, the trees worked into the wall (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 9.184). The Cyclops, in his reading, throws his wood down inside the cave, steps out again, and drives the females in, leaving the males in the yard; the picture is consistent if one reads Rumpf's ἔντοθεν, “within the court”, at *Odyssey* 9.239 and 9.338, as most modern editors do (Merry on *Odyssey* 9.235); the text prints ἔκτοθεν, and the English follows it, “outside in the high-walled fold” (see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 9.235, 9.239, 9.338).

## Elsewhere in the Odyssey

The cave is remembered as the place of the Cyclops' meals and of the escape. Aegyptius' son Antiphus was killed by “the savage Cyclops … in his hollow cave”, the last of his suppers (*Odyssey* 2.19–20; see [[antiphus-son-of-aegyptius]]). Before Scylla, Odysseus tells his men that this evil is no greater than when the Cyclops penned them in his hollow cave with his mighty strength, and that they escaped from it by his prowess, his counsel and his mind (*Odyssey* 12.208–212; see the Book 12 note). In his own house, angry at the maids, he bids his heart endure, as it endured the day the Cyclops ate his companions, until cunning led him out of the cave when he thought he would die (*Odyssey* 20.18–21, a plain rendering).

## Outside Homer

Strabo, arguing that a traveler may be said to have reached a land though he saw little of it, observes that Odysseus says he came to the land of the Cyclopes, though he went no farther from the sea than the cave, which he says lay somewhere at the far edge (*Geography* 1.2.32; see [[strabo]]). Euripides' satyr play sets it under Aetna in Sicily, where “the one-eyed sons of the sea god, / the Cyclopes, man-slayers, live in lonely caves”, and Silenus tells Odysseus that they have caves, not roofed houses (*Cyclops* 20–22, 118; see [[euripides]]). In Virgil, Achaemenides, left behind by Ulysses' men “in the Cyclops' vast cave”, calls it “a house of gore and bloody feasts, dark within, huge” (*Aeneid* 3.616–619; see [[virgil]]). Merry thinks that the massive stone walls of early Greece were ascribed to the Cyclopes probably in allusion to the walls of this fold, and quotes Pausanias for the tradition that the walls of Mycenae were their work, as were the walls of Tiryns, which they made for Proetus (on *Odyssey* 9.184). Strabo says that Proetus walled Tiryns by means of Cyclopes, seven of them, brought from Lycia, and that the caves near Nauplia and the works in them, called Cyclopeia, are perhaps named after them (*Geography* 8.6.2, 8.6.11).
