## The springs

κρουνός is a spring, the place where water gushes out of the ground: the winter torrents of a simile in Book 4 throw their heavy water together “from great springs” (4.454). πηγαί are the springs of rivers, where the nymphs dwell (20.9). Lilaea lies “at the springs of the Cephisus” (2.523; see [[lilaea]] and [[cephisus]]), the Scamander bids the Simoeis fill his streams “with water from your springs” (21.312; see [[simoeis]]), and Peleus vowed fifty rams to the Spercheius at its springs, where it had a precinct and an altar (23.144–148; see [[spercheius]]). For this place the poem uses both words, κρουνὼ … καλλιρρόω, “the two fair-flowing springs”, in the dual, and πηγαὶ δοιαί, “the two sources” (22.147–148; see [[fair-flowing]] and [[eddying]]).

The two springs are unlike. One runs “with warm water” (ὕδατι λιαρῷ, 22.149), the words for the water with which a wound is washed (11.830, 11.846), and smoke rises from it “as if from a blazing fire” (22.150); the other runs in summer “like hail, / or cold snow, or ice formed from water” (22.151–152). κρύσταλλος, “ice”, is found in Homer only here and in Odysseus' tale of a winter night in ambush under the walls of Troy, when ice formed on the shields (*Odyssey* 14.469–477).

## The washing troughs

Beside the springs are πλυνοὶ εὐρέες, “the washing troughs, wide, / fair, and of stone” (22.153–154). λαΐνεοι, “of stone”, is found only here in Homer. πλυνοί are places for washing clothes: the washing places of the Phaeacians lie by the river, “far from the city” (*Odyssey* 6.40), and Nausicaa and her maids tread the clothes there in the pits (*Odyssey* 6.85–92). The Trojan troughs lie near the city, where “the wives of the Trojans and their fair daughters used to wash” their glossy clothes (22.155; see [[glossy]] and [[trojan-women]]), “in former days, in time of peace, before the sons of the Achaeans came” (22.156; see [[eirene]]). Achilles spoke the same verse in Book 9 of the wealth that Ilios possessed before the war, which was not worth his life to him (9.401–403).

## In the poem

The springs are the last of the landmarks that the poem names in the chase around the walls. Achilles and Hector run past the lookout and the fig tree, “always out from under the wall, along the wagon road” (22.146), and come to them; ἀμαξιτός, “wagon road”, is found only here in Homer (see [[the-lookout-at-troy]], [[the-fig-tree]] and [[the-walls-of-troy]]). The narrator stops the chase at the springs to tell of the troughs and of the time before the war, and then goes on: “There they ran past, one fleeing, the other pursuing behind” (22.157; see [[the-pursuit-of-hector]]).

The race is counted at the springs. The two run three times around the city of Priam (22.165), and “when for the fourth time they came to the springs, / then the father held out his golden scales” (22.208–209): Hector's fated day sinks, and Apollo leaves him (22.209–213; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]] and [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]).

## Outside Homer

[[strabo|Strabo]] reports Demetrius of Scepsis, who knew the Troad as a native. The Scamander, Demetrius says, flows from Cotylus, a hill of Ida above Scepsis, from a single spring, and so it is a question how the poet can speak of two springs of the Scamander, one warm and one cold, since no hot water is now found at the place, and the source of the Scamander is not there but in the mountain, and one, not two. His answer is that the hot springs have probably failed, and that the cold water rises there by seeping underground from the Scamander, or else is called a source of the Scamander because it lies near the river, as several springs are called the springs of the same river (*Geography* 13.1.43; see [[ida]]). In the first book of the *Geography* Strabo gives Demetrius' answer again: no one should wonder that the cold spring remains while the hot one is no longer seen, for the hot water has failed, and Demetrius recalls the great earthquakes of old that Democles told of, around Lydia and Ionia as far as the Troad, when a wave flooded Troy (1.3.17; see [[strabo-on-the-chase-around-troy]]).
