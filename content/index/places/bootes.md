# Βοώτην (Od. 5.272), the only form in Homer.
grc: [Βοώτην]
en: [Boötes]
---

Βοώτης is the ox-driver or plowman, from βοῦς, “ox”. Homer names him once, between the Pleiades and the Bear, in the list of the stars by which Odysseus steers (*Odyssey* 5.270–277; see [[pleiades]] and [[the-bear]]):

{{quote:c270d5ba-7478-547c-b59c-e32a86badbbd}}

Πληιάδας τʼ ἐσορῶντι καὶ ὀψὲ δύοντα Βοώτην, “as he watched the Pleiades and Boötes, who sets late” (*Odyssey* 5.272). The Shield of Achilles, whose verses on the Bear follow here, has the Hyades and Orion in his place (*Iliad* 18.486–489; see [[hyades]] and [[orion]]).

**Who sets late.** ὀψὲ δύοντα, “who sets late”, is his one epithet (see [[who-sets-late]]). W. W. Merry gives two explanations. Boötes may be said to set late because the brilliance of his light keeps him visible long after dawn; or, as Sir G. C. Lewis suggested in his *Astronomy of the Ancients*, because his setting is a long process, the constellation standing upright at the time of setting, while his rising is quick, since he comes up lying on his side (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.272). Merry cites Catullus, whose Lock of Berenice, set among the stars, turns to its setting “leading slow Boötes”, *tardum dux ante Booten, / qui vix sero alto mergitur Oceano*, “who scarcely, late, sinks in the deep Ocean” (Catullus 66.67–68). Merry supposes Odysseus to be sailing about the autumn equinox, when near midnight Boötes would be setting in the northwest and the Pleiades rising in the southeast (on 5.273; see [[the-bear]]).

**The Wagon and its driver.** The Bear that follows in the same verse is the one “which they also call by the name of the Wagon” (*Odyssey* 5.273), and the ox-driver goes with the wagon. Merry quotes Göttling's note on Hesiod (*Works and Days* 619): most of the Greeks, with the Boeotians, saw in these stars the hunt of Orion, who with his dog Sirius pursues the Bear, the Pleiades, the Hyades and the Hare, while the Ionians saw in the same part of the sky a wagon with Boötes its driver (on 5.272).

## Outside Homer

[[Hesiod]] does not name Boötes, but he names the star Ἀρκτοῦρος, Arcturus, “the bear-ward”, whom Merry takes to be the same (on 5.272). Sixty days after the winter solstice Arcturus leaves the holy stream of Ocean and first rises, shining, at dusk, and then the vines are to be pruned (*Works and Days* 564–570); the grapes are to be picked when Orion and Sirius have come into mid-heaven and rosy-fingered Dawn looks on Arcturus (*Works and Days* 609–611). The *Astronomy* ascribed to Hesiod, as a later writer summarizes it, said of “Boötes, who is also called Arctophylax”, the keeper of the Bear, that he was Arcas, the son of Zeus and Callisto, who lived near Mount Lycaeum; Callisto became the Bear (*Astronomy* fr. 3 Evelyn-White; see [[the-bear]]).
