Book 2 names Ida twice. Aeneas was born "on the slopes of Ida" (Ἴδης ἐν κνημοῖσι), where Aphrodite lay with Anchises ([[aphrodite-and-anchises]]), and [[Zeleia]] lies "under the lowest foot of Ida" (2.824). κνημοί, literally "shins", are the lower slopes or spurs of a mountain.

{{notes}}

## Elsewhere in Homer

Ida is Zeus' mountain. He drives there in Book 8 to watch the battle from Gargarus, where he has "a precinct and a fragrant altar" (8.47–52), and there Hera seduces him in Book 14 (14.292–351). In prayers before Troy he is Zeus "who rules from Ida" (Ἴδηθεν μεδέων, 3.276, 3.320), and Hector often burned the thighs of oxen for him on its peaks (22.170–171). Its standing epithets are πολυπῖδαξ, "of many springs" (8.47, 14.157), and μήτηρ θηρῶν, "mother of wild beasts" (8.47). The rivers of the Troad flow from it to the sea (12.19–22). On its slopes Trojan princes herd cattle and sheep. Achilles once chased Aeneas from his cattle there (20.188–191), and he captured two sons of Priam, Isus and Antiphus, as they tended their flocks on its spurs (11.104–106). The Achaeans cut its oaks for Patroclus' pyre (23.117–122).

## Outside Homer

Paris judged the three goddesses on Ida, as the *Cypria* told it, and Ganymede was carried off from it in later versions of his story. The Cretan Ida, where Zeus was born in some traditions, is a different mountain. The Trojan Ida is the modern Kaz Dağı.
