## In Homer

Homer names Libya twice, both times in the Odyssey and in tales of wandering in the eastern Mediterranean. Menelaus came there in the eight years in which he gathered his wealth: it is the last of the lands he lists, “where the lambs are horned at once”, for the flocks bear young three times in the year, and neither master nor shepherd lacks cheese or meat or milk (*Odyssey* 4.83–89; see [[the-wanderings-of-menelaus]] and [[sheep]]). In the false tale that Odysseus tells Eumaeus, a Phoenician put him on a ship for Libya, meaning to sell him there (*Odyssey* 14.295–297, a plain rendering; see [[phoenicians]]).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus quotes the verse as rightly said, since in hot lands horns grow quickly, and Aristotle writes that in Libya the horned beasts are born with their horns, not only the lambs, “as Homer says”, but the others too (Herodotus 4.29; *History of Animals* 8.28; see [[the-horned-lambs-of-libya]]). Strabo, who thought that Menelaus spent most of his wanderings on the coasts and islands of the eastern Mediterranean, says that he came to Libya as he came to Ethiopia, by putting in at certain places, and that the harbor at Ardania, beyond Paraetonium, is called Menelaus after him (*Geography* 1.2.32; see [[strabo]]).

The Greeks settled Cyrene on the Libyan coast in the seventh century BCE (Herodotus 4.150–159). Ancient writers knew a class of fables called Libyan, though what set them apart from the Aesopic is not clear. See [[egypt]] and [[aesop]].
