εἰν Ἀρίμοις, ὅθι φασὶ Τυφωέος ἔμμεναι εὐνάς: "among the Arimi, where they say Typhoeus has his bed". The phrase is all that Homer says, and he gives it as hearsay, with φασί, "they say". Hesiod uses the same words of the monster Echidna, who lives "among the Arimi, under the earth" (*Theogony* 304).

The ancients could not agree whether the Arimi were a people or a place, or where they were: in Cilicia, where later poets put Typhon's cave; in Syria, among the Aramaeans; or in the volcanic "Burnt Land" of Lydia and Mysia, which suited a monster of fire. Virgil seems to have read the phrase as the name of an island, Inarime, which Jupiter laid on top of Typhoeus (*Aeneid* 9.715–716); later writers took Inarime for Ischia, in the bay of Naples.
