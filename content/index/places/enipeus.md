## The river and the god

Homer names the Enipeus only in the story of Tyro, the first of the heroines whom Odysseus sees among the dead. She fell in love with the river, θεῖος, “divine”, and used to go often to its streams; Poseidon took his likeness and lay with her at the mouth of the river, under a wave that stood around them like a mountain, and she bore him Pelias and Neleus (*Odyssey* 11.235–257; see [[tyro]], [[poseidon]], [[pelias]] and [[neleus]]). The river is a god and a lover, and the story is of a kind the Iliad also knows: the Axius begot Pelegon with Periboea, and Polydora bore Menesthius to the Spercheius (21.141–143; 16.173–176; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.241; see [[rivers]], [[axius]] and [[spercheius]]).

## Which Enipeus

Two rivers bore the name. The Thessalian Enipeus, Strabo says, flows from Othrys past Pharsalus into the Apidanus, and the Apidanus into the Peneius (*Geography* 9.5.6; see [[peneius]]). The other rises at a spring by Salmone in the Pisatis, in Elis, and falls into the Alpheus; with this river, Strabo says, Tyro is said to have fallen in love, quoting the verse of the Odyssey, because her father Salmoneus was king there, “as Euripides too says in his *Aeolus*” (*Geography* 8.3.32; see [[alpheus]], [[strabo]] and [[euripides]]). Merry explains the double name from Salmoneus' two homes: he lived first in Thessaly and later moved to Elis, where he built Salmone, and so the river by his Elean city was also called Enipeus (on *Odyssey* 11.235). The poem's own words suit neither river well: ἐν προχοῇς can only mean the outfall or mouth of a river, and Poseidon goes back “beneath the billowing sea”, but the Thessalian Enipeus falls into the Peneius, not into the sea (Merry on *Odyssey* 11.242).
