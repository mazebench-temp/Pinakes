Σιμόεις (Simoeis). The name has no clear Greek etymology. The Iliad first names the river in the birth of [[Simoeisius]]: his mother bore him “beside the banks of the Simoeis, coming down from Ida”, and he was named for it (4.474–477). The poplar of the simile that follows lies “by the banks of the river” (4.487; see [[the-poplar]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Simoeis and the Scamander meet in front of Troy. There Hera and Athena leave their chariot, and the Simoeis makes ambrosia grow for their horses to graze (5.773–777). The fighting of Book 6 surges back and forth “between the streams of the Simoeis and the Xanthus” (6.4). It is one of the eight rivers flowing from Ida to the sea that Poseidon and Apollo will turn against the Achaean wall after the war, the river “where many oxhide shields and helmets had fallen in the dust, and the race of men half-divine” (12.17–23). When the gods go to war, Ares shouts to the Trojans now from the height of the citadel, now running along the Simoeis on Callicolone (20.51–53). In Book 21 the Scamander calls to the Simoeis as “dear brother” to help him drown Achilles (21.305–311).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod names “divine Simoeis” among the rivers that Tethys bore to Oceanus (*Theogony* 342). Virgil's Aeneas, caught in the storm, remembers the Simois, which rolls so many shields and helmets and bodies of heroes under its waters (*Aeneid* 1.100–101). In Epirus Andromache pours offerings at Hector's empty tomb by a “false Simois”, a stream named after the river of Troy (*Aeneid* 3.302). The Simoeis is usually identified with the Dümrek Çay, which flows into the Trojan plain from the east, north of Hisarlık.
