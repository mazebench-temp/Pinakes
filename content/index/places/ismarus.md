## The name

Homer names Ismarus twice, both times in *Odyssey* 9. Ἰσμάρῳ stands beside Κικόνεσσι, “near the Cicones, / to Ismarus” (*Odyssey* 9.39–40), and defines the people by their town, as Demodocus' song names an island and then the place in it, “came to Cyprus, / to Paphos” (*Odyssey* 8.362–363; Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 9.39; see [[paphos]]). Ἴσμαρον is the town over which, in the verses on Maron, someone “used to stand guard” (*Odyssey* 9.198). The Cicones, Merry notes, lived on the south coast of Thrace, and Ismarus, their town, lay at the foot of a mountain of the same name; he connects with its name that of the priest Maron and of Maroneia, the later name of the Ciconian city by the lake Ismaris (on *Odyssey* 9.39).

## In Homer

From Ilios the wind brings Odysseus first to the Cicones, to Ismarus, and it is the first city he sacks on the way home: he kills its men, takes their wives and many possessions, and divides them so that no one may go away cheated of his share (*Odyssey* 9.39–42; see [[odysseus]], [[captive-women]] and [[possessions]]). His men stay to drink its wine and slaughter its flocks by the shore, until the Cicones of the inland come against them and kill six men of every ship (*Odyssey* 9.43–61; see [[cicones]] and [[the-raid-on-the-cicones]]). The town is “the sacred citadel of the Cicones”, ἱερὸν πτολίεθρον, the words the proem uses of Troy (*Odyssey* 9.165, 1.2; see [[hieros]] and [[troy]]), and its wine is still in the ships' jars days later (*Odyssey* 9.163–165; see [[two-handled-jar]] and [[wine]]).

Ismarus has a grove of Apollo. Maron, son of Euanthes, the god's priest, lived in the grove “full of trees”, and the raiders protected him with his child and his wife “in reverence”; he gave Odysseus seven talents of gold, a mixing bowl all of silver, and twelve jars of a sweet unmixed wine known only to himself, his wife and one housekeeper, the wine that Odysseus carries up to the Cyclops' cave (*Odyssey* 9.196–211; see [[maron]], [[euanthes]], [[apollo]], [[full-of-trees]] and [[the-cave-of-polyphemus]]). In the verse ὃς Ἴσμαρον ἀμφιβεβήκει, “who used to stand guard over Ismarus” (*Odyssey* 9.198), Merry takes the guardian to be the god, as Chryses calls on Apollo “who stand guard over Chryse” (*Iliad* 1.37): the pluperfect looks back to the time before the city fell, since a god deserts a city whose doom has been decreed (Merry on *Odyssey* 9.198; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 9.198). Aeschylus' Eteocles puts the belief in a line: “they say that the gods of a captured city abandon it” (*Seven against Thebes* 217–218; see [[aeschylus]]).

## Outside Homer

Strabo, going along the Thracian coast beyond Abdera and the lake Bistonis, names “Xanthea, Maroneia and Ismarus, the cities of the Cicones”: Ismarus, he says, is now called Ismara, near Maroneia, and near it the lake Ismaris empties (*Geography* 7 fr. 44; see [[strabo]]). A further fragment, which cites “the Geographer”, adds that the stream flowing from the lake is called Odysseion, and that a hero-shrine of Maron stands there (*Geography* 7 fr. 44a). Herodotus brings Xerxes' army past the Greek cities of the coast, Maroneia, Dicaea and Abdera, and past the lake Ismaris, which lies between Maroneia and Stryme (*Histories* 7.109.1; see [[herodotus]]). Maroneia kept the fame of its wine: in Pliny's day, Merry notes, it was still mixed with eight measures of water to one (on *Odyssey* 9.209, citing Pliny, *Natural History* 14.6). Virgil names the place among the hills to plant with the vine: “iuvat Ismara Baccho / conserere”, “it is a joy to plant Ismarus with Bacchus' vine” (*Georgics* 2.37–38; see [[virgil]]).
