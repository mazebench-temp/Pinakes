summary: The unnamed river of the Phaeacians' land, a place and a god. Its god takes in the shipwrecked Odysseus as a suppliant and brings him safe into its mouth, and at its washing places Nausicaa and her maids find him the next day.
aliases: [the river of the Phaeacians, the river god of Scheria]

The poem never names it. It enters the story at its mouth, on the cliff-bound coast of Scheria, where Odysseus, swimming along outside the breakers, finds a place “smooth, free of rocks, and there was shelter from the wind” (*Odyssey* 5.441–443; see [[scheria]]). It stays in the story through the next day. Up its stream are the washing places to which Nausicaa drives the clothes of the house, where the maids wash them and eat on the river's banks, and where Odysseus washes the brine from his body (*Odyssey* 6.85–97, 6.209–224); and in his own account to Arete it is “the river fallen from Zeus” (διιπετέος ποταμοῖο, *Odyssey* 7.284; see [[fallen-from-zeus]] and [[arete]]).

Like the rivers of the Iliad it is a god as well as a place (see [[rivers]]). Odysseus knows the god in the current itself, prays to him as “lord” without knowing his name, and comes to his stream and to his knees as a suppliant; the god stops his stream and brings him in (*Odyssey* 5.444–453; see [[a-suppliant-is-respected-by-the-gods]] and [[supplication]]). The other river in Homer that acts on a man in its water is the Scamander, which rises against Achilles and nearly drowns him (*Iliad* 21.211–382; see [[scamander]] and [[achilles-and-the-river]]).
