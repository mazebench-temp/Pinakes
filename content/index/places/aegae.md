## The name

Merry takes the name for a word of waves and storms, of the root of ἀίσσω, “dart”, and of ἐπαιγίζω, said of a wind that rushes on (2.148; *Odyssey* 15.293). Artemidorus, he reports, says that big waves are commonly called αἶγες, “goats”, and that the most fearsome sea is called the Aegean; the same root, he thinks, is in Aegina and αἰγιαλός, “beach” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 5.381).

## In Homer

Hera reproaches Poseidon for not grieving over the Danaans as they perish: “Yet they bring you gifts to Helice and Aegae, / many and pleasing; so you should will them victory” (8.203–204; see [[helice]], [[poseidon]] and [[hera]]). He refuses to fight Zeus for them (8.208–211).

Aegae is also the god's home. When Poseidon comes down from the peak of Samothrace to help the Achaeans, the mountains tremble under his feet: “Three strides he took, and with the fourth he reached his goal, / Aegae, where his glorious house is built in the depths of the sea, / golden, gleaming, imperishable forever” (13.20–22). There he yokes his bronze-footed horses in the verses that yoke Zeus' horses for his drive to Ida (13.23–26, the same as 8.41–44 but for the opening words, ἔνθʼ ἐλθών for ὣς εἰπών; see [[chariot-journey]]), and drives over the waves to a cave between Tenedos and Imbros. In the Odyssey, after raising the storm that wrecks Odysseus' raft, he lashes his horses “and came to Aegae, where his glorious house is” (*Odyssey* 5.380–381). The Homeric *Hymn to Poseidon* calls him the god “who holds Helicon and broad Aegae” (line 3).

## Which Aegae

Strabo describes an Aegae in Achaea, the third of the twelve districts of the Achaeans, between Aegeira and Bura, “with a temple of Poseidon”; it was later merged into Aegeira, and in his day it stood empty, its land held by Aegium (8.7.4–5). Another Aegae lay on Euboea, a crossing of 120 stades from Anthedon on the Boeotian coast, with a temple of Aegaean Poseidon on a high mountain (9.2.13). Strabo thinks that Homer means both. The Aegae named with Helice is the Achaean town; the Aegae under the sea, from which Poseidon drives to Troy, is better taken as the Euboean one, from which the Aegean Sea was probably named (8.7.4). The Achaean town suits the pairing with Helice, its neighbor on the Gulf of Corinth; the Euboean one suits the journey from Samothrace to Tenedos. The poem gives no geography of its own, and the question cannot be settled.

Merry, on the Odyssey's verse, prefers the first explanation in the scholia, “Aegae, a city of Achaea”: in the Iliad, he notes, Aegae is coupled with the Achaean Helice, as cities of the cult of Poseidon (8.203; compare 13.21). How the sea raged on that coast he shows from Ovid, who says that if you look for Helice and Buris, the cities of Achaea, you will find them under the water, and sailors still point out the sloping towns with their sunken walls (*Metamorphoses* 15.293–295; see [[ovid]]). Other scholia, he reports, make Aegae a sunken island beyond Euboea, or one near Samothrace (Merry on *Odyssey* 5.381). He also cites Pliny for an Aegae on a steep islet between Tenos and Chios. In the text of Pliny that the Latin Library prints, the place is a rock, “more truly a rock than an island”, between Tenedos and Chios, called Aex, “Goat”, from its likeness to a goat, and it gave its name to the Aegean Sea (*Natural History* 4.51).
