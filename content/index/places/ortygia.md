## In Homer

Homer names Ortygia twice and says nothing of the place itself; both times it goes with the arrows of Artemis. Calypso tells how the gods begrudged Dawn her mortal lover Orion “until in Ortygia chaste Artemis of the golden throne / came upon him with her gentle arrows and killed him” (*Odyssey* 5.123–124; see [[artemis]], [[orion]], [[eos]] and [[gentle-arrows]]). Eumaeus begins the story of his birth with his island: “There is an island called Syrie, if you have heard of it, / above Ortygia, where the turnings of the sun are”, a land of plenty where neither hunger nor sickness comes, but when men grow old Apollo of the silver bow comes with Artemis and kills them with his gentle arrows (*Odyssey* 15.403–411, a plain rendering; see [[apollo]]). The last verse is the verse of Orion's death, with the god for the goddess: οἷς ἀγανοῖς βελέεσσιν ἐποιχόμενος κατέπεφνεν (*Odyssey* 15.411; ἐποιχομένη, 5.124).

The name is formed from ὄρτυξ, “quail” (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 5.123), and Merry reports Welcker's remark that great flights of quails are seen in the islands of the Aegean from May to September.

## Which Ortygia

Merry takes Homer's Ortygia for Delos. The story of Orion confirms it for him, since Apollodorus says that Artemis killed Orion in Delos; the name, he notes, is found in several places, but always joined with the worship of Artemis, and the Syracusan Ortygia, which Völcker saw in this passage, means only that the colonists of Syracuse, in 734 BC, brought the cult of the Delian Artemis to their new home. Syrie “above Ortygia” he takes for the Cycladic island of Syros, west of Rhenaea, and finds this confirmed by the joint guardianship of Artemis and Apollo over the island (*Odyssey* 15.410; Merry on *Odyssey* 5.121, 5.123; see [[apollodorus]]). Whether Ortygia was Delos itself or its twin Rhenaea, across a strait about half a mile wide, he leaves open: the two were once called by one name, and the confusion, he thinks, comes from that.

## Outside Homer

The Homeric *Hymn to Apollo* sets the two births apart: Leto bore “the one in Ortygia, the other in rocky Delos”, Artemis in Ortygia and Apollo in Delos (*Hymn to Apollo* 14–16). Strabo describes Rhenaea as a deserted islet four stades from Delos, where the Delians have their tombs, since no one may be buried or burned on Delos itself, and adds that it “was formerly also called Ortygia” (*Geography* 10.5.5; the sentence follows a remark on Delos, but Merry takes it of Rhenaea). Homer's Syrie, he thinks, is Syros, the island of Pherecydes, and he quotes the verse “There is an island called Syrie, above Ortygia” (*Geography* 10.5.8; see [[strabo]]).

The island of Syracuse was Ortygia too. Strabo describes it as joined to the mainland by a bridge, with the spring Arethusa, which the story made the outlet of the Alpheus, flowing under the sea from the Peloponnese; Pindar calls it “holy resting-place of Alpheus, offshoot of famous Syracuse, Ortygia”, as Strabo quotes him (*Geography* 6.2.4; see [[pindar]]). Eratosthenes, as Strabo reports him, supposed that Hesiod had heard that Odysseus' wanderings were in Sicily and Italy, and so named Aetna, “Ortygia, the islet by Syracuse”, and the Tyrrhenians, places that Homer neither knew nor wished to use (*Geography* 1.2.14; see [[hesiod]]). Near Ephesus, a little above the sea, a grove of every kind of tree, mostly cypress, was called Ortygia; the Ephesians placed the birth of Leto's children there, with the river Cenchrius in which she washed after her labor and a nurse named Ortygia, whom a statue by Scopas showed beside Leto with a child in each arm (*Geography* 14.1.20).
