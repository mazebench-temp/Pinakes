## In Homer

**The cave and its garden.** Hermes, sent by Zeus, comes to the island “which lay far away”, steps out of the sea onto the land and walks on “until he came to the great cave, in which the nymph / of the lovely tresses lived” (*Odyssey* 5.55–58; see [[hermes]], [[ogygia]] and [[calypso]]). The poem describes the place as he sees it:

The ἐσχάρα, “the hearth”, is here perhaps a portable brazier (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 5.59; see [[fire]]). Homer's κέδρος is not the cedar of Lebanon but a juniper, probably *Juniperus oxycedrus*; εὐκέατος is “easily split”, or rather “deftly split” into billets for burning; and θύον is a tree with resinous wood, not incense (Merry on 5.60; see [[cedar]], [[thyine-wood]] and the translation's note on *Odyssey* 5.60). The goddess walks to and fro at her loom with a golden shuttle (see [[loom]] and [[weaving]]). The αἴγειρος is probably the aspen, which in Homer grows by water (Merry on 5.64; see [[trees]]). The birds are owls, hawks and “long-tongued crows / of the sea”, which Merry would render cormorants (on 5.66; see [[birds]]). The vine is a new feature, marked by ἡ δέ: alder, aspen and cypress grow round the cave, but the vine climbs over the cave itself and hangs from its edge, and ἡμερίς is a vine brought into cultivation from the wild (Merry on 5.68, 5.69; see [[vine]]). ὕδατι λευκῷ is “clear” water, or perhaps sunlit, against the dark water of deep wells and shaded pools (Merry on 5.70). The meadows are of violets and parsley; some wrote σίου, “marsh parsley”, for the violet, an emendation that Athenaeus ascribes to King Ptolemy Euergetes (Merry's critical note on 5.72; see [[violet]] and [[parsley]]). The last sentence still belongs to the description and prepares for Hermes (Merry on 5.73): “There the guide, the slayer of Argus, stood and gazed” (*Odyssey* 5.75).

**A goddess' house.** The cave is the nymph's house. The narrator places Odysseus “in the house of the nymph”, and Athena “in the halls of the nymph Calypso” (*Odyssey* 5.6, 5.14; see [[house]]); Calypso goes back “to her house” when she has shown him the trees for his raft (*Odyssey* 5.242), and offers him to “keep this house with me” and be immortal (*Odyssey* 5.208–209). Within are the fire and the loom, a shining, glossy throne for a guest, a table for ambrosia and nectar, slave women to serve her, and an inner part, μυχός, where she and Odysseus lie together (*Odyssey* 5.59–62, 5.86, 5.92–93, 5.199, 5.226; see [[throne]], [[table]] and [[ambrosia]]). By night he sleeps beside her there “in the hollow caves, unwilling beside the willing”, and by day he sits on the rocks and the beaches looking out over the sea (*Odyssey* 5.154–158; see [[hollow-caves]]).

## Elsewhere in the Odyssey

The poem names the cave before it shows it. In the proem Odysseus is held back by the lady nymph Calypso “in her hollow caves, longing for him to be her husband” (*Odyssey* 1.14–15), and Odysseus uses the same half-verses when he tells the Phaeacians of her, and when he tells Penelope how she kept him in her hollow caves, cared for him and promised to make him immortal and ageless all his days (*Odyssey* 9.29–30, 23.333–337, plain renderings). Other goddesses have such houses. Circe's house is built of polished stone in a glen, and she too sings as she walks to and fro at a great loom (*Odyssey* 10.210–211, 10.221–223, plain renderings; see [[circe]]). In the cave of the Naiads on Ithaca there are mixing bowls and jars of stone, where bees store honey, and long looms of stone on which the nymphs weave sea-purple cloth, a wonder to see, with water that flows forever (*Odyssey* 13.102–112, a plain rendering; see [[nymphs]]).

## Outside Homer

Aelian, listing the names by which the ancients sang parts of the Odyssey on their own, gives “the cave of Calypso” among them, next to “the events about the raft” (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14, as [[the-title-of-odyssey-3]] gives the list; see [[the-title-of-odyssey-5]] and [[the-building-of-the-raft]]).
