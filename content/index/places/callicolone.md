## The name

Καλλικολώνη = καλή, “beautiful”, + κολώνη, “hill, mound”: “Beauty Hill”. κολώνη is the word for the steep mound of Batieia in front of the city (2.811) and for the steep hill of Thryoessa (11.711; see [[batieia]] and [[thryum]]). Walter Leaf finds the sudden naming of the place, as though it were well known, a difficulty that nothing will evade, since it recurs only once more (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 20.53).

## In Homer

When the gods come into the battle, Ares shouts to the Trojans “from the topmost height of the city, / and now running beside the Simoeis on Callicolone” (20.52–53; see [[ares]] and [[simoeis]]). Then, when Poseidon has led the gods of the Achaean side to the wall of Heracles, the others take their seats on the hill:

{{quote:09e60ce4-b2d0-5390-a33f-465f1860a539}}

ἐπʼ ὀφρύσι Καλλικολώνης, “on the brows of Callicolone”, is the only place in Homer where ὀφρύς, the eyebrow, is the brow of a hill; elsewhere ἐπʼ ὀφρύσι is the phrase of Zeus' nod, “with his dark brows” (1.528). The two parties of gods sit apart on either side, the Achaeans' gods on the wall of Heracles and the Trojans' gods on the hill by the Simoeis, as spectators of the war they have entered (see [[the-battle-of-the-gods]], [[the-wall-of-heracles]], [[apollo]] and [[watching-the-war]]).

The text is uncertain at 20.53. θέων, “running”, goes with the words that follow; Aristarchus read θεῶν, “on Callicolone of the gods”, which Leaf finds hardly credible, and he reports an ancient note that a place on Ida was so called, which he takes for a deduction from this line (on 20.53; see the translation's note on 20.53).

## Outside Homer

Strabo, describing the Trojan plain after Demetrius of Scepsis, puts Callicolone ten stadia above the village of the Ilians, where the old Ilium was believed to have stood, as a hill past which the Simoeis flows five stadia away, and forty stadia from the Ilium of his day. Ares' shouts from the citadel and from the Simoeis and Callicolone (20.51–53, quoted with ὦρτο, “rose up”, for αὖε, “shouted”) are then reasonable for the old site, since the battle could not have stretched over forty stadia from the new one (*Geography* 13.1.35; see [[strabo]]). Leaf thinks all attempts to identify the spot futile, and reports that tradition made it the scene of the judgment of Paris, adding that whether the name caused the identification or arose from it cannot be said (on 20.53).

The Suda calls Callicolone “a place on Ida” and quotes the half-verse with θεῶν, “of the gods”, the reading that Leaf reports from Aristarchus (κ 221).
