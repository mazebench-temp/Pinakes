## In Homer

Homer names Paphos only at the end of Demodocus' song of Ares and Aphrodite (see [[the-song-of-ares-and-aphrodite]]). When Hephaestus looses the bonds, Ares goes off to Thrace, “and she, laughter-loving Aphrodite, came to Cyprus, / to Paphos, where she has a precinct and a fragrant altar” (*Odyssey* 8.361–363; see [[aphrodite]], [[thrace]] and [[cyprus]]). The verse-end τέμενος βωμός τε θυήεις, “a precinct and a fragrant altar”, marks a god's holy ground: Zeus has the same on Gargarus, and the river Spercheius at his springs (8.48, 23.148; see [[gargarus]], [[spercheius]], [[fragrant]] and [[altar]]). There the Graces bathe and anoint her and clothe her in lovely garments (*Odyssey* 8.364–366; see [[graces]] and [[the-adornment-of-a-goddess]]). The Iliad does not name the town, and calls the goddess Κύπρις, “the Cyprian”, only in *Iliad* 5 (see [[cypris]]).

## Outside Homer

The Homeric *Hymn to Aphrodite* sends the goddess there in the Odyssey's verses: she came to Cyprus and went into her fragrant temple, “at Paphos, where she has a precinct and a fragrant altar”, and there the Graces bathed her and anointed her with immortal oil, before she left fragrant Cyprus for Ida and Anchises (*Hymn* 5.58–68, a plain rendering). Strabo distinguishes two towns. Old Paphos, Palaepaphos, stood about ten stades above the sea, with an anchorage and the ancient sanctuary of the Paphian Aphrodite; Paphos, founded by Agapenor, had a harbor and well-built sanctuaries, sixty stades from it by land, and every year men and women from the other cities as well went along that road to Old Paphos in festival (*Geography* 14.6.3; see [[strabo]] and [[agapenor]]). The Cypriots told Herodotus that their sanctuary of the goddess was founded from the temple of Heavenly Aphrodite at Ascalon in Syria, the oldest of all her temples (*Histories* 1.105.3; see [[herodotus]]).

The Latin poets keep Homer's geography. In Virgil, Venus goes away through the air to Paphos and gladly sees again her own seat, where she has a temple and a hundred altars warm with Sabaean incense (*Aeneid* 1.415–417; see [[virgil]]). Ovid, retelling the song of Demodocus, ends it with the two lovers' departures: “Mars Thracen occupat, illa Paphon”, “Mars makes for Thrace, she for Paphos” (*Ars amatoria* 2.588; see [[ovid]]).
