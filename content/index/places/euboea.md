The Catalogue crosses the strait in a single line. The Locrians live "across from holy Euboea" (ἱερῆς Εὐβοίης; [[hieros]]), and the next entry begins with the island itself (2.535–536). Its people are the [[Abantes]], led by [[Elephenor]] with forty ships. Its towns are [[Chalcis]] and [[Eretria]] in the middle of the island, [[Histiaea]], [[Cerinthus]] and [[Dium]] in the north, and [[Carystus]] and [[Styra]] in the south (2.537–539). [[Aulis]], where the fleet gathered, faces Chalcis across the narrowest point of the strait, the Euripus.

## Elsewhere in Homer

Nestor, sailing home from Troy, crossed the open sea from Lesbos to Geraestus, the southern cape of Euboea, and sacrificed to Poseidon there (*Odyssey* 3.169–179; see [[geraestus]]). For the Phaeacians it is the farthest of lands, Alcinous says, though their ships once carried Rhadamanthys there to visit Tityus and came back the same day (*Odyssey* 7.321–326; see [[the-voyage-of-rhadamanthus]], [[phaeacians]], [[rhadamanthus]] and [[tityus]]).

## Outside Homer

The Homeric *Hymn to Apollo* calls it ναυσικλειτή, “famed for ships” (*Hymn to Apollo* 31, 219), a word Homer gives only to the Phaeacian Dymas (*Odyssey* 6.22; see [[famed-for-ships]]). The name was later explained as "the land of good cattle" (εὖ, "well", + βοῦς, "ox"); the etymology is uncertain. In the eighth century BC Chalcis and Eretria led the Greek settlements in Italy and Sicily and in the north Aegean, and the Euboean alphabet, carried to Cumae, became the ancestor of the Etruscan and Latin alphabets. The two cities fought each other in the Lelantine War, which Thucydides says drew in the rest of Greece on one side or the other (1.15.3). Hesiod won a tripod for song at Chalcis, at the funeral games of Amphidamas (*Works and Days* 654–657). At Lefkandi, between Chalcis and Eretria, a long building of the tenth century BC covered the rich burials of a man and a woman, with horses; it is one of the most important sites of the early Iron Age.
