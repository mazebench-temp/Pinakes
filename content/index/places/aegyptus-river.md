## The name

Homer gives the river the name of its land, Αἴγυπτος, and never calls it the Nile (see [[egypt]]). It is “Aegyptus, the river fallen from Zeus”, Αἰγύπτοιο διιπετέος ποταμοῖο (*Odyssey* 4.477, 4.581), with the verse-end the Iliad gives the Spercheius and the Scamander (16.174, 21.268; see [[fallen-from-zeus]], [[spercheius]] and [[scamander]]). In the false tales of the later books Odysseus comes on the fifth day out of Crete to “the fair-flowing Aegyptus” and halts his ships “in the river Aegyptus” (*Odyssey* 14.257–258, 17.427, plain renderings). Where the poem says only Αἰγύπτῳ, “in Egypt”, the land or the river may be meant: Merry doubts which it is when the gods hold Menelaus there (*Odyssey* 4.351), but has no doubt that the two verses of Proteus' command name the river (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 4.352).

Νεῖλος, the Nile, first appears in Hesiod, among the rivers that Tethys bore to Oceanus (*Theogony* 337–338; see [[hesiod]] and [[oceanus]]).

## Outside Homer

Strabo answers the charge that Homer knew neither the many mouths of the Nile nor even its name, though Hesiod names it: the name, he says, was probably not yet in use in Homer's day, and those who told the poet of “the river Aegyptus and the land and Egyptian Thebes and Pharos” would hardly have been ignorant of the mouths (*Geography* 1.2.22; see [[strabo]], [[thebes-in-egypt]] and [[pharos]]). He finds the river's floods and silt implied in what the poet says of Pharos (1.2.23, 1.2.30), and he explains the epithet: Homer calls all rivers “fallen from Zeus”, since all are filled by rain, and the Nile, filled beyond all torrents, is so twice over (1.2.30). Eustathius, as Merry reports him, took the epithet as “rain-fed”, since the Nile is filled by the heavy summer rains in Ethiopia, “as Aristotle and Eudoxus say” (Merry on *Odyssey* 4.477). Euripides' *Helen* opens on “these streams of the Nile”, which water the plain of Egypt with melting white snow “instead of the rain of Zeus” (*Helen* 1–3; see [[euripides]]).

Herodotus called the land the river's gift: the Egypt to which the Greeks sail is land the Egyptians have gained, “the gift of the river” (Herodotus 2.5.1; see [[herodotus]]). Diodorus, Merry reports, gives the river's older names: first Oceane, then Aetos, “the Eagle”, after a flood that burst out, and later Aegyptus, from the king who ruled the land (Merry on *Odyssey* 4.477, citing Diodorus 1.19).
