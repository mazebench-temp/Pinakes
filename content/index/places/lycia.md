τηλόθεν, "from far away": Lycia is the southern limit of Homer's Trojan world. The Iliad knows it from the story of Bellerophon, who reaches "Lycia and the flowing Xanthus" (6.172), and from Sarpedon's words about the wife and son he left there (5.479–481). When Sarpedon dies, Sleep and Death carry his body home "to the rich land of broad Lycia" (16.673–683).

The name raises a question in the case of [[Pandarus]]. His men from Zeleia are called Trojans, but he speaks of setting out "from Lycia" (5.105), and Aeneas says that no one in Lycia is his better (5.173). Ancient and modern readers have supposed either a second Lycia in the Troad or a looser use of the name; the poem does not settle it.
