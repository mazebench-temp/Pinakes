Σιδονίηθεν, “from Sidon” (6.291), is formed from Σιδονίη, “the land of Sidon”. The Iliad names the place only here: the women whose robes Hecuba keeps came from it with Alexander, on the voyage that brought Helen to Troy (see [[sidonians]], [[paris]] and [[the-abduction-of-helen]]). It does not say why he went there. The Odyssey has “well-peopled Sidon” (Σιδονίην εὖ ναιομένην), the port for which the Phoenicians of Odysseus' false tale sail away (*Odyssey* 13.285; see [[phoenicians]]), and “Sidon, rich in bronze” (Σιδῶνος πολυχάλκου), the home of the Phoenician woman who carried off the child Eumaeus (*Odyssey* 15.425, a plain rendering). Homer never names Tyre.

## Outside Homer

Strabo, coming to Sidon and Tyre in his account of Phoenicia, says that the poets have made more of Sidon, “and Homer does not even mention Tyre”; Sidon, he adds, lies on a well-formed harbor of the mainland (*Geography* 16.2.22; see [[strabo]]). In the *Cypria*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, a storm sent by Hera drove Alexander to Sidon, and he took the city. [[Herodotus]] quotes 6.289–292 to show that Homer knew of the lovers' wanderings, since Sidon is Phoenician, the Phoenicians live in Syria, and Syria borders on Egypt; the *Cypria*, which brought them from Sparta to Troy in three days, cannot then be Homer's (Herodotus 2.116–117; see [[helen-in-egypt]]). In [[apollodorus|Apollodorus]] the storm forces them into Sidon, and Alexander lingers in Phoenicia and Cyprus for fear of pursuit (*Epitome* 3.4). Sidon is modern Saida, in Lebanon.
