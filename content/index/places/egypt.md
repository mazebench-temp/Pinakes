## The name

Homer uses Αἴγυπτος for the river as well as the land. Menelaus has to go back “to the water of Aegyptus, the river fallen from Zeus” (διιπετέος ποταμοῖο, *Odyssey* 4.477, 4.581), and Odysseus in his false tale beaches his ships “in the river Aegyptus” (*Odyssey* 14.258, 17.427); the river has its own entry (see [[aegyptus-river]]). Homer never names the Nile; Νεῖλος first appears in Hesiod (*Theogony* 337–338; see [[hesiod]]). The Egyptians are Αἰγύπτιοι, and the adjective Αἰγύπτιος, “Egyptian”, is also the name of an old hero of Ithaca (*Odyssey* 2.15; see [[aegyptius]]).

## In the Iliad

The land is named once, in an adjective: Achilles would not be persuaded by “all that comes to Thebes in Egypt, where the most treasures lie stored in the houses” (9.381–382; see [[thebes-in-egypt]]). In the Iliad Egypt stands only for wealth.

## In the Odyssey

Egypt belongs to the stories of homecoming. Menelaus was driven there by storm with five of his ships, and gathered wealth among men of foreign speech while Aegisthus plotted at home (*Odyssey* 3.299–303); he wandered to Cyprus and Phoenicia “and the Egyptians” (*Odyssey* 4.83). His palace holds Egyptian gifts: the silver bathtubs, tripods and gold of Polybus of Thebes, and the drugs that Helen had from Polydamna, wife of Thon, a woman of Egypt, where the earth bears the most drugs and every man is a healer (*Odyssey* 4.125–132, 4.227–232; see [[thebes-in-egypt]]). The gods held him back in Egypt, on the island of Pharos “in front of Egypt”, because he had not offered them hecatombs, until Proteus, the Old Man of the Sea, “of Egypt”, told him to go back to the river and sacrifice (*Odyssey* 4.351–586; see [[pharos]] and [[proteus]]). Odysseus, in disguise, twice tells of a raid on Egypt: his men plundered the fields of the Egyptians, and the Egyptians came out at dawn and killed or enslaved them. In the tale he tells Eumaeus he saved himself by clasping the king's knees and stayed seven years, growing rich (Od. 14.245–286); in the one he tells the suitors he was handed over to a guest-friend from Cyprus (Od. 17.426–444). Antinous answers with a threat: let him keep away from the table, or he will soon come to “a bitter Egypt and Cyprus” (Od. 17.446–448).

## Outside Homer

Herodotus devoted his second book to Egypt (see [[herodotus]] and [[helen-in-egypt]]). He reports that in former times the name Egypt belonged to Thebes (Herodotus 2.15).
