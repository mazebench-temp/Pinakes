## In Homer

Circe places the groves at the end of the voyage across Oceanus, where Odysseus is to beach his ship and go on alone:

{{quote:c16dde3c-bc9b-5535-abc6-3804e4235da0}}

An ἄλσος is a grove belonging to a god, as the grove of poplars that Athena has on Scheria, with a spring and a meadow (*Odyssey* 6.291–292; see [[the-grove-of-athena]]), Poseidon's grove at Onchestus (*Iliad* 2.506), Apollo's grove at Ismarus (*Odyssey* 9.200; see [[ismarus]]), and the groves of the nymphs, from which Circe's handmaids are born (*Odyssey* 10.350; *Iliad* 20.8; see [[nymphs]]). Persephone's groves stand on the farther shore of Oceanus, at the edge of the world of the dead, and their trees are the trees of water, poplars and willows (see [[persephone]], [[oceanus]], [[willow]] and [[trees]]). ὠλεσίκαρποι, “that lose their fruit”, is found only here: W. W. Merry quotes Pliny, who says that the willow sheds its seed before it is fully ripe and was therefore called by Homer “fruit-losing”, and the scholia, which say that barren things are fitting for the dead; he thinks that the notion of a waste of life made the willow seem a suitable tree for Persephone's groves (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 10.510; Pliny, *Natural History* 16.26, as Merry quotes it). The shore is λάχεια, “of good soil”, the word for the island of goats (*Odyssey* 9.116; see [[the-island-of-goats]]); the scholia say it is not to be read ἐλάχεια, “very small”, since it is not likely that Persephone's precinct should be called that (Merry's critical note on *Odyssey* 10.509; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 10.509).

When the voyage is told in the next book, Odysseus says that they ran the ship ashore and went along the stream of Oceanus to the place that Circe had shown them, but the groves are not named again (*Odyssey* 11.20–22).
