## Name

Ἠλύσιον πεδίον (Ēlysion pedion), “the Elysian plain”. Merry suggests that the adjective is formed like ἤλυθον, “came, went”, so that it is the place “where men go”, and reports that Gladstone compared the Egyptian Aalu, a field of “spirits of light” (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.563). Apion derived the name from ἰλύς, the mud of the Nile, so that he may have read Ἰλύσιον (Merry's critical note on 4.563, from Eustathius; see the translation's note on 4.563).

## In Homer

The plain lies at πείρατα γαίης, “the limits of the earth” (*Odyssey* 4.563), the words of the journey that Hera pretends to make to Oceanus and Tethys, “the limits of the all-nourishing earth” (*Iliad* 14.200, 14.301; see [[oceanus]]). It is a place for the living. Menelaus will not “die and meet his fate” in Argos; the immortals will send him there, because he has Helen as his wife and is the son-in-law of Zeus (*Odyssey* 4.561–564, 4.569; see [[menelaus]] and [[helen]]). Merry calls it the dwelling set apart for the good, not in Hades but on the surface of the earth, though in the far west. Homer does not say whether it is an island or a mainland, and the favored heroes whom the gods carry there alive keep their bodies, unlike the dead in Hades, who are εἴδωλα καμόντων, “the phantoms of the dead” (*Iliad* 23.72; Merry on 4.563; see [[eidolon]]). The West Wind that blows there shows that the plain belongs to the land of the living, Merry notes, while at “the lowest limits / of earth and sea” Iapetus and Cronus “have no joy in the rays of Hyperion the Sun / or in the winds” (*Iliad* 8.478–481; Merry on 4.567; see [[zephyrus]] and [[tartarus]]). [[rhadamanthus|Rhadamanthys]], the son of Zeus, is the one inhabitant named (*Odyssey* 4.564).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells of heroes whom Zeus, son of Cronus, gave a life and dwelling apart from men and settled “at the limits of the earth” (ἐς πείρατα γαίης), where they live with hearts free of care in the Isles of the Blessed beside deep-eddying Oceanus, happy heroes, for whom the grain-giving earth bears honey-sweet fruit three times a year (*Works and Days* 167–173; see [[hesiod]]). Merry compares the passage, and reports that Pindar too speaks of the island of the blessed, round which the breezes of Ocean blow (on 4.563 and 4.567, citing *Olympian* 2). At the end of Euripides' *Helen* the Dioscuri announce that it is fated by the gods for “the wandering Menelaus” to dwell in the island of the blessed (*Helen* 1676–1677; see [[euripides-helen]]). Its later history is told under [[elysium-and-the-isles-of-the-blessed]].

The *Problems* ascribed to Aristotle quote the verse of the West Wind in another form, ἀλλʼ αἰεὶ ζεφύροιο διαπνείουσιν ἀῆται, “but always the breezes of the west wind blow through”, and ask why the west wind seems to bring fair weather and to be the sweetest of the winds, “as Homer too says in the Elysian plain”. The answer is its temperate mixture: it is neither hot, like the winds from the south and the east, nor cold, like those from the north, but lies on the border between them, and it blows most in spring (*Problems* 26.31; see [[aristotle]] and the translation's note on 4.567).
