## In Homer

{{quote:b545f890-edba-5b24-b03c-489653c5d240}}

The morning star is a mark of time in Homer, the last light of the night. In the Iliad it ends the night in which the winds blew on the pyre of Patroclus and Achilles poured wine on the ground and mourned: ἦμος δʼ ἑωσφόρος εἶσι φόως ἐρέων ἐπὶ γαῖαν, “When the morning star goes forth to herald the light over the earth”, saffron-robed Dawn spreads over the sea after it, and the pyre dies down (23.217–228; see [[the-funeral-of-patroclus]], [[saffron-robed]] and [[eos]]). ἑωσφόρος, “dawn-bringer”, from ἕως, “dawn”, and φέρω, “bring”, is found only here in Homer, and it is an Attic, not an epic, form, which Walter Leaf took for a sign of a late passage (see the translation's note on 23.226–227).

In the Odyssey the star has no name. It is “that brightest star … which most of all / comes to announce the light of early-born Dawn”, and at its rising the Phaeacian ship comes in to Ithaca with Odysseus asleep on board (*Odyssey* 13.93–95). Each time the star is a messenger, ἐρέων, “to herald”, and ἀγγέλλων, “to announce”, and each time the hour is told in the pair of clauses with which Homer gives a time of day, “when … then” (ἦμος … τῆμος, 23.226–228; εὖτε … τῆμος, *Odyssey* 13.93–95).

The star of evening is the fairest star of the Iliad: as Achilles closes with Hector, the point of his spear shines “as a star goes among the stars in the dead of night, / the evening star, which stands in the sky the fairest star of all” (22.317–318; see [[the-evening-star]]). Homer does not say that the two are one star.

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod the morning star is a child of Dawn. To Astraeus she bore the strong-hearted winds, Zephyrus, Boreas and Notus, “and after them the Early-born bore the star Eosphorus, / and the gleaming stars with which the sky is crowned” (*Theogony* 378–382; see [[hesiod]], [[boreas]] and [[notus]]). The last half-verse is that of the Shield of Achilles, on which Hephaestus made “all the constellations with which the sky is crowned” (18.485; see [[sky]]).

The Byzantine lexicon called the Suda defines Ἑωσφόρος as the eastern star of the early morning, also called φωσφόρος, “light-bringer”, and says that in Homer it is this star, not the sun, that brings the day (Suda ε 1910). It reports that Parmenides first found out that Hesperus and Phosphorus are the same star, or, as others say, [[pythagoras|Pythagoras]] (ε 3187). And glossing the Iliad's evening star, “the fairest”, it notes that elsewhere Homer calls Eosphorus φαάντατος, “the brightest”, the word of the Odyssey's star (ε 3188; *Odyssey* 13.93).

The Romans called it Lucifer. [[virgil|Virgil]] gives it to young Pallas, riding out to his first war in the middle of the column: he is like Lucifer, “whom Venus loves above the other fires of the stars”, when, bathed in the wave of Ocean, he lifts his sacred face in the sky and dissolves the darkness (*Aeneid* 8.587–591; see [[the-evening-star]]).
