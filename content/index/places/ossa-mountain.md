## The mountain

Ossa rises in Magnesia, in eastern Thessaly, south of Olympus across the narrow glen of Tempe, through which the Peneius flows to the sea; Pelion lies beyond it to the south (see [[olympus]], [[pelion]] and [[peneius]]). Strabo reports the old account that the plain of Thessaly was once a lake, until an earthquake opened the rift now called Tempe, split Ossa from Olympus, and let the Peneius break through to the sea (*Geography* 9.5.2; see [[strabo]]). The mountain's name has the letters of ὄσσα, “rumor”, the voice that Homer also makes a goddess, Rumor, the messenger of Zeus (see [[ossa]]).

## In Homer

The Iliad never names Ossa, and the Odyssey only once. Iphimedeia's sons by Poseidon, Otus and Ephialtes, the tallest and handsomest men the earth ever reared after Orion, threatened the immortals on Olympus with war: “They were eager to set Ossa on Olympus, and on Ossa / Pelion with its quivering leaves, so that heaven might be scaled”, and they would have done it if they had lived to manhood, but Apollo killed them (*Odyssey* 11.305–320; see [[otus]], [[ephialtes]], [[aloeus]] and [[apollo]]). The three mountains stand in their real order, from north to south, and Strabo cites the verse to show that Homer keeps the order of places where it matters, at home or abroad (*Geography* 1.2.20).

## Outside Homer

Strabo found two mountains called Ossa and Olympus in the Pisatis, in Elis, named like those in Thessaly, between which some placed the city of Pisa (*Geography* 8.3.31). The Gigantomachy, the war of the giants against the gods, is not found in Homer, but is already developed in the Hesiodic epic, and W. W. Merry thinks the two verses on the mountains probably came into the Odyssey from some such poem (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 11.315–316; see [[giants]]).
