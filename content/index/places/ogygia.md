## The name

Homer uses Ὠγυγίη like an adjective with νῆσος, “island”: νῆσον ἐς Ὠγυγίην, “to the island of Ogygia” (*Odyssey* 1.85, 7.254, 12.448), and Ὠγυγίη τις νῆσος, “an island, Ogygia” (*Odyssey* 7.244). The name belongs to the Odyssey alone. Hesiod calls the water of the Styx ὠγύγιον, “primeval” (*Theogony* 805–806); whether the island's name was heard in the same way is not known.

## In Homer

Ogygia lies far out in the sea, ἀπόπροθεν εἰν ἁλί (*Odyssey* 7.244). Athena calls it a sea-washed island “where the navel of the sea is”, an island full of trees, the home of the daughter of Atlas (*Odyssey* 1.50–52; see [[sea-washed]] and [[full-of-trees]]). Elsewhere in Homer ὀμφαλός is the navel of a man's body (4.525, 13.568) or the boss of a shield (11.34); only here is it a place.

Odysseus tells the Phaeacians that Calypso lives on the island, that no god or mortal keeps her company, and that the gods brought him there alone on the tenth night, after nine days adrift on the keel of his ship, which Zeus had shattered with his thunderbolt; there he stayed seven years (*Odyssey* 7.244–261, 12.447–450). When Hermes reaches the island he finds Calypso singing at her loom in a great cave, with a wood of alder, poplar and cypress around it, a vine heavy with grapes, four springs of clear water, and soft meadows of violets and parsley, a sight to delight even a god (*Odyssey* 5.55–75; see [[calypsos-cave]]). At the end of the poem Odysseus tells Penelope how he came to the island and the nymph, who kept him in her hollow caves, wanting him for her husband, and promised to make him immortal and ageless (*Odyssey* 23.333–337). See [[calypso]], [[atlas]] and [[hermes]].

## Outside Homer

Strabo, answering Polybius, holds that the poet's verses on the stream of Oceanus, on Ogygia and the navel of the sea, on the daughter of Atlas who lives there, and on the far-off Phaeacians plainly set these places in the Atlantic, as the poet invented them; he quotes the verse as νήσῳ ἐν Ὠγυγίῃ, “on the island Ogygia”, where the text has ἀμφιρύτῃ, “sea-washed” (*Geography* 1.2.18). Apollodorus faulted Callimachus because, though a scholar, he went against Homer's design of setting the wanderings in the Ocean and called Gaudos the island of Calypso and Corcyra Scheria (Strabo, *Geography* 7.3.6, 1.2.37); Strabo places Gaudos, with Melite, off Cape Pachynus in Sicily (6.2.11). The navel of the earth, for later Greeks, was Delphi, where a navel-stone was shown in the temple (Strabo, *Geography* 9.3.6).
