## Etymology

Τάρταρος (Tartaros) is masculine in Homer. Hesiod also has a neuter plural, Τάρταρα (*Theogony* 119, 841). The word has no Greek etymology.

## In Homer

{{quote:3a0e2a1b-df4f-5bd7-ab27-d9d570a06f03}}

Homer names Tartarus itself only in Book 8, once at dawn and once at nightfall. In the morning it is Zeus' threat to any god who goes off to help either army. It lies "very far off, where the pit beneath the earth is deepest" (8.14). βέρεθρον, "pit", occurs elsewhere in Homer only of Scylla's den (*Odyssey* 12.94). Its gates are of iron and its threshold of bronze, and it lies "as far beneath Hades as the sky is from the earth" (8.15–16). Above the earth is the sky, below it the realm of Hades, and below that, as far again, Tartarus. Its epithet ἠερόεις, "misty, murky" (8.13; see [[misty]]), is the word for "the misty dark" that fell to Hades as his share when the sons of Cronus divided the world (ζόφον ἠερόεντα, 15.191; see [[hades]]).

At nightfall Zeus names it again. Hera may go "to the lowest limits / of earth and sea" in her anger, and he will not care:

{{quote:0eec6ccb-bbef-5d71-886b-fff2591c49d5}}

The prisoners are gods, not dead men: Cronus, whom Zeus overthrew, and Iapetus with him, sitting with no share in the light of the sun or in the winds (see [[iapetus]], [[cronus]], [[hyperion]] and [[fall-of-cronus]]). The rest of the Iliad knows them. Zeus "set Cronus beneath the earth and the barren sea" (14.203–204). Sleep asks Hera to swear an oath that "the gods below, who are around Cronus" may witness (14.274), and she names "all the gods beneath Tartarus, who are called Titans" (τοὺς ὑποταρταρίους οἳ Τιτῆνες καλέονται, 14.278–279). Zeus tells Apollo that if Poseidon had not given way, even those gods would have heard of their fight (15.222–225). The Odyssey never names Tartarus. In Homer the dead go down to the house of Hades, into Erebus, and Tartarus lies far below them (see [[erebus]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod gives Tartarus a place among the first things. First came Chaos, then Earth, "and misty Tartarus in the depth of the wide-pathed earth", and Eros (*Theogony* 116–120). It is the prison of the Titans. The Hundred-Handers overwhelm them with rocks and bind them under the earth, "as far beneath the earth as the sky is from the earth" (τόσσον ἔνερθʼ ὑπὸ γῆς, ὅσον οὐρανός ἐστʼ ἀπὸ γαίης, 717–721), nearly the line Zeus speaks at 8.16, where the measure is taken from Hades. An anvil of bronze would fall nine days and nights from the sky to the earth, and nine more from the earth to Tartarus (722–725). A bronze wall runs around it, night is poured around its neck in three rows, Poseidon has set bronze doors on it, and the Hundred-Handers guard it for Zeus (726–735). Hesiod's gates are of marble and its threshold of bronze (ἔνθα δὲ μαρμάρεαί τε πύλαι καὶ χάλκεος οὐδός, 811); Homer's gates are of iron (8.15). Earth bore her last child, Typhoeus, to Tartarus, and Zeus hurled him into it (821–822, 868; see [[typhoeus]] and [[hundred-handed]]). In the Homeric *Hymn to Hermes* Apollo threatens the infant thief in Zeus' words: "I will take you and hurl you into misty Tartarus" (4.256; compare 8.13).

Plato's Socrates, describing the greatest of the chasms that run through the whole earth, quotes 8.14 and adds that Homer elsewhere and many other poets have called it Tartarus; all the rivers flow into it and out of it again (*Phaedo* 112a). For Plato it is a place of punishment for souls. The unjust go to "the prison of punishment and justice, which they call Tartarus" (*Gorgias* 523b), and the incurable are hurled into it, "from where they never come out" (*Phaedo* 113e). Virgil's Sibyl tells Aeneas that Tartarus goes down twice as far as the sky rises to Olympus (*Aeneid* 6.577–579). Strabo guessed that Homer, hearing of Tartessus in the far west, where the light of the sun falls into Oceanus (he quotes 8.485–486), named the lowest place under the earth after it (3.2.12; see [[strabo]]).
