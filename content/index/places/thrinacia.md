## The name

Θρινακίη is named four times, always as an island, Θρινακίη νῆσος. The ancients identified it with Sicily, taking the name for Τρινακρία, “three-cornered”, from τρεῖς ἄκραι, “three capes” (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 12.127): Strabo explains that Sicily, triangular in shape, was formerly called Trinacria and later, for a more pleasing sound, Thrinacia (*Geography* 6.2.1; see [[sicily]] and [[strabo]]). Merry thinks it more likely that the name comes from θρῖναξ, “a trident, a three-pronged fork”, and may tie the island to some legend of Poseidon, perhaps that he raised it from the sea bed with his trident; Düntzer took it to mean “windswept” (Merry on *Odyssey* 12.127; see [[poseidon]]).

## In Homer

Teiresias names the island first, in the underworld: Odysseus and his companions may still come home, though suffering, if they leave unharmed the cattle and fat sheep of the Sun that they will find grazing on it (*Odyssey* 11.104–115; see [[teiresias]] and [[helios]]). Circe gives the same warning in the same verses, and says what the herds are: seven herds of cattle and as many flocks of sheep, fifty in each, which bear no young and never die, herded by the goddesses Phaethusa and Lampetie, whom Neaera bore to Hyperion the Sun and “settled on the island of Thrinacia, to dwell far off / and guard their father's sheep and his cattle with curving horns” (*Odyssey* 12.127–141, a plain rendering; see [[circe]] and [[hyperion]]).

The island Odysseus reaches after Scylla and Charybdis is “the flawless island of the god”, “the island of the Sun, who gladdens mortals” (*Odyssey* 12.261, 12.269, a plain rendering), with a hollow harbor near sweet water and a hollow cave, where “there were the fair dancing floors and seats of the nymphs” (*Odyssey* 12.305–306, 12.317–318, a plain rendering). No one lives there but the nymphs and the herds; the Sun delights in his cattle “as I went up into the starry sky / and when I turned back again from the sky toward the earth” (*Odyssey* 12.379–381, a plain rendering), and when the ship leaves it “no other / land appeared, but only sky and sea” (*Odyssey* 12.403–404, a plain rendering). In his false tale to Penelope, Odysseus says that the hero lost his companions and his ship on the way from the island of Thrinacia, because Zeus and the Sun were angry with him, since his companions had killed the Sun's cattle (*Odyssey* 19.273–277). For the story see [[the-cattle-of-helios]].

## Where it lies

Merry would place it to the northwest of Scylla's rock and at no great distance from it, comparing the South Wind that holds the ship there for a month and the South Wind that carries Odysseus back to Charybdis in a single night after the wreck (*Odyssey* 12.325–326, 12.427–430). It “belongs still to fable-land”, he says, and is represented as uninhabited, except by the herds of Helios and the nymphs that tend them (on *Odyssey* 12.127). The identification with Sicily, which Merry cites from Strabo and from Thucydides, *History* 6.2, belongs with the ancient attempts to set the wanderings in the real West, which Strabo himself defended (see [[strabo]] and [[aeolia]]).
