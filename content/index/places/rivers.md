## The word

ποταμός is a river, and Homer names many. Their epithets are of water and of the gods: they are “fair-flowing” and “swift-flowing”, “eddying”, and “fallen from Zeus”, fed by his rain (see [[fair-flowing]], [[swift-flowing]], [[eddying]] and [[fallen-from-zeus]]).

## Places

Rivers place peoples and towns. In the Catalogue men live “beside the shining river Cephisus” (2.522), a bride is brought “from Ephyra, from the river Selleis” (2.659), and the Paphlagonians have their homes around the river Parthenius (2.854; see [[cephisus]], [[selleis-ephyra]] and [[parthenius]]). Troy has two rivers, “where the Simoeis and the Scamander join their streams” (5.773–774; see [[simoeis]] and [[scamander]]), and the rivers that flow from Ida to the sea are named together when Poseidon and Apollo lead their might against the Achaean wall after the war, the Rhesus and Heptaporus, the Caresus and Rhodius, the Granicus and Aesepus, the Scamander and the Simoeis; Apollo turns their mouths together, and for nine days they pour against the wall while Zeus rains, until Poseidon has washed it away and turned them back into their channels (12.17–33; see [[the-achaean-wall]] and [[rhesus-river]]).

## Gods

A river is also a god. Agamemnon calls on Zeus, the Sun, “and rivers and earth” to witness the oaths of the duel (3.276–280; see [[oath]]). A river can found a family: Diocles' lineage was from the river Alpheus (5.544–545), and Menesthius was the son of “Spercheius, the river fallen from Zeus”, by Peleus' daughter Polydora (16.173–176; see [[alpheus]], [[spercheius]] and [[menesthius]]). The Trojan river is the eddying Xanthus, “whom immortal Zeus begot” (14.433–434). Rivers receive sacrifice: Nestor's Pylians offered a bull to Alpheus (11.726–728), and in later books Achilles mocks the Trojans for the bulls and live horses they gave to the Scamander (21.130–132), and cuts off the hair that Peleus had vowed to the Spercheius (23.141–151). When Zeus summons the gods to assembly in *Iliad* 20, no river stays away except Oceanus (20.7–9), and Oceanus is the source of them all, “from whom all rivers and all the sea and all springs and deep wells flow” (21.195–197; see [[oceanus]]).

## In the similes

The river in the similes is force. Diomedes sweeps over the plain like a river in flood that bursts the dikes, and Ajax drives the Trojans as a flood swollen by the rains of Zeus comes down to the plain (5.87–92, 11.492–495; see [[river-in-flood]]); winter torrents meet in a gorge (4.452–456; see [[torrents-meeting]]); a river in winter flood thrusts a boulder down a hillside (13.137–139; see [[the-rolling-boulder]]); and in the storm of late summer all the rivers run in flood (16.384–392; see [[the-storm-of-late-summer]]). A traveler stops helpless at a swift river foaming on its way to the sea (5.597–600; see [[man-at-the-river]]), and the shout of battle roars like the sea against the stream at a river's mouth (17.263–266; see [[the-wave-at-the-rivers-mouth]]). Gentler rivers are places of rest and food: the stallion is used to bathing in a fair-flowing river (6.506–508 = 15.263–265; see [[the-stallion]]), and birds feed beside a river when the eagle swoops on them (15.690–692; see [[the-eagle-and-the-birds]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod gives the rivers a genealogy: Tethys bore them to Oceanus, the eddying Rivers, and he names twenty-five, among them the Nile, the Alpheus, the Maeander and the Peneius, and the rivers of Ida that Homer names in *Iliad* 12, the Rhesus, Heptaporus, Granicus, Aesepus, Rhodius, Simoeis and Scamander (*Theogony* 337–345).
