## Etymology

Ἔρεβος (Erebos), genitive Ἐρέβευς (8.368), is an old word for darkness, usually compared with Gothic *riqis*, “darkness”. The adjective ἐρεβεννός, “dark as Erebus, murky” (see [[murky]]), describes night (8.488, 9.474), mist and clouds (5.864, 22.309), and above all the night that covers the eyes of the dying (5.659, 13.580, 22.466).

## In Homer

Erebus is named three times in the Iliad. Athena saved Heracles when Eurystheus sent him to the house of Hades “to bring from Erebus the hound of hateful Hades” (8.367–368; see [[heracles]], [[eurystheus]] and [[hades]]). The Erinys who walks in darkness heard Althaea's curse on her son “out of Erebus” (9.571–572; see [[the-death-of-meleager]]). The two sons of Amisodarus, Sarpedon's companions, killed by two brothers, “went down to Erebus” (16.326–327).

The Odyssey places it toward the west and the dark. Circe tells Odysseus to sacrifice a ram and a black ewe at the edge of the world of the dead, “turning them toward Erebus” (*Odyssey* 10.527–528), and the souls gather to the blood “out of Erebus” (*Odyssey* 11.37, a plain rendering). Ajax, still angry over the arms of Achilles, will not answer him and goes away with the other souls “into Erebus” (*Odyssey* 11.563–564, a plain rendering; see [[ajax]]). Scylla's cave faces “toward the gloom, toward Erebus” (*Odyssey* 12.81, a plain rendering), and in Theoclymenus' vision the suitors' hall is full of ghosts “hastening to Erebus under the gloom” (*Odyssey* 20.355–356, a plain rendering). Erebus is the darkness itself; the realm of the dead is the house of Hades, and Tartarus lies far below it (see [[tartarus]]).

## Outside Homer

In Hesiod, Erebus and black Night come out of Chaos, and Night, lying with Erebus, bears Aether and Day (*Theogony* 123–125). Zeus strikes Menoetius, son of Iapetus, down to Erebus with his thunderbolt (514–516; see [[iapetus]]), and brings the Hundred-Handers up to the light “from Erebus under the earth” (669).
