The palace stands on the height of the city (πόλις ἄκρη), where Athena's temple and the houses of Hector and Paris also stood (6.297, 6.317; see [[pergamus]]). Hector comes to it from the Scaean gates (6.237–242). It is “beautiful” (περικαλλής) and built with “polished colonnades” (ξεστῇς αἰθούσῃσι, 6.243), the words used of the colonnades that Hephaestus built in the house of Zeus (20.11–12). An αἴθουσα is a roofed porch along the courtyard, where guests are given their beds (24.644; Od. 3.399).

Inside are fifty chambers of polished stone for the sons and their wives, and across the courtyard twelve for the daughters and their husbands, Priam's sons-in-law (γαμβροί; see [[in-laws]]). Fifty is the number of Priam's sons (24.495; see [[sons-of-priam]]). τέγεοι (6.248), found only here, is “roofed”, as rendered, or “under the roof”, rooms in an upper story set apart from the sons'.

Here Hecuba meets Hector, leading in Laodice (6.251–252; see [[hecuba]] and [[laodice]]). From the hall she sends her handmaids to gather the old women, and goes down to the fragrant chamber where her Sidonian robes are kept (6.286–295; see [[sidonians]]). In the last book the same words take Priam down to a fragrant chamber of cedar for Hector's ransom (24.191–192; 6.288). See [[house]].

## Elsewhere in Homer

The Trojans hold their assemblies at Priam's doors (2.788, 7.345–346). When Iris comes to him in the last book, he sits in the courtyard among his sons with dung on his head and neck (24.160–165). He drives the Trojans out of the colonnade (24.237–238), prays standing in the middle of the courtyard (24.306), and drives out through the gateway and the echoing colonnade on his way to Achilles (24.322–323).

## Outside Homer

In the *Iliupersis*, as [[proclus|Proclus]] summarizes it, Neoptolemus kills Priam after he has fled to the altar of Zeus Herkeios, Zeus “of the courtyard”; the Odyssey places such an altar in Odysseus' courtyard (Od. 22.334–336). In Euripides' *Trojan Women* Priam lies dead at the steps of that altar (16–17; see [[euripides]]). Virgil shows the palace on its last night: the inner halls laid open, “those fifty bedchambers, so great a hope of grandsons” (*quinquaginta illi thalami, spes tanta nepotum*), and the altar under an ancient laurel in the open court, where Pyrrhus kills Priam (*Aeneid* 2.483–484, 2.503, 2.512–514, 2.550–553; see [[virgil]]).
