Σκάμανδρος (Skamandros). The name is probably not Greek, and its origin is unknown. The river has two names; it is the river “whom the gods call Xanthus, and men Scamander” (20.74; see [[language-of-gods-and-men]]). Its adjective Σκαμάνδριος (“of the Scamander”) names the plain and the meadow where the Achaean army forms up, and men are named after it: the hunter Scamandrius (see [[scamandrius]]) and Hector's son Astyanax, whom Hector calls Scamandrius though the Trojans call him Astyanax (6.402–403).

The Scamander is a god as well as a river. It has a priest and cult among the Trojans, who sacrifice bulls to it and throw live horses into its eddies (21.130–132), and in Book 21 it rises against Achilles and nearly drowns him (21.211–382). The Simoeis joins it on the plain (see [[simoeis]]), and near the city are two springs, one warm and one cold, which the poem calls its sources (22.147–152; see [[the-springs-of-the-scamander]]). The Xanthus of Lycia is a different river (2.877; see [[xanthus-of-lycia]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod names “divine Scamander” last among the rivers that Tethys bore to Oceanus (*Theogony* 345; see [[hesiod]]). Plato's Socrates will not let it be believed that Achilles was “disobedient toward the river, which was a god, and ready to fight it” (*Republic* 3.391b; see [[plato-achilles-and-the-river]]), and Proclus answers that Achilles fought not the god but the visible water that hindered his attack, or one of the local powers (*On the Republic* I 148–149 Kroll; see [[proclus-on-the-battle-of-the-gods]]). Virgil's Neptune remembers how the rivers groaned, choked with the dead whom Achilles killed, and the Xanthus could not find its way out to the sea (*Aeneid* 5.803–808; see [[virgil-the-house-of-aeneas]] and [[achilles-and-the-river]]).

The river is the modern Karamenderes, which crosses the plain west of Hisarlık to reach the sea near the mouth of the Dardanelles.
