## Description

Gargarus is the summit of Ida, Γάργαρον ἄκρον (14.292, 14.352, 15.152; see [[ida]]). The poem never describes the peak, only the god on it. τέμενος βωμός τε θυήεις, "a precinct and a fragrant altar" (8.48), is a formula for a god's holy ground. The river Spercheius has the same at its springs (23.148), and Aphrodite at Paphos (*Odyssey* 8.363). A τέμενος is land "cut off" (τέμνω) and set apart for a god or a king (see [[altar]] and [[fragrant]]).

## In Homer

Zeus' journey ends here at dawn on the second day of battle. He halts his horses, hides them in mist, and sits on the peaks "exulting in his glory, / looking out on the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans" (8.51–52; see [[chariot-journey]] and [[watching-the-war]]). From here he weighs the fates of the two armies, thunders, and sends a blazing flash among the Achaeans (8.69–77; see [[the-scales-of-zeus]] and [[zeus]]). The two verses return in Book 11, where he sits apart from the other gods watching the next day's battle (11.81–82), before he comes down from the sky to the peaks of Ida (11.181–184).

In Book 14 Hera comes to him "on Gargarus, the peak of high Ida" (14.292–293; see [[hera]]), and Zeus lies with her in a golden cloud and sleeps "on the peak of Gargarus" (14.346–353) while Poseidon drives the Trojans back. When he wakes, Apollo and Iris find him there, "and a fragrant cloud was wreathed around him" (15.151–153). Hector used to burn the thighs of oxen for him on the peaks of Ida (22.170–171).

## Outside Homer

Strabo says that Homer, having marked the outermost spurs of Ida with Lectum and Zeleia, rightly names Gargarus as its summit, "and even now a place called Gargarum is pointed out in the upper parts of Ida, from which the present Gargara, an Aeolian city, takes its name" (13.1.5). The town of Gargara lay on a headland of the gulf of Adramyttium (13.1.51).
