## In the Odyssey

In his hut the swineherd Eumaeus, asked how he came to Ithaca, offers the stranger, who is Odysseus, a story for a story: “let us two, drinking and feasting in the hut, / take delight in each other's wretched sorrows / as we remember them; for afterward a man takes delight even in his sorrows, / whoever has suffered very much and wandered much” (*Odyssey* 15.398–401). Then he tells how he was a king's son, stolen by Phoenician traders and sold to Laertes (see [[eumaeus]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]).

## Aristotle's text

Our text ends “whoever has suffered very much and wandered much” (ὅς τις δὴ μάλα πολλὰ πάθῃ καὶ πόλλʼ ἐπαληθῇ). Aristotle has “remembering, one who has suffered much and done much” (μνημένος, ὅστις πολλὰ πάθῃ καὶ πολλὰ ἐόργῃ), which makes memory, his subject, part of the verse.

## Outside Homer

Cicero gives the thought in Latin without Homer's verse. Against the Epicurean rule that the wise man should not remember past evils, he asks what if the memory of past evils is even pleasant, and cites the common saying *Iucundi acti labores*, “pleasant are labors done”, and the line of Euripides that Aristotle sets before Homer's, in his own Latin, *Suavis laborum est praeteritorum memoria*, “sweet is the memory of past labors” (*On Ends* 2.105; see [[sweet-to-remember-toils]] and [[cicero]]). Writing to the historian Lucceius, he hopes that his own misfortunes, not to be wished for in the living, will be pleasant in the reading, “for the untroubled recollection of past pain has delight” (*habet enim praeteriti doloris secura recordatio delectationem*), while to others, who have had no trouble of their own and look on another's misfortunes without pain, even pity is pleasant (*Letters to Friends* 5.12.4–5, plain renderings). The two pleasures are those of the swineherd's night in the hut, the teller's in remembering and the listener's in hearing.
