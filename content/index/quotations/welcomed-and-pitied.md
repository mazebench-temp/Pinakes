## In the Odyssey

At sunset the wagon of Nausicaa reaches the grove of Athena outside the city, and Odysseus sits down there, as she told him, to wait until she has reached her father's house (*Odyssey* 6.291–299, 6.321–322; see [[the-grove-of-athena]] and [[nausicaa]]). At once he prays to the goddess:

{{quote:e2842380-ef94-57e3-b1d3-de8ef2ab1e17}}

The prayer is a reproach as well as a request. Athena “never listened” when “the famous shaker of the earth” was wrecking him (*Odyssey* 6.325–326; see [[poseidon]]), and the poem has said why since its first council of the gods: Poseidon is angry with him for blinding the Cyclops, the god's son, and drives him from his native land (*Odyssey* 1.68–75; see [[polyphemus-the-cyclops]]). Even now Athena will not appear to him face to face, “for she respected / her father's brother” (*Odyssey* 6.329–330; see [[athena]]). φίλον, “welcomed”, is what a stranger hopes to become in a house not his own, and ἐλεεινόν, “pitied”, what a man who has nothing must be to be received there (see [[philos]] and [[pity]]). Odysseus is going to the Phaeacians as a suppliant, and Nausicaa has told him how: he is to pass by her father and throw his arms around the knees of her mother (*Odyssey* 6.310–315; see [[supplication]] and [[arete-queen-of-the-phaeacians]]).

The goddess hears him, and answers in the next book in her own way. In the likeness of a young girl carrying a pitcher she meets him at the edge of the city, pours a mist around him so that no Phaeacian may mock him or ask who he is, leads him to the house of Alcinous and tells him to go first to the queen (*Odyssey* 7.14–77, plain renderings). He clasps Arete's knees, and the old Echeneus tells the king that it is not fitting for a stranger to sit in the ashes of the hearth; Alcinous raises him and seats him on a shining chair (*Odyssey* 7.139–171). He has come to them as one welcomed and pitied.

## In the Iliad

The verse was Priam's before it was Odysseus'. Before he drives out of Troy to ransom the body of Hector, the old king pours a libation in the middle of the courtyard and prays to Zeus:

{{quote:a326be1d-8d82-5be9-afa9-618b6f209b76}}

The words are the same but for the name, Ἀχιλλῆος for Φαίηκας (*Iliad* 24.309). Both men are going to a house where they must be suppliants: Priam to the man who killed his son, Odysseus to a people whose first king was a son of the god who wrecked him (*Odyssey* 6.326, 7.56–62; see [[the-ransom-of-hector]] and [[nausithous]]). Priam asks for a sign as well, and Zeus sends an eagle on the right (*Iliad* 24.310–321); Odysseus asks for nothing more, and the goddess gives no sign.
