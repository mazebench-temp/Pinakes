{{quote:d1d1edb0-2495-55fb-8b62-00da2ede7aa0}}

Creon has come to banish Medea because he fears her cleverness. She answers that her reputation for wisdom has always harmed her, and that no sensible man should have his children educated too well: besides the charge of idleness, they earn the hostile envy of their fellow citizens (*Medea* 292–301). Aristotle quotes the first two lines as a maxim, and the next two as the reason that, added to it, makes the whole an enthymeme (*Rhetoric* 2.21, 1394a26–35). His text has “from the citizens”, παρʼ ἀστῶν, where the manuscripts of Euripides have πρὸς ἀστῶν. Later in the chapter he cites the opening words again as a maxim that is part of an enthymeme (1394b17–19), and gives his own version, with the reason put first: “since one should neither be envied nor be idle, I say one should not be educated” (1394b31–32).
