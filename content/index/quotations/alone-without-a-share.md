## In Homer

On the shield that Hephaestus makes for Achilles are the earth, the sky and the sea, the sun, the moon and the constellations, the Pleiades, the Hyades, Orion, “and the Bear, which they also call by the name of the Wagon, / which turns in its place and watches Orion, / and alone has no share in the baths of Oceanus” (*Iliad* 18.483–489; see [[the-bear]]). The same lines name the stars by which Odysseus steers from Calypso's island (*Odyssey* 5.272–275). In Greek latitudes the Great Bear never sets: it never bathes in the river Ocean that circles the earth (see [[oceanus]]). Walter Leaf explains “alone” by the history of astronomy: the other constellations that never set, the Little Bear, the Dragon and the rest, none of them conspicuous, were later creations, and the Little Bear is said to have been brought to Greece from Phoenicia by Thales (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.489).

## Outside Homer

**Strabo.** Strabo answers the critics as Aristotle does, but as a geographer. Homer means by the Bear the arctic circle, the circle of the stars that never set, and by Oceanus the horizon; with so many stars turning in that part of the ever-visible sky, he would not have called the Bear alone without a share in the baths of Oceanus. Those who charge him with knowing one Bear instead of two are wrong, for the second was probably not yet a constellation: it came to the Greeks only after the Phoenicians had marked it and used it for sailing, as the Lock of Berenice and Canopus were named only the other day, and many stars, as Aratus says, are still nameless. Crates was wrong to write οἶος, “he alone”, for οἴη, avoiding what did not need avoiding; Heraclitus did better, and more in Homer's manner, when he too named the Bear for the arctic circle (*Geography* 1.1.6; see [[strabo]] and [[heraclitus-of-ephesus]]).

**Virgil and Ovid.** The Latin poets keep Homer's Bear out of the sea. In Virgil's heavens the great Serpent glides “around and through the two Bears, the Bears that fear to be dipped in the water of Ocean”, *Arctos Oceani metuentis aequore tingi* (*Georgics* 1.244–246); Virgil has two Bears where Homer has one. Ovid's Ulysses, listing the carvings of the shield that Ajax cannot understand, has “the Bear untouched by the sea”, *inmunemque aequoris Arcton* (*Metamorphoses* 13.293; see [[ovid-the-carvings-of-the-shield]]), and in the same book Pelorus, the northern cape of Sicily, faces “the Bears that have no share in the sea”, *arctos / aequoris expertes* (*Metamorphoses* 13.726–727). Merry sets Virgil's verse and Ovid's Pelorus beside Homer's (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 5.275).
