The speech answered Harmodius, a descendant of the tyrant-slayer, who seems to have challenged the honors voted to Iphicrates for his victories, a statue among them, and who taunted him with his low birth (see [[iphicrates]] and [[harmodius]]). Plutarch preserves Iphicrates' retort to the descendant: his own nobility began with him, and Harmodius' ended with him (*Sayings of Kings and Commanders*, *Moralia* 187B). Aristotle quotes the speech for two topics. From the time: the people would have promised the statue before the deeds; they should not take it away now that they have received them (*Rhetoric* 2.23, 1397b30–32). From definition: the best man is the most nobly born, for the tyrant-slayers themselves had no nobility before they did a deed of nobility, and Iphicrates' deeds are nearer to theirs than his opponent's (1398a17–22).
