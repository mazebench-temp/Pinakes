## The argument

{{quote:8c3d9406-9269-5d17-b257-cde6c74d0de7}}

Homer, “as he excels in everything else”, saw this clearly: he did not put into the Odyssey everything that happened to Odysseus, the wound on Parnassus or the feigned madness at the mustering, but built the poem around one action, and the Iliad likewise (1451a22–30; see [[the-boar-hunt-on-parnassus]] and [[odysseus-feigns-madness]]). The parts must be so constructed “that if any part is moved or removed, the whole is disjointed and disturbed; for a thing whose presence or absence makes no visible difference is no part of the whole” (1451a32–35).

The principle is applied to epic in chapter 23. Plots must not be like histories, which set out one period of time and whatever happened in it, as the battles of Salamis and Himera were fought at the same time but tended to no common end (1459a21–29; see [[salamis-and-himera]] and [[poetry-and-history]]). Homer did not try to make the whole war into a poem, but took one part and used others as episodes, like the catalogue of ships; the poets of the *Cypria* and the *Little Iliad* composed about one man, one period, and “one action of many parts”, which is why many tragedies can be made from them and only one or two from each of Homer's poems (1459a30–b7; see [[cypria]] and [[little-iliad]]).

## After Aristotle

The Renaissance critics made the unity of action one of three: Lodovico Castelvetro (1570) added unity of time and of place, and French classical theory made all three rules. Aristotle names only the unity of action; on time he notes only that tragedy tries to keep “within a single circuit of the sun, or to exceed it a little” (1449b12–13), and he says nothing of place.
