## Words

φάτις and φῆμις are both from φημί, “say”: what is said of someone, talk, rumor and reputation. The translation of the Odyssey renders both “talk” and keeps “report”, what is heard of a man, for [[kleos|κλέος]] (see its table of key words). The Iliad has each once: “the talk of the people” that a god set in Phoenix's heart (δήμου … φάτιν, 9.460) and the “talk among the Trojans” that a spy might overhear (φῆμιν, 10.207). The Odyssey uses them more, and almost always of the judgment of a community: the talk that rises from a girl's fine clothes (*Odyssey* 6.29), the bitter talk Nausicaa shuns (*Odyssey* 6.273), the harsh talk of the people that sent a man to war (*Odyssey* 14.239), Penelope's respect for the talk of the people (δήμοιό τε φῆμιν, *Odyssey* 16.75 = 19.527), the suitors' shame before the talk of men and women (φάτιν ἀνδρῶν ἠδὲ γυναικῶν, *Odyssey* 21.323), and the talk that will go through the town when the suitors are found dead (*Odyssey* 23.362). φήμη, the chance word taken as an omen (*Odyssey* 2.35), is another thing.

The talk is feared rather than heard. Its voice is the imagined speech of “someone”, often “some baser man”, which the speaker quotes in advance (see [[tis-speech]]), and its weapons are [[oneidos|ὄνειδος]], “reproach”, and the blame of [[nemesis|νέμεσις]], which [[aidos|αἰδώς]] fears.

## In the poems

**What a man may say.** Hector, waiting for Achilles before the gate, will not go back into the city: “I would feel shame before the Trojans and the Trojan women with trailing robes, / lest some day another man, baser than I, should say: / ‘Hector trusted in his own strength and destroyed the army.’ / So they will say” (22.105–108; see [[the-death-of-hector]]). The same shame kept him from shrinking from the war (6.441–443). Helen will not go to Paris' bed, for the Trojan women would blame her hereafter (3.410–412), and she wishes she were the wife of a man who felt “the blame and the many reproaches of men” (6.350–351; see [[husbands-and-wives]]). In four verses that are in no manuscript and that Plutarch quotes, Phoenix tells how a god stopped his anger against his father, “who put in my heart / the talk of the people and the many reproaches of men, / so that I might not be called a father-killer among the Achaeans” (9.459–461; see [[phoenix-patricide-lines]] and [[the-flight-of-phoenix]]).

**The Odyssey.** Telemachus will not send his mother away against her will, for “blame will come upon me from men” (*Odyssey* 2.136–137). Nausicaa's speech in *Odyssey* 6 is the fullest account of the fear: she names the talk, quotes what some baser man would say if he met her with the stranger, and ends, “So they will say, and this would be a reproach to me” (*Odyssey* 6.273–288). Eurymachus gives the same reason for refusing the beggar the bow: the suitors are ashamed of the talk of men and women, lest some baser man of the Achaeans say that far worse men woo the wife of a noble man and cannot string his bow, while a wandering beggar strung it, “so they will say, and this would be a disgrace to us” (*Odyssey* 21.323–329, a plain rendering). Penelope ponders whether to stay with her son and keep the house, respecting her husband's bed and the talk of the people, or to follow the best of the suitors (*Odyssey* 16.73–77, 19.524–529, plain renderings). The shade of Agamemnon foresees a hateful song of Clytemnestra among men, which will bring evil talk on all women, even the virtuous (*Odyssey* 24.199–202, a plain rendering).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod ends his precepts with it: do as he bids, and shun the dread talk of mortals; for talk is an evil thing, light and easy to raise but hard to bear and hard to put down, and no talk wholly perishes that many people spread abroad: she too is a goddess (*Works and Days* 760–764, φήμη). His verb, ὑπαλεύεο, “shun”, is of the same root as Nausicaa's ἀλεείνω (*Odyssey* 6.273).
