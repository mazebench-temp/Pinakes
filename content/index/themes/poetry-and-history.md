## The contrast

The difference is not verse: the work of Herodotus put into verse would still be history (see [[herodotus]] and [[historia]]). The historian tells what happened, what Alcibiades did or suffered (see [[alcibiades]]); the poet tells the kind of thing a person of a certain kind will say or do by probability or necessity (see [[katholou]] and [[eikos]]). Comedy makes this plain, since its poets build the plot and then supply names; tragedy keeps to the names of people who existed, because what has happened is evidently possible, and the possible is convincing (1451b11–19). Even a poet who takes his plot from real events is no less a poet, since some real events are such as would probably happen (1451b29–32). An epic should not be built like a history, which sets out one period of time and all that happened in it, as the battles of Salamis and Himera happened at once without tending to one end (1459a21–29; see [[salamis-and-himera]]).

Renaissance defenders of poetry made the passage the center of their case. Sidney sets the philosopher's precept against the historian's example and gives the palm to the poet, who joins them: “for whatsoever the philosopher saith should be done, he giveth a perfect picture of it in some one by whom he presupposeth it was done” (*The Defence of Poesy*).
