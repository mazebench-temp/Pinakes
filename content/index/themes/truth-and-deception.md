The Iliad is not a poem of lies in the way the Odyssey is, but deception runs through it. Gods deceive men: Athena, in the likeness of Deiphobus, draws Hector into the fight with Achilles, and he knows at the end that “Athena has deceived me” (22.299). Gods deceive one another, as Hera beguiles Zeus to sleep in Book 14 so that Poseidon can help the Achaeans. Men test and deceive each other. Achilles hates like the gates of Hades “that man who hides one thing in his mind and says another” (9.312–313), and he tells the embassy of Book 9 that Agamemnon has deceived him once and will not persuade him again (9.344–345). The Odyssey makes its hero a master of the art: “he made the many lies he told like the truth” (Odyssey 19.203).

The words for deception are [[pseudos|ψεῦδος]] (“lie, falsehood”), [[apate|ἀπάτη]] (“deceit”) and δόλος (“trick, cunning”). The words for truth are ἀτρεκέως (“exactly, truly”), ἐτεός and ἔτυμος (“true, real”), and ἀληθής (“true”), usually explained as “not escaping notice”, from λήθω. A mortal has few ways to tell one from the other. Signs and oracles prove true or false by what follows, and a messenger is believed for the sake of the one who sent him. Only the Muses, who were present at everything, know all things (see [[memory-and-the-muses]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod's Muses, meeting him on Helicon, tell him that they “know how to say many false things like true ones, and know, when they wish, how to utter true things” (*Theogony* 27–28). Plato would not admit the lying Dream to his ideal city, since a god neither changes his form nor deceives, in waking or in dreams (*Republic* 2.382e–383a; see [[plato-lying-dream]]).
