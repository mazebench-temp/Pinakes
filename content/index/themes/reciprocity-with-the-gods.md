Prayer in Homer is a relationship of exchange. The one who prays reminds the god of what he has given, a temple, sacrifices, a service, and asks for something in return. Book 1 has three such prayers. Two turn on the words “if ever” (εἴ ποτε); the third, on “once before”.

## Chryses

{{quote:b2d52fe4-6282-554d-8148-4c0f865af324}}

The priest's prayer has the full shape. He calls the god by his titles and places. He recalls his gifts: if ever he roofed a temple “to your pleasure” (χαρίεντα), if ever he burned the fat thigh-pieces of bulls and goats. Then he asks: let the Danaans pay for my tears with your arrows. Scholars have called the pattern *da quia dedi*, “give, because I have given”.

{{quote:74549688-d5d0-56c2-a3cb-ff2fd29fe3d0}}

The second prayer, at Chryse, uses the first as its credit. “Once before you heard me”, says Chryses, “and honored me, and struck the army of the Achaeans; so now once more fulfill for me this wish”: ward off the ruin. The god's past answer is itself a claim on his next one.

## Thetis

Achilles primes his mother with the argument. She should go to Zeus “if ever in the past you have gladdened the heart of Zeus by word or deed” (1.394–395), and remind him how she once freed him from bonds (1.396–407; see [[binding-of-zeus]]).

{{quote:507c82a1-87a4-53cd-9c8c-090c54edb7f7}}

Thetis uses the formula as he suggested. Her favor to Zeus was greater than any temple: she saved his rule. What she asks in return is honor for her son.

## The gods' side

The exchange is not only material. Achilles gives it a moral form: “whoever obeys the gods, to him they gladly listen” (1.218; see [[whoever-obeys-the-gods]]). When the plague begins he assumes that the god's anger must come from a failure in exchange, some vow or hecatomb left unpaid (1.65). Calchas tells him otherwise. The offense was the dishonor of a priest (1.93–95).

The gods, for their part, want their share. In Book 4 Zeus says that his altar at Troy never lacked its equal feast, “libation and savor, for that is the privilege (γέρας) we have received” (4.48–49). At Chryse the hecatomb is called “gifts to the lord” (1.390), and after a day of song the paean pleases him: “his heart was glad as he listened” (1.474).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates asks whether piety is then “a kind of trading skill between gods and men” (*Euthyphro* 14e). Robert Parker's essay on reciprocity in Greek religion (“Pleasing Thighs”, 1998) takes its title from the burned thigh-pieces that prayers like Chryses' recall.
