Only in Book 5 of the Iliad does a mortal draw a god's blood. With Athena's leave Diomedes wounds Aphrodite in the hand (5.330–351), and with Athena beside him driving the spear home he wounds Ares in the belly (5.855–863). Between the two wounds Dione comforts her daughter with three stories of gods whom men have hurt: Ares, bound in a bronze jar for thirteen months by the sons of Aloeus; Hera, struck in the breast by Heracles' three-barbed arrow; and Hades, shot by the same Heracles “in Pylos among the dead” (5.382–404; see [[the-aloadae-bind-ares]] and [[heracles-wounds-hera-and-hades]]). No mortal wounds a god in the rest of the poem.

## What a wounded god suffers

A god bleeds ichor, the “immortal blood” of beings who eat no bread and drink no wine (5.339–342; see [[ichor]]). The wound hurts. Aphrodite lets her son fall with a great cry, and goes away distraught, her skin darkening (5.343, 5.352–354); Hera was seized by “incurable pain” (5.394); Hades went up to Olympus “grieving at heart, pierced through with pains” (5.399). Ares bellows as loud as nine or ten thousand men, and both armies tremble (5.859–863). Then the healing is quick. Dione wipes the ichor from Aphrodite's hand, and the hand is healed (5.416–417); Paeëon spreads pain-killing drugs on Hades and on Ares, “for he was not made to be mortal” (5.401–402, 5.899–901), and Ares heals as fast as fig juice curdles milk (5.902–904; see [[paeeon]], [[healing]] and [[fig-juice]]).

What a god cannot do is die, and the book comes close to saying that he might. Dione says of Ares in the jar that he “would have perished there” (ἀπόλοιτο, 5.388), in the verse used of Aeneas under Diomedes' stone (5.311; see [[would-have-had-not]]). Ares, complaining to Zeus, imagines what might have happened had his feet not carried him away: he would have suffered among the grim heaps of the dead, “or lived on strengthless from the blows of the bronze” (ζὼς ἀμενηνός, 5.885–887). ἀμενηνός, “strengthless”, is the Odyssey's word for the dead, “the strengthless heads of the dead” (*Odyssey* 10.521, 11.29). The god's worst case is to live on as the dead do.

## Gods against gods

The mortal is the weapon; the quarrel is among the gods. Dione says so: “many of us who have our homes on Olympus have endured cruel pains at the hands of men, bringing them on one another” (5.383–384), and it was Athena who set Diomedes on (5.405). Ares says the same to Zeus: “we gods are forever enduring the most chilling things by one another's will, when we do favors for men” (5.873–874). Hera asks Zeus' leave to strike Ares, and he tells her to rouse Athena against him (5.757–766). Athena, who led Ares out of the battle by the hand early in the book (5.29–36), drives her mortal's spear into him near its end.

Aphrodite's outrage names what has changed: “now the Danaans are fighting even with the immortals” (5.380). Dione answers with a threat (see [[who-fights-the-gods]]), and Apollo gives the warning in person (see [[gods-and-men-not-alike]]). The poem lets Diomedes live, but it does not repeat his deed. Later the gods wound one another. In the battle of the gods Ares attacks Athena, reminding her of the day she set Diomedes on him, and she fells him with a stone and strikes down Aphrodite with her hand; Hera boxes Artemis' ears with her own bow (21.391–426, 21.489–492). When Apollo cheats Achilles of Hector, Achilles can only say, “I would pay you back, if I had the power” (22.15–20).

## Outside Homer

Plato would not admit into his city the stories of Hera bound by her son and Hephaestus thrown from heaven by his father, “or all the battles of the gods that Homer composed” (*Republic* 2.378d). The author of *On the Sublime* thought that Homer, in telling of the gods' wounds, quarrels, revenges, tears, bonds and sufferings, made the men at Troy gods, as far as he could, and the gods men (9.7; see [[longinus]]).
