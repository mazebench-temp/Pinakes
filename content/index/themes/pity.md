The Iliad has two verbs for pity, ἐλεέω and ἐλεαίρω (the noun is ἔλεος), and beside them ὀλοφύρομαι, “lament, grieve for”, which is often the same feeling put into words. οἰκτίρω, the common verb of later Greek, is rare in the poem and found only from Book 11 on (11.814, 16.5, 23.534, 23.548, 24.516). Pity in Homer is felt by someone who looks on, and it often moves him to act: Menelaus and Ajax, seeing comrades fall, pity them and step out to fight over the bodies (5.561, 5.610).

## Asking for pity

Pity is what the weak ask of the strong, and they ask for it beside αἰδώς, “respect” (see [[aidos]]). The Trojan women vow a sacrifice to Athena “if she will pity the city and the wives of the Trojans and their little children” (6.94–95, 6.309–310), and she refuses (6.311). Andromache asks Hector to have pity and stay on the tower (6.407, 6.431). On the battlefield the formula of the suppliant is “respect me and pity me” (αἴδεο καί μʼ ἐλέησον), which Lycaon says to Achilles and Hecuba to Hector, both in vain (21.74, 22.82; see [[supplication]]). Priam, in the last book, asks Achilles to respect the gods and pity him, remembering his own father, and adds, “I am more pitiable still” (24.503–504).

## The pity of the gods

The gods pity men as they watch them (see [[watching-the-war]]). A formula marks it, “seeing him, he pitied him” (τὸν δὲ ἰδὼν ἐλέησε): Zeus pities Hector struck down by Ajax's stone (15.12), Sarpedon, his own son (16.431), Achilles' immortal horses weeping for Patroclus (17.441), Achilles and the elders mourning him (19.340), and Priam on his way to the ships (24.332). Twice the narrator says, in the same verse, that “the father pitied him as he shed tears”, of Agamemnon and of Ajax, and each time Zeus grants a small request, an omen of safety or the clearing of a mist (8.245, 17.648). The gods' pity is real, but it seldom changes the end. Zeus pities Sarpedon and lets him die (16.431–461), and his heart grieves for Hector as he is chased around the walls, but he lets him die too (22.168–185). The Dream that deceives Agamemnon in Book 2 begins its message with the claim that Zeus “cares greatly for you and pities you” (2.27, 2.64).

## Pity lost and found

The poem's last movement turns on pity. Apollo, “pitying the man even in death”, keeps Hector's body from disfigurement, and the gods pity him as they watch it dragged (24.18–23). Apollo complains to them that Achilles “has destroyed pity” and has no sense of shame (24.44). When Priam has made his plea and the two have wept together, Achilles raises the old man from the ground, “pitying his gray head and gray beard” (οἰκτίρων, 24.507–516).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle defines pity as a pain at an evil, destructive or painful, that befalls someone who does not deserve it, an evil we might expect to suffer ourselves (*Rhetoric* 2.8, 1385b13–16). In the *Poetics* tragedy brings about, through pity and fear, the catharsis of such emotions (*Poetics* 6, 1449b27–28).
