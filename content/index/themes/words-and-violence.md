Book 1 of the Iliad contains no battle, and almost no blow is struck. Its violence is threatened, remembered, or turned into words. The book is a study of what speech can do in place of force, and what it cannot.

## The sword

{{quote:0bb7e262-87ee-5871-8a31-044c537bb731}}

When Agamemnon threatens to take Briseis, Achilles weighs killing him on the spot. He is already drawing the great sword from its sheath when Athena arrives.

{{quote:a83412e7-0dea-5882-8775-a32d2d7ff4a2}}

She gives him a substitute. He may not draw the sword, but he may reproach Agamemnon “with words” ([[epos|ἔπεσιν]] [[oneidos|ὀνείδισον]]), telling him how it will be. The promise of gifts makes the exchange worthwhile.

{{quote:eedaec2f-9e8c-5e95-a6ef-a4fc6856197e}}

The sword goes back into the sheath. The anger does not: the narrator adds at once that Achilles did not yet cease from it (1.224). See [[athena-restrains-achilles]].

## Fighting with words

{{quote:04dd31fc-f487-52cf-8914-893d4ff75565}}

Achilles uses the license in full: drunkard, dog-eyed, deer-hearted, a coward who never goes to battle or on ambush, a people-devouring king. Then he swears a great oath on the scepter (1.233–244) and throws it down. The oath is the strongest thing words can do: it binds the future.

{{quote:958feb37-ed77-57ad-b0c0-91e6ba47ae21}}

The narrator sums up the quarrel with the vocabulary of battle. The two men stand up “having fought (μαχεσσαμένω) with hostile words”. Both know where the line lies. Achilles will not fight with his hands for the girl (1.298), but if Agamemnon touches anything else of his, his dark blood will gush round Achilles' spear (1.301–303). Agamemnon threatens to come himself with more men if Briseis is refused (1.324–325), and sends heralds instead. In Achilles' retelling, the threat was a word “which has now been carried out” (1.388).

## On Olympus

The pattern repeats among the gods, with a difference. Hera attacks with “cutting words” ([[kertomia|κερτόμια]], 1.539). Zeus answers with a threat of force: all the gods on Olympus will be no help to her when he lays his irresistible hands on her (1.566–567). Hephaestus urges gentle words instead (1.582), and he makes the threat real with a memory.

{{quote:18a34e43-afa8-523a-866b-4b8170a60bdd}}

Once, when he tried to defend his mother, Zeus seized him by the foot and threw him from heaven. On Olympus violence is a memory and a threat, and the quarrel ends in laughter (1.599). In the camp the words have done their work, and the violence is still to come.
