**The question to strangers.** The host asks who the strangers are and why they have come by sea: “Is it on some business, or do you wander at random / over the sea, like pirates, who wander / staking their lives and bringing evil to foreigners?” Nestor asks it at Pylos when the meal is over (*Odyssey* 3.69–74), and the Cyclops asks it in the same words when he finds the strangers in his cave (*Odyssey* 9.251–255; see [[xenia]] and [[prexis]]). Merry takes οἷά τε ληιστῆρες with μαψιδίως ἀλάλησθε, “idly rove”, with κατὰ πρῆξιν joined to the same verb by a kind of zeugma (Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 3.73). The pirate “stakes his life”, ψυχὰς παρθέμενοι (see [[psyche]]), and brings evil to ἀλλοδαποί, men of another land.

**Pirates in the Odyssey.** The Taphians are the poem's pirates. Taphian raiders, ληΐστορες ἄνδρες, snatched the Phoenician woman of Eumaeus' tale as she came from the fields and sold her to his father's house (*Odyssey* 15.425–429; see [[eumaeus]], [[the-phoenician-nurse]] and [[the-kidnapping-of-eumaeus]]), and Antinous' father Eupeithes once followed Taphian pirates against the [[thesprotians|Thesprotians]], friends of Ithaca, and fled to Odysseus from the people's anger (*Odyssey* 16.424–430; see [[taphians]] and [[eupeithes]]). Mentes, whose likeness Athena takes in *Odyssey* 1, is their lord (see [[mentes-the-taphian]]). Odysseus' false tales make him a sea raider himself. The Cretan of his tale to Eumaeus raided foreign coasts before the war and Egypt after it (*Odyssey* 14.229–272; see [[the-tale-to-eumaeus]], [[the-raid-on-egypt]] and [[egypt]]), and to Antinous he says that Zeus sent him to Egypt “with far-wandering pirates” (*Odyssey* 17.424–426, a plain rendering).

**Raiders and heroes.** The line between piracy and the heroes' own raiding is thin. Nestor remembers the Achaeans at Troy “roaming after plunder wherever Achilles led” (πλαζόμενοι κατὰ ληίδʼ, *Odyssey* 3.106), and Merry sets κατὰ ληίδα beside the κατὰ πρῆξιν of the question (on 3.72; see [[raiding]] and [[achilles]]), and Odysseus begins the tale of his wanderings with the sack of Ismarus, the city of the Cicones (*Odyssey* 9.39–42).

## Outside Homer

Thucydides cites the old poets' question (Thucydides 1.5.2; see [[thucydides-and-the-pirates]]) to all who sail in, whether they are pirates, as evidence that piracy was “a recognized employment” in early Greece; Merry adds Caesar's remark on the Germans that raids beyond the borders of each state carry no disgrace (Merry on 3.73, citing Thucydides 1.5 and *Gallic War* 6.23; see [[raiding]]). Strabo says that the early kings of Egypt, content with what they had, distrusted all who sailed to their coast and the Greeks above all, “for they were plunderers, and coveted other men's land for want of their own”; so they set a guard at the harbor of the later Alexandria and gave the country around the village of Rhacotis to herdsmen able to keep strangers off (*Geography* 17.1.6; see [[strabo]]).
