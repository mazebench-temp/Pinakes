Homer admires beauty without embarrassment. Heroes are handsome, and the poem says so: Achilles is the most handsome of the Achaeans (2.673–674), Agamemnon stands out among the heroes in his looks like Zeus, Ares and Poseidon (2.477–483), and at the end of the poem Priam and Achilles sit and marvel at each other, Priam at Achilles' size and beauty, like the gods', and Achilles at Priam's noble look and speech (24.629–632). The words are [[eidos|εἶδος]] (“looks”), καλός (“beautiful”) and κάλλος (“beauty”); their opposite, αἰσχρός, means both “ugly” and “shameful” (see [[aischros]]).

## A gift of the gods

Beauty is given, not earned. Hector calls Paris' hair and looks “the gifts of Aphrodite” (3.54–55), and Paris answers that the gifts of the gods are not to be thrown away, since no one could take them by his own will (3.64–66; see [[gifts-of-the-gods]] and [[doron]]). In the *Odyssey* Odysseus says that the gods do not give every gift to one man: one man is plain, but a god crowns his words with beauty, while another has looks like the immortals and no grace in his speech (*Odyssey* 8.167–177).

## Looks and worth

The Iliad keeps measuring looks against what they do not include. Nireus was the most handsome man who came to Ilios after Achilles, “but he was a weakling, and few people followed him” (2.673–675). Paris is “best in looks” and has no strength or courage in his heart (3.39, 3.44–45). Odysseus, unimpressive to look at, was irresistible when he spoke, and then “we did not so much marvel at Odysseus' looks” (3.216–224). At the other extreme Thersites is the ugliest man who came to Ilios, and in Odysseus' judgment the worst of all who came there with the sons of Atreus (2.216, 2.248–249). Beauty that matches worth, as in Agamemnon or Achilles, is the exception the poem admires; beauty without it is suspect.

## The price

The price of beauty is paid by others. Helen's is never described. It is shown by its effect on the old men of Troy, who say that no one could blame the Trojans and Achaeans for suffering so long for such a woman, and in the next breath that she should go home (3.156–160; see [[no-blame]]). Helen calls herself “dog-faced” and speaks of the shame that is hers (3.180, 3.242). In death, beauty is a last gift and a last insult. Priam says that everything is beautiful in a young man killed in battle, but pitiful in an old man's body mauled by dogs (22.71–76), and the Achaeans gather round Hector's body to marvel at his build and his splendid looks, and stab it (22.369–371).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod's Pandora is beauty made as a punishment. Zeus orders Hephaestus to make her face like the immortal goddesses' (ἀθανάτῃς δὲ θεῇς εἰς ὦπα ἐίσκειν), the words the old men of Troy use of Helen (3.158), and Aphrodite to pour grace about her head; the gods all give her gifts, and she is named for them, “a bane to men who eat bread” (*Works and Days* 60–82, the line at 62). In the *Theogony* she is “a beautiful evil” (καλὸν κακόν, 585).

Sappho answers the question of beauty with Helen. Some say a host of horsemen or of ships is the most beautiful thing on the dark earth, “but I say it is whatever one loves” (ὄττω τις ἔραται, with the verb of [[eros|ἔρος]]); Helen, who far surpassed all mortals in beauty, left the best of husbands and sailed to Troy (fragment 16; see [[sappho-helen]]).

Lucian's Menippus, shown Helen's skull among the dead, asks whether the thousand ships sailed for this (see [[lucian-helens-skull]]), and Marlowe's Faustus turns the question into praise: “Was this the face that launched a thousand ships?” (see [[marlowe-helen]]).

G. E. Lessing took the old men on the wall as proof that poetry shows beauty by its effect rather than by description. Homer does not describe Helen's features; he shows old men, done with war because of their age, conceding that she was worth it (*Laokoon*, 1766, chapter 21; see [[lessing-helen]]).
