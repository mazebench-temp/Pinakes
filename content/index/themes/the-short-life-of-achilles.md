The Iliad does not narrate the death of Achilles, but it is present from the first book. Achilles and his mother speak of it as a known fact, and the words they use for it are among the book's most striking. See [[death-of-achilles-foretold]].

## Short-lived, and owed honor

{{quote:1e4d90ee-fc8e-5b3c-8cc5-6ac50da8457b}}

The first thing Achilles says to his mother is that she bore him μινυνθάδιος, “short-lived” (from μίνυνθα, “for a little while”). Everything else follows from it. Because his life will be short, Zeus ought to have given him honor at least, and instead Agamemnon has taken his prize. The logic is compensation: a short life should be made up with great honor. See [[mother-since-you-bore-me]].

## Swift-fated

{{quote:376f719c-c3b5-5071-8c14-d3a2262bc62c}}

Thetis confirms it. His [[aisa|αἶσα]] (“portion”) is brief, “not long at all”; he is ὠκύμορος, “swift-fated”, and wretched beyond all men; she bore him to an evil fate (see [[why-did-i-raise-you]]). The adjective ὠκύς, “swift”, serves Achilles twice. He is πόδας ὠκύς, “swift-footed” (see [[swift-footed]]), and he is swift to his death.

{{quote:316479fc-8d31-5f44-9aee-e0063ee6ccc6}}

Before Zeus, Thetis makes the superlative her argument: honor my son, who is ὠκυμορώτατος, “swifter to his fate than any other man”. The shortest life should receive the greatest honor, and Zeus grants it.

## The choice

Book 1 does not yet name the choice that lies behind this fate. Achilles explains it in Book 9. His mother told him of two destinies: if he stays and fights at Troy he loses his homecoming but his glory will never perish; if he goes home his glory is lost but his life will be long (9.410–416). In Book 1 he threatens to take the second road and sail home to Phthia (1.169–171), but he does not go. He sits by the ships, and the idle days consume him: he “wasted away his own heart” (φθινύθεσκε φίλον κῆρ, 1.491). The verb belongs with φθίνω, “waste, perish”.

The choice is made in Book 18. When Achilles resolves to kill Hector, Thetis tells him he will indeed be ὠκύμορος, since his own death is ready straight after Hector's (18.95–96).

## Outside Homer

In Plato's *Apology* Socrates, on trial for his life, cites Achilles' choice: told that his death would follow Hector's, he chose to die avenging his friend rather than live on in disgrace (28c–d).
