## The division

Every speech has a speaker, a subject and a hearer, and the end lies with the hearer. The member of the assembly judges what is to come and the juror what has happened; the spectator of a speech of praise judges the speaker's skill. So there are three kinds: deliberative speech (συμβουλευτικόν), which exhorts or dissuades about the future and aims at the advantageous; judicial speech (δικανικόν), which accuses or defends about the past and aims at the just; and epideictic speech (ἐπιδεικτικόν), the speech of display, which praises or blames, chiefly about the present, and aims at the noble. The division organizes the whole first book of the *Rhetoric* (see [[sympheron]], [[kalon]] and [[adikia]]).

## After Aristotle

The scheme became the frame of ancient rhetoric. Quintilian discusses the three kinds of cause at length, citing Aristotle for them (*Education of the Orator* 3.4), and the Latin names, *genus deliberativum*, *iudiciale* and *demonstrativum*, passed into every later handbook.
