## The three portraits

The young are given to appetite, changeable, passionate, fond of honor and still more of victory, trusting and hopeful, because they have little past and much future; they live by character more than by calculation, do everything too much, and think they know everything. The old, deceived and disappointed, are the reverse: positive about nothing, suspicious, small-souled, ungenerous, cowardly, fond of life and of themselves, living by memory and by calculation, prone to pity from weakness rather than from love of mankind. Those in their prime are between the two, with the virtues of both and the excesses of neither; the body is in its prime from thirty to thirty-five, the soul at about forty-nine. The portraits serve the speaker: everyone accepts speeches spoken in his own character (*Rhetoric* 2.12–14, 1388b31–1390b13).

The contrast of youth and age is old in Greek poetry. Nestor's speeches in the Iliad are the type of an old man's memory (see [[nestor]]), and the *Rhetoric*'s old men, who “live by memory rather than by hope”, are recognizably his heirs. Horace's portrait of the four ages in the *Art of Poetry* follows Aristotle's (156–178).
