An oath in Homer is a curse laid on oneself. The one who swears calls gods to witness and accepts their punishment if his words prove false (see [[oath]]). Its power lies in the future. It binds what the swearer will do, and it waits, sometimes long, to be fulfilled.

## Oaths kept and broken

Achilles' great oath in Book 1 is kept. He swears on the scepter that a longing for him will come upon the Achaeans when they are dying at Hector's hands (1.233–244), and the rest of the poem fulfills it (see [[longing-for-achilles]]).

The army's own oaths are nearly broken. When the men run for the ships in Book 2, Odysseus reminds Agamemnon of the promise they made on the way from Argos, to stay until Ilios was sacked (2.284–288), and Nestor asks what will become of their compacts and oaths: “into the fire, then, with the counsels and plans of men, the libations of unmixed wine and the right hands in which we trusted!” (2.339–341). An army that goes home before the city falls is an army of oath-breakers.

## The truce

In Book 3 both armies swear a truce, and the terms are guarded by the strongest witnesses a mortal can call: Zeus who rules from Ida, the Sun who sees and hears all things, the rivers, the earth, and the powers below who punish perjurers after death (3.276–280). The victims' throats are cut and the wine poured out, and the men of both armies say what the ritual means: whoever first does harm in defiance of the oaths, may their brains pour out on the ground like this wine, theirs and their children's, and may their wives be subdued by others (3.298–301). The narrator adds at once that Zeus “was not yet going to fulfill their prayer” (3.302).

In Book 4 the gods decide how the truce will end. Hera proposes and Zeus agrees that Athena should go and see to it that the Trojans are the first to break the oaths (ἄρξωσι πρότεροι ὑπὲρ ὅρκια δηλήσασθαι, 4.64–72), in the words of the curse and of Menelaus' warning (3.107, 3.299). She persuades Pandarus to shoot at Menelaus, and the arrow wounds him (4.86–147). Agamemnon, holding his wounded brother, states the doctrine of the broken oath: the oath and the blood of lambs are not in vain; if the Olympian does not fulfill it at once, he fulfills it late, and the breakers pay with their heads, their wives and their children. A day will come when sacred Ilios will perish (4.155–168). Later in the book he tells the army that father Zeus will not help liars (4.235–239).

In Book 7 the Trojans admit it. Antenor proposes giving Helen and the possessions back, “for now, having played false with the trusty oaths, we are fighting” (7.351–352). Paris refuses to give up the woman (7.357–364). The poem makes the Trojans guilty twice over: of the first offense, against a host, and of the second, against the oath that was meant to settle the first.

## Outside Homer

Hesiod says that the family of a man who knowingly swears a false oath is left more obscure in later generations, while the family of the man who keeps his oath is better (*Works and Days* 282–285).

Later writers told of an oath that Homer never mentions. When the suitors of Helen gathered at the house of her father Tyndareus, they swore, on Odysseus' advice, to defend the marriage of the man she chose against anyone who wronged it (Apollodorus, *Library* 3.10.9). Thucydides doubted that the oath explained the war: Agamemnon, he thought, gathered the expedition because he was the most powerful man of his time, rather than because he led suitors bound by the oaths of Tyndareus (1.9.1).
