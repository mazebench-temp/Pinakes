## Tradition and invention

Most tragedies keep the names of legendary people, but a few use invented names, and Agathon's *Antheus* invented everything and gave no less pleasure. So the poet need not keep to the traditional stories at all costs; even the familiar ones are familiar to few, and they please everyone (1451b19–26; see [[antheus]]). The finest tragedies, in fact, are composed about a few houses, Alcmaeon, Oedipus, Orestes, Meleager, Thyestes and Telephus, because the poets found by chance, not by art, that these supplied the right kind of suffering (1453a17–22, 1454a9–13; see [[the-few-houses]]). What the tradition fixes, the poet may not change: Clytemnestra dies at the hands of Orestes, Eriphyle at the hands of Alcmaeon; but he must find out how to use the material well (see [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]] and [[alcmaeon-kills-eriphyle]]).

A comic poet saw the advantage from the other side. Tragedy is a blessed art, says a speaker in Antiphanes' *Poetry*: the audience knows the story before anyone speaks, and the poet need only say “Oedipus” for them to know the rest, his father Laius, his mother, his daughters and sons, what he will suffer and what he has done (fr. 189 Kassel–Austin, quoted by Athenaeus 6.222a–b).
