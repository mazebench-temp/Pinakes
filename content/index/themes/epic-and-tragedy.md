## The comparison

Epic agrees with tragedy as an imitation of serious people in verse, but differs in using a single meter and narrative, and in length: tragedy tries as far as it can to keep within one revolution of the sun, while epic is unlimited in time (1449b9–20; see [[epic]] and [[tragedy]]). Epic has the same kinds as tragedy, and the same parts except song and spectacle; its length lets it show many parts happening at once, which gives it grandeur and variety, and it admits the irrational more readily, because we do not see the person acting (1459b7–1460a17). So a tragedy should not be given an epic structure (1456a10–19). In chapter 26 Aristotle answers those who prefer epic as addressed to a better audience, and tragedy as vulgar because its performers overact: the fault is the performers', not the art's; tragedy has everything epic has, music and spectacle besides, it is vivid in reading as in performance, and it achieves its end in a shorter compass and with more unity; so it is the better art (1462a5–b15; see [[mynniscus-and-callippides]]). Later critics often reversed the verdict: for Dryden, an epic was “undoubtedly the greatest work which the soul of man is capable to perform” (*Dedication of the Aeneis*, 1697).
