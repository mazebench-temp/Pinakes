## The four aims

Characters should be good, and goodness is possible in every class, even in a woman or a slave; appropriate, since a manly or clever character does not suit a woman; like; and consistent, so that even an inconsistent person must be consistently inconsistent (see [[ethos]] and [[epieikes]]). The failures: Menelaus in Euripides' *Orestes* is base without necessity; Odysseus' lament in the *Scylla* and Melanippe's speech are unseemly; Iphigenia at Aulis is inconsistent, the girl who pleads for her life nothing like the later one (see [[orestes-play]], [[scylla-of-timotheus]], [[melanippe-the-wise]] and [[iphigenia-at-aulis]]). In characters as in plots, the poet should look for the necessary or the probable, and like a good portrait painter make people better while keeping the likeness (1454a33–36, 1454b8–15). Horace makes the same demands: follow tradition or invent a consistent character, and keep it to the end as it began (*Ars Poetica* 119–127; see [[horace]]).
