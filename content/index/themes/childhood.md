## On the knees

A child in Homer belongs on an elder's knees. Phoenix's father cursed him that “he would never set upon his knees a dear son born of me” (9.455), and the gods fulfilled it. Phoenix set another man's son there instead. Achilles as a small boy would eat with no one else, “until I set you on my knees, and fed you your fill of meat, cutting it up first, and held the wine to your lips”, and he spat the wine over Phoenix's tunic “in your troublesome infancy” (9.486–491; see [[phoenix]] and [[fathers-and-sons]]). The childless man made the boy his son, “so that one day you might ward off shameful ruin from me” (9.494–495). Andromache, foreseeing her son's life without Hector, remembers the same place: Astyanax, “who once on his father's knees ate only marrow and the rich fat of sheep”, will go begging among his father's friends, and a boy whose parents live will drive him from the feast (22.490–501; see [[astyanax]]).

## Rearing

To rear a child is a debt that the child repays. Simoeisius died young and “did not repay his dear parents for his rearing” (4.477–478; see [[threptra]]). Thetis says that she reared Achilles “like a plant on the slope of an orchard” and sent him to Troy, never to come home (18.56–60). Patroclus came to Peleus' house as a small boy, after he had killed another boy over a game of knucklebones, and Peleus reared him with Achilles (23.84–90; see [[exile-and-refuge]]). Achilles was taught healing by Chiron (11.830–832; see [[chiron]]), and Peleus sent Phoenix with him to Troy because he was still “a child, who knew nothing yet of war, common to all, nor of assemblies” (9.440–441; see [[speaker-of-words-doer-of-deeds]]).

## Children seen

The one child the poem shows in its action is Hector's son, in Book 6. A handmaid carries him, “an infant of tender mind, quite small, the beloved son of Hector, like a lovely star” (6.399–401); when his father reaches for him, he shrinks back crying into his nurse's bosom, frightened by the bronze and the nodding plume, and his father and mother laugh (6.466–471; see [[hector-and-andromache]]). Hector takes off his helmet, kisses the boy and prays that he may be better than his father (6.472–481).

## Children in the similes

Children appear most often in comparisons. Athena turns Pandarus' arrow from Menelaus “as a mother brushes a fly from her child, when he lies in sweet sleep” (4.130–131). Teucer slips back behind Ajax's shield “like a child under its mother” (8.271; see [[like-a-child-under-its-mother]]). Apollo knocks down the Achaean wall as easily as a boy by the sea scatters the sand castles he has made in play (15.362–364), and Achilles asks Patroclus why he weeps like a little girl who runs beside her mother, tugging at her dress and begging to be picked up (16.7–11). Odysseus says that the army longing to go home wails “like young children or widowed women” (2.289–290; see [[children-and-widows]]).
