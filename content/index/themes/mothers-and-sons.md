The second half of Book 1 is framed by two scenes between a son and his divine mother. In the first, the mother acts for the son; in the second, the son for the mother.

## Thetis and Achilles

{{quote:1452c14a-45ce-5bdc-a4d1-59bed955422d}}

When Briseis has been taken, Achilles weeps, withdraws from his companions, and prays to his “dear mother”. It is the only prayer in the book addressed to a parent.

{{quote:2d12018b-1eb2-5c68-ba68-cfdb69aefc8c}}

She hears him from the depths where she sits beside her own father, rises like a mist, sits before him, strokes him with her hand and calls him by name: “My child, why are you weeping?” Achilles answers, “You know” (1.365), and tells her anyway.

His request makes her his advocate. She is to go to Zeus and remind him of the time she saved him from bondage, a story Achilles had often heard her tell in his father's house (1.393–407; see [[thetis-in-the-house-of-peleus]] and [[binding-of-zeus]]). He begins by asking her to “protect your own son” (περίσχεο παιδὸς ἑῆος, 1.393; see [[aristarchus-heeos]]).

{{quote:44840485-97f1-5d2e-938e-c30596917470}}

Her answer is a lament for his short life. Then she goes to Zeus and does all that he asked, clasping Zeus' knees and speaking of her son (1.495–516).

A divine mother is also part of Achilles' standing. Nestor weighs it against Agamemnon's power: “even if you are strong, and a goddess was the mother who bore you” (1.280).

## Hephaestus and Hera

{{quote:6d435a40-6fe1-55e3-8a44-f379ae84443d}}

After Zeus has silenced Hera, Hephaestus places a cup in his mother's hand and urges her to endure it. He does not want to see her struck, for he could not help her. Once, when he tried to defend her, Zeus threw him from heaven, and he fell all day and landed on Lemnos. The memory of a son's failed rescue persuades the mother to yield.

{{quote:02590d94-91da-5505-a434-049e80dfe8a6}}

Hera smiles and takes the cup “from her son's hand”. The quarrel of the gods ends with a mother's smile at her son.

## Two mothers

The scenes answer each other. Thetis goes to a stronger power on her son's behalf and wins. Hephaestus tells his mother to yield to that same power, because he cannot win for her. Apollo, too, is first named as a mother's son: “the son of Leto and of Zeus” (1.9; see [[leto|Leto]]).

In Book 18 Thetis, among her sisters the Nereids, laments that she is the unhappy mother of the best of sons (18.54). The same question she asks in Book 1, “why are you weeping? what grief has reached your heart?”, returns there word for word (18.73), when Patroclus is dead.

## Outside Homer

Plato would keep from children the stories of Hera bound by her son and of Hephaestus hurled from heaven by his father “when he was about to defend his mother” (*Republic* 2.378d; see [[plato-hephaestus-hurled]]).
