title: The jealousy of the gods
kind: theme
tags: [divine-will, divine-scene]
summary: The gods' grudge against mortals who come too near what the gods keep for themselves. In the Odyssey Calypso calls the gods “jealous beyond all others”, who begrudge goddesses their mortal husbands, and Homer's verb for the grudge, ἄγαμαι, recurs when gods begrudge men a victory, a life together or a safe passage. Later Greeks called the divine φθονερός, “jealous”, and Plato and Aristotle denied that it could be.
aliases: [jealous gods, the gods begrudge, the envy of the gods, phthonos theon]

## In Homer

**The word.** The plainest statement of the theme in Homer is Calypso's, when Hermes tells her that she must let Odysseus go: “You are hard, you gods, and jealous beyond all others, / you who begrudge goddesses that they should lie with men / openly, if one makes a man her dear husband” (*Odyssey* 5.118–120). ζηλήμων, “jealous”, is found only here in Homer; the scholia and Eustathius record δηλήμονες, “destructive”, the word of Apollo's protest to the gods over Hector's body (*Iliad* 24.33), and W. W. Merry keeps ζηλήμονες, which the next verse explains (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on *Odyssey* 5.118; see the translation's note on *Odyssey* 5.118). Her verb is ἄγαμαι, “begrudge” (*Odyssey* 5.119, 5.122, 5.129), and her proofs are Dawn's Orion, killed by Artemis, and Demeter's Iasion, killed by Zeus (*Odyssey* 5.121–128; see [[dawn-and-orion]], [[demeter-and-iasion]] and [[calypso]]).

**Other grudges.** The same verb carries the gods' grudge elsewhere. Menelaus would easily have carried off the armor of Euphorbus, “had not Phoebus Apollo begrudged him that” (*Iliad* 17.71; see [[apollo]]). In the Odyssey, when he learns who his young guest is, he says that he would have given Odysseus a city in Argos to dwell in near him, “But the god himself, it seems, was to begrudge us this, / who has made that unhappy man alone without a homecoming” (*Odyssey* 4.181–182). Alcinous remembers his father's prophecy that Poseidon would begrudge (ἀγάσασθαι) the Phaeacians their safe escort of all men, and one day wreck one of their ships (*Odyssey* 8.564–569, and again when it comes true, 13.172–177; see [[phaeacians]] and [[poseidon]]). And Penelope, when she knows her husband at last, says that the gods gave them sorrow, “who begrudged us that we should stay beside each other” and enjoy their youth together and come to the threshold of old age (θεοὶ … οἳ νῶϊν ἀγάσαντο παρʼ ἀλλήλοισι μένοντε, *Odyssey* 23.210–212, a plain rendering), in the words used of Calypso and Odysseus on their last night, “staying by each other” (παρʼ ἀλλήλοισι μένοντες, *Odyssey* 5.227). The gods' ἄγαμαι is not always a grudge: Telemachus warns the Ithacans that the gods may turn on them “indignant at your evil deeds” (*Odyssey* 2.67; see [[nemesis]]).

**The gods who live at ease.** The theme turns on the difference between gods and men. The gods who begrudge Dawn her lover are θεοὶ ῥεῖα ζώοντες, “gods who live at ease” (*Odyssey* 5.122; see [[who-live-at-ease]]), and what they keep from mortals is what makes them gods: Calypso had meant to make Odysseus “immortal and ageless all his days” (*Odyssey* 5.136; see [[immortality]], [[immortal-and-ageless]] and [[gods-and-mortals]]).

## Outside Homer

Near its end the *Hymn to Aphrodite* gives the goddess's warning to Anchises, her mortal lover: if he boasts that he lay with her, “Zeus in his anger will strike you with a smoking thunderbolt”, and she closes with θεῶν δʼ ἐποπίζεο μῆνιν, “fear the wrath of the gods” (*Hymn to Aphrodite* 286–290), nearly the words of Hermes' warning to Calypso, Διὸς δʼ ἐποπίζεο μῆνιν, “have regard for the wrath of Zeus” (*Odyssey* 5.146; see [[aphrodite-and-anchises]]).

In Herodotus the jealousy of the divine is a rule of history. Solon tells Croesus that he knows “the divine is altogether jealous and disruptive” (τὸ θεῖον πᾶν ἐὸν φθονερόν τε καὶ ταραχῶδες, Herodotus 1.32.1; see [[solon]] and [[croesus]]); Amasis writes to Polycrates that his great good fortune does not please one who knows “that the divine is jealous” (Herodotus 3.40.2; see [[polycrates]]); Artabanus warns Xerxes that the god strikes with his thunderbolt the creatures and houses and trees that stand out, “for the god allows no one but himself to think great thoughts” (Herodotus 7.10ε), and says that the god, who gives men a taste of the sweetness of life, is found to be jealous in it (Herodotus 7.46.4; see [[xerxes]] and [[herodotus]]). The philosophers denied it. Plato's Socrates says that “jealousy stands outside the divine chorus” (*Phaedrus* 247a), and Timaeus that the maker of the world was good, and in the good no jealousy of anything ever arises (*Timaeus* 29e; see [[plato]]). Aristotle grants that if there were anything in what the poets say, and the divine were by nature jealous, all who excelled in wisdom would be unfortunate; “but the divine cannot be jealous, and, as the proverb has it, bards tell many lies” (*Metaphysics* 1.2, 982b32–983a4; see [[aristotle]]). His own account of envy, φθόνος, among the emotions, is in the *Rhetoric* (see [[phthonos]]).
