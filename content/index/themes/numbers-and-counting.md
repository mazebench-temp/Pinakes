Homer's numbers are few and formal, and they are chosen for their shape rather than their accuracy. The same ones recur: three, for an attempt made three times and changed on the fourth; nine, followed by a turn on the tenth; twelve, twenty, fifty and a hundred. For nine days Apollo's arrows range through the camp, and on the tenth Achilles calls the assembly (1.53–54). Bellerophon's host entertains him for nine days, and on the tenth dawn asks to see the message he carries (6.174–176). The gods stay twelve days among the Ethiopians (1.423–425). A hundred is the number of plenty, as in the [[hecatomb]], whose name was heard as “a hundred oxen”.

Wealth is counted in cattle. At the funeral games a tripod is valued at twelve oxen and a skilled woman at four (23.702–705), and Glaucus gives Diomedes golden armor worth a hundred oxen for bronze worth nine (6.234–236). Numbers also serve rhetoric. Refusing Agamemnon's gifts, Achilles piles one count on another: not ten times or twenty times as much, not all the wealth of Egyptian Thebes with its hundred gates, not gifts as many as sand and dust (9.379–385).

Counting is a way of taking hold of the world, and the Iliad knows its limits. A king counts his forces; a crowd too large to count is given by likeness, as many as leaves and flowers, flies or sand. The poem's great act of counting is the Catalogue of Ships in Book 2, which gives the number of ships for every Achaean contingent (see [[catalogue-of-ships]]).
