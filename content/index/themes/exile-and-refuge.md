## The pattern

A killing at home sets a man on the road. In the Odyssey Odysseus states the rule: a man who has killed even one man among his people, with few to avenge him, “flees, leaving his kin and his native land” (*Odyssey* 23.118–120). The alternative is a price. Ajax tells Achilles that a man accepts the blood-price even from the killer of his brother or his son, “and the killer stays there among his people, having paid much” (9.632–636; see [[poine]]). When no price is accepted, or the dead man's kin are too strong, the killer flees, and he comes as a suppliant to a lord in another land, who receives him and gives him a place (see [[supplication]] and [[xenia]]).

The Iliad tells such stories in a line or two, often at the moment the exile dies. Tlepolemus killed his father's uncle Licymnius, built ships and fled from the other sons and grandsons of Heracles: “And he came to Rhodes in his wanderings, suffering sorrows” (2.661–667; see [[the-flight-of-tlepolemus]]). Medon, the bastard son of Oileus, lived in Phylace “away from his native land”, because he had killed a kinsman of his stepmother Eriopis (13.694–697 = 15.333–336; see [[medon]]). Lycophron of Cythera lived with Ajax as his attendant, “since he had killed a man in holy Cythera” (15.430–432). Epeigeus, once king of Boudeion, killed his cousin and came as a suppliant to Peleus and Thetis, and they sent him with Achilles to Troy (16.570–576). Patroclus, as a boy, killed the son of Amphidamas in a quarrel over knucklebones, “not meaning to”; Menoetius brought him to Peleus, who reared him and named him Achilles' attendant (23.85–90; see [[patroclus]]). The house of Peleus is the poem's great refuge. In the Odyssey the seer Theoclymenus, fleeing Argos after killing a kinsman, begs Telemachus to take him on board (*Odyssey* 15.223–225, 15.272–278).

## Phoenix

Phoenix's story is the fullest, and he is not a killer. He fled the anger and the curse of his father Amyntor, after a quarrel over his father's concubine (9.447–457); in the four lines that the manuscripts lack, he tells how he planned to kill his father and was held back by a god and by the fear of being called a father-killer (9.458–461; see [[phoenix-patricide-lines]]). His kinsmen kept him under guard for nine nights; on the tenth he broke out, fled through Hellas, and came to Phthia, “to lord Peleus; and he received me gladly, and loved me as a father loves his son”, made him wealthy and gave him the Dolopians to rule at the edge of his land (9.474–484; see [[phoenix]], [[peleus]] and [[the-flight-of-phoenix]]). The refugee becomes a son and a lord, and then the tutor of his host's son.

## The man without a place

The exile is a man without the protection of his kin. Nestor's maxim names the outcast who has made himself one, “without clan, without law, without hearth” (9.63; see [[without-clan-law-or-hearth]]). Achilles uses the figure for his own humiliation: Agamemnon treated him “as if I were some dishonored vagrant” (ἀτίμητον μετανάστην, 9.648 = 16.59; see [[like-a-dishonored-vagrant]]). A μετανάστης is one who has moved away from his own people, and among strangers he can be wronged without anyone exacting a price (see [[time]]).

## Priam

When Priam enters Achilles' hut and kisses his hands, the poem compares him to a fugitive killer: “as when blind folly seizes a man who has killed a man in his own country, and he comes to the land of others, to the house of a rich man, and wonder holds those who see him”, so Achilles wondered at the sight of Priam (24.480–484; see [[ate]]). The roles are reversed. The man who comes as a suppliant to the killer's house is the father of the man who was killed.
