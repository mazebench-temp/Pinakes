A hero of the Iliad is his father's son before he is anything else. The first line of the poem names Achilles as Πηληϊάδης, “son of Peleus”, and patronymics name men throughout it: sons of Atreus, of Tydeus, of Priam. When two men meet in battle they may ask each other's lineage, and Glaucus answers Diomedes with six generations of his family (6.145–211). To name a father is to make a claim, and Glaucus ends his account with the verb of claiming: “This is the lineage and the blood I claim” (6.211; see [[euchomai]]).

## Sent by their fathers

The sons came to Troy with their fathers' words in their ears. Hippolochus sent Glaucus off “always to be the best and to excel above the others, and not to shame the race of my fathers” (6.207–209), and Peleus gave Achilles the same charge (11.783–784). Peleus sent Phoenix with him, to make him “a speaker of words and a doer of deeds” (9.438–443). Some fathers could not hold their sons back. Merops of Percote, who knew prophecy above all men, would not let his sons go to the war, and they would not obey him, “for the fates of black death were leading them on” (2.830–834).

## Measured against them

The sons are weighed against the fathers, and the scale usually tips toward the dead. Nestor tells the quarreling kings that he once kept company with better men than they are (1.260–272). In Book 4 Agamemnon holds up Tydeus to his son, and ends: “the son he fathered is worse than he in battle, though better in assembly” (4.400); in Book 5 Athena says it again, “Tydeus has begotten a son little like himself” (5.800). Sthenelus answers with the opposite boast, that the sons are far better than their fathers (4.405; see [[better-than-our-fathers]]). The Odyssey gives the rule as Athena states it to Telemachus: “few sons are like their fathers; most are worse, and few are better” (*Odyssey* 2.276–277). A father can wish the reverse. Hector prays that one day someone may say of his son, “he is far better than his father” (6.479).

A son may not remember his father at all. Diomedes tells Glaucus that he does not remember Tydeus, “since I was still little when he left me, when the army of the Achaeans perished at Thebes” (6.222–223), and Agamemnon admits that he never met him (4.374–375). The father who is held up as a model is known by report.

## Lost

The obituaries of the battle books often end with a father. Diomedes kills the two sons of Phaenops, an old man worn down by grievous age who had no other son to leave his property to; he leaves the father lamentation and sorrow, and distant kin divide his goods (5.152–158). A young man killed before his time does not repay his parents for his rearing (4.477–478; see [[threptra]]). Priam, who could not bear to watch his son fight Menelaus (3.306–307), watches Hector die from the wall (22.25–91, 22.405–409). Zeus himself sheds a rain of blood for his son Sarpedon, whom he will not save (16.459–461).

The poem ends with fathers. Priam comes to Achilles and asks him to “remember your father” (24.486), and the two weep together, the one for Hector and the other for his father and for Patroclus (24.507–512). Achilles knows that he will not care for Peleus in his old age, since he sits far away at Troy (24.538–542), and the armor the gods gave Peleus is lost with Patroclus: “the son did not grow old in his father's armor” (17.194–197). The Iliad began with a father trying to ransom his daughter (see [[fathers-and-daughters]]) and ends with a father ransoming his son (see [[book-1-and-book-24]]).
