## In Homer

**Proteus.** The one true shape-changer of the poems is the Old Man of the Sea of Egypt. His daughter warns Menelaus that “He will make his attempt by becoming all the things that upon the earth / creep, and water, and wondrous fire” (*Odyssey* 4.417–418), and when the men seize him “first of all he became a bearded lion, / and then a serpent, and a leopard, and a great boar; / and he became flowing water, and a tree with high foliage” (*Odyssey* 4.456–458). They hold him until he tires and speaks to them in his own shape (*Odyssey* 4.420–424, 4.459–461; see [[proteus]] and [[eidothea]]). Merry notes that his power to take many shapes was never in doubt, and that it belongs above all to the gods of the sea and of rivers, Nereus, Glaucus and Achelous (*Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 4.417 and 4.419).

**In a mortal's likeness.** The gods who come among men usually come as someone else. Athena visits Telemachus as Mentes the Taphian and sails with him as Mentor, and meets Odysseus in Phaeacia as a girl carrying a pitcher (*Odyssey* 1.105, 2.268, 2.401, 7.19–20; see [[in-the-likeness-of-mentor]]); Poseidon comes up out of the sea to urge on the Argives “in the likeness of Calchas in form and in tireless voice” (*Iliad* 13.43–45). The verbs are εἴδομαι and ἔοικα, “be like” (see [[eidos]]), and the change is of looks and voice, not of nature: the lesser Ajax knows Poseidon by his feet as he goes (*Iliad* 13.71–72), and Noemon can only say that he saw “Mentor, or a god, who was like him in every way” (*Odyssey* 4.654; see [[seeing-the-gods]]). One of the suitors gives the belief its general form: the gods, “in the likeness of strangers from abroad, / taking every shape”, go about the cities, watching men's insolence and their good order (*Odyssey* 17.485–487, plain renderings).

**Birds and phantoms.** Athena leaves Pylos in the likeness of a sea eagle (*Odyssey* 3.371–372; see [[phene]]). A god may also leave a likeness behind and go: Apollo makes a phantom of Aeneas for the armies to fight over (*Iliad* 5.449–453), and Athena makes a phantom like Penelope's sister Iphthime and sends it to her in a dream (*Odyssey* 4.795–797; see [[eidolon]] and [[iphthime]]).

**Changing mortals.** The gods change the shapes of men as well. Athena touches Odysseus with her wand, withers his skin, takes the hair from his head and makes him an old beggar, and later gives him back his youth and color (*Odyssey* 13.429–433, 16.172–176; see [[odysseus]] and [[athena]]). Circe strikes Odysseus' companions with her wand and they have the heads, the voices, the bristles and the bodies of swine, but their minds are as before (*Odyssey* 10.237–240; see [[circe]]). Odysseus can make the change himself without a god: Helen tells how he disfigured himself with blows and slipped into Troy in the likeness of a beggar (*Odyssey* 4.244–250; see [[odysseus-enters-troy-as-a-beggar]]), and she herself, at the Wooden Horse, made her voice like the voices of the Achaean wives (*Odyssey* 4.277–279; see [[helen]]).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates argues that a god, being in every way the best, would least of all take many forms, and could change himself only for the worse, so that each god remains always simply in his own form; and so no poet may tell that gods go about the cities in the likeness of strangers, or tell lies about Proteus and Thetis (*Republic* 2, 381b–d; see [[platos-proteus]] and [[plato]]). Merry quotes Ovid's Proteus advising Peleus to hold Thetis fast through all her changes until she takes again her own form (*Metamorphoses* 11.254, quoted on 4.419), and his list of Proteus' shapes (*Metamorphoses* 8.732 and following, quoted on 4.458; see [[virgils-proteus]]).
