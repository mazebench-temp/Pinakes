The gods of the Iliad share the form, the passions and the speech of men, and differ from them in the one thing that matters most: they do not grow old or die. They eat no bread and drink no wine, and ichor, not blood, flows in their veins (5.339–342). Apollo warns Diomedes that the race of the immortal gods and the race of men who walk on the earth are never alike (5.440–442), and Achilles tells Priam that the gods have spun for wretched mortals a life of grief, while they themselves are free of care (24.525–526). Yet gods and men are bound together as parents and children, patrons and favorites, and the poem keeps the two orders side by side, each measured by the other. The contrast is drawn in the vocabulary itself.

## The words of difference

The gods are θεοὶ αἰὲν ἐόντες, “the gods who live forever” (1.290, 1.494), ἀθάνατοι, “immortals”, and μάκαρες, “the blessed” (1.339, 1.406, 1.599). Men are θνητοί, “mortals” (1.339, 1.574), and βροτοί, as in “the mortals now living on earth” (1.272). Nestor has outlived two generations of “mortal men” (1.250). Even names differ. The hundred-hander whom Thetis summoned is called Briareus by the gods and Aegaeon by men (1.403–404).

Jasper Griffin (*Homer on Life and Death*, 1980) argued that the lightness of the gods is what makes human seriousness visible. Men, who die, cannot afford to laugh at their quarrels.

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates objects to the gods' laughter at 1.599–600: good men should not be shown overcome by laughter, still less gods (*Republic* 3.388e–389a; see [[plato-homeric-laughter]]).
