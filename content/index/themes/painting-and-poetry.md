## The comparison

The *Poetics* turns to painting at every stage of its argument. Some people imitate with colors and shapes, others with the voice (1447a18–20). Polygnotus portrayed people better than we are, Pauson worse, Dionysius like us (1448a5–6; see [[better-worse-or-like-us]]). We enjoy exact images of things painful to see (1448b10–19; see [[pleasure-of-imitation]]). Polygnotus is a good painter of character, while the painting of Zeuxis has none (1450a26–29), and the most beautiful colors smeared at random give less pleasure than an image outlined in white, which is why the plot comes before character (1450a38–b3; see [[the-soul-of-tragedy]]). Good portrait painters make their subjects like and yet more beautiful (1454b9–11). The poet is an imitator just like a painter (1460b8–9); it is a lesser fault not to know that a female deer has no horns than to paint one unrecognizably (1460b31–32); and Zeuxis painted people better than they are, as the model should surpass reality (1461b12–13; see [[polygnotus]], [[pauson]], [[zeuxis]] and [[dionysius-of-colophon]]).

## Before and after

Simonides is said to have called painting silent poetry and poetry painting that speaks (Plutarch, *On the Fame of the Athenians* 3, 346f; see [[simonides]]). Horace's “as a painting, so a poem” (*ut pictura poesis*, *Ars Poetica* 361) became the motto of centuries of criticism, until Lessing's *Laocoon* (1766) argued that painting, an art of space, and poetry, an art of time, must work by different laws (see [[horace]] and [[lessing]]).
