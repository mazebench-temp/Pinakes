## In the Iliad

**The call not heard.** In Book 8, as Zeus' lightning sends the Achaean leaders running, Diomedes calls on Odysseus by his full titles to help him save Nestor: “where are you fleeing, turning your back like a coward in the throng?” (8.93–94). Odysseus “did not hear”, and Diomedes goes in “alone as he was” (8.97–99; see [[diomedes]] and [[odysseus]]).

**The choice.** That night Nestor asks for a man to spy on the Trojans, and Diomedes offers to go if another will go with him, for “When two go together, one sees before the other / how the advantage lies” (10.224–225; see [[when-two-go-together]]). Six others are willing; Agamemnon tells him to take the best man and not to look to birth, and he names Odysseus:

{{quote:d7ca6f82-cb87-51d8-ba83-2e6cbbbf06c4}}

He says nothing of the day before. Together they kill Hector's spy Dolon and the Thracian king Rhesus with twelve of his men, and drive Rhesus' horses back to the ships (see the note on Book 10 below, and [[the-doloneia]]). Nestor's welcome takes them together: “for Zeus the cloud-gatherer loves you both” (10.552; see [[athena]]).

**Standing together.** The next day reverses Book 8. When Hector routs the Achaeans, Odysseus calls on Diomedes to stand by him, and he stays (11.310–319). They charge like two boars turning on the hunting dogs (11.324–325), the third beast the poem makes of the pair, after the two lions and the two hounds of the night (10.297, 10.360–364; see [[two-lions]] and [[two-hounds]]). They kill the two sons of the seer Merops (11.328–335; see [[the-sons-of-merops]]), and Diomedes stuns Hector with a spear-cast to the helmet; then Paris, shooting from beside the pillar on the tomb of Ilus, wounds him in the foot (11.343–378; see [[the-tomb-of-ilus]]). Odysseus covers him while he draws out the arrow, and is left alone when he goes (11.396–410). Wounded, both come with Agamemnon to Nestor (14.27–29), and in Book 19 the two limp to the assembly leaning on their spears, Ἄρεος θεράποντε, a pair of “attendants of Ares”, as the two Ajaxes are called in Book 10 (19.47–49; 10.228).

**Old and young.** Diomedes is the youngest of the kings, as he says himself (14.112), and Nestor says he could be his own youngest son (9.57–58); Odysseus, says Antilochus, is προτέρης γενεῆς, of an earlier generation (23.790–791). The young fighter chooses the older man for his mind, “since he is wise beyond all others” (10.247), but the poem does not divide them simply into strength and cunning. Nestor calls Diomedes the best in counsel of the men of his age (9.53–54), and the narrator says of Odysseus that his heart “was always daring” (10.232; see [[metis]]).

**After Troy.** The Odyssey parts them at Tenedos: Odysseus turned back to Agamemnon, while Diomedes sailed on with Nestor and reached Argos on the fourth day (*Odyssey* 3.159–167, 3.180–182).

## Outside Homer

**The Palladium and Philoctetes.** In the epic *Little Iliad*, as Proclus summarizes it, Diomedes brought Philoctetes from Lemnos after Odysseus had captured the seer Helenus, and Odysseus, after spying in Troy alone and in disguise, carried the Palladium out of the city with Diomedes' help (see [[proclus]] and [[philoctetes-on-lemnos]]). Apollodorus sends both men to Lemnos, where Odysseus gets Heracles' bow by craft (*Epitome* 5.8), and tells how Odysseus, leaving Diomedes waiting, went into the city by night as a beggar, stole the image with Helen's help, and brought it to the ships with Diomedes (*Epitome* 5.13; see [[apollodorus]]). In Sophocles' *Philoctetes*, Odysseus' man, disguised as a merchant, tells Neoptolemus that the son of Tydeus and Odysseus have sworn to bring Philoctetes back (542–627; see [[sophocles]]). Virgil's Sinon dates the Greeks' decline from the day when *impius … Tydides sed enim scelerumque inventor Ulixes*, “the impious son of Tydeus and Ulysses, inventor of crimes”, tore the Palladium from its temple with bloody hands (*Aeneid* 2.162–170; see [[virgil]]).

**A quarrel.** Among the Suda's explanations of the proverb “Diomedean compulsion” (Διομήδειος ἀνάγκη) is a story of the night of the Palladium: Odysseus, walking behind, meant to kill Diomedes, who saw the shadow of the sword in the moonlight and drove Odysseus before him, beating him on the back with his sword (Suda δ 1164).

**Virgil.** The night raid of Nisus and Euryalus, two friends who go out together from the Trojan camp in Italy and kill sleeping men before they are caught, takes its shape from this one (*Aeneid* 9.176–502; see [[nisus-and-euryalus]]).

**Ovid and Dante.** Ovid's Ajax says that Ulysses did nothing by daylight and nothing without Diomedes, naming Rhesus and “unwarlike Dolon” (*Metamorphoses* 13.98–102); Ulysses answers that it is something to be chosen by Diomedes out of so many thousands of Greeks, and claims the killings of Dolon and Rhesus for himself (13.238–253; see [[ovid-dolon-and-rhesus]] and [[ovid]]). Dante sees the two in a single flame divided at the top, among the fraudulent counselors, punished together for the horse, for the trick that parted Achilles from Deidamia, and for the Palladium (*Inferno* 26.52–63; see [[dante]]).
