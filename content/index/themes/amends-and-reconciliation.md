## Making amends

The verb is ἀρέσκομαι, “make amends, set right”. Agamemnon uses it when he takes back a rebuke he has given Odysseus at the review: “we will make these things good later, if anything bad has been said now” (4.357–363; see [[the-review-of-the-army]]), and Hector uses it to Paris, “we will make these things right later”, if Zeus ever lets the Trojans drive the Achaeans away (6.526). In Book 9 it names the purpose of the embassy. Nestor proposes that they consider “how we may make amends to him and win him over with kindly gifts and with soothing words” (9.112–113), and Agamemnon answers, “I am willing to make amends and give a boundless ransom” (9.120; see [[ate]] and [[apoina]]). Amends are made with gifts, with words, and with an oath, and they restore honor by paying it (see [[time]] and [[doron]]).

## Amends refused

The embassy fails. Achilles answers that no gifts will persuade him until the whole insult is paid off, that the king is worth nothing to him, and that nothing is worth his life (9.378–409; see [[honor-and-dishonor]] and [[lobe]]). Phoenix argues from the Prayers, who come after Blind Folly to heal the harm, and from Meleager, who refused the gifts and fought without them (9.502–605; see [[litai]] and [[the-wrath-of-meleager]]). Ajax argues from the blood-price, which a man accepts even for a brother or a son (9.632–636; see [[poine]]). Diomedes ends by wishing the king had never begged him (9.698).

## Amends accepted

In Book 19 the quarrel is ended in public. Achilles calls the assembly and renounces his wrath; Agamemnon blames Zeus, Moira, the Erinys and Ate, and offers the gifts again (19.56–144). Odysseus gives the rule for such endings: let Agamemnon make amends with a rich feast, and in future be more just, “for it is no blame for a king to make amends to a man when he was the first to be angry” (19.179–183). The gifts are brought out and the oath is sworn over Briseis (19.238–268). Achilles takes them with indifference (19.146–148). What moves him is Patroclus' death, not the gifts.

## Other endings

Smaller quarrels end in other ways. Hephaestus ends the quarrel of Zeus and Hera with a cup and a laugh, urging his mother to do a kindness to his father, “then at once the Olympian will be gracious to us” (1.571–600; see [[quarrel-on-olympus]]). Hector and Ajax end their duel with gifts, so that men will say “They fought in heart-devouring strife, and then parted in friendship, reconciled” (7.299–302; see [[parted-in-friendship]]). Glaucus and Diomedes exchange armor because their grandfathers were guest-friends (6.230–236; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]]). After the chariot race Antilochus, the younger man, gives way and offers Menelaus the mare, and Menelaus gives it back, “so that these men may know that my heart is never overbearing or harsh” (23.586–611): a quarrel over a prize settled by both men yielding. Last, Achilles accepts Priam's ransom, eats with him, and grants a truce for Hector's funeral (24.560–670).
