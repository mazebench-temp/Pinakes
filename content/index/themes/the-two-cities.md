## The city at peace

{{quote:2cb8171e-c572-5ebb-a227-01b4a0d6fd15}}

The first city is shown in two scenes. In the streets brides are led from their chambers under blazing torches with the bridal song, young men whirl in the dance to flutes and lyres, and the women stand at their doors and marvel (18.491–496; see [[marriage]], [[dance]], [[aulos]] and [[lyre]]). Walter Leaf takes the plurals as indefinite, one marriage standing for many (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 18.491). In the place of assembly two men quarrel over the penalty for a dead man and seek a decision before the elders, who judge in turn for a prize of two talents of gold (18.497–508; see [[the-trial-on-the-shield]]).

## The city at war

“And around the other city sat two armies of men, / flashing in their armor; and they were divided in their counsel” (18.509–510): they cannot agree whether to sack it or to divide its wealth in two (18.511–512). Leaf explains the second course as half of the city's property left to the citizens and half taken as ransom, the terms Hector thinks of offering Achilles (on 18.511; 22.117–120), and takes the two armies as one besieging host divided in two groups for the picture (on 18.510).

The besieged “would not yet yield, and were secretly arming for an ambush” (18.513). Their wives, little children and old men guard the wall (18.514–515; see [[women-and-war]]); Ares and Pallas Athena lead the rest out, golden and taller than the people, “as gods should be” (18.516–519). Leaf calls the two gods the only distinctively Hellenic touch on the Shield (on 18.516). At a river where the herds come to drink the men sit down in ambush, with two lookouts watching for the cattle (18.520–524; see [[ambush]] and [[spies-and-scouts]]). The herds come with two herdsmen playing their pipes, who “did not foresee the trick at all”; the ambushers cut off the cattle and the sheep and kill the herdsmen (18.525–529; see [[syrinx]] and [[raiding]]).

The besiegers hear the din as they sit in front of their places of assembly, mount behind their horses and come up; the two sides “set their battle in array and fought beside the river's banks” (18.530–534; see [[battle-order]]). Strife, Tumult and Doom are in the throng, Doom holding one man freshly wounded, another unwounded, and dragging a dead man by the feet, her cloak red with blood (18.535–538; see [[strife]], [[tumult]] and [[ker-doom]]). The men “fought like living mortals, / and they dragged away the bodies of one another's dead” (18.539–540; see [[fighting-over-the-body]]).

## Peace and war

The pair follows the earth, sky, sea and stars at the Shield's center and comes before the countryside of plowing, harvest, vintage and herds (see [[the-shield-of-achilles]], [[the-plowing]], [[the-kings-harvest]], [[the-vintage]] and [[the-herd-and-the-lions]]). The city at war is drawn in the terms of the Iliad's own war: a wall manned by women, children and old men, besiegers who debate whether to sack the city or take a ransom, and a fight over the dead. The city at peace has weddings, and a quarrel brought before judges instead of fought out. Ancient readers looked for real places in the two. Leaf reports that Agallis of Corcyra, quoted by a scholiast, took the Shield for the early history of Attica, the two cities being Attica and Eleusis, and that Maximus of Tyre took them for Phaeacia and Ithaca, as types of good government and of anarchy (on 18.483; see the translation's note on 18.483–608).
