The warriors of the Iliad fear the name of coward, κακός or δειλός, more than wounds (see [[deos]]), and the poem shows what flight costs. A man who turns his horses is speared in the back “between the shoulders” (5.40–41, 8.257–259, 11.447–448), and Agamemnon tells the army that when men feel shame more are saved than are killed, “but when men flee, there rises neither fame nor any courage” (5.529–532; see [[shame-saves-more]]). Paris, shrinking back into the crowd from Menelaus like a man who has seen a snake, earns his brother's rebuke (3.30–57). Yet retreat is not always shameful. The speakers of the poem allow it before a power that a man cannot fight, and they argue over where that line lies.

## Before a god

A man may give way to a god. When Diomedes sees Ares beside Hector, he orders the Achaeans to “keep your faces turned toward the Trojans and give ground always backward, and do not be eager to fight in force against gods” (5.605–606), and the army does so, neither fleeing to the ships nor charging (5.699–702; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]). Giving way to Zeus is the same case, and in Book 8 Nestor has to persuade Diomedes of it (see the note on Book 8). Menelaus, alone over the body of Patroclus, reasons it out for himself: whoever fights a man whom a god honors brings a great disaster on himself, so no Danaan will blame him for yielding to Hector, “since he fights with the gods' help” (17.91–105).

## The voice of others

The argument is always held with an imagined voice. Diomedes fears what Hector will say among the Trojans (8.148–150; see [[may-the-earth-gape]]). Odysseus, left alone when Zeus has put the other Danaans to flight, tells himself that to flee is bad and to be caught alone worse, but that cowards leave the fighting, and the man who would be best must stand his ground (11.403–410). Hector, waiting for Achilles before the gates, imagines that Polydamas will reproach him first and that someone worse than he will say that Hector destroyed the people, and he stays (22.99–110; see [[tis-speech]] and [[aidos]]). He asks only that Achilles not plant a spear in his back as he flees (22.283).

Flight by sea is the last retreat, and Agamemnon proposes it three times: to test the army (2.139–141), in earnest at the night assembly of Book 9, where Diomedes answers him (9.26–49), and in Book 14, when he says that “there is no blame in fleeing ruin, even by night”, and Odysseus answers him with scorn (14.74–102; see [[war-and-night]]).

## Outside Homer

Archilochus made a poem of what Homer's men dread: one of the Saians, a Thracian tribe, now delights in the shield he left by a bush, unwillingly, but he saved himself, and he will get another no worse (fr. 5 West). Tyrtaeus says in elegy what Agamemnon says in Book 5: those who dare to stand together die fewer and save the people behind them, but when men run, all excellence is lost (fr. 11.11–14 West). In Plato's *Laches* Socrates, arguing that courage is not only standing one's ground, recalls that Homer praises the horses of Aeneas for knowing how to pursue and flee swiftly this way and that, and calls Aeneas himself a “master of rout”, though in the Iliad the phrase, in the dual, belongs to the horses (*Laches* 191a–b; see 5.222–223, 5.272, 8.106–108 and [[master-of-rout]]).
