The gods of the Iliad come among men, but men seldom see them as they are. A god may appear to one man and to no one else, as Athena appears to Achilles, “to him alone; none of the others saw her” (1.198). A god may take a mortal's shape: Iris speaks to the Trojans in the voice of Priam's son Polites (2.786–807), Aphrodite comes to Helen as an old wool-worker (3.385–389), and Athena looks for Pandarus in the likeness of Laodocus (4.86–88). A god may go unseen, wrapped in cloud. The poem states the difficulty both ways. Hera says that “the gods are hard to face when they appear in their own form” (χαλεποὶ δὲ θεοὶ φαίνεσθαι ἐναργεῖς, 20.131). The lesser Ajax, who has just recognized Poseidon from behind by his feet and legs as he left, says that “gods are easy to know” (ἀρίγνωτοι δὲ θεοί περ, 13.68–72). Helen knows Aphrodite by her neck, her breasts and her eyes (3.396–398). Recognition usually comes late, when the god is leaving or lets the disguise slip.

## Book 5: god and man

Book 5 makes recognition its subject. After Diomedes' prayer Athena gives him a new kind of sight:

{{quote:85a7e4df-a946-5a51-9baa-282144c09f8b}}

The gift comes with a rule, to fight no god but Aphrodite (5.129–132), and the rest of the book turns on who can see what. The Trojans cannot. Aeneas wonders whether the man sweeping the field is “some god who bears resentment against the Trojans” (5.177), and Pandarus, who has shot him, does “not know for sure whether he is a god”, and supposes that an immortal stands beside him, “his shoulders wrapped in cloud”, turning his arrows aside (5.183–191). He is right, but he cannot see her. Diomedes can. He goes after Aphrodite “knowing that she was a feeble god” (5.331), and he springs at Aeneas “though he knew that Apollo himself was holding his hands over him” (5.433). Seeing the god does not stop him; Apollo has to tell him what the sight means (5.440–442; see [[gods-and-men-not-alike]]).

Ares, meanwhile, goes among the Trojans in the likeness of Acamas, leader of the Thracians (5.462). When he comes on beside Hector, Diomedes sees him and shudders, and tells the Achaeans that the god is there “in the likeness of a mortal man”; they must give ground, keeping their faces to the enemy (5.596–606). This time the sight saves the army. Near the end Diomedes knows Athena herself, “I know you, goddess, daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus”, and explains that he has held back because “I recognize Ares ruling over the battle” (5.815–824). The verb is γιγνώσκω, “know, recognize”, the word Athena used of her gift. In the last scene of the fighting it is a god who cannot see. Athena puts on the helmet of Hades so that Ares may not see her (5.845; see [[helmet-of-hades]]), and he aims at the man in the chariot and never sees the goddess who turns his spear.

## Mist and cloud

The mist on mortal eyes lets gods pass unrecognized, and gods also hide themselves and their favorites in cloud: Apollo hides Aeneas in a dark cloud (5.345; see [[achlys]]). In the Odyssey Odysseus says of Circe, who passed them unseen, “who could see a god with his eyes, if the god did not wish it, going this way or that?” (*Odyssey* 10.573–574). When Athena stands at the door of Eumaeus' hut, Odysseus and the dogs see her, but Telemachus does not, “for the gods do not appear plainly to everyone” (*Odyssey* 16.159–163).

## Outside Homer

Virgil gives Aeneas the sight that Athena gave Diomedes, on the night Troy falls. Venus takes from his eyes the cloud that dulls mortal sight, and he sees the gods themselves pulling down the city: Neptune shaking the walls from their foundations, Juno at the Scaean gates, Pallas on the citadel with her Gorgon, and Jupiter rousing the gods against the Trojans (*Aeneid* 2.604–623).
