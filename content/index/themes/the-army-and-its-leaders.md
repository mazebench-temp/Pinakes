The Achaean army at Troy is an alliance of contingents, each from its own country and under its own leaders. Homer's words for it are [[laos|λαός]] (“the people, the host”, often in the plural λαοί), στρατός (“army, camp”) and πληθύς (“the multitude”). Its leaders are ἡγεμόνες, ἡγήτορες and ἀρχοί (see [[hegemon]]), and in the formulas the “shepherd of the people” and the “marshals of the people” (see [[shepherd-of-the-people]] and [[marshals-of-the-people]]). Each leader is a king among his own men, and the kings together serve, uneasily, under Agamemnon (see [[kingship-and-authority]]).

The bond runs both ways. The leader's error falls on his people: Apollo's plague kills the λαοί for Agamemnon's offense (1.10), and Hector, waiting for Achilles before the walls, admits that he has destroyed the people by his own recklessness (22.104). The people, for their part, follow, obey, and long for a leader who is gone. They seldom speak. The poem gives them a voice through an anonymous “someone” (see [[tis-speech]]), and through one man, Thersites, whom it silences.
