In the Iliad honor, [[time|τιμή]], is not a private sense of worth. It is recognition that others can see: a prize from the spoils, a seat, the best cuts of meat, the willingness of others to listen and obey. It can be given and taken away, and it can be paid back.

The vocabulary is concrete. τίω and τιμάω mean “honor, pay honor”; ἀτιμάζω and ἄτιμος, “dishonor, dishonored”. The [[geras|γέρας]] (“prize”) is honor in material form, and to take it away is to dishonor its holder. Honor is conferred by others: by the army, which shares out the spoils, and by the gods, above all Zeus, who give kings their standing and guard their own honors closely. It reaches into the future as [[kleos|κλέος]] (“fame”), what later men will hear, and its loss is felt as shame before others (see [[aidos|αἰδώς]]). Because honor is shared out, it can be taken as well as given, and an injury to it rarely stays private: the offender's people pay with him.

Sarpedon gives the reason kings are honored with seats, meat, full cups and land: they must earn it by fighting in the front rank (12.310–321). Achilles' complaint is that in the camp at Troy the honor and the fighting have come apart.
