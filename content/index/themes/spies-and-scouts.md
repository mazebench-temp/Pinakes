σκοπός, from the root of σκέπτομαι, “look about, watch”, is a watcher: a lookout on a height, a man set to observe and report, and in Book 10 a spy. A σκοπιή is the high place he watches from. ἐπίσκοπος is an overseer or guardian, and in Book 10 a man who goes “to spy on” the enemy (10.38, 10.342); διοπτήρ, “scout”, and διοπτεύω, “spy”, both from a verb of looking through, occur only in that book (10.562, 10.451). The verb σκοπιάζω is to keep watch or spy on (10.40; 14.58), and διασκοπιάομαι to make out each thing in turn (10.388; 17.252).

**Lookouts.** The Trojans keep a lookout on the plain. Before the first battle Iris comes to them in the likeness of Priam's son Polites, “who sat as a lookout for the Trojans, trusting in his speed of foot, on top of the tomb of old Aesyetes, waiting for the moment when the Achaeans would set out from their ships” (2.791–794; see [[polites]] and [[aesyetes]]). A lookout needs a height and fast feet, to see first and bring the news. On the shield of Achilles an ambush on a river sets “two lookouts apart from the people”, waiting to see the sheep and cattle come down to drink (18.520–524; see [[ambush]]). At the chariot race Achilles sets Phoenix as a σκοπός by the turning post, to mark the running and report the truth (23.359–361), and at Hector's burial the Trojans post lookouts all around while they heap the mound, in case the Achaeans attack before the truce is over (24.799–800). In the Odyssey Aegisthus posts a watchman for a whole year, for the wage of two talents of gold, to see Agamemnon land (*Odyssey* 4.524–528), and the suitors keep lookouts on the windy heights by day while they wait to ambush Telemachus (*Odyssey* 16.365–367).

**Spies.** To go into the enemy's ground and learn his plans is a rarer and more dangerous task, and in the Iliad it is done only in the night of Book 10, by both sides at once. Nestor asks for a man who will go among the Trojans and learn what they plan, and Hector for a man who will go close to the Achaean ships and learn whether they mean to flee (10.204–217, 10.303–312; see the note on Book 10). The spy has to be daring, a man who will “trust his own daring heart” (10.204–205), and both leaders promise him rewards (see [[doron]] and [[kleos]]).

The Odyssey remembers the Iliad's master spy doing it alone and in disguise. Helen tells how Odysseus once disfigured himself with blows, put rags on his shoulders like a servant, and “slipped into the wide-streeted city of hostile men” disguised as a beggar; she alone knew him, and when she had sworn not to betray him he told her all the Achaeans' plan, and went back to the Argives, having killed many Trojans, “and brought back much knowledge” (*Odyssey* 4.242–258). On his voyage Odysseus sends men ahead “to find out who the men were who ate bread on that land”, two of them and a herald as the third (*Odyssey* 9.88–90, 10.100–102), and climbs a lookout himself to see where smoke rises (*Odyssey* 10.146–152). See [[odysseus]].

## Outside Homer

The *Rhesus*, handed down among the plays of Euripides, makes the night of the spies its plot. Aeneas proposes sending a κατάσκοπος, “spy”, the later word, and Hector asks who will go, as in Homer (*Rhesus* 122–153); later Hector remembers that Odysseus had come into Troy before as a beggar in rags, “sent as a spy of Ilium”, and got out again after killing the guards of the gates (*Rhesus* 503–507); the chorus tells the same story (*Rhesus* 710–719). See [[euripides-rhesus]] and [[dolon]].
