## The image

The two sons of Cronus have “their minds set on different sides” (13.345). Zeus wills victory for the Trojans, to give glory to Achilles; Poseidon helps the Achaeans, but in secret, because Zeus is the elder (13.347–357; see [[zeus]], [[poseidon]] and [[poseidon-rallies-the-achaeans]]). The narrator then says in three verses what their two wills do to the battle, and the subject of the verb is both gods together: τοὶ δʼ … τάνυσσαν, “the two of them … stretched it tight”.

πεῖραρ is a rope, as in the Odyssey, where Odysseus is to be bound to the mast with ropes made fast to it (πείρατʼ, *Odyssey* 12.51, 12.162, 12.179). It is also an end or bound, as in “the bounds of victory” (7.102) and “the bounds of destruction” (7.402, 12.79; see [[the-bounds-of-victory]]). ἐπαλλάσσω, “cross, interchange”, occurs only here, and the picture is read in two ways (see the translation's note). Each god hauls at one end, as in a tug of war, with the armies in the middle; or the gods knot a cord over both armies and draw it tight, binding them together in the fight so that neither can get free. ὁμοίϊος, rendered “common to all”, has a disputed sense (see [[common-to-all]]).

The rope is “unbreakable and not to be loosed” (ἄρρηκτόν τʼ ἄλυτόν τε), the words of the golden hobbles with which Poseidon tethers his horses in the cave (13.36–37), and of the chains Hephaestus forged to hold Ares and Aphrodite (*Odyssey* 8.275). The verse ends in a turn on its own words: the rope that cannot be loosed (ἄλυτον) “loosened the knees of many” (γούνατʼ ἔλυσεν), the phrase of a man killed in battle (13.412; see [[gounata]] and [[loosened-his-limbs]]).

## The stretched battle

The rope gives a body to a verb that the poem uses of the gods and the fighting. “The son of Cronus stretched the battle evenly” for the two sides, looking down from Ida (κατὰ ἶσα μάχην ἐτάνυσσε, 11.336–337). At the wall “so evenly was their battle and war stretched tight” (12.436 = 15.413; see [[the-working-womans-scales]]). In Book 14 Poseidon and Hector stretch “the most dread strife of war” (14.389–390); over Sarpedon the son of Cronus stretches “mighty strife”, ἔριδα κρατερήν, the words of this rope (16.662), and over Patroclus Zeus stretches “an evil toil of men and horses” (17.400–401). In the same fight the two armies drag Patroclus' body this way and that, like men stretching an oxhide between them (17.389–395).

The rope's words return when the long day of battle ends: at sunset the Achaeans cease “from the mighty din of battle and from war common to all” (φυλόπιδος κρατερῆς καὶ ὁμοιΐου πολέμοιο, 18.242; see [[the-third-day-of-battle]]).

## The golden rope

In Book 8 Zeus dares all the gods to hang a golden rope from the sky and pull on it together; they could not drag him down, but he could haul them up with earth and sea (8.18–27; see [[the-golden-rope]]). That rope measures one god against all the others, and no one takes up the dare. The rope of war is held by two brothers of two minds, and it is the armies, not the gods, that are caught in it.
