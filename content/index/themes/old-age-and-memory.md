Old age in Book 1 is both weakness and authority. The same word, γέρων, “old man”, is used with contempt and with respect, and the old are the keepers of the past.

## The old priest

{{quote:dc0ef194-6a11-523a-9ad0-f64770b3d055}}

Agamemnon addresses Chryses as γέρον, “old man”, and the address comes with a threat: go, before the god's emblems fail to protect you. He threatens Chryseis with old age too. She will grow old (γῆρας) in his house in Argos, far from home (1.29). The old man is afraid and obeys (1.33), and goes to pray alone (ὁ γεραιός, 1.35). He cannot fight; he can only call on his god. At the end of his story the old man burns the thigh-pieces at Chryse (1.462) with his daughter restored.

## The old counselor

{{quote:7520a570-2b7c-5c09-83ec-4417bf81ed00}}

Nestor's age is his claim to be heard. His voice flows sweeter than honey (see [[sweeter-than-honey]]). Two generations of the men born and raised with him have passed away, and he is king among the third. He begins by telling both quarreling men that they are younger than he (1.259).

{{quote:8ce9109f-39dc-56b9-9e3e-5c482e6e4548}}

His argument is memory. He once kept company with better men than these, Peirithous, Dryas, Caeneus and the rest, the mightiest men on earth, who fought the Centaurs; and they heeded his counsels (see [[nestor-and-the-centaurs]]). The past is used as a model, a paradeigma, to shame the present into obedience. It fails. Agamemnon answers “Yes, old man (γέρον), all that you have said is fair and right”, and goes on as before (1.286). Nestor's later stories of his youth work better. In Book 7 his rebuke brings nine heroes to their feet to answer Hector's challenge, and in Book 11 his long tale moves Patroclus to act. Phoenix's story of Meleager, told to Achilles in Book 9, fails as this one does.

## Memory among the gods

The gods remember too, and their memories persuade better.

{{quote:3d959868-6a87-5fb0-b732-6fda0cf72d28}}

Achilles grew up hearing his mother tell, in his father's halls, how she saved Zeus when the other Olympians wanted to bind him. He sends her to Zeus with the instruction “remind him of this” (μνήσασα, 1.407), and the reminder is the ground of her plea (see [[binding-of-zeus]]).

{{quote:c008d9f0-adb8-53ed-bc07-b59060606a44}}

Hephaestus persuades his mother with a memory of his own: Zeus seized him by the foot and threw him from heaven. The memory works, where Nestor's does not, because it is a warning rather than a model (see [[fall-of-hephaestus]]).

## Old men of the sea

Thetis herself is the daughter of an old man, the Old Man of the Sea, beside whom she sits in the depths (1.358, 1.538, 1.556). And the book's young hero will never grow old at all. See [[the-short-life-of-achilles]].

## Outside Homer

In Cicero's dialogue *On Old Age*, Cato cites Nestor's honeyed speech as proof that age does not take away eloquence (10.31; see [[cicero-nestor-honey]]).
