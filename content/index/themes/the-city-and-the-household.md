## For wives and children

The men of Troy fight for their households, and they say so. The Trojans, fewer than the Achaeans, arm “by necessity, for their children and their wives” (πρό τε παίδων καὶ πρὸ γυναικῶν, 8.56–57). Hector tells his army that it is no dishonor to die defending one's country, for then “his wife and children after him are safe, and his house and his lot unharmed” (15.494–499), and he tells the allies that he brought them to Troy to protect “the Trojans' wives and little children” (17.223–224). Agenor, facing Achilles, says that the Trojans are many and brave, “who guard Ilios in front of our dear parents and wives and sons” (21.586–588). Sarpedon's reproach to Hector is that he does not tell the people “to stand firm and defend their wives” (5.485–486; see [[oaristys]]).

The city's prayer to Athena names what is to be saved in one line, said three times: “the city and the wives of the Trojans and their little children” (6.95, 6.276, 6.310). Hector's son is named for the defense of the whole. Hector called him Scamandrius, “but the others Astyanax; for Hector alone was guarding Ilios” (6.402–403; see [[astyanax]]). At the end Andromache laments him as the one “who protected the city and kept safe its faithful wives and little children” (24.729–730).

## The divided world

Most of the Iliad is spent on the plain and among the ships, and until Book 6 its city is seen from its wall, where the old men sit and Helen names the Achaeans (3.146–244). Book 6 goes in. Hector walks from the gate to Priam's palace, with its chambers for his brothers and their wives and his sisters and their husbands (6.242–250), to the house that Paris built on the height of the city, and to his own house, where his wife is not to be found (6.313–317, 6.370–380; see [[priams-palace]], [[house]] and [[hectors-visit-to-troy]]). Inside the walls are the women, the old and the children; their men are outside. The women of the gate ask him after their sons, brothers, kinsmen and husbands (6.238–240), and each of the women who meet him inside tries in her way to keep him there, with wine, with a seat, with a plea to stay on the tower (see [[hecuba]], [[helen]] and [[andromache]]).

The two worlds meet at the threshold. Hector stands on the threshold of his own house to ask where his wife has gone (6.375), and he meets her at the Scaean gates, the boundary between city and plain (6.392–394; see [[scaean-gates]]). Andromache crosses into his world with her advice on the defense of the wall (6.433–439; see [[andromaches-advice]]). He sends her back into hers: “go into the house and attend to your own work … and war will be the concern of men” (6.490–493; see [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]]). She goes home, and the household mourns him while he is still alive (6.495–502; see [[lament]]). Marylin Arthur called Book 6 “the divided world” of the Iliad (“The Divided World of Iliad VI”, 1981), and James Redfield made Hector, the defender of city and household, the tragic hero of the poem (*Nature and Culture in the Iliad: The Tragedy of Hector*, 1975).

## The city at peace and at war

The shield Hephaestus makes for Achilles holds two cities. In one there are weddings and feasts and a trial in the market place; the other is besieged, “and their dear wives and little children stood on the wall to guard it, and with them the men whom old age held”, while the fighting men go out to lay an ambush (18.490–540). The second city is Troy's case. Its fall will be the ruin of its houses, and the Iliad pictures it through them: Priam's sons killed, his daughters dragged away, his bridal chambers ravaged, his infants dashed to the ground (22.62–65); Andromache led away to weave at another woman's loom (6.454–458); the child hurled from the tower (24.734–738).
