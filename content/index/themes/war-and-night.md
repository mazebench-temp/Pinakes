The Iliad's battles are fought by daylight, and night ends them. Its days are counted from dawn to sunset (see [[dawn-line]] and [[sunset-lines]]), and its nights, which the narrator passes over in a line or tells at the length of a book, belong to other business than killing.

## Night ends the fighting

Agamemnon, sending the army to its first battle, says that there will be no pause “unless night comes and parts the fury of men” (2.387), and prays that the sun may not set before he has thrown down Priam's palace (2.412–415). Book 7 shows night doing what he said. As Hector and Ajax draw their swords, the heralds part them: “Night is already coming on; it is good to yield to night too” (7.282), and Hector repeats the line as he proposes to stop (7.293; see [[yield-to-night]] and [[the-duel-of-hector-and-ajax]]). The day of battle that began in Book 4 ends there (see [[the-first-battle]]).

Later books make night a limit that the gods set and the armies feel. Zeus promises Hector the power to kill until he reaches the ships “and the sun goes down and sacred darkness comes on” (11.191–194, 11.206–209), and in Book 17 he grants the Trojans the same glory in the same words (17.453–455). At the end of Book 8 the light falls into Oceanus against the Trojans' will, but to the Achaeans the dark night comes welcome, thrice prayed for (8.485–488), and Hector admits that darkness came first and saved the Argives and their ships (8.500–502). In Book 18 Hera sends the unwilling sun down early, and the Achaeans rest from battle (18.239–242); Polydamas warns the Trojans that ambrosial night has stopped Achilles only for now (18.267–268).

## What night holds

When the fighting stops, the armies eat, keep watch and sleep. Priam sends the Trojans to supper with orders to keep watch and stay awake, and at dawn his herald goes to the ships (7.370–372, 7.380–381). The night belongs to Zeus as well. At the start of Book 2 “the other gods and the men who fight from chariots slept all night long, but sweet sleep did not hold Zeus”, and he pondered how to honor Achilles (2.1–4). Here he devises evil for both armies with his thunder, and the men pour out their wine before they dare to drink (see [[libation]] and [[zeus]]). The same shape of verse, all the others asleep and one awake, begins the night of Agamemnon's cares in Book 10 (10.1–4) and the night in which Hermes brings Priam out of Achilles' camp (24.677–681).

The longest nights of the poem are made of what cannot be done by day. The Trojans camp on the plain around a thousand fires, like the stars around the moon (8.553–565); the Achaeans send an embassy to Achilles and a night raid to the Trojan camp (Books 9–10), where Odysseus measures the hours by the stars: two parts of the night are gone, a third is left (10.251–253). The Myrmidons mourn Patroclus all night (18.354–355), and his pyre burns all night (23.217). Achilles lies awake grieving for him (24.3–5), and Priam crosses the plain by night to ransom his son (Book 24).

## Night as a power

Night is a goddess too. Sleep tells Hera that Zeus once searched his house for him in a rage and would have hurled him from heaven into the sea, had not Night, “subduer of gods and men”, saved him; Zeus stopped, angry as he was, “for he shrank from doing what would displease swift Night” (14.256–261). In the battle night is an image of what cannot be resisted. Apollo comes down from Olympus “like the night” (1.47), and Hector leaps through the broken gate of the Achaean wall, his face “like swift night” (12.462–463). Death is a night that covers a man's eyes (5.310, 5.659, 13.580), and Zeus stretches “deadly night” over the fight for Sarpedon's body (16.567).

## Outside Homer

Fighting by night stayed rare in Greek war. Thucydides calls the Athenian attack on Epipolae at Syracuse the only night battle between great armies in the whole Peloponnesian War, and asks how anyone could know clearly what happened in it (7.44.1; see [[thucydides]]). The tragedy *Rhesus*, handed down among the plays of Euripides though its authorship has long been doubted, stages the night of Book 10.
