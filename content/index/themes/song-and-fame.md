## Song for men to come

The Iliad's plainest words about its own existence are spoken by one of its characters. Helen, asking Hector to sit with her, says that Zeus has set an evil fate on her and on Paris,

{{quote:68891035-1f22-566e-874e-02ece79cf6f4}}

ἀοίδιμος, “sung of, a subject of song”, is found only here in Homer. The purpose clause (ὡς) makes the song the reason for the suffering: Zeus has given them their fate so that there will be something for later men to sing. Helen knows what kind of song it will be. She has just called herself a dog, and she names Paris' blind folly (see [[ate]] and [[helen]]). The fame she foresees is the fame of shame.

The Odyssey says the same of the whole war. When Odysseus weeps at the song of Troy, Alcinous tells him that the gods wrought it, “and spun destruction for men, so that there might be song for those to come” (ἵνα ᾖσι καὶ ἐσσομένοισιν ἀοιδή, *Odyssey* 8.579–580). And the dead Agamemnon sets two songs side by side: the gods will make a lovely song among men for faithful Penelope, whose fame will never perish, but Clytemnestra's will be “a hateful song” (*Odyssey* 24.196–202). Song keeps the good and the bad alike.

## What men will say

The heroes' κλέος is what is heard of them, and it lasts as long as it is told (see [[kleos]]). They picture it in advance. Hector imagines a sailor passing a tomb on the Hellespont, “This is the mound of a man who died long ago, whom once, when he was the best in the fighting, shining Hector killed”, and ends: “and my fame will never perish” (7.87–91; see [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). In Book 6 he imagines the same kind of speaker pointing at his widow in Argos, “This is the wife of Hector, who was always the best in the fighting” (6.459–462), and prays that someone may say of his son, “He is far better than his father” (6.479; see [[tis-speech]]). At his death, with no escape left, he asks only to die “having done some great thing for men to come to hear of” (22.304–305). Achilles' mother has told him of two fates, a long life without fame or a short one in which “my fame will be imperishable” (9.410–416; see [[the-short-life-of-achilles]]).

The poem shows the song being made. When the embassy comes to Achilles' hut, it finds him singing to the lyre “the famous deeds of men” (κλέα ἀνδρῶν), the matter of epic, while Patroclus sits in silence waiting to take up the song when he stops (9.186–191). The lyre came from the spoils of Eetion's city (9.188), the Thebe whose sack Andromache tells in Book 6 (6.414–428; see [[sack-of-thebe]]). Helen, at her loom in Book 3, is weaving the trials of Trojans and Achaeans into a purple cloth while they fight for her (3.125–128; see [[helens-web]]). In the Odyssey the singers Phemius and Demodocus sing the Trojan War and its returns before those who lived it, and Penelope and Odysseus weep to hear it (*Odyssey* 1.325–342, 8.83–86, 8.521–531).

## Song and grief

The songs are made of suffering. The Iliad's first line asks the goddess to sing the wrath that put countless pains on the Achaeans (1.1–2). When Hector's body is brought home, singers are set beside it “to lead the dirge”, and the women answer them with their laments (24.719–722; see [[lament]]). Helen's “theme of song” and the widow's lament are the same material. The Muses who know all things give the singer his memory (2.484–493; see [[memory-and-the-muses]]), and the characters who suffer know that their suffering will be remembered.
