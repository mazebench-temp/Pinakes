When the Iliad lets a minor warrior die with more than a name, it often looks past him to his parents: who his father was, what the old man hoped, and what the death leaves in his house. Book 5 does it again and again.

## The fathers of Book 5

Diomedes' first victims are the sons of Dares, a wealthy priest of Hephaestus. Phegeus is killed; Hephaestus hides Idaeus in night and saves him, “so that the old man might not be wholly stricken with grief” (5.9–24; see [[dares]]). A god spares a father one son of two. Eurydamas has no such help:

{{quote:b1338a04-76e4-5ce2-a9f7-81ddb17ea4cc}}

The interpreter of dreams did not foresee, or did not tell, what his sons' going would bring (see [[eurydamas]] and [[onar]]). The two sons of Phaenops come next:

{{quote:82b513e0-5741-5602-a943-bbe41a4064ab}}

τηλυγέτω, “darlings”, is a word for cherished children, perhaps born late to their parents (see [[darling]]). The father is left γόος and κήδεα, “lamentation and grievous sorrows”, and when he dies his property goes to the χηρωσταί, the distant kinsmen who inherit a house left empty (from χῆρος, “bereft”). The word occurs only here in Homer. Hesiod uses it of the man who shuns marriage and comes to old age with no one to care for him: when he dies, the χηρωσταί divide his livelihood (*Theogony* 603–607). See [[phaenops]].

Later in the book Aeneas kills the twin sons of Diocles of Pherae, descendants of the river Alpheus, who came to Troy to win satisfaction for the sons of Atreus: they fall like two young lions reared by their mother in the forest, and like tall fir trees (5.541–560; see [[diocles]], [[two-lions]] and [[fir-trees]]). Other fathers are named in passing: Dolopion, priest of the Scamander, “honored by the people like a god”, whose son Hypsenor loses his arm and his life (5.76–83), and Lycaon, whose son Pandarus ignored his advice and dies without coming home (5.197–216). Sarpedon, wounded, fears that he will never go home to gladden “my dear wife and my infant son” (5.688). Dione's threat against Diomedes is put in the same terms: the man who fights the gods will have no children to call him papa at his knees, and his wife will wake the household with her wailing (5.406–415; see [[who-fights-the-gods]]).

## Gods as parents

The gods in Book 5 are parents too, and they can do what the mortal fathers cannot. Aphrodite throws her arms around Aeneas and carries him out of the battle, and is wounded for it (5.311–318; see [[mothers-and-sons]]). Zeus keeps death from his son Sarpedon: “his father still kept ruin away” (5.662). Zeus has Ares healed, hateful as he is, because he is his son (5.895–898). The gods save their children for a time. The mortal fathers can only send them, advise them, and lose them.

## Elsewhere in Homer

The same pattern runs through the poem. Merops of Percote, who “above all men knew the art of prophecy”, would not let his two sons go to the war, and they would not obey him; Diomedes kills them too (2.830–834, 11.328–334; see [[the-sons-of-merops]]). Simoeisius “did not repay his dear parents for his rearing” (4.477–478; see [[threptra]]). Zeus weeps tears of blood for Sarpedon, whom he will not save a second time (16.459–461). Achilles knows that he will not care for Peleus in his old age (24.538–542), and the poem ends with Priam and Hecuba mourning Hector. In the Odyssey, Laertes lives in the country, grieving and longing for his son's return, and Anticleia died of longing for Odysseus (*Odyssey* 11.195–203).

## Outside Homer

Croesus, saved from the pyre, tells Cyrus why no one would choose war over peace: “in peace sons bury their fathers, in war fathers bury their sons” (Herodotus 1.87.4; see [[herodotus]]).
