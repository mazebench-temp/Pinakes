Once in Book 5 the narrator speaks from his own time. Diomedes has killed Pandarus, and Aeneas stands over the body like a lion:

{{quote:b5c86ba6-9bcf-503e-ab9c-b9e88ab96f20}}

The stone crushes Aeneas' hip joint and brings him to his knees, and only Aphrodite saves him (5.305–318). οἷοι νῦν βροτοί εἰσι, “such as mortals are now”, sets the heroes against the poet's audience. The men who fought at Troy were bigger and stronger than those who now hear of them.

The same half-line comes back whenever a hero lifts a stone. Ajax throws one from the Achaean wall that a man could not easily hold in both hands, “not even one in his prime, such as mortals are now” (12.380–383). Hector breaks the gate of the wall with a stone that two of the best men of the people could not easily lever onto a wagon, “such as mortals are now”; he carries it easily alone, for Zeus has made it light (12.445–450). And Aeneas, facing Achilles, takes up a stone “which two men could not carry, such as mortals are now” (20.285–287), in the same words that describe Diomedes' stone here. In Book 5 Aeneas is struck by the stone that two men could not carry; in Book 20 he lifts it. The last line of the verse is the same in all three books, ὃ δέ μιν ῥέα πάλλε καὶ οἶος, “but he wielded it easily even alone” (5.304 = 12.449 = 20.287).

## Men of former times

The heroes, for their part, look back on better men. Nestor tells the quarreling kings that he once fought beside men against whom “no one of the mortals now living on earth could fight” (1.271–272; see [[old-age-and-memory]]). Tlepolemus tells Sarpedon that he falls far short of the sons of Zeus born “among men of former times”, such as Heracles, who sacked Troy with six ships (5.635–642; see [[heracles-sacks-troy]]). In the Odyssey, Odysseus says that he is better than all the mortals “now on earth, who eat bread”, but he will not contend with the men of before, Heracles and Eurytus, who challenged even the gods (*Odyssey* 8.221–228). Each generation measures itself against the last and comes off worse, except in Sthenelus' boast (4.405; see [[better-than-our-fathers]]).

Once the Iliad gives its heroes the name that later Greek used for them. Looking forward to the time after the war when the gods will wash away the Achaean wall, the narrator speaks of the heroes who fell by the rivers of Troy as ἡμιθέων γένος ἀνδρῶν, “the race of men half-divine” (12.22–23). The word ἡμίθεοι is found only there in Homer (see [[hemitheos]]).

## Outside Homer

Hesiod tells the history of mankind as a succession of races. The fourth was “the godlike race of hero men, who are called half-gods (ἡμίθεοι), the race before ours”, who died at Thebes and at Troy; the fifth, of iron, is his own, and he wishes he had died before it or been born after it (*Works and Days* 156–201). The Roman poets took up Homer's measure of the stone. Virgil's Turnus lifts a stone that twelve chosen men could hardly raise onto their shoulders, “such bodies of men as the earth now brings forth” (*Aeneid* 12.896–900). Juvenal, describing a street fight in Egypt, says that the stones were not like those of Turnus or Ajax, or the one with which the son of Tydeus struck Aeneas on the hip, “for this race was already growing smaller while Homer was alive; the earth now rears wicked and puny men” (*Satires* 15.65–70).
