## The two causes

Imitating is innate in human beings from childhood: man is the most imitative of animals and learns his first lessons through imitation (see [[most-imitative-of-animals]] and [[mimesis]]). And everyone takes pleasure in imitations, even of things painful to see, because learning is pleasant (see [[pleasure-of-imitation]]). Melody and rhythm are natural too, and those most gifted for them, advancing little by little, gave birth to poetry out of their improvisations. Poetry then split according to the poets' own characters: the more dignified imitated noble actions, in hymns and encomia and then in epic, the more trivial the actions of inferior people, in invectives and lampoons; from these came tragedy and comedy (1448b4–1449a6; see [[hymn]], [[encomium]], [[invective]], [[the-origins-of-tragedy]] and [[the-origins-of-comedy]]).

## Against Plato

Plato had called poetry an imitation at the third remove from the truth, which waters the passions it should starve (*Republic* 10, 597e, 605a–606d), and in the *Ion* and the *Phaedrus* had traced it to divine madness (see [[the-gifted-or-the-mad]]). Aristotle grounds it instead in human nature and in the pleasure of learning. Democritus had said that men learned their most important arts by imitating animals, weaving from the spider, building from the swallow, and song from the swan and the nightingale (fr. 154 DK).
