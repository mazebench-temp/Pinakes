The war at Troy is fought to return a wife to her husband. Achilles says the army came to win satisfaction for Menelaus (1.159), and Nestor tells the men not to go home until each has lain with a Trojan's wife, to pay back the struggles and groans for Helen's sake (2.354–356). The reward of the war is the enemy's wives, and its cause is one man's.

## Marriages at home and in the camp

The first marriage the Iliad mentions is slighted. Agamemnon prefers the captive Chryseis to Clytemnestra, his wedded wife (κουριδίη ἄλοχος), since she is no worse in form or stature, in mind or in handiwork (1.113–115; see [[marriage]] and [[captive-women]]). Achilles later calls Briseis his wife, won by his spear, and asks whether the sons of Atreus are the only men who love their wives (9.336–343). On Olympus the king of the gods and his wife quarrel and threaten, and go to bed side by side (1.536–611).

The army's own wives are at home. Agamemnon, testing the men, reminds them that their wives and children sit in their halls and wait (2.136–137), and Odysseus grants that a man chafes when he is a single month away from his wife (2.292). The Catalogue remembers a marriage the war has already ended: Protesilaus, the first Achaean to die, left in Phylace a wife with her cheeks torn in mourning, and a house half-finished (2.700–701).

## Two husbands

Helen has two. The duel of Book 3 is fought to decide whose wife she will be (3.138), and she calls Menelaus her “former husband” to Paris' face (3.429; see [[posis]]). In Book 6 she wishes she were the wife of a better man, one who felt the blame and reproaches of others (6.349–351); Paris has just told Hector that she urged him back to battle “with gentle words” (6.337–338).

## Hector and Andromache

Against these the poem sets one marriage whole. Andromache tells Hector that he is father, mother and brother to her, and her “flourishing husband” (θαλερὸς παρακοίτης, 6.429–430), the phrase Hector used in Book 3 of Menelaus' wife (θαλερὴν παράκοιτιν, 3.53). Hector answers that what grieves him most is the thought of her led away captive to weave at another woman's loom (6.450–465). The curse of the oath in Book 3, “may their wives be subdued by others” (3.301), is the fate he foresees for her. In the last book she mourns him as his widow (24.725–745).

In the *Odyssey* the ideal is spoken outright: nothing is better than when a husband and wife keep house together with one mind (ὁμοφρονέοντε, *Odyssey* 6.182–184).
