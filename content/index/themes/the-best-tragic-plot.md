## Chapter 13

The finest tragedy must be complex, not simple, and must imitate fearful and pitiable things (1452b30–33). Its hero is the man between, who falls through error (see [[the-tragic-hero]]); its change is from good fortune to misfortune, which is why the critics of Euripides are wrong, for his plays that end in misfortune are “the most tragic” on the stage (1453a12–30; see [[euripides]]). The double structure, ending opposite ways for the good and the bad, like the Odyssey, comes second, and is preferred only through the weakness of audiences (1453a30–39; see [[the-double-plot]]).

## Chapter 14

Pity and fear should come from the incidents, not from spectacle (1453b1–14). The deeds that produce them are done among those dear to each other:

{{quote:88be8f53-dc1c-56c6-b872-9b5f09947071}}

The traditional stories cannot be undone, Clytemnestra killed by Orestes, Eriphyle by Alcmaeon, but the poet must use them well (1453b22–26; see [[orestes-kills-clytemnestra]] and [[alcmaeon-kills-eriphyle]]). There are four possibilities, since one must either act or not, knowingly or not (1453b36–37). To be about to act knowingly and not act is the worst, repugnant and untragic, as Haemon threatens Creon in the *Antigone* (1453b37–1454a2; see [[haemon-threatens-creon]]). To act knowingly, as Euripides' Medea kills her children, is second (1453b27–29, 1454a2; see [[medea-kills-her-children]]). Better is to act in ignorance and recognize afterwards, as Sophocles' Oedipus, Astydamas' Alcmaeon, or Telegonus in *Odysseus Wounded* (1453b29–34, 1454a2–4). Best of all is to be about to act in ignorance and recognize in time: Merope in the *Cresphontes*, Iphigenia and her brother, and the son in the *Helle* (1454a4–9; see [[merope-and-her-son]] and [[the-recognition-of-orestes-and-iphigenia]]).

The ranking of chapter 14 has long been felt to contradict chapter 13, which seemed to prefer the *Oedipus*, a deed done and recognized, to plays that end well. Aristotle's reason here is that the deed recognized in time keeps the fearful without the repugnant, and the recognition is astonishing (1454a2–4).
