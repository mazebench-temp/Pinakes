## The method

Since the poet is an imitator, he imitates things as they were or are, as they are said to be, or as they ought to be, in a diction with its licenses (see [[as-they-ought-to-be]]). The correctness of poetry is not that of other arts, and errors of art differ from incidental errors of fact, such as not knowing that a female deer has no horns (see [[deer]]). The solutions draw on the objects of imitation, on the custom of the time, on the speaker and the occasion, and above all on language: rare words, metaphor, accent, punctuation, ambiguity, usage, and the many senses a word may bear (1460b6–1461b25). Most of the examples are from Homer, and many of them are entries of this index (see [[aristotle-mules]], [[aristotle-dolons-face]], [[stronger-or-quicker]], [[all-the-gods-slept]], [[alone-without-a-share]], [[aristotle-and-hippias]], [[the-stump-that-rots]], [[aristotle-portions-of-the-night]], [[aristotle-wine-of-the-gods]], [[aristotle-upright-spears]], [[the-bronze-spear-stopped]] and [[the-icarius-problem]]).

## The tradition

Critics had been finding fault with Homer since the sixth century, and in the fourth Zoilus of Amphipolis won the name “scourge of Homer” for it. Aristotle collected such difficulties and their answers in a lost work in six books, the *Homeric Problems* (Diogenes Laertius 5.26), from which fragments survive in Porphyry's *Homeric Questions*; the chapter of the *Poetics* is its summary (see [[aristotle]] and [[homer]]).
