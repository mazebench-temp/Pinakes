The first quarrel of the Iliad is a quarrel about a title. Three times in Book 1 the phrase ἄριστος Ἀχαιῶν, “best of the Achaeans”, is claimed for a man: once for Agamemnon, twice for Achilles. Gregory Nagy took the phrase as the title of his study of the heroic tradition (*The Best of the Achaeans*, 1979).

## Two claims

{{quote:1c328d00-13a1-58dc-8462-b480058b2d6b}}

The title first appears as a claim that Achilles reports with scorn. Agamemnon “claims” (εὔχεται) to be far the best. His case rests on rank. He leads the most men, holds the scepter, and has glory from Zeus (1.279); when he takes Briseis, it is so that Achilles may know “how much greater (φέρτερος) I am” (1.186).

{{quote:c2dd9e17-c98d-53e4-8f6a-9dacda2e7a9c}}

Achilles' case rests on fighting. He has done “the greater part of the furious fighting” for the smaller prize (1.165–168). Even his enemy concedes his strength. Agamemnon calls him “most terrible of all men” (1.146), then belittles it: “If you are very strong, it was a god, I think, who gave you that” (1.178; see [[strength-is-a-gift]]). Achilles swears that Agamemnon will regret not honoring “the best of the Achaeans”, and he asks Thetis for exactly that recognition (1.412).

His taunt that Agamemnon never goes on ambush “with the best of the Achaeans” (σὺν ἀριστήεσσιν, 1.227) aims at the same point. Ambush was where a man's courage showed most plainly (13.276–277).

## Nestor's balance

{{quote:e42705bc-3601-5f13-8c7f-c021ffabb115}}

Nestor tries to divide the title. Achilles is καρτερός, “strong”, and the son of a goddess; Agamemnon is φέρτερος, “greater”, because he rules over more. Achilles is “a mighty bulwark for all the Achaeans against evil war”. To make both men listen, Nestor reaches back to men “better” (ἀρείοσιν) than either of them, the mightiest of the earth, who fought the Centaurs and still heeded his advice (1.260–273; see [[nestor-and-the-centaurs]]). Neither man is moved.

## The poem's answer

The Iliad gives its own verdict in the Catalogue of Ships: “Of the men the best by far was Telamonian Ajax, while Achilles kept up his wrath; for he was by far the strongest” (2.768–769). The rest of the poem proves Achilles' claim by his absence, as Achilles meant it to (1.240–244, 1.407–412). Patroclus repeats Achilles' words about the best of the Achaeans when he leads the Myrmidons out (16.273–274).

Achilles himself, grieving for Patroclus, admits a limit to the title. He is such as no other Achaean in war; in the assembly others are better (18.105–106). The two measures that Nestor tried to hold together, the best in battle and the greatest in rule, never meet in one Achaean.
