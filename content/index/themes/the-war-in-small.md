The Iliad begins in the tenth year of the war and ends before the fall of Troy. It does not tell the war's beginning or its end in order. It makes both present in other ways: the end by foreshadowing (see [[sack-of-troy-foretold]]), the beginning by a series of scenes in Books 2–4 that act out the war's first days again in the tenth year.

## The beginning retold

**The muster.** In Book 2 the army nearly sails home, and then is gathered, marshaled and counted as if for the first time. The Catalogue of Ships counts the contingents by their ships, as if the fleet were assembling at Aulis rather than camped at Troy in the tenth year, and many scholars think it began as a list of the fleet that sailed. Odysseus and Nestor make the army remember the portent at Aulis and the day the fleet set out (2.301–335, 2.350–353). See [[catalogue-of-ships]] and [[the-portent-at-aulis]].

**The leaders named.** In Book 3 Priam asks Helen to name the Achaean leaders he sees on the plain, and she names Agamemnon, Odysseus, Ajax and Idomeneus (3.161–242). Why he needs to ask in the tenth year is an old question. Some readers explain it by the old king's eyes or by his courtesy to Helen; many modern readers take it as a scene that belongs to the war's first days, set here because these books retell the beginning. See [[the-view-from-the-wall]] and [[priams-question]].

**The quarrel at its source.** The duel is the war reduced to the two men it concerns, fighting for the woman and the possessions that Paris took (3.67–75, 3.281–287). Antenor remembers the embassy that came to ask for her before the fighting began (3.204–224), and Paris remembers the first night with Helen on the island of Cranae (3.443–445). See [[the-duel-of-paris-and-menelaus]] and [[the-embassy-to-troy]].

**The crime repeated.** The war began with a broken bond, a guest who took his host's wife. The truce of Book 3 is sworn under the eyes of Zeus, and in Book 4 the Trojan archer Pandarus breaks it with an arrow, so that the Trojans are guilty again (see [[xenia]] and [[oaths-and-their-breaking]]).

## An end that might have been

Twice in these books the war comes to the edge of ending, and a god turns it back. In Book 2 the army would have had its homecoming “against fate” (ὑπέρμορα), had not Hera spoken to Athena (2.155–156). In Book 3 Menelaus would have dragged Paris off and won untold glory, had not Aphrodite seen it and broken the helmet strap (3.373–375). Both armies hope for the end: they rejoice at the duel, “hoping for an end to miserable war” (3.111–112), and they pray that whoever brought these troubles may die, “and that friendship and trusty oaths may be ours” (3.320–323). The poem shows the war that could have been settled in an afternoon, and why it was not.
