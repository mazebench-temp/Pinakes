The gods of the Iliad are not distant. They have sides in the war, favorites among the fighters and quarrels of their own, and they act in the story in several ways. They come in person, seen by one man or by all. They come disguised as mortals, and are known, if at all, by a voice, a gait or a sudden departure. They send messengers, dreams and signs: birds of omen, portents, thunder and lightning. They put a thought into a man's mind or strength into his heart, turn a spear aside or hide a man in mist. And they are the source of what men are and have: strength, beauty, skill with the bow, the art of prophecy.

The narrator often knows what a god has done where the characters see only the effect, and the characters in turn explain their own acts and failures by the gods. Rarely does a god simply replace a human decision. The god's prompting usually meets a man already inclined to act, and the act remains his own, as it remains the god's; scholars call this double motivation. The gods also have limits. As a rule they act within what is fated, and in the end within the will of Zeus.
