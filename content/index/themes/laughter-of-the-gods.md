The gods of Homer laugh easily. The same line, “and unquenchable laughter rose among the blessed gods” (ἄσβεστος δʼ ἄρʼ ἐνῶρτο γέλως μακάρεσσι θεοῖσιν), is used twice, and both times Hephaestus is the cause. At the end of Iliad 1 the gods laugh at him bustling about as cupbearer (1.599–600); in the song of Demodocus they laugh at the net in which he has caught Ares and Aphrodite (Odyssey 8.326–327). Zeus' heart laughs with joy when he sees the gods clash in battle (21.389–390), and Hera can laugh “with her lips” while her brow stays dark (15.101–102). The gods' laughter is the sign of their ease: what would be a disaster among men is a spectacle among them.

Men laugh too, and in the Iliad their laughter usually falls on someone brought low, as when the army laughs at a runner who has slipped in the dung at the funeral games (23.784). In the Odyssey Athena rouses the gods' own “unquenchable laughter” in the suitors, men about to die (Odyssey 20.345–347).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates objected to the scene in Iliad 1. Gods should not be shown overcome by laughter; see [[plato-homeric-laughter]]. The phrase gave European languages "Homeric laughter"; see [[homeric-laughter-idiom]].
