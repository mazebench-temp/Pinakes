{{quote:69181bf4-ac13-5bc9-9b6b-8eb16d345a61}}

The scene turns on two smiles and a laugh. Hera smiles (μείδησεν) at her son's story and takes the cup. Then the whole company breaks into ἄσβεστος γέλως, "unquenchable laughter", at the sight of Hephaestus playing cupbearer.

The laughter is not cruel, and it does real work. It dissolves the tension of Zeus' threat and restores the "equal feast" (1.602). It also marks the difference between gods and men. On earth a quarrel costs lives and an old man's counsel fails. On Olympus a lame god's clowning settles it by sunset. Hephaestus has just said that the gods should not quarrel "for the sake of mortals" (1.574), and the laughter shows how lightly they can let the matter go.

Plato's Socrates objected to the scene. Gods should not be shown overcome by laughter; see [[plato-homeric-laughter]]. The phrase gave European languages "Homeric laughter"; see [[homeric-laughter-idiom]]. See also [[gods-and-mortals]] and [[the-gods-mirror-the-mortals]].
