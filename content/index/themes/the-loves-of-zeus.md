Zeus is “the father of men and gods” (1.544), and in Homer he is also the father, in the plain sense, of many gods and of some of the greatest heroes. The poems name his children far more often than their mothers, and they tell how he came to a woman only in allusions.

## In Homer

**The gods.** Apollo is “the son of Leto and of Zeus” (1.9; see [[leto]] and [[apollo]]). Aphrodite is the daughter of Dione (5.370–371; see [[dione]]), Ares the son of Zeus and Hera (5.892–896; see [[ares]]), and Hebe the daughter of Zeus and Hera (*Odyssey* 11.604; see [[hebe]]). Hermes is the son of Maia (*Odyssey* 14.435; see [[hermes]]). Athena is a daughter whose mother Homer never names; Ares complains that Zeus himself “begot” her (5.875–880; see [[athena]]). Persephone, the Muses, the Prayers and Ate are all called daughters of Zeus (*Odyssey* 11.217; 2.491, 9.502, 19.91; see [[persephone]], [[muses]], [[litai]] and [[ate]]).

**The heroes.** Laodameia, Bellerophon's daughter, bore him Sarpedon (6.198–199; see [[laodameia]] and [[sarpedon]]). Peirithous is the son “whom immortal Zeus begot” (2.741; see [[peirithous]]). Idomeneus boasts that “Zeus first fathered Minos, a guardian over Crete” (13.450; see [[minos]]). Heracles is “the son of aegis-bearing Zeus” (5.396), born of Alcmene in Thebes on the day Hera deceived Zeus (19.95–133; see [[heracles]] and [[alcmene]]). Helen is “daughter of aegis-bearing Zeus” (3.426; see [[helen]]); Aeacus, Achilles' grandfather, was a son of Zeus (21.189; see [[aeacus]]); and the Trojan line begins with Dardanus, whom the son of Cronus loved above all the children born to him of mortal women (20.215, 20.304–305; see [[dardanus]]). Tlepolemus taunts Sarpedon with the men “who were born of Zeus among men of former times” (5.637), and names his own father Heracles as the greatest of them (5.638–639).

**The wives.** Hera is the wife, κασιγνήτην ἄλοχόν τε, “his sister and wife” (16.432, 18.356; see [[hera]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]). But Hermes, refusing to fight Leto, counts her among the wives of Zeus (ἀλόχοισι Διὸς νεφεληγερέταο, 21.498–499), and the Odyssey calls her Διὸς κυδρὴν παράκοιτιν, “the glorious consort of Zeus” (*Odyssey* 11.580).

**The one list.** Homer gathers Zeus' loves together only in Book 14, where Zeus, overcome by desire for Hera on the peak of Gargarus, names seven of them, and their sons, to tell her that he never loved any of them as he loves her now (14.315–328). The Odyssey has a catalogue of the same kind on a larger scale: in the underworld Odysseus sees the women “who were wives and daughters of the best men”, among them Antiope and Alcmene, who bore sons to Zeus (*Odyssey* 11.225–330, 11.260–268).

## Outside Homer

**Hesiod.** The *Theogony* gives Zeus his wives in order: Metis, Themis, Eurynome, Demeter, who bore Persephone, Mnemosyne, Leto, who bore Apollo and Artemis, and “last” Hera (λοισθοτάτην, *Theogony* 886–929). After a digression come his children by other women: Hermes by Maia, Dionysus by “Cadmeian Semele”, a mortal who bore an immortal, and Heracles by Alcmene (938–944; see [[hesiod]]). Four of the women in Homer's list are there, Demeter, Leto, Semele and Alcmene, and both lists end with Leto and then Hera. The Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women* told of the women who bore children to gods. A papyrus preserves its verses on Europa, the daughter of Phoenix, whom Zeus carried over the sea to Crete, and a scholium on the Iliad gives the story and her sons, Minos, Sarpedon and Rhadamanthys, “in Hesiod and Bacchylides” (fr. 19, 19a Evelyn-White; see [[europa]]). The Hesiodic *Shield* opens with Alcmene, who conceived Heracles by Zeus and Iphicles by Amphitryon on the same night and bore them as twins (*Shield* 1–56).

**The family of the list.** The later genealogies join the women whom Homer names one by one. In Apollodorus, Alcmene is the daughter of Electryon, a son of Perseus, and so the great-granddaughter of Danae (*Library* 2.4.5); and after Amphitryon's death she married Rhadamanthys, the son of Zeus and Europa (*Library* 2.4.11, 3.1.2; see [[danae]], [[perseus]] and [[rhadamanthus]]).

**Disguises.** Homer does not say how Zeus came to any of these women. The later stories give him a shape for each, and Ovid's Arachne weaves them into her tapestry among the deceptions of the gods: the bull that carried Europa, Amphitryon's likeness for Alcmene, gold for Danae, a speckled serpent for Proserpina (*Metamorphoses* 6.103–114).
