The Iliad is full of people watching the fighting who do not take part in it, and the poem often shows the war through their eyes.

## The gods

The gods are the war's first audience. They watch from Olympus, and Zeus watches from the peaks of Ida, “exulting in his glory, looking out on the city of the Trojans and the ships of the Achaeans” (8.51–52). Their watching can be pleasure. Athena and Apollo sit on the oak of Zeus “like birds, like vultures”, “delighting in the men” (7.58–61); in Book 20 Zeus sends the other gods into the battle and stays on Olympus to watch, “and there, looking on, I will delight my heart” (20.22–23); Strife rejoices as she looks on, the only god in the battle (11.73–75). It can also be grief. Zeus pities Sarpedon, whom he must let die (16.431–461), and cries out as he watches Hector chased around the walls, “Ah, a man dear to me I see with my own eyes pursued around the wall, and my heart grieves” (22.166–173). Jasper Griffin argued that the gods as spectators give the human action its seriousness and its dignity (“The Divine Audience and the Religion of the Iliad”, *Classical Quarterly* 28, 1978).

## The wall

Troy watches its own war. The old men sit on the tower above the Scaean gates, “done with war now because of their age”, and see Helen come (3.146–160); Helen is called to the wall to see the duel fought for her (3.130–138). Priam cannot bear to watch his son fight Menelaus (3.304–309), but he sees Hector die: he is the first to see Achilles coming across the plain, and he and Hecuba watch from the wall what follows (22.25–91, 22.405–409). Andromache runs to the wall and sees her husband's body dragged behind Achilles' chariot (22.462–465). Achilles too is a watcher while he stays out of the fighting, standing on the stern of his ship, “watching the sheer toil and the tearful onslaught” (11.599–601).

## The armies and the onlooker

When the fighting stops, the armies become spectators: at the duel of Book 3, “amazement held those who watched, the horse-taming Trojans and the well-greaved Achaeans” (3.342–343). The poet also imagines an onlooker of his own. He tells the listener what “you would have said” or “would not have seen” (4.223, 4.429–431, 15.697–698, 17.366–367). At the end of the first battle he invents a man who walks unhurt through the middle of the fighting, led by Athena, to see it (4.539–544), and later he names the feeling such a sight should bring: “he would be very bold of heart / who could then have rejoiced at the sight of the toil, and not grieved” (13.343–344). The listener is placed where the gods are, watching from safety, and asked to feel as a man would.

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates would not let the poets show the greatest of the gods lamenting at what he sees, and quotes Zeus' words over Hector (22.168–169) and over Sarpedon (16.433) as examples (*Republic* 3.388c–d). Lucretius begins the second book of his poem with the pleasure of watching from safety: it is sweet to watch from the shore another man's struggle on a stormy sea, and sweet to see great contests of war drawn up on the plain, without sharing in the danger (*On the Nature of Things* 2.1–6).
