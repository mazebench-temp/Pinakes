At death the soul leaves the body for the house of Hades, beneath the earth or beyond Oceanus at the edge of the world, and lives there as a phantom of the man, without strength and, in the Odyssey, without wits until it has drunk blood. The dead keep their griefs and angers; a few are judged or punished, and a few are spared death altogether.

## The dead

At death the ψυχή, the soul, leaves the limbs and goes to Hades, “lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth” (*Iliad* 16.856–857, 22.362–363), and the body stays behind for burial or for the dogs (see [[psyche]] and [[honoring-the-dead]]). What goes is a likeness. The soul of Patroclus is “like him in all things, in stature and in the fair eyes / and the voice”, and wears his clothes (*Iliad* 23.65–67); but when Achilles reaches for it, “the soul went beneath the earth like smoke, / gibbering”, and he learns that “even in the house of Hades there is some / soul and phantom, but there is no mind in it at all” (*Iliad* 23.99–104; see [[eidolon]] and [[the-ghost-of-patroclus]]). The Odyssey calls the dead “the phantoms of dead mortals” (βροτῶν εἴδωλα καμόντων, *Odyssey* 11.476; again 24.14), καμόντες, “the weary”, being a euphemism for the dead (W. W. Merry, *Commentary on the Odyssey*, 1886, on 11.476), and “the strengthless heads of the dead” (*Odyssey* 10.521, 11.29; see [[the-strengthless-heads]], [[the-dead-who-have-died]] and [[the-tribes-of-the-dead]]). The soul cannot be held: Odysseus' mother flies from his arms three times, and she tells him that it is no trick of Persephone's but “the way of mortals, when one of them dies” (*Odyssey* 11.204–222; see [[the-way-of-mortals]]). Its voice is a gibbering or a shrill cry, the clamor of birds scattering in terror or of bats in a cave (*Iliad* 23.101; *Odyssey* 11.605–606; 24.5–9, a plain rendering).

## Without wits

In the Odyssey the dead have no mind of their own. Persephone gave a mind to Teiresias alone, “that he alone should be wise; but the others flit as shadows” (*Odyssey* 10.494–495; see [[teiresias]] and [[phrenes]]), and Achilles' soul asks how Odysseus dared to come down to Hades, “where the dead / dwell without sense, the phantoms of dead mortals” (ἀφραδέες, *Odyssey* 11.475–476). Blood gives them back for a time what they lack. The souls gather to the blood of the victims, and only those whom Odysseus lets drink can know him and tell him the truth (*Odyssey* 11.95–96, 11.146–149, 11.153, 11.390; see [[the-offering-to-the-dead]]). The rule is not kept through the book: no drinking is mentioned when Achilles and Ajax come, and Heracles knows Odysseus “when he saw me with his eyes” (*Odyssey* 11.471, 11.615; Merry on 11.467).

## Where they go

The Iliad puts the house of Hades “beneath the depths of the earth” (*Iliad* 22.482): when Poseidon shakes the earth, Hades leaps from his throne in fear that its roof will split and show his dwellings, “terrible and dank, which even the gods hate”, to mortals and immortals (*Iliad* 20.61–65), and Tartarus lies as far below Hades as the sky is above the earth (*Iliad* 8.13–16; see [[hades]] and [[tartarus]]). The dead pass its gates and a river once their bodies have been burned, and the unburied wander outside (*Iliad* 23.71–74). The Odyssey reaches the same house by sea: across Oceanus, past the land of the Cimmerians, to the groves of Persephone and the meeting of its rivers, where Odysseus digs his pit at the edge rather than going down (*Odyssey* 10.508–515, 11.13–22; Merry on 10.512; see [[oceanus]], [[erebus]], [[acheron]] and [[the-groves-of-persephone]]). The souls range over “the meadow of asphodel” (*Odyssey* 11.539, 11.573, 24.13), and in the last book Hermes leads the souls of the suitors there, past the streams of Oceanus, the White Rock, the gates of the Sun and the land of dreams (*Odyssey* 24.1–14, a plain rendering; see [[hermes]]).

## What they keep

The dead keep the passions of their lives. They ask about those they left behind (*Odyssey* 11.542), Agamemnon still grieves for his murder, Achilles for his father and his son, and Ajax is still angry over the arms of Achilles and will not answer (*Odyssey* 11.387–464, 11.492–503, 11.543–564; see [[agamemnon]], [[achilles]] and [[ajax]]). They keep their rank, and Achilles refuses the comfort that he is “mighty among the dead”: he would rather serve another for hire on earth (*Odyssey* 11.485–491; see [[rather-a-hired-laborer]]). Minos goes on giving judgment among them and Orion hunting the beasts he killed, each continuing the pursuits of his life (*Odyssey* 11.568–575; Merry on 11.568, 11.572; see [[minos]] and [[orion]]).

**Punishment.** In the Iliad the powers beneath the earth punish the dead who swore false oaths (*Iliad* 3.278–279, 19.259–260; see [[erinyes]] and [[oath]]). Only the last sights of *Odyssey* 11 show punishments: Tityus, whose liver two vultures tear, Tantalus in his lake, and Sisyphus with his stone (*Odyssey* 11.576–600; see [[tityus]], [[tantalus]] and [[sisyphus]]). Merry, with a scholium that marks 11.568–627 as spurious, thought that the passage begins at 11.565 and gives a view of the punishment of the dead later than Homer: Odysseus sits at the edge of the pit and questions the souls who come to the blood, but here he speaks as an eyewitness of the depths of Erebus (Merry on 11.565; see the Odyssey translation's note on 11.565–567).

**The exceptions.** A few escape the common end. Menelaus is promised the Elysian plain instead of death (*Odyssey* 4.561–569; see [[elysium]]); the sons of Leda are held alive beneath the earth and live and die on alternate days (*Odyssey* 11.301–304; see [[castor]] and [[polydeuces]]); and of Heracles Odysseus sees only “his phantom”, while he himself feasts among the immortal gods (*Odyssey* 11.601–604, verses the ancient critics suspected; see [[heracles]] and [[immortality]]).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates would strike out of poetry the verses that make Hades fearful, beginning with Achilles' answer to Odysseus (*Republic* 3.386c–387c; see [[plato-the-terrors-of-hades]]), and he cites Homer's Tantalus, Sisyphus and Tityus as witness that the incurable, punished “for all time in Hades”, are kings and potentates, not private men like Thersites (*Gorgias* 525d–e; Merry on 11.565; see [[plato]]).
