Prophecy in the Iliad has several sources. There are the professionals: the μάντις (“seer”), who reads the will of the gods from birds and other signs, the priest, and the interpreter of dreams (1.62–63; see [[divination]]). There are the signs themselves, sent by Zeus: birds of omen, portents, thunder and lightning. There are the words of the gods, above all the nod of Zeus, which cannot go unfulfilled. And there are the prophecies of the dying. Patroclus foretells Hector's death at the hands of Achilles (16.851–854), and Hector foretells Achilles' death at the Scaean gates (22.358–360).

A prophecy is measured by what follows it, and the language of prophecy is the language of accomplishment. Its central verb is τελέω, “fulfill, bring to an end”, with its noun τέλος (“end”) and relatives such as ἀτελεύτητος (“unfulfilled”, 1.527). Beside it stands κραιαίνω, “accomplish”, the verb of a prayer's request: “fulfill for me this wish” (1.41; see [[fulfill-this-wish]]). Speakers who are sure of the future say that their word “will surely come to pass” (see [[it-will-be-fulfilled]]).
