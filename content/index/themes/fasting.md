## Words

νῆστις, “fasting”, is usually explained as formed from the negative prefix νη- and the root of ἔδω, “eat”: one who has not eaten. The Iliad has it only in Book 19 (νήστιας, 19.156, 19.207), and the Odyssey once, when Odysseus challenges a suitor to mow a meadow with him “fasting until the dusk” (νήστιες ἄχρι μάλα κνέφαος, *Odyssey* 18.370). ἄκμηνος, “unfed”, occurs four times in Homer, all in *Iliad* 19 (19.163, 19.207, 19.320, 19.346); with a genitive it is “going without” food or drink. A scholium calls it Aeolic, and Walter Leaf reports that there is no other evidence for such a word and that its origin is unknown (*Commentary on the Iliad*, 1900, on 19.163; see the translation's note on 19.163). ἄπαστος, “having tasted nothing”, belongs with πάσασθαι, “eat” (19.160). The Odyssey uses it of Penelope, who lies “without food, tasting neither food nor drink” (ἄσιτος, ἄπαστος ἐδητύος ἠδὲ ποτῆτος, *Odyssey* 4.788) when she learns that Telemachus has sailed, and of Odysseus, long unfed when Nausicaa's maids give him food (*Odyssey* 6.250).

## Grief and the meal

In Homer the meal is the sign that life goes on. The formula that ends a feast, “when they had put away the desire for drink and food” (1.469; see [[desire-for-drink-and-food]] and [[feast]]), marks the return of order, and a host's first duty is to feed his guest (see [[xenia]]). To refuse food is to stand outside that order. The mourner does it, and so does the man bent on vengeance, and in Achilles they are one man: he will eat when he has avenged Patroclus, and not before.

The Iliad sets the refusal in a debate. Odysseus argues for the army that a man who has not eaten cannot fight all day, that a soldier cannot mourn with his belly, and that the dead must be wept for a day and buried (see [[food-and-wine-are-might-and-courage]], [[satiety-of-battle]] and [[weep-for-a-day-and-bury]]). Achilles does not answer the argument. He lets the others eat and goes without, and the gods feed him with nectar and ambrosia, the food of the immortals (see [[nectar]] and [[ambrosia]]). He eats again only when Hector is dead. That evening he gives the Myrmidons a funeral feast beside Patroclus' body, and in Agamemnon's hut, refusing to wash until Patroclus is burned, he says that they must yield to the hateful meal (23.29–48).

The last book reverses the scene. Thetis asks her son how long he will eat his heart, mindful neither of food nor of his bed (24.128–130). When Priam comes to ransom Hector, Achilles himself urges the meal on him: even Niobe, whose twelve children were killed, remembered food when she was weary of weeping (24.601–620; see [[niobe]]). Priam eats, and says that he has tasted nothing since his son died, lying in the dung of his courtyard (24.637–642). The man who would not eat in *Iliad* 19 is the one who gives the mourning father food.
