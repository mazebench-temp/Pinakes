The Iliad has no inscriptions. Its monuments are mounds of heaped earth, called τύμβος or σῆμα, “sign” (see [[tomb]]). A mound is a sign because it is seen and read: a man who passes it asks whose it is, and the answer is a story. The heroes know this, and they imagine what will be said at their tombs and at the tombs of the men they kill.

## The passer-by

When Hector challenges the best of the Achaeans, he sets out what will happen to the loser's body. His own, if he dies, is to go home to be burned; his victim's is to be given back, so that the Achaeans may bury him and heap up a mound “beside the broad Hellespont” (7.76–86; see [[honoring-the-dead]]). Then he imagines the mound in years to come:

{{quote:49b62789-a319-5614-b469-0ea22b08bbda}}

The speaker is “someone” of the men born hereafter, a sailor who sees the mound from the sea and reads it (see [[tis-speech]]). He does not know the dead man's name, only that he died long ago and was the best in the fighting when Hector killed him. The tomb of one man has become the monument of another. Agamemnon had imagined the same scene the other way round: if Menelaus died, his bones would rot at Troy, and one of the Trojans would leap on his tomb and mock the Achaeans who went home in vain (4.174–182; see [[may-the-earth-gape]]). Both speeches end with the same half-line, “so someone will say one day” (ὥς ποτέ τις ἐρέει, 4.182, 7.91; see [[kleos]]).

The Odyssey builds the mound that Hector imagined. The Achaeans heaped a great tomb over the bones of Achilles and Patroclus “on a jutting headland, beside the broad Hellespont” (ἐπὶ πλατεῖ Ἑλλησπόντῳ, the words of 7.86), so that it might be seen far out at sea “by men, those who are born now and those who will be hereafter” (*Odyssey* 24.80–84; see [[hellespont]]). One of the two men it covers was killed by Hector.

Elsewhere in the Odyssey a tomb is the condition of fame. Telemachus says, and the swineherd Eumaeus after him, that if Odysseus had died at Troy the Achaeans would have made him a tomb, “and he would have won great fame for his son too in days to come”; as it is, the Harpies have snatched him away “without fame” (ἀκλειῶς, *Odyssey* 1.239–241, 14.369–371). Menelaus heaped a mound for Agamemnon in Egypt “so that his fame might be unquenchable” (ἄσβεστον κλέος, *Odyssey* 4.584), and the ghost of Elpenor asks for a mound on the shore, “of an unlucky man, for men to come to learn of” (*Odyssey* 11.75–76).

## A tomb without a name

The heroes speak as if the mound will keep their story. The poem shows that it may keep only the mound. Before the chariot race Nestor shows his son the turning post, a dry stump with two white stones leaning against it: “either the mound of some mortal who died long ago, or a turning post set up by men of earlier times” (23.326–333). The words are nearly Hector's (ἤ τευ σῆμα βροτοῖο πάλαι κατατεθνηῶτος, 23.331; ἀνδρὸς μὲν τόδε σῆμα πάλαι κατατεθνηῶτος, 7.89), but no one can now say who lies there, or whether anyone does. The plain of Troy has other old mounds that serve as landmarks: the tomb of Aesyetes, from which Polites watched the ships (2.793), the tomb of Ilus in the middle of the plain (10.415, 11.166), and the mound before the city that men call Batieia and the gods the tomb of Myrine (2.811–814; see [[batieia]]). Gregory Nagy made the turning post a starting point for his study of the σῆμα as a sign that has to be recognized and interpreted (“Sēma and Noēsis: Some Illustrations”, *Arethusa* 16, 1983).

## The wall and its fame

The Achaeans' own monument in Book 7 is a single mound heaped over all their dead, “one for all alike”, with no man set apart (7.336–337, 7.435–436; see [[the-truce-for-the-dead]]). Close against it they build a wall with towers and gates, and a ditch outside (7.337–341, 7.436–441; see [[the-achaean-wall]]). The gods watch the work, and Poseidon complains:

{{quote:d1ff4c64-f30c-58eb-b8b9-2a8e35dcc30e}}

Poseidon's fear is the fear of being forgotten. The fame of the new wall “will reach as far as the dawn spreads”, a phrase found only in this passage, and men “will forget” (ἐπιλήσονται) the wall that he and Apollo built for Laomedon (see [[the-walls-of-troy]] and [[poseidon-and-apollo-serve-laomedon]]). Zeus answers him with his own line, “your fame will reach as far as the dawn spreads”, and gives him leave, when the Achaeans have sailed home, to break the wall, sweep it into the sea and cover the beach with sand, “so that the great wall of the Achaeans may be brought to nothing” (7.455–463). In Book 12 the narrator tells how it was done. After Troy fell, Poseidon and Apollo turned the rivers of Ida against the wall for nine days while Zeus rained, where so many shields and helmets “and the race of men half-divine” had fallen, and left the shore smooth again (12.13–33; see [[men-of-today]]). It did not stand long, the narrator says, because “it was built against the will of the gods, the immortals” (12.8–9).

The wall survives only in the poem that tells of its destruction. Ruth Scodel read the story as part of a wider myth of the end of the race of heroes (“The Achaean Wall and the Myth of Destruction”, *Harvard Studies in Classical Philology* 86, 1982). Aristotle is reported to have said that the poet who made the wall also made it vanish (Strabo 13.1.36; see [[the-building-of-the-wall]]).

## Outside Homer

Hector's imagined words have the form that Greek verse epitaphs later took: the monument, the dead man and the manner of his death. The epitaph on the base of the kouros of Kroisos from Anavyssos in Attica, carved about 530 BCE and now in Athens, asks the passer-by to “stand and mourn beside the monument (σῆμα) of dead Kroisos, whom furious Ares destroyed one day as he fought in the front ranks” (see [[hectors-epitaph]]). The funeral speech that Thucydides gives to Pericles puts fame beyond any monument: “the whole earth is the tomb of famous men” (2.43.3; see [[thucydides]]). Horace claims for his poems what Poseidon feared from the Achaeans' wall, a monument “more lasting than bronze” (*Odes* 3.30.1; see [[horace]]).
