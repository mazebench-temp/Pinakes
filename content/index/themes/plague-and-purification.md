The Iliad opens with an epidemic. A god is angry; the army dies; a cause must be found and a cure applied. The pattern, a plague sent by a god for an offense and ended by expiation, recurs in Greek story, most famously at the opening of Sophocles' *Oedipus the King*.

## The plague

{{quote:81f71558-473e-5825-857f-3b7d2487a319}}

The narrator calls it an evil sickness ([[nousos|νοῦσος]], 1.10) and shows it as archery. Apollo sits apart from the ships and shoots, first the mules and the dogs, then the men, and the pyres of the dead burn constantly. It lasts nine days (1.53). Ancient readers asked why the god shot the animals first (see [[aristotle-mules]]).

## The diagnosis

{{quote:9d087dfc-ca3e-573e-9dca-8eee0c7316e6}}

Achilles names it [[loimos|λοιμός]], “plague”, and proposes consulting a seer, a priest or an interpreter of dreams. His guess is that the god is angry over some vow or hecatomb left unpaid. The seer Calchas gives the true cause: the dishonor of Apollo's priest (1.93–95). The cure has two parts. The girl must go back to her father without ransom, and a holy hecatomb must go to Chryse (1.97–100).

## Purification

{{quote:9a6d3887-abbf-5d2f-abbc-09d21bb8a279}}

While Odysseus sails to Chryse, Agamemnon orders the army to purify itself. They wash, throw the defilements (λύματα) into the sea, and offer hecatombs to Apollo on the shore, and the savor rises to heaven in the smoke. Homer seldom describes purification; here the whole army performs it. See [[purification]].

## Sacrifice and paean

{{quote:74549688-d5d0-56c2-a3cb-ff2fd29fe3d0}}

At Chryse, Odysseus returns Chryseis to her father, and Chryses prays in the words of his first prayer, now reversed: as the god once struck the Achaeans, let him now ward off the ruin ([[loigos|λοιγός]]). The sacrifice follows in full detail, then the feast. All day the young men of the Achaeans sing the paean to the Far-Worker, and his heart is glad as he listens (1.472–474). The next morning he sends them a following wind (1.479). See [[return-of-chryseis]], [[sacrifice]] and [[hecatomb]].

## Plague and war

Achilles fears “war and plague together” (1.61). The pair recurs in Greek thought. Hesiod names famine and plague together (λιμὸν ὁμοῦ καὶ λοιμόν) as the punishment Zeus sends on an unjust city (*Works and Days* 243). When plague struck Athens in the first years of the Peloponnesian War, Thucydides reports, people recalled an old verse, “a Dorian war will come, and plague with it” (2.54.2–3).
