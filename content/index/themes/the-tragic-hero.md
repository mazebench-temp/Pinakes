## The argument

Chapter 13 derives the hero from the emotions tragedy must arouse. Three patterns are excluded: the decent man passing from good fortune to bad, which is repugnant; the wicked man passing from bad to good, the most untragic thing of all; and the utter villain falling, which may satisfy human feeling but brings neither pity, felt for the undeserving, nor fear, felt for one like us (1452b34–1453a7; see [[pity]], [[phobos]] and [[philanthropon]]). What remains is the man between:

{{quote:f07108de-7934-5557-b006-a9a4205adb64}}

The fall should come through “a great error” of such a man, or of one “better rather than worse” (1453a15–17; see [[hamartia]]), and the families the best tragedies are made about are those in which such things happened: Alcmaeon, Oedipus, Orestes, Meleager, Thyestes, Telephus (1453a17–22; see [[the-few-houses]]). Chapter 15 adds that characters should be good, and chapter 2 that tragedy imitates people better than we are; the hero is better than us, but not so far above us that his suffering cannot be feared for ourselves.

## After Aristotle

The phrase “tragic hero” is modern; Aristotle never names a type of person, only the structure of a plot. Hegel's account of tragedy as the collision of two justified powers (*Lectures on Aesthetics*), and A. C. Bradley's Shakespearean heroes, each with “a marked imperfection or defect” that contributes to his fall (*Shakespearean Tragedy*, 1904), are the most influential later versions.
