Honor in the Iliad is standing that others acknowledge (see [[honor-and-dishonor]]), and speech is one of the ways they acknowledge it or withhold it. A man is praised for what he has done and is expected to do, and blamed for what he fails to do. Both are public. The poem's word for rebuke is νεικέω, and it has a range of others: ὄνειδος, the reproach thrown in a man's face; ἐνιπή, the rebuke of a superior; κερτόμια, taunts; ἐλέγχεα, “disgraces” (see [[neikos]], [[oneidos]], [[kertomia]] and [[elenchos]]). Praise uses the language of honor and fame (see [[time]] and [[kleos]]).

## Blame

The poem opens with a quarrel fought in blame. Agamemnon calls Calchas a “prophet of evils” (1.106); Achilles calls Agamemnon “clothed in shamelessness, with your mind set on gain”, and a “wine-sodden drunk, with the eyes of a dog and the heart of a deer” (1.149, 1.225). Achilles would rather be called a coward and a nobody than give way in everything (1.293–294). In Book 2 Thersites, “a man of unmeasured words”, abuses Agamemnon before the army, and Odysseus answers him with blows (2.212–277). In Book 3 Hector rebukes Paris as “best in looks, mad for women, seducer”, and Paris takes it well: “since you rebuke me fairly and not beyond what is fair” (3.39, 3.59). Helen, after the duel, rebukes him more bitterly (3.428–436).

Gregory Nagy argued that the tradition set praise and blame against each other as two kinds of poetry, and that Thersites, the ugliest man at Troy and the one most hateful to Achilles and Odysseus (2.216–221), is the figure of the blame poet inside the epic (*The Best of the Achaeans*, 1979).

## Rebuke as a spur

A leader's rebuke is meant to make men fight, and a good rebuke is answered by action. Agamemnon's review of the army in Book 4 is the fullest sequence of praise and blame in the poem (see [[the-review-of-the-army]]). Later, Sarpedon rebukes Hector, “where has the might gone that you used to have?” (5.472), Hector rebukes Paris again (6.325–331), and Glaucus rebukes Hector, “best in looks, you are far lacking in battle” (17.140–143). The rebuke works when it is fair, and when the man rebuked accepts it. When it is not, it breeds anger: Diomedes remembers Agamemnon's rebuke for five books, and answers it in the assembly of Book 9, “as is the custom, my lord, in the assembly” (9.32–36).

## The word returned

Aeneas, facing Achilles in Book 20, sums up the whole business. Both of them could speak reproaches (ὀνείδεα) enough to overload a ship of a hundred benches; “the tongue of mortals is a twisting thing”, and “whatever word you speak, that is the word you will hear back” (20.246–250).

## Outside Homer

Pindar, a poet of praise, sets himself against “blame-loving Archilochus”, fattening on hard-worded hatreds in his poverty (*Pythian* 2.52–56).
