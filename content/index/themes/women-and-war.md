## Cause and prize

The war is fought for a woman. The old men of Troy say there is no blame in suffering so long “for such a woman” (3.156–157), and the Achaeans fight to avenge “the struggles and groans for Helen's sake” (2.356; see [[helen]] and [[war-for-menelaus]]). Women are also its reward. The quarrel of Book 1 is over two captive women, and Nestor promises each man a Trojan's wife before he goes home (2.354–356; see [[captive-women]]). For the women of a city that falls, the war ends in slavery (see [[slavery-and-freedom]]).

## Watching, praying, weaving

The women of the Iliad see the war from the city. Helen watches the duel from the wall (3.121–244); Andromache hurries to the great tower when she hears that the Trojans are hard pressed, “like a woman out of her mind” (6.386–389; see [[like-a-woman-out-of-her-mind]]); and on the shield of Achilles the wives and children of the besieged city stand on the wall to guard it (18.514–515). See [[watching-the-war]].

They act for it in the ways open to them. At Helenus' word Hecuba gathers the old women, and the priestess Theano lays the finest robe on Athena's knees and prays for the city, its wives and its children (6.269–311; see [[the-robe-for-athena]] and [[theano]]). They weave: Helen weaves the war itself into a purple cloth (3.125–128), and directs her handmaids at their work while Paris polishes his armor (6.321–324); Andromache is at her loom, weaving flowers into a purple cloth, when she hears the wailing for Hector (22.440–441). See [[weaving]].

## Advising

In Book 6 three women speak to Hector, and one to Paris. Hecuba offers wine for strength (6.258–262), Helen urges Paris back to the war “with gentle words” (6.337–338), and Andromache gives Hector a plan of defense: post the army by the wild fig tree, where the wall can be scaled (6.433–439). The ancient editors rejected those lines as unfit for her (see [[andromaches-advice]]), and Hector answers with the division of labor: she is to go home to her loom and distaff, “and war will be the concern of men” (6.490–493; see [[war-is-the-concern-of-men]] and [[the-works-of-war]]).

## Mourning

The laments of the Iliad are women's. Hector's handmaids mourn him in his house while he still lives (6.499–502). Briseis, weeping over Patroclus, tells what war has taken from her (19.282–300), and at Hector's funeral the three women closest to him lament him in turn, Andromache, Hecuba and Helen (24.723–776). See [[lament]], [[tears]] and [[chere]].

## Like a woman

The fighting men define themselves against women. Thersites calls the army “Achaean women, Achaean men no longer” (2.235). Hector tells Ajax not to test him “as if I were some feeble boy or a woman, who does not know the works of war” (7.235–236), and mocks the retreating Diomedes, “you are no better than a woman” (8.163). Diomedes, wounded by Paris' arrow, says he cares no more than if a woman or a senseless child had hit him (11.389). Facing Achilles, Hector fears that if he lays down his arms he will be killed unarmed, “just like a woman” (22.124–125). Women who fight belong to the stories of the past, the Amazons, “the equals of men”, whom Priam fought as a young man and Bellerophon killed (3.189, 6.186; see [[amazons]]); among the gods, Athena, Hera and Artemis take the field.

Book 6 is the book of the women of Troy. Every scene inside the walls is played among women, and each of them speaks: Hecuba, Theano, Helen, the housekeeper, Andromache. See [[trojan-women]], [[handmaids]] and [[the-city-and-the-household]].
