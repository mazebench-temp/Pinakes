The proem promises deaths: the wrath of Achilles “hurled down to Hades many mighty souls of heroes, and made the men themselves the spoil of dogs and of every bird” (1.3–5). The first deaths of the poem are the plague's: Apollo's arrows strike the mules and the dogs and then the men, and “the pyres of the dead burned on, thick and constant” (1.50–52). The first deaths in battle wait until the end of Book 4. Before them come three books of assemblies, a catalogue and a duel in which, against its own terms, no one dies (3.101–102, 3.373–382). From then on killing is the poem's main business.

## Named

Nearly every man the Iliad shows killed is named, and many are given a little history as they fall. Axylus lived in a house by the road and was a friend to all men, and entertained everyone who passed; none of them was there to save him (6.12–19). Iphidamas was raised in Thrace by his grandfather, married his aunt, and left his bride to come to Troy, “from whom he had seen no joy, though he had given much” (11.221–245). Othryoneus came for the fame of the war and asked for Cassandra without a bride-price, promising to drive the Achaeans from Troy (13.363–382). The Catalogue of Ships has already given such lines to men whose deaths lie ahead or behind: Protesilaus, the first Achaean killed at Troy, who left a wife with her cheeks torn in mourning and a house half-finished (2.698–702), and the Mysian augur whose birds did not save him from black doom (2.858–861). Jasper Griffin showed how much these short histories add to the weight of each death (*Homer on Life and Death*, 1980).

Often the history is a family. The two sons of Phaenops leave their father, worn down by old age, with lamentation and sorrow, and distant kin divide his property (5.152–158). Simoeisius dies without repaying his parents for his rearing (4.477–478; see [[threptra]]).

## How death is told

The poem has a small set of phrases for the moment of death, and it repeats them: darkness covered his eyes; his limbs were loosened; his spirit left him, or was breathed out, or taken away; he fell with a thud, and his armor clattered upon him (see [[darkness-covered-his-eyes]], [[loosened-his-limbs]], [[fell-with-a-thud]] and [[thymos]]). Around them the wounds are told with exact anatomy: the point through the forehead into the bone, through both temples, beside the navel so that the bowels pour out (4.460–461, 4.502, 4.525–526). A falling man is a tower, or a poplar cut down by a chariot-maker and left to dry by the river (4.462, 4.482–487). When Patroclus and Hector die, the soul goes down to Hades, “lamenting its fate, leaving manhood and youth” (16.856–857, 22.362–363). See [[androktasia]].

## After death

A dead man's body is fought over. The victor wants the armor, and the dead man's companions want to save the body from being stripped and from the dogs and birds of the proem. The first battle already has the whole pattern, and the fights over Sarpedon and Patroclus are its largest forms (16.419–683, Book 17). Deaths call for more deaths, as companions and brothers take revenge (see [[kasignetos]] and [[hetairos]]).

## Why

The Iliad's warriors know why they fight. Sarpedon tells Glaucus that if they could escape the war and live forever, ageless and deathless, he would not fight in the front rank; “but now, since in any case the fates of death stand over us, countless, which no mortal can flee or escape, let us go, and either hold out triumph to another, or another to us” (12.322–328). Achilles, before he kills Lycaon, says the same thing without the hope: “Patroclus died too, who was far better than you”, and death and strong fate are on him as well (21.106–113). See [[gods-and-mortals]] and [[the-short-life-of-achilles]].

## Outside Homer

Simone Weil's essay “The Iliad, or the Poem of Force” (1940–41) reads the poem as a record of what force does to the men who suffer it and the men who use it, turning both into things. Alice Oswald's *Memorial* (2011) strips the Iliad down to its dead: it opens with a list of their names, and then gives each man the few lines the poem gives him, set among its similes.
