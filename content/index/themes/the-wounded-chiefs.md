In Book 11 the best of the Achaeans are wounded one after another, in the hours after the woodcutter's meal on the third day of battle (see [[the-third-day-of-battle]]). The book kills many men on both sides, but none of these five. Taking its leading Achaeans out of the fighting without killing them, the poem opens the way for Hector to reach the ships while Achilles sits apart, as Thetis asked and Zeus promised with his nod (1.508–510, 1.524–527; see [[plan-of-zeus]]).

## Five wounds

- **Agamemnon**, at the height of his aristeia. Coon, the eldest son of Antenor, stabs him through the forearm below the elbow; Agamemnon kills him over his brother's body and fights on while the blood is warm, until sharp pains come over him like a woman's labor pangs and he drives back to the ships (11.248–283; see [[agamemnon]] and [[the-aristeia-of-agamemnon]]).
- **Diomedes**. Paris, leaning on the pillar of Ilus' tomb, shoots him through the flat of the right foot as he strips Agastrophus. Diomedes calls it a scratch, but Odysseus must stand in front of him while he draws the arrow out, and he leaves (11.369–400; see [[diomedes]] and [[paris]]).
- **Odysseus**. Socus' spear goes through his shield and corselet and tears the skin from his ribs; Athena keeps it from his entrails (11.434–438). He shouts three times for help, and Menelaus leads him out by the hand while Ajax covers them (11.459–488; see [[odysseus]]).
- **Machaon**, the healer. Paris hits him in the right shoulder with a three-barbed arrow, and Idomeneus sends Nestor to carry him off, “for a healer is a man worth many others” (11.505–520; see [[machaon]] and [[a-healer-is-worth-many]]).
- **Eurypylus**, coming to the help of Ajax. Paris shoots him in the right thigh, and the reed breaks off in the wound (11.575–585; see [[eurypylus]]).

Paris' arrows account for three of the five. The two men who wound with the spear, Coon and Socus, are killed at once by the men they wounded (11.256–261, 11.446–449); the archer, shooting from cover, is not touched, and Diomedes can only tell him what would happen “if you should make trial of me face to face in armor” (11.386; see [[bow]]).

## Counted

The wounds are counted twice, in the same verses, by the two men who send Patroclus on his way. Nestor answers Achilles' question with them: “the best men lie among the ships, shot or stabbed” (11.658–659). He names them in two kinds, those hit by missiles and those stabbed with the spear (βέβληται, οὔτασται): Diomedes shot, Odysseus and Agamemnon stabbed, Eurypylus shot in the thigh, and the man he has just brought in (11.660–664). He names Eurypylus, though he left the field with Machaon before Paris shot him (see the translation's note on 11.662). Eurypylus tells Patroclus the same, “all those who were the best before lie among the ships, shot or stabbed” (11.825–826), and adds that the healers are no help: Machaon lies wounded in the huts, “himself in need of a noble healer”, and Podalirius is fighting on the plain (11.833–836; see [[podalirius]] and [[healing]]).

## What the wounds set in motion

Achilles, watching from the stern of his ship, sees Nestor's chariot carry a wounded man past and sends Patroclus to find out who it is (11.599–615); the narrator calls the errand “the beginning of evil for him” (11.604; see [[the-beginning-of-evils]]). Nestor turns the count of the wounded into a reproach of Achilles, who “does not care for the Danaans or pity them” (11.665), and the reproach into a plan: let Patroclus go out in Achilles' armor (11.794–803). On the way back the sight of Eurypylus, limping out of the battle, stops Patroclus; he “pitied him”, and tends the wound with the drugs Achilles learned from Chiron (11.809–848; see [[pity]] and [[chiron]]). When at last he reaches Achilles, his first argument is the same list in the same verses (16.23–27 ≈ 11.825–826, 11.660–662), with the healers, ἰητροὶ πολυφάρμακοι, “rich in drugs”, busy about them (16.28–29). See [[patroclus]] and [[the-death-of-patroclus]].

## Later in the poem

The wounded do not fight again that day, but they do not vanish. Machaon sits drinking in Nestor's hut while Hecamede heats a bath for him (14.1–8; see [[hecamede]]). Diomedes, Odysseus and Agamemnon come up from the ships, leaning on their spears, to look at the battle; Nestor tells them that a wounded man cannot fight (14.27–40, 14.62–63), and Diomedes persuades them to go into the battle all the same, keeping out of range so that no one gets “wound on wound” (ἐφʼ ἕλκεϊ ἕλκος), and urging the others on (14.128–134). They marshal the army and see that the best armor goes to the best men (14.379–382). Patroclus stays with Eurypylus, easing his wound with drugs and talk, until he sees the Trojans storm the wall (15.390–404; see [[the-persuasion-of-a-friend]]). On the next morning Diomedes and Odysseus limp to the assembly leaning on their spears, and Agamemnon comes last, with the wound that Coon gave him (19.47–53).

## Outside Homer

Plato's Socrates recalls the wounded at Troy to show that the sons of Asclepius cured wounds with drugs and did not nurse a sickly life with diets. He gives Eurypylus the drink of Pramnian wine, barley and grated cheese that Hecamede made for Machaon, and says that they did not blame the woman who gave it, nor Patroclus who was treating him, and prescribed nothing more for him, or for Menelaus, once the drugs were on the wound (*Republic* 3.405e–406a, 408a; see [[plato-asclepiads]]).
