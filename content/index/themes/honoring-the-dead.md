The dead in Homer are owed a set of acts, and the poem names them as a due. The body is washed and laid out, the kin lament over it, it is burned on a pyre, the bones are gathered, and a mound is heaped with a stone set on it: “that is the prize of the dead” (τὸ γὰρ γέρας ἐστὶ θανόντων, 16.457, 16.675; of lament, 23.9; see [[geras]], [[lament]], [[cremation]] and [[tomb]]). Until the body is burned, the dead man cannot pass the gates of Hades. The ghost of Patroclus asks Achilles to bury him “as quickly as possible”, since the souls keep him away, and says that he will not come back from Hades once they have given him his portion of fire (23.71–76; see [[psyche]]). The poem's first lines name the other fate, the one it holds over all its dead: to be left as “the spoil of dogs and of every bird” (1.4–5; see [[dogs]] and [[birds]]).

## The body given back

Hector's challenge in Book 7 makes the body a term of the fight. If he is killed, the victor may strip his armor but must give his body back, “so that when I am dead the Trojans and the wives of the Trojans may give me my portion of fire”; if he kills his man, he will hang the armor in Apollo's temple and give the corpse back to the ships for burial (7.76–86; see [[stripping-the-dead]] and [[fame-and-the-tomb]]). No one dies in the duel, and the terms are not tested until the end of the poem. Facing Achilles, Hector asks for them again in the same words, “give my body back to be taken home, so that when I am dead the Trojans and the wives of the Trojans may give me my portion of fire” (22.342–343 = 7.79–80). Achilles refuses: the dogs and the birds will share out all of him (22.345–354).

Between those two scenes the gods show what the dead are owed. At Zeus' bidding Apollo washes Sarpedon's body in the river, anoints it with ambrosia, and has Sleep and Death carry it to Lycia, where his kin will bury him “with a mound and a gravestone” (16.666–683; see [[sarpedon]]). Thetis keeps the flies from Patroclus' body with ambrosia and nectar (19.23–39). Aphrodite keeps the dogs from Hector's, and Apollo covers it with a dark cloud and then with his golden aegis, so that the dragging does not tear it (23.184–191, 24.18–21). Apollo tells the gods that Achilles, in his fury, “does outrage to the senseless earth” (κωφὴν γαῖαν, 24.54; see [[water-and-earth]]). The last book is a ransom for a body, and it ends with a funeral.

## The truce for the dead

Book 7 has the poem's first truce for the dead, and both sides want it. Nestor proposes that the Achaeans stop fighting at dawn, gather their dead with oxen and mules, and burn them near the ships (7.327–335). Priam, the same evening, sends his herald to ask for a truce “until we have burned the dead” (7.375–377, 7.394–396). Agamemnon refuses the Trojan offer of Helen's goods but grants the truce, with a maxim and an oath (7.406–411; see [[the-comfort-of-fire]] and [[oath]]). Then both sides go out to the plain:

{{quote:eb92c69c-e76d-580f-88b5-2de23e902772}}

The scene is almost wordless. “It was hard to tell each man apart” until the clotted blood was washed away (7.424–425). The Trojans weep as they lift their dead onto the wagons, but Priam does not allow them to wail, and they heap the pyre in silence (7.426–428); the narrator does not say why (see [[tears]] and [[silence]]). The Achaeans do the same, and before the next dawn they heap a single mound over their pyre (7.430–436). Nestor's plan had one more clause, that each man would carry the bones home to the children of the dead (7.334–335). Some ancient critics rejected those lines, since the dead are then buried together at Troy, and elsewhere in the Iliad the bones of the dead stay there (4.174–175, 23.83–92; see [[nostos]]).

The last book performs the same rite for one man. Priam asks Achilles for eleven days to mourn, burn and bury Hector, and Achilles holds back the war (24.656–670). The Trojans yoke oxen and mules to wagons and gather wood for nine days; on the tenth they burn him; at dawn the people is astir around the pyre, in the words of 7.434 (24.788–789); and they gather the white bones, lay them in a golden chest and heap a mound (24.782–801). See [[the-truce-for-the-dead]].

## Outside Homer

In the wars of classical Greece the side that sent heralds to ask for a truce to take up its dead thereby admitted defeat, and to refuse the dead to their own people was an outrage against the customs of the Greeks. Euripides' *Suppliants* is built on such a refusal: the Thebans will not give up the Argive dead after the war of the Seven, and Theseus recovers them by force (see [[euripides]]). Thucydides describes the Athenian public funeral for the dead of each year of war: their bones are brought home and laid out, then carried in cypress coffins, one for each tribe, to the public tomb outside the city; only the dead of Marathon were buried where they fell, for their outstanding courage (2.34; see [[thucydides]]). In the first stasimon of Aeschylus' *Agamemnon* the chorus sings of Ares, “the money-changer of bodies”, who sends home from Troy urns of ash in place of men, while others lie in the Trojan earth they won (see [[aeschylus]]).
