## The argument

A well-made plot, Aristotle says, must be single rather than double, “as some say it should be”, changing from good fortune to misfortune (1453a12–15). The double structure, which rewards the good and punishes the bad, seems best only because of “the weakness of the audiences”, and the poets follow them, composing to suit their wishes (1453a33–35). Its pleasure is comic: in comedy “the bitterest enemies in the story, such as Orestes and Aegisthus, go off at the end as friends, and nobody is killed by anybody” (1453a36–39; see [[comedy]] and [[aegisthus]]). The example is the Odyssey, where Odysseus is saved and the suitors destroyed (see [[odyssey]]). The English name for such endings, “poetical justice”, is Thomas Rymer's (*The Tragedies of the Last Age Consider'd*, 1678).
