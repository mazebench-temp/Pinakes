## How the free become slaves

The Iliad seldom says where its slaves came from. What it shows is the moment at which free people become slaves, and that moment is the fall of a city. Meleager's wife, begging him to fight, lists what happens then: the men are killed, the city burned, “and strangers lead away the children and the deep-girdled women” (9.593–594). Priam foresees it for Troy, his sons killed, his daughters dragged away, his bridal chambers ravaged, infants dashed to the ground, “and my sons' wives dragged off by the deadly hands of the Achaeans” (22.62–65). Agamemnon makes it a promise to his army: “we will lead away their dear wives and little children in our ships, when we take the citadel” (4.238–239). The women of the sacked towns of the Troad are already in the camp, and two of them start the quarrel of Book 1 (see [[captive-women]]).

Men taken alive are worth a price. Before Patroclus died, Achilles took many Trojans alive and sold them (21.101–102). He sold Lycaon, a son of Priam, to Lemnos, and Lycaon, caught again, says that he fetched “the worth of a hundred oxen” then and offers three times as much now (21.40–44, 21.78–80); Hecuba remembers her other sons sold across the sea to Samos, Imbros and Lemnos (24.751–753). Or they are ransomed by their fathers (see [[apoina]]). Persons are priced as goods are, in oxen. At the funeral games a woman “skilled in much handiwork” is the second prize, valued at four oxen (23.704–705); Eurycleia was bought for twenty (*Odyssey* 1.430–431); and Glaucus' golden armor was worth a hundred (6.236). In the Odyssey slaves change hands in peace as well as war. Taphian pirates and Phoenician traders steal women and children and sell them across the sea, as the swineherd Eumaeus was stolen as a child and bought by Laertes (*Odyssey* 15.427–429, 15.449–453, 15.483), and Eumaeus has bought a slave of his own (*Odyssey* 14.449–452; see [[mesaulius]], [[trade]], [[piracy]] and [[eumaeus]]).

## What a slave does

Agamemnon tells Chryses what his daughter's life will be: old age “in our house, in Argos, far from her native land, as she walks to and fro at the loom and comes to share my bed” (1.29–31). Hector's picture of Andromache's future is the same life. She will be led away in tears, and “in Argos you may weave at the loom at another woman's bidding, and carry water from Messeis or Hypereia, much against your will, and harsh necessity will lie upon you” (6.456–458; see [[weaving]], [[messeis]] and [[hypereia]]). The houses of Troy have their own slave women (δμῳαί): Helen sits among hers at their work, and it is Hector's own slave women whom he asks where his wife has gone (6.323–324, 6.375–376; see [[handmaids]]). The Odyssey shows the hardest of the work, a woman grinding grain through the night for the suitors (*Odyssey* 20.105–119), and gives the verdict in the words of Eumaeus, who was sold as a child: “far-seeing Zeus takes away half the good / of a man, when the day of slavery takes him” (*Odyssey* 17.322–323, a plain rendering; see [[doulios]]).

## Days of freedom and slavery

Homer has no noun for freedom and uses the adjective “free” only of a day and of a mixing bowl (see [[eleutheros]]). ἐλεύθερον ἦμαρ, “the day of freedom”, is what a conqueror takes from the women of a city (6.455, 16.831, 20.193); δούλιον ἦμαρ, “the day of slavery”, is what Hector will not be there to ward off (6.463; see [[doulios]]). The condition is a day because it comes on a single day, the day the city falls. Hector's vision ends with the moment itself, “your cries as they drag you away” (σοῦ θʼ ἑλκηθμοῖο, 6.465), and the verb is the one Priam uses of his daughters (ἑλκηθείσας, 22.62). Against it he sets a hope, “the mixing bowl of freedom” that the Trojans may set up for the gods when the Achaeans are driven off (6.528).

## Outside Homer

Plato sets the two opinions about slaves side by side, and takes the second from the Odyssey. Everyone says that slaves should be as well disposed and as good as possible, for many have had slaves better in every virtue than brothers or sons, who saved their masters, their goods and their whole houses; but it is also said that there is nothing sound in a slave's soul, and “the wisest of our poets” declared, speaking for Zeus, that far-seeing Zeus takes away half the understanding of men “whomever the day of slavery lays hold of”. Plato's text of the verse has νόου, “understanding”, where ours has ἀρετῆς, “good” (*Laws* 6.776d–777a, quoting *Odyssey* 17.322–323; see [[doulios]] and [[plato]]). The treatise on household management ascribed to Aristotle divides slaves into two kinds, the overseer and the worker, gives the more liberal of them a share of honor, and holds it just and expedient that freedom be set before them as a prize (*Oeconomica* 1.5). Aristotle himself denies that there can be friendship with a slave in so far as he is a slave, “but there is in so far as he is a man” (*Nicomachean Ethics* 8.11, 1161b5–6; see [[aristotle]]). His *Constitution of the Ithacans*, as Plutarch reports it, carried the swineherd's story beyond the poem: after the slaying of the suitors Telemachus freed the people around Eumaeus and made them citizens, and the Ithacan clan of the Koliadai traced its descent from Eumaeus (*Constitution of the Ithacans* fr. 511 Gigon).
