## The theme

A warrior's armor is his own: it is described when he puts it on, it is what his killer strips from him, and it is what the enemy sees coming (see [[arming-scene]] and [[stripping-the-dead]]). Yet armor passes from man to man among the living, by gift, by loan, and by order, and the poem uses one verb for the exchange, ἀμείβω, “exchange, give in turn”.

**As a gift.** Glaucus and Diomedes, finding that their grandfathers were guest-friends, agree to exchange armor, “so that these men too / may know that we claim to be guest-friends from our fathers' time”; and Glaucus, whose wits Zeus took away, “exchanged armor with Diomedes, son of Tydeus, / gold for bronze, the worth of a hundred oxen for the worth of nine” (τεύχεʼ ἄμειβε, 6.230–236; see [[glaucus-and-diomedes]] and [[guest-gifts]]). Hector and Ajax, parting after their duel, give each other a sword and a belt (7.303–305).

**By order.** In *Iliad* 14 Poseidon, leading the Achaeans while Zeus sleeps, orders the best and biggest shields, the all-gleaming helmets and the longest spears to go to the men who will use them: “And whoever is a man steadfast in battle, but has a small shield on his shoulder, / let him give it to a worse man, and himself put on a bigger shield” (14.370–377; see [[poseidon]] and [[shield]]). The wounded kings carry it out, going among them all: “they exchanged the armor of war: / the brave man put on the good, and gave the worse to the worse man” (ἀρήϊα τεύχεʼ ἄμειβον· / ἐσθλὰ μὲν ἐσθλὸς ἔδυνε, χέρεια δὲ χείρονι δόσκεν, 14.381–382; see [[the-wounded-chiefs]]). The verse sorts the army by worth in its own sounds, ἐσθλὰ … ἐσθλός, χέρεια … χείρονι (see [[polyptoton]]). Nestor, at the start of the book, has already taken his son's shield, “and the son had his father's shield” (14.9–11; see [[nestor]] and [[thrasymedes]]).

**On loan.** For the night raid Thrasymedes gives Diomedes a sword, a shield and a helmet, and Meriones gives Odysseus a bow, a quiver, a sword and the boar's-tusk helmet (10.255–271; see [[the-doloneia]]). Patroclus asks Achilles for his armor, so that the Trojans may take him for Achilles and draw back (16.40–43), and when they see him in it, they think that Achilles has given up his wrath (16.278–282).

**From the dead.** Hector, having stripped Patroclus, steps apart from the battle and “changed his armor” (ἔντεʼ ἄμειβεν, 17.192), sending his own to Troy and putting on the immortal armor of Achilles, which the gods gave to Peleus (17.192–197; see [[the-armor-of-peleus]]).
